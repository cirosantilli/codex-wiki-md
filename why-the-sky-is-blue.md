# Why the sky is blue

↑ **Parent:** [Rayleigh scattering](rayleigh-scattering.md)

In a clear [terrestrial atmosphere](terrestrial-atmosphere.md), [Rayleigh scattering](rayleigh-scattering.md) by air [molecules](molecule.md) redirects some sunlight toward an observer looking away from the [Sun](sun.md). Shorter [wavelengths](wavelength.md) are preferentially scattered, enriching the scattered [light](light.md) in blue compared with the incoming sunlight. Along the longer atmospheric path to the low [Sun](sun.md), more short-[wavelength](wavelength.md) [light](light.md) is removed from the direct beam, helping produce red sunsets. [NASA Space Place, Why Is the Sky Blue?: scattered sunlight and red sunsets](https://spaceplace.nasa.gov/blue-sky/en/).

Violet [light](light.md) has an even shorter [wavelength](wavelength.md) than blue [light](light.md), so the [Rayleigh scattering wavelength dependence](rayleigh-scattering-wavelength-dependence.md) alone cannot predict the perceived sky color. The incoming solar [optical spectrum](optical-spectrum.md) and the eye's differing response to [wavelengths](wavelength.md) both matter; human vision is less sensitive to violet than to blue. [NOAA National Weather Service, Why Is The Sky Blue?: solar spectrum and visual response](https://www.weather.gov/fgz/SkyBlue). [Multiple scattering](multiple-scattering.md) and other atmospheric particles also affect the observed sky. Cloud droplets are generally too large for this simple [Rayleigh scattering](rayleigh-scattering.md) model: [Mie scattering](mie-scattering.md) describes spherical droplets, with much weaker visible [wavelength](wavelength.md) selectivity, while repeated scattering helps make clouds appear white. [NASA GISS, Aerosols in the Atmosphere: particle size, Mie scattering and white clouds](https://www.giss.nasa.gov/edu/icp/research/ppa/1997/reyes/).
