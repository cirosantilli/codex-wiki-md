# Existence hypothesis of the monodromy theorem

↑ **Parent:** [Monodromy theorem](monodromy-theorem.md)

The [monodromy theorem](monodromy-theorem.md) gives equality of continuations that exist under its hypothesis. It does not assert that every [holomorphic function](holomorphic-function-split.md) admits [analytic continuation](analytic-continuation.md) everywhere, even within a [simply connected domain](simply-connected-domain.md).

For an explicit obstruction, take $f(z)=1/(1-z)$ near $a=0$ and attempt to apply the theorem on $D=\mathbb C$. The [path](continuous-path.md) $\eta(t)=t$ ends at $1$, where no terminal [germ of a holomorphic function](germ-of-a-holomorphic-function.md) exists. If a [holomorphic](holomorphic-function-split.md) representative $G$ near $1$ continued $f$, then the [identity theorem](identity-theorem.md) would extend $(1-z)G(z)=1$ to that [neighbourhood](neighbourhood-mathematics.md). Substituting $z=1$ would give $0=1$. Thus the [pole](pole.md) prevents the existence hypothesis, although $\mathbb C$ is a [simply connected domain](simply-connected-domain.md). The [analytic continuation](analytic-continuation.md) article explains why the same [rational function](rational-function.md) does continue throughout $\mathbb C\setminus\{1\}$.

Existence at an endpoint by some route is also weaker than existence along every [path](continuous-path.md) from the base point. Along the real segment from $1$ towards $0$, a [holomorphic square root](holomorphic-square-root.md) of $z$ tends to $0$, yet cannot continue holomorphically through $0$: a local representative $G$ would satisfy $G^2=z$ by the [identity theorem](identity-theorem.md), and differentiating at $0$ would give $2G(0)G'(0)=1$ with $G(0)=0$. A finite limiting value therefore does not remove a [branch point](branch-point.md). More severe obstructions appear at a [natural boundary of a holomorphic function](natural-boundary-of-a-holomorphic-function.md).
