<h1 id="s2/2/b/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [B](../b.md)

The [effective radius](../../../../../effective-radius.md) is one tenth of the optical outer [radius](../../../../../radius.md): $R_e\simeq2.13$ kpc. A flat [rotation](../../../../../rotation-mathematics.md) [speed](../../../../../speed.md) then gives $M_{\rm dyn}(<R_e)\simeq4.9\times10^{10}M_\odot$. Half the [luminosity](../../../../../luminosity.md), with the same [mass-to-light ratio](../../../../../mass-to-light-ratio.md), implies $M_{\rm luminous}(<R_e)\simeq4.9\times10^{10}M_\odot$.

$$
\boxed{\text{The inner mass budget does not require a large dark-matter fraction.}}
$$

The concentration of light into a small fraction of the optical [radius](../../../../../radius.md) suggests a high-surface-brightness, centrally concentrated disk [galaxy](../../../../../galaxy-split.md), plausibly an early-type spiral or lenticular with a substantial bulge. The kinematic and photometric data do not uniquely distinguish those morphologies. Disk geometry, an unknown inclination correction, or a varying stellar [mass-to-light ratio](../../../../../mass-to-light-ratio.md) would alter the exact numerical inner comparison.
