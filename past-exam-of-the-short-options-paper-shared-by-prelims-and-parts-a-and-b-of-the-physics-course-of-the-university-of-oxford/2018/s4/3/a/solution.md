<h1 id="s4/3/a/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [A](../a.md)

Fission divides a heavy nucleus into more tightly bound fragments, releasing [energy](../../../../../energy-split.md) through the increase in [binding energy per nucleon](../../../../../binding-energy-per-nucleon.md). A neutron-induced chain reaction can sustain [heat](../../../../../heat-split.md) production in a controlled reactor. Fusion combines light nuclei into more tightly bound products and needs sufficiently high [temperature](../../../../../temperature-split.md) and confinement to obtain a useful reaction rate despite Coulomb repulsion. Both ultimately supply [heat](../../../../../heat-split.md) that must be converted to electricity, unless a different energy-conversion scheme is explicitly introduced.

A typical fission releases about $200$ MeV per event, corresponding to roughly $8\times10^{13}$ J per kilogram of fully fissioned heavy fuel. The deuterium-tritium fusion [energy](../../../../../energy-split.md) of $17.6$ MeV per reaction corresponds to about $3.4\times10^{14}$ J per kilogram of reacting mixture. These are [nuclear reaction](../../../../../nuclear-reaction.md) energies, not electricity per kilogram of mined or engineered fuel. Actual fuel use, thermal efficiency, parasitic [power](../../../../../power.md) and incomplete burn change plant output.

Fission has substantial operating experience and can provide high-capacity-factor generation, but faces capital, waste, decommissioning, fuel-cycle and safety constraints. Fusion avoids a self-sustaining [neutron](../../../../../neutron-split.md) chain reaction in the fuel, but its systems still face [neutron](../../../../../neutron-split.md) damage, activated materials, tritium management and demanding [heat](../../../../../heat-split.md) extraction. Demonstrating a hot [plasma](../../../../../plasma-physics.md) or a favorable reaction-energy balance is not the same as demonstrating economical net electricity. The useful comparison distinguishes scientific feasibility, engineering availability and whole-system cost.
