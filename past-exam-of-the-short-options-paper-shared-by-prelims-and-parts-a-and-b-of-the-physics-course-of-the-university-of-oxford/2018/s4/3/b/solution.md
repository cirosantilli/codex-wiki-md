<h1 id="s4/3/b/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [B](../b.md)

Distributed generation changes the shape of infrastructure rather than eliminating it. A $1$ GW [power](../../../../../power.md) station is large, but supplying a national mean demand of tens of gigawatts from smaller intermittent generators still requires transmission, distribution, [power](../../../../../power.md) conversion, forecasting, balancing and often storage or dispatchable reserve. Geographic diversity can reduce weather correlations only if [power](../../../../../power.md) can move between regions.

For scale, delivering $30$ GW through a network is a continuous transfer of $30$ billion joules each second. Even a distributed average installation of $3$ kW per household needs ten million participating households to reach that [power](../../../../../power.md), before allowing for [capacity factor](../../../../../capacity-factor.md). Solar roofs can reduce some local flows while export peaks or winter deficits increase others. District [heat](../../../../../heat-split.md), offshore wind connections, interconnectors and fuel transport also have economies of scale.

Large centralized assets can introduce single-point failure, slow construction and financing risk; modular systems can be installed incrementally and offer local control. Conversely, network reinforcement and seasonal adequacy cannot be inferred from the size of an individual panel. A defensible conclusion is that an effective mix may combine small generators with large shared infrastructure. The categorical claim that large-scale infrastructure has become obsolete does not follow from the existence of distributed generation.
