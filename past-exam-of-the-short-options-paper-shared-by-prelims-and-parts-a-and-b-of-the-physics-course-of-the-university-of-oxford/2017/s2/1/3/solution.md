<h1 id="s2/1/3/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [3](../3.md)

A [luminosity](../../../../../luminosity.md) does not determine a stellar [mass](../../../../../mass.md) without a [mass-luminosity](../../../../../mass-luminosity-relation.md) [calibration](../../../../../calibration-differential-geometry.md). Using the usual approximate [main-sequence](../../../../../main-sequence.md) law $L/L_\odot=(M_s/M_\odot)^\alpha$ gives $M_s=3^{1/\alpha}M_\odot$. With $\alpha=3.5$, the numerical results are

$$
\boxed{M_s\simeq1.37M_\odot,\quad M_p\simeq0.39M_J,\quad a\simeq0.101\ \mathrm{AU},\quad \vartheta\simeq2.46\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{arcsec}.}
$$

The angular [radius](../../../../../radius.md) follows directly from $a(\mathrm{AU})/d(\mathrm{pc})$. Taking $\alpha=4$ changes these estimates by only a few percent, but the [calibration](../../../../../calibration-differential-geometry.md) must be stated. Hubble's 2.4 m aperture has visible-light diffraction scale $1.22\lambda/D\simeq0.05$ arcsec at 500 nm, hundreds of times larger. The separation is therefore unresolved even before the severe star/planet contrast is considered.
