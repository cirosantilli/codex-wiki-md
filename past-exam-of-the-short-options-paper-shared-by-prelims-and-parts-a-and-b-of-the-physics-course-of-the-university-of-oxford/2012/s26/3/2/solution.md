<h1 id="s26/3/2/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [2](../2.md)

Massive young stars emit ionizing ultraviolet radiation that creates [H II regions](../../../../../h-ii-region.md). Recombination and radiative cascades produce [H-alpha](../../../../../h-alpha.md) emission, including the $n=3\to2$ transition. Shocks or other ionizing sources can also produce it, but disk star-forming gas is the natural interpretation for an extended spiral-galaxy rotation measurement.

The systemic [redshift](../../../../../redshift.md) and inclination of an intrinsically circular thin disk are

$$
\boxed{z=\frac{689.1-656.3}{656.3}\simeq0.04998,\qquad
\cos i=\frac{7.5}{30}=\frac14,\quad i\simeq75.52^\circ.}
$$

The rotation-induced half-width is $(689.7-688.5)/2=0.6\,\mathrm{nm}$. Divide by the systemic observed wavelength to remove the common redshift factor before applying the small-velocity [Doppler shift](../../../../../doppler-effect.md):

$$
v_{\rm los}=c\frac{0.6}{689.1}\simeq261.0\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}},\qquad
\boxed{v_{\max}=\frac{v_{\rm los}}{\sin i}\simeq269.6\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}.}
$$

The circular-orbit and thin-disk assumptions neglect intrinsic thickness, warps and noncircular gas motion.
