<h1 id="s4/3/e/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [E](../e.md)

A common minimum [carbon price](../../../../../carbon-price.md) can make the external cost of emissions visible in investment and dispatch decisions, encourage reductions where they are cheapest, and reduce incentives to relocate emissions-intensive production to unpriced jurisdictions. A credible rising or durable price signal can be more useful for long-lived investment than a volatile short-term spot price.

For an illustrative price of $40$ currency units per tonne of [carbon dioxide](../../../../../carbon-dioxide.md), generation emitting $0.9$ tonnes per MWh incurs $36$ units per MWh, while generation at $0.4$ tonnes per MWh incurs $16$. Those changes are large enough to affect some operating choices, but the actual switching point depends on fuel prices, efficiency and capacity constraints.

A price is not sufficient by itself. Measurement, enforcement, distributional effects and revenue use matter; infrastructure, innovation spillovers, information failures and landlord-tenant incentives can justify complementary policies. Nor is a uniform international minimum a logical prerequisite for any effective reduction: standards, clean-power deployment and efficiency policies can reduce emissions even before every country agrees. The strongest defensible claim is that broad, credible pricing is a valuable coordination instrument, not a uniquely necessary or automatically sufficient solution.[Chapter 11, policy, financing and implementation: instruments operate within wider institutional and market conditions](../../../../../ipcc-special-report-on-renewable-energy-sources-and-climate-change-mitigation-2011.md)
