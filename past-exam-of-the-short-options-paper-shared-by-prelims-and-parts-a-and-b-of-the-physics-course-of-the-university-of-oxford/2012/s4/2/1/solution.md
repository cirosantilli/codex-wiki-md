<h1 id="s4/2/1/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [1](../1.md)

An ideal [Rankine cycle](../../../../../rankine-cycle.md) has four main steps: liquid water is pumped reversibly to high pressure; heat is added at high pressure to produce hot vapour or supercritical working fluid; the fluid expands approximately isentropically through a turbine to produce work; and low-pressure heat rejection in a condenser returns it to liquid. For steady flow, the main work and heat transfers are differences of [specific enthalpy](../../../../../specific-enthalpy.md).

A steam implementation of the [Carnot cycle](../../../../../carnot-cycle.md) would require compression of a wet liquid-vapour mixture and reversible isothermal heat transfer, both difficult to achieve with practical machinery. Complete condensation before the Rankine pump makes compression work small because liquid specific volume is small. High-pressure heating and superheating can then occur in a boiler without demanding isothermal addition of all the heat. Superheating also reduces turbine-exit moisture, although the calculated exit may still be wet. The pump, turbine, condenser and boiler can operate continuously as separate flow devices. The [Rankine cycle](../../../../../rankine-cycle.md) sacrifices some ideal [thermal efficiency](../../../../../thermal-efficiency.md) in return for a practicable steam-plant configuration; no real engine can exceed the [Carnot efficiency](../../../../../carnot-efficiency.md) for the same extreme reservoir temperatures.
