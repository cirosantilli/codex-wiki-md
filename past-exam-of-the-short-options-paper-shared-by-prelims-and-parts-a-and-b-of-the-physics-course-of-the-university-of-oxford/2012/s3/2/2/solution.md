<h1 id="s3/2/2/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [2](../2.md)

A classical orbiting electron accelerates and therefore radiates according to the [Larmor formula](../../../../../larmor-formula.md). Its mechanical energy becomes more negative, its radius decreases, and the orbit spirals inward rather than remaining a stable atom. The [Bohr model](../../../../../bohr-model.md) replaces this prediction with postulated nonradiating stationary states and quantized orbital [angular momentum](../../../../../angular-momentum.md), together with photon emission or absorption only between states, $h\nu=|E_n-E_m|$.

For a heavy nucleus, $m_ev r=n\hbar$. Since the [de Broglie wavelength](../../../../../de-broglie-wavelength.md) is $h/(m_ev)$, this implies

$$
\boxed{2\pi r=n\lambda,\qquad n=1,2,\ldots.}
$$

The electronic wave fits an integer number of wavelengths around the orbit. This is a standing-wave consistency interpretation of the quantization rule, not a classical mechanism that prevents an accelerating charge from radiating. For finite nuclear mass, the two-body relative coordinate uses [reduced mass](../../../../../reduced-mass.md) $\mu=m_eM/(m_e+M)$; the next derivation retains that correction. In the limit $M\gg m_e$, it reduces to the fixed-nucleus electron description above.
