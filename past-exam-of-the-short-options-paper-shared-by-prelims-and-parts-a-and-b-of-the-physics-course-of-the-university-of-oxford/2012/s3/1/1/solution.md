↑ **Parent:** [1](../1.md)

[Black-body radiation](../../../../../black-body-radiation.md) and the [photoelectric effect](../../../../../photoelectric-effect.md) exposed failures of classical continuous-wave energy exchange. Classical equipartition predicted an unbounded short-wavelength thermal spectrum, the ultraviolet catastrophe. Quantized oscillator energies $E_n=nh\nu$ give the mean excitation energy $h\nu/(e^{h\nu/(k_BT)}-1)$ and suppress high-frequency modes, producing [Planck's law](../../../../../planck-s-law.md). In the modern description, thermal radiation consists of [photons](../../../../../photon.md) with energy $h\nu$ and Bose occupation numbers.

For the [photoelectric effect](../../../../../photoelectric-effect.md), increasing intensity below a threshold frequency does not eject electrons, whereas the maximum electron [kinetic energy](../../../../../kinetic-energy.md) grows with frequency above threshold. Absorption of one [photon](../../../../../photon.md) transfers $h\nu$ to one electron, giving $\boxed{K_{\max}=h\nu-W}$ and threshold $\nu_0=W/h$, where $W$ is the work function. Intensity primarily changes the photon arrival rate and hence the photocurrent in the ordinary single-photon regime. Thus changing energy per quantum and changing the number of quanta have distinct consequences. Historically the thermal derivation first quantized matter's energy exchange; it should not be described as an already complete derivation of modern photon theory.
