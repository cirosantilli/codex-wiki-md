<h1 id="s12/2/2/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [2](../2.md)

The excitation rate in the weak, unsaturated [fluorescence](../../../../../fluorescence-split.md) regime is proportional to the time-averaged squared [electric field](../../../../../electric-field-split.md) amplitude. Thus the illumination profile relevant to excitation is

$$
\boxed{I(y)=I(0)e^{-2\kappa y}=I(0)e^{-y/d_I},\qquad d_I=\frac1{2\kappa}=\frac{\lambda_0}{4\pi\sqrt{n_g^2\sin^2\theta-n_w^2}}.}
$$

The amplitude decay length $1/\kappa$ is twice the intensity decay length. This profile is often called the evanescent excitation intensity; it does not imply a propagating normal energy flux through an ideal lossless totally reflecting interface.

[Total internal reflection fluorescence microscopy](../../../../../total-internal-reflection-fluorescence-microscopy.md) restricts excitation to a shallow layer adjacent to the glass, typically on the scale of hundreds of nanometres for visible wavelengths and suitable angles. Fluorophores near the basal [cell membrane](../../../../../cell-membrane-split.md) are excited while much of the cytoplasm and bulk solution remain dark. The resulting reduction in out-of-focus fluorescence and autofluorescence improves the signal-to-background ratio for individual molecules, allows camera-based parallel observation, and reduces unnecessary excitation of deeper regions. Its limitation is the same selectivity: it does not image arbitrary molecules deep inside a cell. This near-interface selectivity was directly demonstrated in cell–substrate contact imaging.[evanescent excitation and reduction of fluorescence background at cell–substrate contacts](../../../../../cell-substrate-contacts-illuminated-by-total-internal-reflection-fluorescence-by-daniel-axelrod.md)
