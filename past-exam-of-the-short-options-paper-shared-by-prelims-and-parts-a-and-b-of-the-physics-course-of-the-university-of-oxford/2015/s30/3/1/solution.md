During a [exoplanet transit](../../../../../exoplanet-transit.md), starlight traverses the planetary limb. The effective opaque radius varies with wavelength because the altitude of slant optical depth near unity depends on molecular absorption and scattering. Differencing the in-transit and out-of-transit stellar spectra measures this wavelength-dependent blocked area, the [exoplanet transmission spectrum](../../../../../exoplanet-transmission-spectrum.md). Stellar spots and faculae can contaminate that difference and require control.

During a [exoplanet secondary eclipse](../../../../../exoplanet-secondary-eclipse.md), the disappearance of planetary light permits subtraction of the stellar contribution. Visible planetary light is predominantly reflected starlight, sensitive to clouds, hazes, [Rayleigh scattering](../../../../../rayleigh-scattering.md), and atomic sodium and potassium absorption. Infrared planetary emission and limb absorption show rotational-vibrational features of species such as water, carbon monoxide, carbon dioxide and methane, together with collision-induced absorption involving molecular hydrogen. Thermal emission also depends on the atmospheric temperature-pressure profile; a temperature inversion can turn a molecular absorption feature into emission. Which species are prominent depends on composition, temperature and the observed band, rather than being universal to every planet.
