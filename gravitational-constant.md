# Gravitational constant

↑ **Parent:** [Newtonian gravitational field](newtonian-gravitational-field.md)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_constant)

The [gravitational constant](gravitational-constant.md) $G$ sets the strength of [Newton's law of universal gravitation](newton-s-law-of-universal-gravitation-split.md). Two [point masses](point-mass.md) $M$ and $m$ a center-to-center distance $r$ apart attract with [force](force-split.md) magnitude

$$
F=\frac{GMm}{r^2}.
$$

For fixed [masses](mass.md) and separation, doubling $G$ would double the [force](force-split.md). For bodies with [spherical symmetry](spherical-symmetry.md), the same exterior formula follows from the [spherical shell theorem](spherical-shell-theorem.md); for arbitrary extended bodies, add the attractions of their [mass density](density.md) elements instead. The [gravitational constant](gravitational-constant.md) connects a laboratory attraction to the [Newtonian gravitational field](newtonian-gravitational-field.md) controlling a [Kepler orbit](kepler-orbit.md).

The [gravitational constant](gravitational-constant.md) $G$ and local [gravitational acceleration](gravitational-acceleration.md) $g$ are different quantities. Applying [Newton's second law](newton-s-second-law.md) to a [test particle](test-particle.md) gives $g=F/m=GM/r^2$ for one spherical source. The source's [mass](mass.md) and the observer's [position](position.md) change $g$; $G$ remains the same parameter of the law. A local falling-body experiment therefore measures a field, not $G$ alone. On rotating [Earth](earth.md), an instrument fixed to the surface also responds to [centrifugal acceleration](centrifugal-acceleration.md), while surrounding [mass density](density.md) and altitude change the gravitational contribution.

[Dimensional analysis](dimensional-analysis.md) explains the units. Denote the base [physical dimensions](physical-dimension.md) of [length](length.md), [mass](mass.md) and [time](time-in-physics.md) by $\mathsf L,\mathsf M,\mathsf T$. Since [force](force-split.md) has dimension $\mathsf M\mathsf L\mathsf T^{-2}$,

$$
[G]=\frac{[F][r]^2}{[M][m]}=\mathsf L^3\mathsf M^{-1}\mathsf T^{-2},\qquad G\text{ has SI unit }\mathrm{m^3\,kg^{-1}\,s^{-2}}.
$$

By contrast, $[g]=\mathsf L\mathsf T^{-2}$ and the [standard gravitational parameter](standard-gravitational-parameter.md) $\mu=GM$ has dimension $\mathsf L^3\mathsf T^{-2}$. A small numerical value of a dimensional constant depends on the units chosen: it is the resulting [force](force-split.md) for specified physical [masses](mass.md) and separation that conveys the laboratory scale.

NIST's current complete listing gives

$$
G=6.67430(15)\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm{m^3\,kg^{-1}\,s^{-2}}
$$

for the _2022 CODATA adjustment_, the latest adjustment listed when checked on 14 October 2026. Parentheses indicate a [standard uncertainty](standard-uncertainty.md) $u(G)=0.00015\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm{m^3\,kg^{-1}\,s^{-2}}$, approximately $22$ parts per million relative to $G$. The adjustment year is 2022; NIST's May 2024 data update is a different date.[2022 CODATA adjustment, Newtonian constant of gravitation row: value, uncertainty and units](fundamental-physical-constants-complete-listing-by-national-institute-of-standards-and-technology-and-others.md) [Current adjustment heading, next-adjustment notice and data-content update](fundamental-physical-constants-complete-listing-by-national-institute-of-standards-and-technology-and-others.md)

For an original scale calculation, stipulate two $1\,\mathrm{kg}$ [point masses](point-mass.md) separated by $1\,\mathrm m$ and insert this recommended $G$: $F=6.67430\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm N$. The corresponding [acceleration](acceleration.md) of either [mass](mass.md) is $6.67430\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$ at that instant. Reducing the separation to $0.10\,\mathrm m$ multiplies both by $100$. These are ideal model predictions, not observations; their tiny scale explains why stray [forces](force-split.md) matter.

Being a constant of a physical law does not make the numerical value exact. The [SI](international-system-of-units.md) fixes the numerical values of seven defining constants, including the [speed of light](speed-of-light.md) and [Planck constant](planck-constant.md); $G$ is determined experimentally. Exact unit definitions still require physical realizations with [measurement uncertainty](measurement-uncertainty.md): calibrating a [length](length.md) or a [mass](mass.md) does not become error-free.[English §§1.3 and 2.2–2.2.1, printed pp. 122–124: realization, seven defining constants and exact numerical values](the-international-system-of-units-si-ninth-edition-si-brochure.md)
