<h1 id="10/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [10](../10.md)

A simple [polariscope](../../../../../polariscope.md) contains a fixed [polarizer](../../../../../polarizer.md) and a rotatable [optical analyzer](../../../../../optical-analyzer.md), with a space for a specimen between them. A historically suitable implementation uses two [Nicol prisms](../../../../../nicol-prism.md). Each selects one linearly polarized component from the incident light through the crystal's optical action. A lamp and a collimating arrangement illuminate the first prism, and the observer examines the transmitted field through the second.

With no specimen and ideal elements, [Malus's law](../../../../../malus-s-law.md) predicts

$$
\boxed{I=I_0\cos^2\theta,}
$$

where $I_0$ is the intensity after the polarizer and $\theta$ is the relative transmission-axis angle. Parallel settings are brightest; crossed settings give extinction. Rotating the analyser supplies an immediate check on the first element's polarization.

Insert a transparent [birefringent](../../../../../birefringence.md) plate between crossed elements with its principal directions oblique to their axes. The two components can acquire different phases, so they need not recombine into the original [linear polarization](../../../../../linear-polarization.md); light can then pass the crossed analyser. With white light, wavelength-dependent retardation gives colours. Rotation of the plate, and a check without it, distinguish this response from simple sample opacity or a poor extinction adjustment. An optically active sugar solution can instead rotate the polarization plane, as in [Biot's saccharimeter](../../../../../biot-s-saccharimeter.md). The instrument therefore reveals changes of polarization through an analysed intensity, rather than making polarization directly visible to the eye.
