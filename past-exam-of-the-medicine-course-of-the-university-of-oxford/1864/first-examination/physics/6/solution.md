<h1 id="6/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [6](../6.md)

[Temperature](../../../../../temperature-split.md) describes thermal state and determines the direction of spontaneous [heat transfer](../../../../../heat-transfer.md). A quantity of [heat](../../../../../heat-split.md) is energy transferred because of a temperature difference. It depends on the process and is measured in energy units; it is not another name for temperature. Equal heat inputs need not produce equal temperature rises in different masses or substances.

For a specified process, the [specific heat capacity](../../../../../specific-heat-capacity.md) is

$$
\boxed{c=\frac1m\frac{\delta Q}{dT}.}
$$

Over a modest range with nearly constant $c$ and no phase change, $Q=mc\Delta T$. For a gas the constraint matters: [heat capacity at constant pressure](../../../../../heat-capacity-at-constant-pressure.md) and [heat capacity at constant volume](../../../../../heat-capacity-at-constant-volume.md) generally differ because expansion can do work.

In [method-of-mixtures calorimetry](../../../../../method-of-mixtures-calorimetry.md), a body of known mass $m_s$ at $T_s$ is transferred into water of mass $m_w$ at $T_w$ in a calibrated [calorimeter](../../../../../calorimeter.md) initially also at $T_w$. If the final uniform temperature is $T_f$, the vessel and immersed fittings have heat capacity $C_{\rm cal}$, and losses are negligible,

$$
m_sc_s(T_s-T_f)=(m_wc_w+C_{\rm cal})(T_f-T_w).
$$

Thus

$$
\boxed{c_s=\frac{(m_wc_w+C_{\rm cal})(T_f-T_w)}
{m_s(T_s-T_f)}.}
$$

Stirring, quick transfer, a lid, a temperature-time record and allowance for surrounding heat exchange improve the inference. Ignoring the calorimeter's heat capacity systematically misattributes part of the energy uptake.

An alternative is [electrical calorimetry](../../../../../electrical-calorimetry.md): measure $E=\int VI\,dt$ supplied to a heater and compare the temperature rise with a blank or separately calibrated vessel. At nearly constant properties, $E=(mc+C_{\rm cal})\Delta T+Q_{\rm loss}$. An [ice calorimeter](../../../../../ice-calorimeter.md) instead measures the mass melted at a known phase-change temperature, using $Q=m_{\rm melt}L_f$ with appropriate corrections. Steam condensation similarly uses latent heat. These methods infer transferred energy from different measurable effects; a thermometer reading alone does not measure a quantity of heat.
