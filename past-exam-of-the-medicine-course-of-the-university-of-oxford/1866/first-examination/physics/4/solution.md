<h1 id="4/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [4](../4.md)

A clean narrow tube dipped in a wetting [liquid](../../../../../liquid.md) displays a curved [meniscus](../../../../../meniscus-liquid.md) and a rise above the external free surface. With a nonwetting [liquid](../../../../../liquid.md), such as [Mercury](../../../../../mercury-planet.md) against clean glass under the usual conditions, there is a convex [meniscus](../../../../../meniscus-liquid.md) and a depression. The material pair and surface cleanliness matter; small diameter alone does not determine the sign.

For a circular tube of radius $r$, [surface tension](../../../../../surface-tension-split.md) $\gamma$, and equilibrium [contact angle](../../../../../contact-angle.md) $\theta$ measured through the [liquid](../../../../../liquid.md), the vertical force at the contact line is $2\pi r\gamma\cos\theta$. Neglecting the small meniscus-volume correction, its balance with the excess column's [gravitational weight](../../../../../weight-physics.md) gives [Jurin's law](../../../../../jurin-s-law.md):

$$
2\pi r\gamma\cos\theta=(\rho_l-\rho_g)g\pi r^2h,
\qquad
\boxed{h=\frac{2\gamma\cos\theta}{(\rho_l-\rho_g)gr}.}
$$

Thus the signed height is inversely proportional to radius for a fixed [liquid](../../../../../liquid.md), wall, [temperature](../../../../../temperature-split.md) and [contact angle](../../../../../contact-angle.md). Increasing the tube length beyond the required column does not increase the equilibrium height. A short tube that cannot contain that column is a different boundary problem.

The same result follows from the [Young–Laplace equation](../../../../../young-laplace-equation.md): a curved interface supports a [capillary pressure](../../../../../capillary-pressure.md) difference, balanced by the hydrostatic head. Molecular interactions establish the [surface tension](../../../../../surface-tension-split.md) and the preference for wetting the solid, encoded in the [contact angle](../../../../../contact-angle.md). The force balance does not require molecules to attract across the whole height of the column. To test the inverse-radius law, use tubes of several measured radii with the same surface preparation, and compare $hr$ after equilibrium; different cleaning or contact-angle hysteresis can otherwise imitate a failure of the radius law.
