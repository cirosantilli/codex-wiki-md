<h1 id="12/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [12](../12.md)

For [Ørsted's experiment](../../../../../orsted-s-experiment.md), place a long straight conducting wire close to and parallel with a freely moving horizontal compass needle. With no [electric current](../../../../../electric-current.md), the needle follows the horizontal terrestrial [magnetic field](../../../../../magnetic-field-split.md). Closing the circuit deflects it; reversing the [electric current](../../../../../electric-current.md) reverses the deflection, and moving the wire from above to below the needle also reverses the effect. For a long straight wire, the modern idealization is

$$
B_{\rm wire}=\frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r},
$$

with the circular direction given by the [right-hand rule](../../../../../right-hand-rule.md). These controls distinguish a magnetic action of the current from a fixed magnetized piece of wire or a mechanical disturbance.

[Nobili's astatic galvanometer](../../../../../nobili-s-astatic-galvanometer.md) increases sensitivity in two ways. A coil with many turns multiplies the magnetic action of a given [electric current](../../../../../electric-current.md). Two nearly equal magnetized needles are rigidly connected with opposite [magnetic dipole moments](../../../../../magnetic-dipole-moment.md) and suspended by a fine thread. Their torques in the approximately uniform terrestrial field nearly cancel. In a common arrangement one needle lies inside the coil and the other above it, outside its strongest field, so the coil retains a substantial torque while the terrestrial restoring torque is reduced. A weak residual magnetic or suspension torque still provides a reproducible equilibrium; “astatic” means nearly cancelled terrestrial torque, not magnetic shielding. A museum instrument dated 1840 has many turns of fine wire and is identified with measurements of muscle currents.[Inventory 1417: construction and muscle-current provenance](../../../../../astatic-galvanometer-nobili-pattern-by-museo-galileo.md)

For weak physiological sources, use **a long fine-wire winding with many turns**, rather than the short thick winding appropriate to a low-resistance thermoelectric source. The physiological source commonly has a large effective [internal resistance](../../../../../internal-resistance.md); a moderate added coil resistance is then outweighed by the gain in turns. This is a source-and-detector matching argument, not a claim that arbitrarily much wire always improves sensitivity.

For example, at fixed mean turn length and fixed copper winding volume, $R_{\rm coil}=aN^2$. For source [electromotive force](../../../../../electromotive-force.md) $\mathcal E$ and resistance $R_s$, the small-angle driving torque is proportional to

$$
\frac{N\mathcal E}{R_s+aN^2}.
$$

Differentiation gives its maximum at $\boxed{R_{\rm coil}=R_s}$. This simple [optimal turn count for fixed winding volume](../../../../../optimal-turn-count-for-fixed-winding-volume.md) model explains why high-source-resistance work favors more turns, while also exposing the limit. Actual instruments additionally trade response time, damping, geometry and source loading against their steady deflection.
