<h1 id="8/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [8](../8.md)

A [liquid-in-glass thermometer](../../../../../liquid-in-glass-thermometer.md) has a bulb connected to a narrow graduated capillary. [Thermal expansion](../../../../../thermal-expansion.md) of its liquid relative to the glass raises or lowers the column. A simple linear model is

$$
\Delta\ell\simeq\frac{V_0(\beta_{\rm liquid}-\beta_{\rm glass})}{A}\Delta T,
$$

with capillary area $A$ and volumetric expansion coefficients $\beta$.

For the historical Celsius graduation, establish an ice point in an equilibrium mixture of pure ice and water, and a steam point in saturated steam at standard atmospheric [pressure](../../../../../pressure-split.md), here a barometer height of $760\,\mathrm{mm}$. Mark $0^\circ\mathrm C$ and $100^\circ\mathrm C$, then divide the interval into one hundred equal parts. This is a historical two-fixed-point instrument calibration, not the modern definition of the [kelvin](../../../../../kelvin.md) or of Celsius [temperature](../../../../../temperature-split.md). Nonuniform capillary area and nonlinear expansion limit exactness.

**Bulb contraction raises the column and makes the reading too high; retained expansion makes it too low.** Glass volume changes with age and thermal history; heating can cause temporary zero-point depression followed by slow recovery. Rechecking the ice point detects a shifted zero, although it does not diagnose every scale error.[NIST SP 1088, printed p. 4 and §§7.4–7.5: permanent and temporary bulb-volume changes](../../../../../maintenance-and-validation-of-liquid-in-glass-thermometers-by-christina-d-cross-and-others.md)
