<h1 id="6/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [6](../6.md)

**[Typhoid fever](../../../../../typhoid-fever.md) is a specific [infection](../../../../../infection.md) spread from infected human excreta to another person's mouth, most characteristically through contaminated [water](../../../../../water.md) or food. Decaying matter and a bad smell do not by themselves generate it.**

For an answer situated in 1867, the important established observations were the association of outbreaks with polluted drinking supplies, the spread from particular cases, and the infectious importance of intestinal discharges. A leaking privy or cesspool near a well, contamination of a stream used for drinking, and household transfer from soiled material to food offered intelligible chains of transmission. Merely saying “defective drainage” names a circumstance; the explanation must show how material from an infected person reaches someone else.

[William Budd](../../../../../william-budd.md) had already published a particularly clear argument in 1861: the intestinal discharges of sufferers conveyed the specific infectious material, and sewers propagated the disease because they received those discharges. He rejected the claim that inability to trace a previous case established spontaneous generation from [putrefaction](../../../../../putrefaction.md). This is a contemporary argument that an informed 1867 candidate could have used; it does not require importing a discovery made decades afterwards.[Part III, British Medical Journal, 30 November 1861, p.575, three opening propositions and discussion of spontaneous origin](../../../../../observations-on-typhoid-or-intestinal-fever-the-pythogenic-theory-by-william-budd.md)

The distinction between observation and interpretation matters. Some contemporary writers treated decomposing excreta or “sewer air” as a direct fever-producing agent, under the historical [pythogenic theory of typhoid](../../../../../pythogenic-theory-of-typhoid.md). The observed association of [typhoid fever](../../../../../typhoid-fever.md) with foul drains did not prove that theory: the same drain could contaminate [water](../../../../../water.md), hands or food. Nor should [typhoid fever](../../../../../typhoid-fever.md) be conflated with [typhus](../../../../../typhus.md), despite the similar names.

In present terms, the causal organism is [Salmonella Typhi](../../../../../salmonella-typhi.md), a human-associated [bacterium](../../../../../bacteria.md). People ill with [typhoid fever](../../../../../typhoid-fever.md) and people who continue carrying the [bacterium](../../../../../bacteria.md) after recovery can contaminate food or [water](../../../../../water.md). [Faecal–oral transmission](../../../../../faecal-oral-transmission.md) explains why someone need never enter a sickroom to become infected. The contaminated material may pass through a drinking supply, food handling, or hands rather than by direct contact with the original sufferer.[“Causes and spread”: Salmonella Typhi, faeces, food, water and continued carriage after recovery](../../../../../about-typhoid-fever-and-paratyphoid-fever-by-the-centers-for-disease-control-and-prevention.md)

To investigate an outbreak, construct a dated list of cases, compare drinking and food exposures among affected and unaffected households, trace wells and pipes relative to drains, and ask whether cases follow distribution of a common supply. A supply-specific pattern supports a transmission hypothesis more strongly than a general complaint that a neighbourhood smells bad. Modern [bacterial culture](../../../../../bacterial-culture.md) and organism comparison can supplement this [epidemiology](../../../../../epidemiology.md), but a modern laboratory identification must not be represented as an accomplished experiment in the 1867 paper.
