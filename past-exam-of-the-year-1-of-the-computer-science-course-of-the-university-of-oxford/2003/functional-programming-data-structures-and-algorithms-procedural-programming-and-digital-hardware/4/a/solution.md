[Computational complexity theory](../../../../../computational-complexity-theory.md) studies the resources needed by an [algorithm](../../../../../algorithm-split.md) as a [function](../../../../../function-split.md) of input size, after specifying the resource and computational model. For example, [time complexity](../../../../../time-complexity.md) may count comparisons, and [space complexity](../../../../../space-complexity.md) may count stored words. Worst-case [time complexity](../../../../../time-complexity.md) $T(n)$ is the greatest cost among inputs of size $n$; best-case and average costs are different measures.

An [upper bound](../../../../../upper-bound-in-a-partially-ordered-set.md) guarantees that a cost is no greater than the stated bound. A [lower bound](../../../../../lower-bound-in-a-partially-ordered-set.md) guarantees that it is no smaller. For a problem, an [algorithm](../../../../../algorithm-split.md) can establish an [upper bound](../../../../../upper-bound-in-a-partially-ordered-set.md) on what is achievable, while a problem [lower bound](../../../../../lower-bound-in-a-partially-ordered-set.md) must apply to every eligible [algorithm](../../../../../algorithm-split.md) in the chosen model. Bounds need not be tight. Concisely,

$$
\boxed{L(n)\leq T(n)\leq U(n),\quad\text{with the resource and case specified}.}
$$
