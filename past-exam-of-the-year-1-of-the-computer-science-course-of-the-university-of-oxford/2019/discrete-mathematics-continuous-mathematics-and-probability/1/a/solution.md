↑ **Parent:** [A](../a.md)

For the first [set inclusion](../../../../../set-inclusion.md), take $x\in(A\cap B)\setminus C$. By the definitions of [set intersection](../../../../../set-intersection.md) and [set difference](../../../../../set-difference.md), $x\in A$, $x\in B$ and $x\notin C$. Consequently $x\in A\setminus C$ and $x\in B\setminus C$, so $x\in(A\setminus C)\cap(B\setminus C)$.

For the reverse [set inclusion](../../../../../set-inclusion.md), take $x\in(A\setminus C)\cap(B\setminus C)$. Membership in the first [set difference](../../../../../set-difference.md) gives $x\in A$ and $x\notin C$, while membership in the second gives $x\in B$ and $x\notin C$. Thus $x\in A\cap B$ and $x\notin C$, proving $x\in(A\cap B)\setminus C$. The two inclusions establish **the required equality**.
