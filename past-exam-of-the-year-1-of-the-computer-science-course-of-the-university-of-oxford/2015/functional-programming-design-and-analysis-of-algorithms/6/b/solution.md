<h1 id="6/b/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [B](../b.md)

Both [divide-and-conquer algorithms](../../../../../divide-and-conquer-algorithm.md) and [dynamic programming](../../../../../dynamic-programming.md) derive answers from smaller problems. A typical [divide-and-conquer algorithm](../../../../../divide-and-conquer-algorithm.md), such as [merge sort](../../../../../merge-sort.md), splits an instance into separate parts and combines their answers. [Dynamic programming](../../../../../dynamic-programming.md) additionally identifies repeated states across different decompositions and reuses their answers; [matrix-chain multiplication](../../../../../matrix-chain-multiplication.md) is a standard example.

Thus a recursion tree may repeatedly solve the same state, whereas a [dynamic programming](../../../../../dynamic-programming.md) table or [memoization](../../../../../memoization.md) stores it once. The resulting recurrence and dependency order determine the [running time](../../../../../running-time.md). The [principle of optimality](../../../../../principle-of-optimality.md) supports combining optimal state answers; it does not by itself justify a [greedy algorithm](../../../../../greedy-algorithm.md).
