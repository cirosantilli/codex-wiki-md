<h1 id="1/a/ii/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](../ii.md)

For the first [set inclusion](../../../../../../set-inclusion.md), take $x\in(A\cap B)\setminus C$. Membership in the [intersection](../../../../../../set-intersection.md) gives $x\in A$ and $x\in B$, while the [set difference](../../../../../../set-difference.md) gives $x\notin C$. Consequently $x\in(A\setminus C)\cap(B\setminus C)$.

For the reverse [set inclusion](../../../../../../set-inclusion.md), an element of $(A\setminus C)\cap(B\setminus C)$ belongs to both $A$ and $B$ and does not belong to $C$. It therefore belongs to $(A\cap B)\setminus C$. By [double inclusion proof](../../../../../../double-inclusion-proof.md),

$$
\boxed{(A\cap B)\setminus C=(A\setminus C)\cap(B\setminus C).}
$$
