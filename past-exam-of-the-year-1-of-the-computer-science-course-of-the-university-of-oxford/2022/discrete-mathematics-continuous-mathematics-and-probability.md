## Discrete mathematics

↑ **Parent:** [Discrete mathematics, continuous mathematics, and probability](discrete-mathematics-continuous-mathematics-and-probability.md)

<h3 id="discrete-mathematics/1">1</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [Discrete mathematics](#discrete-mathematics)

<h4 id="discrete-mathematics/1/a">a</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#discrete-mathematics/1)

<h5 id="discrete-mathematics/1/a/i">i</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#discrete-mathematics/1/a)

<h6 id="discrete-mathematics/1/a/i/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#discrete-mathematics/1/a/i)

For pairwise [disjoint](../../set.md#disjoint-sets) [finite sets](../../set.md#finite-set), the [rule of sum](../../combinatorics.md#addition-principle) gives

$$
\left|\bigcup_{i=1}^n A_i\right|=\sum_{i=1}^n|A_i|.
$$

The password sets of different lengths are [disjoint](../../set.md#disjoint-sets), so the required number is **$P_4+P_6+P_8$**.

<h5 id="discrete-mathematics/1/a/ii">ii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#discrete-mathematics/1/a)

<h6 id="discrete-mathematics/1/a/ii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#discrete-mathematics/1/a/ii)

The [rule of subtraction](../../combinatorics.md#rule-of-subtraction) is

$$
\boxed{|A\setminus B|=|A|-|A\cap B|.}
$$

In particular, if $B\subseteq A$ are [finite sets](../../set.md#finite-set), the [set difference](../../set.md#set-difference) has [cardinality](../../cardinal-number.md#cardinality) $|A|-|B|$.

<h5 id="discrete-mathematics/1/a/iii">iii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#discrete-mathematics/1/a)

<h6 id="discrete-mathematics/1/a/iii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#discrete-mathematics/1/a/iii)

The [rule of product](../../combinatorics.md#rule-of-product) says that a sequence of $k$ choices, with $n_i$ possibilities at stage $i$ regardless of earlier choices, has **$\prod_{i=1}^k n_i$** possible outcomes. Applied to the [Cartesian product](../../set-theory.md#cartesian-product) of [finite sets](../../set.md#finite-set), it gives $|A\times B|=|A||B|$. Here independence means that the available choices do not restrict one another; no [probability](#probability) assumption is needed.

<h5 id="discrete-mathematics/1/a/iv">iv</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#discrete-mathematics/1/a)

<h6 id="discrete-mathematics/1/a/iv/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#discrete-mathematics/1/a/iv)

The [rule of product](../../combinatorics.md#rule-of-product) counts $7^4$ unrestricted passwords and $6^4$ passwords containing no A. By the [rule of subtraction](../../combinatorics.md#rule-of-subtraction), the answer is

$$
\boxed{7^4-6^4=2401-1296=1105.}
$$

<h4 id="discrete-mathematics/1/b">b</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#discrete-mathematics/1)

<h5 id="discrete-mathematics/1/b/i">i</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#discrete-mathematics/1/b)

<h6 id="discrete-mathematics/1/b/i/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#discrete-mathematics/1/b/i)

The [power set](../../set.md#power-set) is $\mathcal P(A)=\{B:B\subseteq A\}$, the [set](../../set.md) of all [subsets](../../set.md#subset) of $A$. In this case,

$$
\boxed{\mathcal P(A)=\{\varnothing,\{a\},\{b\},\{c\},\{a,b\},\{a,c\},\{b,c\},\{a,b,c\}\}.}
$$

<h5 id="discrete-mathematics/1/b/ii">ii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#discrete-mathematics/1/b)

<h6 id="discrete-mathematics/1/b/ii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#discrete-mathematics/1/b/ii)

Each of the $n$ elements is either included or excluded, so the [rule of product](../../combinatorics.md#rule-of-product) gives $|\mathcal P(\{1,\ldots,n\})|=2^n$. Alternatively, partition the [power set](../../set.md#power-set) into [disjoint](../../set.md#disjoint-sets) families according to [cardinality](../../cardinal-number.md#cardinality). The family of $k$-element [subsets](../../set.md#subset) has size equal to the [binomial coefficient](../../binomial-coefficient.md) $\binom nk$. The [rule of sum](../../combinatorics.md#addition-principle) therefore gives

$$
\boxed{2^n=\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nk.}
$$

<h4 id="discrete-mathematics/1/c">c</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#discrete-mathematics/1)

<h5 id="discrete-mathematics/1/c/i">i</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#discrete-mathematics/1/c)

<h6 id="discrete-mathematics/1/c/i/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#discrete-mathematics/1/c/i)

For [finite sets](../../set.md#finite-set), the [inclusion-exclusion principle](../../combinatorics.md#inclusion-exclusion-principle) states

$$
\boxed{\left|\bigcup_{i=1}^n A_i\right|=\sum_{\varnothing\ne J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}}(-1)^{|J|+1}\left|\bigcap_{j\in J}A_j\right|.}
$$

Thus single-set [cardinalities](../../cardinal-number.md#cardinality) are added, pairwise-intersection [cardinalities](../../cardinal-number.md#cardinality) subtracted, triple-intersection [cardinalities](../../cardinal-number.md#cardinality) added, and so on.

<h5 id="discrete-mathematics/1/c/ii">ii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#discrete-mathematics/1/c)

<h6 id="discrete-mathematics/1/c/ii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#discrete-mathematics/1/c/ii)

Let $A_d$ contain the multiples of $d$ among $1,\ldots,999$. Its [cardinality](../../cardinal-number.md#cardinality) is $\lfloor999/d\rfloor$, using the [floor function](../../calculus.md#floor-function). Because $3,7,11$ are pairwise [coprime](../../divisibility.md#coprime-integers), their pairwise intersections correspond to divisibility by $21,33,77$, and their triple intersection to divisibility by $231$. The [inclusion-exclusion principle](../../combinatorics.md#inclusion-exclusion-principle) gives

$$
\boxed{333+142+90-47-30-12+4=480.}
$$

<h3 id="discrete-mathematics/2">2</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [Discrete mathematics](#discrete-mathematics)

<h4 id="discrete-mathematics/2/a">a</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#discrete-mathematics/2)

<h5 id="discrete-mathematics/2/a/i">i</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#discrete-mathematics/2/a)

<h6 id="discrete-mathematics/2/a/i/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#discrete-mathematics/2/a/i)

The [pigeonhole principle](../../combinatorics.md#pigeonhole-principle) states that **if $N$ objects are placed in $k$ boxes and $N>k$, at least one box contains at least two objects**.

<h5 id="discrete-mathematics/2/a/ii">ii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#discrete-mathematics/2/a)

<h6 id="discrete-mathematics/2/a/ii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#discrete-mathematics/2/a/ii)

Set $m=\lceil N/k\rceil$, using the [ceiling function](../../calculus.md#ceiling-function). If no box contains at least $m$ objects, all boxes contain at most $m-1$, so the total number of objects is at most $k(m-1)$. Taking the [contrapositive](../../mathematical-logic.md#contrapositive), a total greater than $k(m-1)$ forces some box to contain at least $m$ objects. But $m-1<N/k$, hence $k(m-1)<N$. This proves the [generalized pigeonhole principle](../../combinatorics.md#generalized-pigeonhole-principle): **some box contains at least $\lceil N/k\rceil$ objects**.

<h5 id="discrete-mathematics/2/a/iii">iii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#discrete-mathematics/2/a)

<h6 id="discrete-mathematics/2/a/iii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#discrete-mathematics/2/a/iii)

By the [generalized pigeonhole principle](../../combinatorics.md#generalized-pigeonhole-principle), **13 cards** guarantee $\lceil13/4\rceil=4$ cards in one suit. The guarantee is sharp: 12 cards can consist of three cards from each suit, with no four cards of a common suit.

<h4 id="discrete-mathematics/2/b">b</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#discrete-mathematics/2)

<h5 id="discrete-mathematics/2/b/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#discrete-mathematics/2/b)

Use [mathematical induction](../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#mathematical-induction) on $n\ge0$. For $n=0$, the sum is $0!\,0=0=1!-1$. Suppose it is $(n+1)!-1$ at $n$. Then

$$
\sum_{i=0}^{n+1}i!\,i=(n+1)!-1+(n+1)!(n+1)=(n+2)!-1.
$$

The induction is complete, proving **$\sum_{i=0}^n i!\,i=(n+1)!-1$**. The [factorial](../../combinatorics.md#factorial) identity $i!\,i=(i+1)!-i!$ also explains the cancellation directly.

<h4 id="discrete-mathematics/2/c">c</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#discrete-mathematics/2)

<h5 id="discrete-mathematics/2/c/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#discrete-mathematics/2/c)

In [big O notation](../../real-analysis.md#big-o-notation), $f(n)=O(g(n))$ means that there exist $C>0$ and $n_0$ such that $|f(n)|\le C|g(n)|$ for every integer $n\ge n_0$. For the required [polynomial versus exponential growth](../../real-analysis.md#polynomial-versus-exponential-growth) comparison, $3^3=3^3$. If $n^3\le3^n$ and $n\ge3$, then

$$
(n+1)^3=n^3\left(1+\frac1n\right)^3\le3^n\left(\frac43\right)^3<3^{n+1}.
$$

By [mathematical induction](../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#mathematical-induction), $n^3\le3^n$ for all $n\ge3$. Thus **$n^3=O(3^n)$**, with $C=1$ and $n_0=3$.

<h4 id="discrete-mathematics/3/a">a</h4>

<h5 id="discrete-mathematics/3/a/i">i</h5>

<h6 id="discrete-mathematics/3/a/i/solution">Solution</h6>

A [binary relation](../../set-theory.md#binary-relation) $R$ on $A$ is a [reflexive relation](../../set-theory.md#reflexive-relation) precisely when **$aRa$ for every $a\in A$**.

<h5 id="discrete-mathematics/3/a/ii">ii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#discrete-mathematics/3/a)

<h6 id="discrete-mathematics/3/a/ii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#discrete-mathematics/3/a/ii)

A [binary relation](../../set-theory.md#binary-relation) $R$ on $A$ is a [symmetric relation](../../set-theory.md#symmetric-relation) precisely when **$aRb\Rightarrow bRa$ for all $a,b\in A$**.

<h5 id="discrete-mathematics/3/a/iii">iii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#discrete-mathematics/3/a)

<h6 id="discrete-mathematics/3/a/iii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#discrete-mathematics/3/a/iii)

A [binary relation](../../set-theory.md#binary-relation) $R$ on $A$ is an [antisymmetric relation](../../set-theory.md#antisymmetric-relation) precisely when **$(aRb\ \text{and}\ bRa)\Rightarrow a=b$ for all $a,b\in A$**.

<h5 id="discrete-mathematics/3/a/iv">iv</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#discrete-mathematics/3/a)

<h6 id="discrete-mathematics/3/a/iv/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#discrete-mathematics/3/a/iv)

A [binary relation](../../set-theory.md#binary-relation) $R$ on $A$ is a [transitive relation](../../set-theory.md#transitive-relation) precisely when **$(aRb\ \text{and}\ bRc)\Rightarrow aRc$ for all $a,b,c\in A$**.

<h5 id="discrete-mathematics/3/a/v">v</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#discrete-mathematics/3/a)

<h6 id="discrete-mathematics/3/a/v/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [V](#discrete-mathematics/3/a/v)

A [binary relation](../../set-theory.md#binary-relation) is an [equivalence relation](../../set-theory.md#equivalence-relation) precisely when it is **[reflexive](../../set-theory.md#reflexive-relation), [symmetric](../../set-theory.md#symmetric-relation), and [transitive](../../set-theory.md#transitive-relation)**.

<h4 id="discrete-mathematics/3/b">b</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#discrete-mathematics/3)

<h5 id="discrete-mathematics/3/b/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#discrete-mathematics/3/b)

For a [counterexample](../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#counterexample) to closure under [union of antisymmetric relations](../../set-theory.md#union-of-antisymmetric-relations), take $A=\{0,1\}$, $R=\{(0,1)\}$, and $S=\{(1,0)\}$. Each is an [antisymmetric relation](../../set-theory.md#antisymmetric-relation): neither contains an off-diagonal pair together with its reverse. But $R\cup S$ contains both $(0,1)$ and $(1,0)$ while $0\ne1$. Therefore **$R\cup S$ is not an [antisymmetric relation](../../set-theory.md#antisymmetric-relation)**.

<h4 id="discrete-mathematics/3/c">c</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#discrete-mathematics/3)

<h5 id="discrete-mathematics/3/c/i">i</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#discrete-mathematics/3/c)

<h6 id="discrete-mathematics/3/c/i/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#discrete-mathematics/3/c/i)

Write $q(x,y)=x^2+y^2$. Equality of $q$-values defines an [equivalence relation induced by a function](../../set-theory.md#equivalence-relation-induced-by-a-function): equality is [reflexive](../../set-theory.md#reflexive-relation), [symmetric](../../set-theory.md#symmetric-relation), and [transitive](../../set-theory.md#transitive-relation), so all three properties hold for $R$. The [equivalence class](../../set-theory.md#equivalence-class) of $(x,y)$ is

$$
\boxed{[(x,y)]=\{(u,v):u^2+v^2=x^2+y^2\}.}
$$

For $(x,y)\ne(0,0)$ this is the [circle](../../topology.md#circle) centred at the origin with radius $\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$; the remaining [equivalence class](../../set-theory.md#equivalence-class) is the singleton $\{(0,0)\}$.

<h5 id="discrete-mathematics/3/c/ii">ii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#discrete-mathematics/3/c)

<h6 id="discrete-mathematics/3/c/ii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#discrete-mathematics/3/c/ii)

This [binary relation](../../set-theory.md#binary-relation) is **not an [equivalence relation](../../set-theory.md#equivalence-relation)**, because it is not [symmetric](../../set-theory.md#symmetric-relation): $(0,0)R(0,1)$ since $0\le1$, but $(0,1)R(0,0)$ is false. In fact the injective encoding $(a,b)\mapsto10a+b$ gives a [total order](../../set.md#total-order). There are no [equivalence classes](../../set-theory.md#equivalence-class) for this [binary relation](../../set-theory.md#binary-relation).

<h5 id="discrete-mathematics/3/c/iii">iii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#discrete-mathematics/3/c)

<h6 id="discrete-mathematics/3/c/iii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#discrete-mathematics/3/c/iii)

Every nonzero integer $a$ has $a^2>0$, proving [reflexivity](../../set-theory.md#reflexive-relation). Because $ab=ba$, the relation is [symmetric](../../set-theory.md#symmetric-relation). If $ab>0$ and $bc>0$, then $ac=(ab)(bc)/b^2>0$, proving [transitivity](../../set-theory.md#transitive-relation). Thus this is an [equivalence relation](../../set-theory.md#equivalence-relation), whose **two [equivalence classes](../../set-theory.md#equivalence-class) are the positive integers and the negative integers**:

$$
\{1,2,3,\ldots\},\qquad\{-1,-2,-3,\ldots\}.
$$

<h6 id="continuous-mathematics/4/a/i/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#continuous-mathematics/4/a/i)

The [second-derivative criterion for convexity](../../real-analysis.md#second-derivative-criterion-for-convexity) is

$$
\boxed{f\text{ is convex on }\mathbb R\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad f''(x)\ge0\text{ for every }x\in\mathbb R.}
$$

The non-strict inequality is necessary and sufficient; the stronger condition $f''>0$ is sufficient for [strict convexity](../../real-analysis.md#strictly-convex-function).

<h5 id="continuous-mathematics/4/a/ii">ii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#continuous-mathematics/4/a)

<h6 id="continuous-mathematics/4/a/ii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#continuous-mathematics/4/a/ii)

Expand before taking the [second derivative](../../calculus.md#second-derivative):

$$
f(x)=e^{3x}+3e^x+3e^{-x}+e^{-3x},\qquad f''(x)=9e^{3x}+3e^x+3e^{-x}+9e^{-3x}>0.
$$

The [second-derivative criterion for convexity](../../real-analysis.md#second-derivative-criterion-for-convexity) proves that **$f$ is [strictly convex](../../real-analysis.md#strictly-convex-function)**, hence [convex](../../real-analysis.md#convex-function).

<h4 id="continuous-mathematics/4/b">b</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#continuous-mathematics/4)

<h5 id="continuous-mathematics/4/b/i">i</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#continuous-mathematics/4/b)

<h6 id="continuous-mathematics/4/b/i/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#continuous-mathematics/4/b/i)

The [Hessian criterion for convexity](../../real-analysis.md#hessian-criterion-for-convexity), in the usual twice-differentiable multivariate setting, is

$$
\boxed{g\text{ is convex on }\mathbb R^d\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad v^{\mathsf T}H(g)(x)v\ge0\text{ for all }x,v\in\mathbb R^d.}
$$

Thus the [Hessian matrix](../../multivariable-calculus.md#hessian-matrix) must be a [positive semidefinite matrix](../../quadratic-form.md#positive-semidefinite-matrix) everywhere. The criterion follows by applying the [second-derivative criterion for convexity](../../real-analysis.md#second-derivative-criterion-for-convexity) to each line restriction $t\mapsto g(x+tv)$, whose [second derivative](../../calculus.md#second-derivative) is $v^{\mathsf T}H(g)(x+tv)v$. Continuous second partials are a sufficient regularity hypothesis for this line-restriction argument.

<h5 id="continuous-mathematics/4/b/ii">ii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#continuous-mathematics/4/b)

<h6 id="continuous-mathematics/4/b/ii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#continuous-mathematics/4/b/ii)

Writing $g=e^{x^{\mathsf T}x}$, the [gradient](../../multivariable-calculus.md#gradient) and [Hessian matrix](../../multivariable-calculus.md#hessian-matrix) are

$$
\nabla g(x)=2gx,\qquad H(g)(x)=g(2I+4xx^{\mathsf T}).
$$

Consequently, for any nonzero $v$,

$$
v^{\mathsf T}H(g)(x)v=g\left(2\|v\|^2+4(x^{\mathsf T}v)^2\right)>0.
$$

In particular the [Hessian matrix](../../multivariable-calculus.md#hessian-matrix) is a [positive semidefinite matrix](../../quadratic-form.md#positive-semidefinite-matrix) everywhere, so the [Hessian criterion for convexity](../../real-analysis.md#hessian-criterion-for-convexity) proves **$g$ is [convex](../../real-analysis.md#convex-function)**. The strict inequality along every nonconstant line also proves [strict convexity](../../real-analysis.md#strictly-convex-function).

<h4 id="continuous-mathematics/4/c">c</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#continuous-mathematics/4)

<h5 id="continuous-mathematics/4/c/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#continuous-mathematics/4/c)

The map $u\mapsto u^2$ is [strictly increasing](../../calculus.md#strictly-increasing-function) on $[0,\infty)$: for $u,v\ge0$, $u\le v$ if and only if $u^2\le v^2$. Therefore a point has the smallest $f$-value if and only if it has the smallest $f^2$-value, and the same applies to $g$. By [minimizers under strictly increasing transformations](../../mathematical-optimization.md#minimizers-under-strictly-increasing-transformations), **both asserted [argmin](../../mathematical-optimization.md#argmin) equalities hold**, including when the [argmin](../../mathematical-optimization.md#argmin) sets are empty. The differentiability assumptions are unnecessary for this order argument.

<h4 id="continuous-mathematics/4/d">d</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#continuous-mathematics/4)

<h5 id="continuous-mathematics/4/d/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#continuous-mathematics/4/d)

Minimize the square of the [Euclidean distance](../../topological-analysis.md#euclidean-distance), using [minimizers under strictly increasing transformations](../../mathematical-optimization.md#minimizers-under-strictly-increasing-transformations). For $s=x+y>0$,

$$
x^2+y^2+\ln^2(x+y)=\frac{s^2}{2}+\frac{(x-y)^2}{2}+\ln^2s.
$$

For fixed $s$ the unique minimum has $x=y=s/2$. Put $x=y=\alpha>0$ and $J(\alpha)=2\alpha^2+\ln^2(2\alpha)$. Its [derivative](../../calculus.md#derivative) is

$$
J'(\alpha)=\frac{2}{\alpha}\left(2\alpha^2+\ln(2\alpha)\right).
$$

Let $h(\alpha)=2\alpha^2+\ln(2\alpha)$. Since $h'(\alpha)=4\alpha+1/\alpha>0$, it is [strictly increasing](../../calculus.md#strictly-increasing-function), with limits $-\infty$ and $+\infty$ at the two ends of $(0,\infty)$. It therefore has exactly one zero. The sign of $J'$ changes there from negative to positive, proving the unique [global minimum](../../analysis.md#global-minimum). The nearest point is **$P=(\alpha,\alpha,\ln(2\alpha))$** at that zero.[Question 4(d), p. 4: the logarithm is interpreted as ln(2α)](../../mathematics-bibliography.md#discrete-mathematics-continuous-mathematics-and-probability-examination-paper-a10100w1-2022)

The [Newton root-finding iteration](../../numerical-analysis.md#newton-root-finding-iteration) is

$$
\alpha_{k+1}=\alpha_k-\frac{2\alpha_k^2+\ln(2\alpha_k)}{4\alpha_k+1/\alpha_k}.
$$

Starting at $0.4$ gives $0.376376476$, $0.376544572$, and $0.376544582$. Moreover $h(0.3765)<0<h(0.3766)$ certifies the rounding. Thus **$\alpha=0.377$ to three significant figures**; more accurately $P\approx(0.3765445825,0.3765445825,-0.2835716452)$.

<h5 id="continuous-mathematics/5/a/iii">iii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#continuous-mathematics/5/a)

<h6 id="continuous-mathematics/5/a/iii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#continuous-mathematics/5/a/iii)

Use [tensor-product quadrature](../../numerical-integration.md#tensor-product-quadrature) with [composite Simpson's rule](../../numerical-integration.md#composite-simpson-s-rule) in each coordinate. If there are $m$ subintervals in each direction, then $N=(m+1)^2$ and the [quadrature rule](../../numerical-integration.md#quadrature-rule) error is $O(m^{-4})$: apply the one-dimensional [error bound for composite Simpson's rule](../../numerical-integration.md#error-bound-for-composite-simpson-s-rule) successively, using bounded fourth partial [derivatives](../../calculus.md#derivative). Hence the error is **$O(N^{-2})$** in [big O notation](../../real-analysis.md#big-o-notation). The rectangular domain makes the product grid convenient.

<h5 id="continuous-mathematics/5/a/iv">iv</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#continuous-mathematics/5/a)

<h6 id="continuous-mathematics/5/a/iv/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#continuous-mathematics/5/a/iv)

A convenient choice for this irregular region is [Monte Carlo integration](../../monte-carlo-method.md#monte-carlo-integration). The region is contained in $[-1,1]^2$. For independent uniform points $U_j$ in that square, let

$$
\widehat I_N=\frac4N\sum_{j=1}^N\mathbf1_{\{U_j\in R\}}\exp\!\left(e^{(U_j)_1}+e^{(U_j)_2}+e^{(U_j)_1(U_j)_2}\right).
$$

The [indicator random variable](../../expected-value.md#indicator-random-variable) incorporates the curved boundary directly. This estimator is unbiased; the integrand is bounded on the square, so its [variance](../../variance.md) is finite. The [variance of a sum](../../variance.md#variance-of-a-sum) of independent terms gives [root mean square](../../analysis.md#root-mean-square) error **$O(N^{-1/2})$** in [big O notation](../../real-analysis.md#big-o-notation). This is a stochastic error rate. Applying a rectangular [Simpson's rule](../../numerical-integration.md#simpson-s-rule) to the indicator-weighted integrand would lose the smoothness required for its fourth-order error bound.

<h5 id="continuous-mathematics/5/a/v">v</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#continuous-mathematics/5/a)

<h6 id="continuous-mathematics/5/a/v/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [V](#continuous-mathematics/5/a/v)

For a generic integrand in 20 dimensions, [Monte Carlo integration](../../monte-carlo-method.md#monte-carlo-integration) gives [root mean square](../../analysis.md#root-mean-square) error $O(N^{-1/2})$, whereas [tensor-product quadrature](../../numerical-integration.md#tensor-product-quadrature) with [composite Simpson's rule](../../numerical-integration.md#composite-simpson-s-rule) gives only $O(N^{-4/20})=O(N^{-1/5})$. One can sample uniformly in the [Euclidean unit ball](../../normed-vector-space.md#euclidean-unit-ball), for example by an independent uniform direction and radius $U^{1/20}$ with $U$ uniform on $[0,1]$.

For this particular integrand, the most efficient elementary choice uses [radial integration over a ball](../../multivariable-calculus.md#radial-integration-over-a-ball) first. The area of the unit 19-sphere is $2\pi^{10}/9!$, as follows from the [volume of an n-ball](../../multivariable-calculus.md#volume-of-an-n-ball). Hence

$$
I=\frac{2\pi^{10}}{9!}\int_0^1 e^{r^2}r^{19}\,dr=\frac{\pi^{10}}{9!}\int_0^1 e^t t^9\,dt.
$$

The reduced integrand is smooth. Apply [composite Simpson's rule](../../numerical-integration.md#composite-simpson-s-rule) to obtain **$O(N^{-4})$** deterministic error in [big O notation](../../real-analysis.md#big-o-notation), with one original radial integrand evaluation per node. If the selection is restricted to direct multidimensional methods, **[Monte Carlo integration](../../monte-carlo-method.md#monte-carlo-integration) with $O(N^{-1/2})$ [root mean square](../../analysis.md#root-mean-square) error** is the appropriate choice.

<h4 id="continuous-mathematics/5/b">b</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#continuous-mathematics/5)

<h5 id="continuous-mathematics/5/b/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#continuous-mathematics/5/b)

By [Taylor's theorem](../../calculus.md#taylor-s-theorem) with [Lagrange remainder](../../calculus.md#lagrange-remainder), for an increment $t$,

$$
\boxed{f(x+t)=f(x)+tf'(x)+\frac{t^2}{2}f''(x)+R_x(t),\qquad R_x(t)=\frac{t^3}{6}f'''(\xi_t),}
$$

where $\xi_t$ lies between $x$ and $x+t$. The given third [derivative](../../calculus.md#derivative) bound implies $|R_x(t)|\le |t|^3/6$.

<h4 id="continuous-mathematics/5/c">c</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#continuous-mathematics/5)

<h5 id="continuous-mathematics/5/c/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#continuous-mathematics/5/c)

Apply the preceding [Taylor's theorem](../../calculus.md#taylor-s-theorem) at $t=h$ and $t=-h$, with $h>0$. The even-order terms cancel in the [central finite difference](../../finite-difference.md#central-finite-difference), giving

$$
d(x,h)-f'(x)=\frac{R_x(h)-R_x(-h)}{2h}.
$$

Both [Lagrange remainders](../../calculus.md#lagrange-remainder) have magnitude at most $h^3/6$, so the [triangle inequality](../../topological-analysis.md#triangle-inequality) gives the [sharp central-secant derivative error](../../finite-difference.md#sharp-central-secant-derivative-error)

$$
\boxed{|d(x,h)-f'(x)|\le\frac{h^2}{6}.}
$$

<h4 id="continuous-mathematics/5/d">d</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#continuous-mathematics/5)

<h5 id="continuous-mathematics/5/d/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#continuous-mathematics/5/d)

Write the computed function values as $f(x\pm h)+e_\pm$, where $|e_\pm|\le\delta$. The computed [central finite difference](../../finite-difference.md#central-finite-difference) $\widetilde d$ satisfies

$$
\boxed{|\widetilde d-d|=\left|\frac{e_+-e_-}{2h}\right|\le\frac\delta h,\qquad |\widetilde d-f'(x)|\le\frac{h^2}{6}+\frac\delta h.}
$$

The two contributions are respectively evaluation [absolute error](../../numerical-analysis.md#absolute-error) and [truncation error](../../numerical-analysis.md#truncation-error). For $\delta>0$, [step-size choice for a noisy central derivative](../../finite-difference.md#step-size-choice-for-a-noisy-central-derivative) minimizes their sum: its [derivative](../../calculus.md#derivative) is $h/3-\delta/h^2$, negative before and positive after

$$
\boxed{h=(3\delta)^{1/3},\qquad \min_{h>0}\left(\frac{h^2}{6}+\frac\delta h\right)=\frac{(3\delta)^{2/3}}2.}
$$

If $\delta=0$, the bound tends to zero as $h\downarrow0$ and has no positive minimizing step.

<h3 id="continuous-mathematics/6">6</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [Continuous mathematics](#continuous-mathematics)

<h4 id="continuous-mathematics/6/a">a</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#continuous-mathematics/6)

<h5 id="continuous-mathematics/6/a/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#continuous-mathematics/6/a)

The [derivative](../../calculus.md#derivative) is represented by the [gradient](../../multivariable-calculus.md#gradient), and the [Hessian matrix](../../multivariable-calculus.md#hessian-matrix) contains the second partial [derivatives](../../calculus.md#derivative):

$$
\boxed{\nabla f(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}4x^3-4y\\4y-4x\end{pmatrix},\qquad H(f)(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}12x^2&-4\\-4&4\end{pmatrix}.}
$$

Equivalently the [derivative](../../calculus.md#derivative) as a linear map is $Df(x,y)(u,v)=(4x^3-4y)u+(4y-4x)v$.

<h4 id="continuous-mathematics/6/b">b</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#continuous-mathematics/6)

<h5 id="continuous-mathematics/6/b/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#continuous-mathematics/6/b)

The [stationary point](../../calculus-of-variations.md#stationary-point) equations give $y=x$ and $x^3=x$, hence $(0,0),(1,1),(-1,-1)$. At $(0,0)$ the [Hessian matrix](../../multivariable-calculus.md#hessian-matrix) has negative [determinant](../../multilinear-algebra.md#determinant) $-16$, so **$(0,0)$ is a [saddle point](../../analysis.md#saddle-point)**. At each of $(1,1)$ and $(-1,-1)$, the [Hessian matrix](../../multivariable-calculus.md#hessian-matrix) has positive leading minors $12$ and $32$, proving a strict [local minimum](../../analysis.md#local-minimum). More strongly,

$$
f(x,y)=(x^2-1)^2+2(y-x)^2+3\ge3.
$$

Thus **$(1,1)$ and $(-1,-1)$ are exactly the [global minima](../../analysis.md#global-minimum), with value $3$**. There are no [local maxima](../../analysis.md#local-maximum), since any differentiable interior [local maximum](../../analysis.md#local-maximum) would be a [stationary point](../../calculus-of-variations.md#stationary-point), and all three have been classified.

<h4 id="continuous-mathematics/6/c">c</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#continuous-mathematics/6)

<h5 id="continuous-mathematics/6/c/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#continuous-mathematics/6/c)

For the equality-constrained [minimization problem](../../mathematical-optimization.md#minimization-problem), $y=8/x$ and $x\ne0$. Put $t=x^2>0$. The restricted objective is

$$
f(x,8/x)=t^2+\frac{128}{t}-28=:F(t),\qquad F'(t)=2t-\frac{128}{t^2}.
$$

The only zero has $t^3=64$, so $t=4$. Since $F''(t)=2+256/t^3>0$, the [second-derivative criterion for convexity](../../real-analysis.md#second-derivative-criterion-for-convexity) proves a unique minimizing $t$. Consequently **the equality constraint has minimizers $(2,4)$ and $(-2,-4)$, with minimum $20$**.

For the inequality-constrained [minimization problem](../../mathematical-optimization.md#minimization-problem), the identity $f=(x^2-1)^2+2(y-x)^2+3$ supplies the universal lower bound $3$. Both equality points for that bound, $(1,1)$ and $(-1,-1)$, satisfy $xy=1\le8$. Hence **the inequality constraint has exactly these two minimizers, with minimum $3$**.

<h4 id="continuous-mathematics/6/d">d</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#continuous-mathematics/6)

<h5 id="continuous-mathematics/6/d/i">i</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#continuous-mathematics/6/d)

<h6 id="continuous-mathematics/6/d/i/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#continuous-mathematics/6/d/i)

For [Newton's method in optimization](../../mathematical-optimization.md#newton-s-method-in-optimization), let $\Delta=H(f)^{-1}\nabla f$. Its second row equation is $-4\Delta_x+4\Delta_y=4y-4x$, so $\Delta_y=\Delta_x+y-x$. Therefore $y_{n+1}=y_n-\Delta_y=x_n-\Delta_x=x_{n+1}$. The given first coordinate yields

$$
\boxed{\varphi(x,y)=\frac{2x^3}{3x^2-1}.}
$$

The [Newton step](../../mathematical-optimization.md#newton-step) is defined where $\det H(f)=16(3x^2-1)\ne0$; both coordinates after any defined step are equal.

<h5 id="continuous-mathematics/6/d/ii">ii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#continuous-mathematics/6/d)

<h6 id="continuous-mathematics/6/d/ii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#continuous-mathematics/6/d/ii)

By the [diagonal reduction in a two-variable Newton iteration](../../mathematical-optimization.md#diagonal-reduction-in-a-two-variable-newton-iteration), $y_n=x_n$ for $n\ge1$. Put $e_n=x_n-1$. Direct factorization gives the exact [Newton map in optimization](../../mathematical-optimization.md#newton-map-in-optimization) error identity

$$
e_{n+1}=\frac{2x_n^3-(3x_n^2-1)}{3x_n^2-1}=e_n^2\frac{2x_n+1}{3x_n^2-1}.
$$

The assumed convergence $x_n\to1$ makes the final factor tend to $3/2$, so it is bounded for all sufficiently large $n$. Hence $|e_{n+1}|\le C|e_n|^2$. For the [Euclidean norm](../../normed-vector-space.md#euclidean-norm) error $E_n=\|(x_n,y_n)-(1,1)\|=\sqrt2|e_n|$, this becomes **$E_{n+1}\le(C/\sqrt2)E_n^2$**, proving [quadratic convergence](../../numerical-analysis.md#quadratic-convergence). Unless the exact limit is reached in finitely many steps, $E_{n+1}/E_n^2\to3/(2\sqrt2)$.

## Probability

↑ **Parent:** [Discrete mathematics, continuous mathematics, and probability](discrete-mathematics-continuous-mathematics-and-probability.md)

<h3 id="probability/7">7</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [Probability](#probability)

<h4 id="probability/7/a">a</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [7](#probability/7)

<h5 id="probability/7/a/i">i</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#probability/7/a)

<h6 id="probability/7/a/i/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#probability/7/a/i)

Assume finite second moments and set $Y_i=X_i-\mathbb E X_i$. By [linearity of expectation](../../expected-value.md#linearity-of-expectation), centring $X_1+X_2$ gives $Y_1+Y_2$. Expanding its square and using the definitions of [variance](../../variance.md) and [covariance](../../variance.md#covariance),

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{var}(X_1+X_2)=\mathbb E(Y_1+Y_2)^2=\operatorname{var}(X_1)+\operatorname{var}(X_2)+2\operatorname{cov}(X_1,X_2).}
$$

No independence assumption is required for this [variance of a sum](../../variance.md#variance-of-a-sum) identity.

<h5 id="probability/7/a/ii">ii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#probability/7/a)

<h6 id="probability/7/a/ii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#probability/7/a/ii)

For finite second moments, the [variance of a sum](../../variance.md#variance-of-a-sum) is

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{var}\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^nX_i\right)=\sum_{i=1}^n\operatorname{var}(X_i)+2\sum_{1\le i<j\le n}\operatorname{cov}(X_i,X_j)=\sum_{i,j=1}^n\operatorname{cov}(X_i,X_j).}
$$

<h4 id="probability/7/b">b</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [7](#probability/7)

<h5 id="probability/7/b/i">i</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#probability/7/b)

<h6 id="probability/7/b/i/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#probability/7/b/i)

In the [uniform balls-in-bins allocation](../../discrete-probability-distribution.md#uniform-balls-in-bins-allocation), each ball enters box 1 independently with [probability](#probability) $1/n$. Thus **$M_1$ has the [binomial distribution](../../discrete-probability-distribution.md#binomial-distribution) $\operatorname{Bin}(n,1/n)$**, with [probability mass function](../../probability-distribution.md#probability-mass-function)

$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(M_1=k)=\binom nk\left(\frac1n\right)^k\left(1-\frac1n\right)^{n-k},\quad k=0,\ldots,n,}
$$

and zero outside that range.

<h5 id="probability/7/b/ii">ii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#probability/7/b)

<h6 id="probability/7/b/ii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#probability/7/b/ii)

For both boxes to be empty, each ball must avoid two specified boxes. The individual avoidance [probability](#probability) is $(n-2)/n$, and the choices of different balls are independent. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(M_1=0,M_2=0)=\left(1-\frac2n\right)^n.}
$$

This also covers $n=2$, when the answer is zero.

<h5 id="probability/7/b/iii">iii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#probability/7/b)

<h6 id="probability/7/b/iii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#probability/7/b/iii)

Write $p_n=(1-1/n)^n$, the [probability](#probability) that all balls avoid a specified box. Each of the [empty-box indicators in uniform allocation](../../discrete-probability-distribution.md#empty-box-indicators-in-uniform-allocation) is an [indicator random variable](../../expected-value.md#indicator-random-variable) with success [probability](#probability) $p_n$. Since $I_i^2=I_i$, its [expectation](../../expected-value.md) and [variance](../../variance.md) are

$$
\boxed{\mathbb E I_i=p_n,\qquad\operatorname{var}(I_i)=p_n(1-p_n).}
$$

<h5 id="probability/7/b/iv">iv</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#probability/7/b)

<h6 id="probability/7/b/iv/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#probability/7/b/iv)

Let $q_n=(1-2/n)^n$. The product of two distinct [empty-box indicators in uniform allocation](../../discrete-probability-distribution.md#empty-box-indicators-in-uniform-allocation) is the [indicator random variable](../../expected-value.md#indicator-random-variable) of both boxes being empty, so $\mathbb E(I_iI_j)=q_n$. Thus

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{cov}(I_i,I_j)=q_n-p_n^2=\left(1-\frac2n\right)^n-\left(1-\frac1n\right)^{2n}<0.}
$$

Indeed $0\le1-2/n<(1-1/n)^2$ for $n\ge2$, and taking the positive integer power $n$ preserves this strict inequality. This proves the negative [covariance](../../variance.md#covariance) directly.

<h5 id="probability/7/b/v">v</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#probability/7/b)

<h6 id="probability/7/b/v/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [V](#probability/7/b/v)

By [linearity of expectation](../../expected-value.md#linearity-of-expectation), $\mathbb E N_n=np_n$. The [variance of a sum](../../variance.md#variance-of-a-sum) and the negative [covariance](../../variance.md#covariance) just proved give

$$
\operatorname{var}(N_n)=np_n(1-p_n)+n(n-1)(q_n-p_n^2)\le np_n(1-p_n)\le\frac n4.
$$

Apply [Chebyshev's inequality](../../probability-inequality.md#chebyshev-s-inequality) to the [empty-box indicators in uniform allocation](../../discrete-probability-distribution.md#empty-box-indicators-in-uniform-allocation):

$$
\boxed{\mathbb P\!\left(\left|\frac{N_n}{n}-p_n\right|>\epsilon\right)\le\frac{\operatorname{var}(N_n)}{n^2\epsilon^2}\le\frac1{4n\epsilon^2}\longrightarrow0.}
$$

This proves the required [concentration of the empty-box fraction](../../discrete-probability-distribution.md#concentration-of-the-empty-box-fraction). Since $p_n\to e^{-1}$, it also yields [convergence in probability](../../convergence-of-random-variables.md#convergence-in-probability) of $N_n/n$ to $e^{-1}$.

<h3 id="probability/8">8</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [Probability](#probability)

<h4 id="probability/8/a">a</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [8](#probability/8)

<h5 id="probability/8/a/i">i</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#probability/8/a)

<h6 id="probability/8/a/i/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#probability/8/a/i)

For a nonnegative integer-valued [random variable](../../random-variable.md), its [probability generating function](../../probability-distribution.md#probability-generating-function) is

$$
\boxed{G_X(s)=\mathbb E[s^X]=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\mathbb P(X=k)s^k.}
$$

The series converges absolutely at least for $|s|\le1$, with $0^0=1$ in the $k=0$ term; it may converge on a larger domain.

<h5 id="probability/8/a/ii">ii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#probability/8/a)

<h6 id="probability/8/a/ii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#probability/8/a/ii)

This is the [geometric distribution](../../discrete-probability-distribution.md#geometric-distribution) on $\{0,1,2,\ldots\}$ with success parameter $1/2$. Its [probability generating function](../../probability-distribution.md#probability-generating-function) is a [geometric series](../../sequence-and-series.md#geometric-series):

$$
\boxed{G_N(s)=\frac12\sum_{k=0}^\infty\left(\frac s2\right)^k=\frac1{2-s},\qquad |s|<2.}
$$

<h4 id="probability/8/b">b</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [8](#probability/8)

<h5 id="probability/8/b/i">i</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#probability/8/b)

<h6 id="probability/8/b/i/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#probability/8/b/i)

The ancestor contributes one individual. Given its offspring count $N$, the descendant family rooted at each child is an independent [branching process](../../stochastic-process.md#branching-process) with the same [offspring distribution](../../galton-watson-process.md#offspring-distribution-of-a-branching-process) as the original one. Its [total progeny](../../galton-watson-process.md#total-progeny-of-a-branching-process) therefore has the same distribution as $Z$. The independent reproduction choices let these descendant [total progeny](../../galton-watson-process.md#total-progeny-of-a-branching-process) variables be represented by independent copies $Z_1,Z_2,\ldots$, independent of $N$. Hence, on this family-tree construction,

$$
\boxed{Z=1+\sum_{i=1}^N Z_i,}
$$

where the sum is zero when $N=0$.

<h5 id="probability/8/b/ii">ii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#probability/8/b)

<h6 id="probability/8/b/ii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#probability/8/b/ii)

For $0\le s\le1$, condition on $N=k$. Independence of the descendant [total progeny](../../galton-watson-process.md#total-progeny-of-a-branching-process) variables gives

$$
\mathbb E\!\left[s^{\sum_{i=1}^N Z_i}\mid N=k\right]=\prod_{i=1}^k\mathbb E[s^{Z_i}]=G_Z(s)^k,
$$

including the empty product $1$ when $k=0$. Taking [expectation](../../expected-value.md) over $N$ proves the [probability generating function of an independent random sum](../../probability-distribution.md#probability-generating-function-of-an-independent-random-sum) formula

$$
\boxed{\mathbb E\!\left[s^{\sum_{i=1}^N Z_i}\right]=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\mathbb P(N=k)G_Z(s)^k=G_N(G_Z(s)).}
$$

The same identity holds for complex $|s|<1$ by absolute convergence.

<h5 id="probability/8/b/iii">iii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#probability/8/b)

<h6 id="probability/8/b/iii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#probability/8/b/iii)

The extra ancestor multiplies the [probability generating function](../../probability-distribution.md#probability-generating-function) by $s$. The preceding [probability generating function of an independent random sum](../../probability-distribution.md#probability-generating-function-of-an-independent-random-sum) formula therefore yields

$$
G_Z(s)=sG_N(G_Z(s))=\frac{s}{2-G_Z(s)},\qquad G_Z(s)^2-2G_Z(s)+s=0.
$$

For $0\le s<1$ the two roots are $1\pm\sqrt{1-s}$. A [probability generating function](../../probability-distribution.md#probability-generating-function) lies in $[0,1]$ there, so the plus root is impossible. Also $Z\ge1$ gives $G_Z(0)=0$. Consequently the [total progeny with critical geometric offspring](../../galton-watson-process.md#total-progeny-with-critical-geometric-offspring) has

$$
\boxed{G_Z(s)=1-\sqrt{1-s},\qquad 0\le s\le1.}
$$

At $s=1$ the value is $1$, consistent with almost-sure finiteness. For complex $|s|<1$, the same expression uses the analytic square root that equals $1$ at $s=0$.

<h3 id="probability/9">9</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [Probability](#probability)

<h4 id="probability/9/a">a</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [9](#probability/9)

<h5 id="probability/9/a/i">i</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#probability/9/a)

<h6 id="probability/9/a/i/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#probability/9/a/i)

The [cumulative distribution function](../../probability-distribution.md#cumulative-distribution-function) is **$F_X(x)=\mathbb P(X\le x)$**. In the absolutely continuous setting of a [probability density function](../../continuous-probability-distribution.md#probability-density-function), the [probability density function](../../continuous-probability-distribution.md#probability-density-function) is a nonnegative integrable function $f_X$ satisfying

$$
\boxed{F_X(x)=\int_{-\infty}^x f_X(t)\,dt,\qquad\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_X(t)\,dt=1.}
$$

Thus $\mathbb P(a<X\le b)=\int_a^b f_X(t)\,dt$ and $F_X'=f_X$ almost everywhere. A merely continuous [cumulative distribution function](../../probability-distribution.md#cumulative-distribution-function) need not possess a [probability density function](../../continuous-probability-distribution.md#probability-density-function); the density assumption means absolute continuity.

<h5 id="probability/9/a/ii">ii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#probability/9/a)

<h6 id="probability/9/a/ii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#probability/9/a/ii)

Every [cumulative distribution function](../../probability-distribution.md#cumulative-distribution-function) is **nondecreasing, right-continuous, valued in $[0,1]$, and has limits $0$ at $-\infty$ and $1$ at $+\infty$**. For a continuous [random variable](../../random-variable.md) it is continuous. When a [probability density function](../../continuous-probability-distribution.md#probability-density-function) exists, it is in fact absolutely continuous, with nonnegative [derivative](../../calculus.md#derivative) almost everywhere.

<h5 id="probability/9/a/iii">iii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#probability/9/a)

<h6 id="probability/9/a/iii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#probability/9/a/iii)

The proposed [cumulative distribution function](../../probability-distribution.md#cumulative-distribution-function) satisfies $0<F(x)<1$ and is smooth. Its [derivative](../../calculus.md#derivative) is

$$
F'(x)=e^{-x}\exp(-e^{-x})>0,
$$

so it is increasing and continuous, hence right-continuous. As $x\to-\infty$, $e^{-x}\to\infty$ and $F(x)\to0$; as $x\to\infty$, $e^{-x}\to0$ and $F(x)\to1$. Thus **it has all the required [cumulative distribution function](../../probability-distribution.md#cumulative-distribution-function) properties**, and defines the standard [Gumbel distribution](../../extreme-value-theory.md#gumbel-distribution).

<h5 id="probability/9/a/iv">iv</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#probability/9/a)

<h6 id="probability/9/a/iv/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#probability/9/a/iv)

Because $Z=e^{-Y}>0$, its [cumulative distribution function](../../probability-distribution.md#cumulative-distribution-function) is zero for $z\le0$. For $z>0$, the transformation is decreasing, and $Y$ has a continuous [cumulative distribution function](../../probability-distribution.md#cumulative-distribution-function), so

$$
\mathbb P(Z\le z)=\mathbb P(Y\ge-\ln z)=1-F(-\ln z)=1-e^{-z}.
$$

Thus the [exponential transform of a standard Gumbel variable](../../extreme-value-theory.md#exponential-transform-of-a-standard-gumbel-variable) gives

$$
\boxed{F_Z(z)=\begin{cases}0,&z\le0,\\1-e^{-z},&z>0,\end{cases}\qquad Z\sim\operatorname{Exp}(1).}
$$

This is the [exponential distribution](../../continuous-probability-distribution.md#exponential-distribution) with rate $1$.

<h4 id="probability/9/b">b</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [9](#probability/9)

<h5 id="probability/9/b/i">i</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#probability/9/b)

<h6 id="probability/9/b/i/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#probability/9/b/i)

Write $M_n=\max_iE_i$. Each [exponential distribution](../../continuous-probability-distribution.md#exponential-distribution) has [cumulative distribution function](../../probability-distribution.md#cumulative-distribution-function) $1-e^{-x}$ for $x\ge0$. Independence therefore gives the [maximum of independent exponential random variables](../../extreme-value-theory.md#maximum-of-independent-exponential-random-variables) formula

$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(M_n\le x)=\begin{cases}0,&x<0,\\(1-e^{-x})^n,&x\ge0.\end{cases}}
$$

For any fixed $y$, the threshold $y+\ln n$ is nonnegative for all sufficiently large $n$. Then

$$
\mathbb P(M_n-\ln n\le y)=\left(1-\frac{e^{-y}}n\right)^n\longrightarrow\boxed{\exp(-e^{-y})}.
$$

This is [convergence in distribution](../../convergence-of-random-variables.md#convergence-in-distribution) to the standard [Gumbel distribution](../../extreme-value-theory.md#gumbel-distribution).

<h5 id="probability/9/b/ii">ii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#probability/9/b)

<h6 id="probability/9/b/ii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#probability/9/b/ii)

First $0\le M_n\le\sum_iE_i$, so [linearity of expectation](../../expected-value.md#linearity-of-expectation) gives $\mathbb E M_n\le n<\infty$. The [tail integral formula for expectation](../../expected-value.md#tail-integral-formula-for-expectation), followed by the finite [binomial theorem](../../binomial-coefficient.md#binomial-theorem) expansion, gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E M_n&=\int_0^\infty\left[1-(1-e^{-x})^n\right]dx\\
&=\sum_{k=1}^n(-1)^{k+1}\binom nk\int_0^\infty e^{-kx}dx\\
&=\boxed{\sum_{k=1}^n\binom nk\frac{(-1)^{k+1}}k.}
\end{aligned}
$$

The exchange involves only a finite sum. As a useful check, substitution $t=e^{-x}$ rewrites the integral as $\int_0^1[1-(1-t)^n]/t\,dt=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\int_0^1(1-t)^jdt=\sum_{k=1}^n1/k$, the [harmonic number](../../analytic-number-theory.md#harmonic-number) $H_n$.
