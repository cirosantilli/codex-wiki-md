↑ **Parent:** [B](../b.md)

A [parity symmetry in quantum field theory](../../../../parity-symmetry-in-quantum-field-theory.md) reverses spatial orientation, so its spatial matrix must lie in the determinant-$-1$ component of the [orthogonal group](../../../../orthogonal-group.md). In two spatial dimensions, reversing both coordinates gives $-I_2$, which has determinant $+1$ and is a rotation through $\pi$ in the [special orthogonal group in two dimensions](../../../../special-orthogonal-group-in-two-dimensions.md). It cannot test a discrete orientation-reversing symmetry independent of ordinary rotations. Reflecting one coordinate, for example $\operatorname{diag}(-1,1)$, has determinant $-1$ and supplies the required [parity symmetry in quantum field theory](../../../../parity-symmetry-in-quantum-field-theory.md).

In three spatial dimensions, $-I_3$ has determinant $-1$, so simultaneous inversion of all three coordinates is the standard choice. A single-coordinate reflection also reverses orientation there, and differs from $-I_3$ by a proper rotation through $\pi$. Thus, in a rotationally invariant theory, it tests the same discrete symmetry. The word _is_ in the source specifies conventional representatives: the essential distinction is orientation reversal, rather than a unique choice of axis. Both transformations preserve the ordinary spatial integration measure through the absolute value of their [Jacobian determinants](../../../../jacobian-determinant.md).
