<h1 id="section-b/5/c/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [C](../c.md)

The electron's [electric dipole moment](../../../../../electric-dipole-moment.md) is $\mathbf d=-e\langle x\rangle\hat{\mathbf x}$. It vanishes in the unperturbed ground [energy eigenstate](../../../../../energy-eigenstate.md). The first correction to the [wavefunction](../../../../../wave-function-split.md) is linear in the [electric field](../../../../../electric-field-split.md) and has the opposite parity; its interference with the unperturbed state therefore gives a nonzero position [expectation value](../../../../../expectation-value.md) of first order.

In detail, the normalized ground [quantum state](../../../../../quantum-state.md), to the order needed, is

$$
|g\rangle=|\phi_0\rangle+
 eE\sum_{k\geq1}\frac{x_{k0}}{\epsilon_0^{(0)}-\epsilon_k^{(0)}}|\phi_k\rangle+O(E^2).
$$

The normalization correction starts at second order and does not affect the linear term below. The two cross terms give

$$
\langle x\rangle=2eE\sum_{k\geq1}
\frac{|x_{k0}|^2}{\epsilon_0^{(0)}-\epsilon_k^{(0)}}+O(E^3).
$$

The absence of an $E^2$ term follows from the [parity operator](../../../../../parity-operator.md): $\langle x\rangle$ changes sign under field reversal. Comparing with the [second-order nondegenerate perturbation theory](../../../../../second-order-nondegenerate-perturbation-theory.md) energy sum yields

$$
\boxed{\mathbf d=-\frac{2\delta\epsilon_0^{(2)}}{E^2}\mathbf E+O(E^3)
=\alpha_{\mathrm{pol}}\mathbf E+O(E^3),\qquad
\alpha_{\mathrm{pol}}=-\frac{2\delta\epsilon_0^{(2)}}{E^2}>0.}
$$

Thus the induced [electric dipole moment](../../../../../electric-dipole-moment.md) points along the [electric field](../../../../../electric-field-split.md), with positive [electric polarizability](../../../../../electric-polarizability.md). The [induced dipole moment and quadratic energy shift](../../../../../induced-dipole-moment-and-quadratic-energy-shift.md) obey $\delta\epsilon_0^{(2)}=-\alpha_{\mathrm{pol}}E^2/2$. This factor of one half accounts for the energy needed to induce the dipole. As a check, the [Hellmann–Feynman theorem](../../../../../hellmann-feynman-theorem.md) gives $d_x=-\partial\epsilon_0/\partial E$, with the same result.
