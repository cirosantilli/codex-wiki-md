## 1

↑ **Parent:** [2023](2023.md)

<h3 id="1/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1/a)

Write $C=V(P)$, where $P\in\mathbb C[x,y,z]$ is a [squarefree polynomial](../polynomial.md#square-free-polynomial) and a [homogeneous polynomial](../algebra.md#homogeneous-polynomial) of degree $d$. A [projective point](../projective-space.md#projective-point) $p\in C$ is a [singular point](../algebraic-geometry.md#singular-point-of-an-algebraic-variety) precisely when

$$
P_x(p)=P_y(p)=P_z(p)=0.
$$

If $p=[p_0:p_1:p_2]$ is a [nonsingular point of an algebraic curve](../algebraic-variety.md#nonsingular-point-of-an-algebraic-curve), its [projective tangent line](../algebraic-variety.md#tangent-line-to-a-projective-plane-curve) is

$$
\boxed{T_pC=\{[x:y:z]:P_x(p)x+P_y(p)y+P_z(p)z=0\}.}
$$

The coefficients are not all zero, and the [Euler theorem for homogeneous functions](../real-analysis.md#euler-theorem-for-homogeneous-functions) gives $p_0P_x(p)+p_1P_y(p)+p_2P_z(p)=dP(p)=0$, so the line passes through $p$. Changing the representative of the [projective point](../projective-space.md#projective-point) only rescales the equation.

The strong form of [Bézout's theorem](../algebraic-geometry.md#bezout-s-theorem) says that two [projective plane curves](../algebraic-geometry.md#projective-plane-curve) $C,D$ of degrees $d,e$, with no common [irreducible component](../algebraic-geometry.md#irreducible-component), have finitely many intersections and

$$
\boxed{\sum_{p\in C\cap D}I_p(C,D)=de.}
$$

Each [intersection multiplicity](../algebraic-geometry.md#intersection-multiplicity) is a positive integer. At $p\in C\cap D$,

$$
\boxed{I_p(C,D)=1\iff p\text{ is nonsingular on both curves and }T_pC\ne T_pD.}
$$

This is the criterion for a [transverse intersection of plane curves](../algebraic-geometry.md#transverse-intersection-of-plane-curves). In particular, an intersection at a [singular point](../algebraic-geometry.md#singular-point-of-an-algebraic-variety) of either curve contributes at least two.

<h3 id="1/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

If $d=1$, $C$ is a line and has no [singular points](../algebraic-geometry.md#singular-point-of-an-algebraic-variety). Suppose $d>1$ and take $P$ to be an [irreducible polynomial](../polynomial.md#irreducible-polynomial). First, $P_x$ is not the zero [polynomial](../polynomial.md): otherwise $P\in\mathbb C[y,z]$, and a [homogeneous polynomial](../algebra.md#homogeneous-polynomial) in two variables of degree greater than one factors into linear factors over $\mathbb C$, contrary to irreducibility.

Let $D=V(P_x)$ be the [polar curve](../algebraic-geometry.md#polar-curve) in this coordinate direction. Since $\deg P_x=d-1<d$, the [irreducible polynomial](../polynomial.md#irreducible-polynomial) $P$ cannot divide $P_x$. Thus $C,D$ have no common [irreducible component](../algebraic-geometry.md#irreducible-component), and [Bézout's theorem](../algebraic-geometry.md#bezout-s-theorem) applies. One may count repeated factors of $P_x$ with their multiplicities; alternatively, passing to its reduced equation only decreases $\deg D$.

Every [singular point](../algebraic-geometry.md#singular-point-of-an-algebraic-variety) $p$ of $C$ satisfies $P_x(p)=0$ and has $I_p(C,D)\geq2$ by part (a). Consequently, if $s$ is the number of distinct [singular points](../algebraic-geometry.md#singular-point-of-an-algebraic-variety),

$$
2s\leq\sum_{p\in C\cap D}I_p(C,D)\leq d(d-1),
\qquad
\boxed{s\leq\frac{d(d-1)}2.}
$$

This argument also proves that the [singular locus](../algebraic-geometry.md#singular-locus) is finite.

<h3 id="1/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#1/c)

There is a [nonsingular point of an algebraic curve](../algebraic-variety.md#nonsingular-point-of-an-algebraic-curve) on $C$, since part (b) makes its [singular locus](../algebraic-geometry.md#singular-locus) finite whereas a [projective curve](../projective-space.md#projective-curve) has infinitely many complex points. Apply a [projective transformation](../projective-space.md#projective-linear-transformation) taking one such point to $p=[1:0:0]$, and use $P$ for the transformed [homogeneous polynomial](../algebra.md#homogeneous-polynomial). As in part (b), $P_x$ is nonzero and $D=V(P_x)$ has no common [irreducible component](../algebraic-geometry.md#irreducible-component) with $C$.

The [Euler theorem for homogeneous functions](../real-analysis.md#euler-theorem-for-homogeneous-functions) and its differentiated identities give

$$
P_x(p)=dP(p)=0,
\qquad
P_{xx}(p)=(d-1)P_x(p)=0,
$$

and

$$
P_{xy}(p)=(d-1)P_y(p),
\qquad
P_{xz}(p)=(d-1)P_z(p).
$$

Since $p$ is nonsingular on $C$, at least one of $P_y(p),P_z(p)$ is nonzero. Because $d>1$, the [polar curve](../algebraic-geometry.md#polar-curve) $D$ is also nonsingular at $p$, and these identities show that $T_pD=T_pC$. Hence $I_p(C,D)\geq2$.

This additional intersection is separate from all the [singular points](../algebraic-geometry.md#singular-point-of-an-algebraic-variety) of $C$. Thus [Bézout's theorem](../algebraic-geometry.md#bezout-s-theorem) gives

$$
2s+2\leq\sum_{q\in C\cap D}I_q(C,D)\leq d(d-1),
\qquad
\boxed{s\leq\frac{d(d-1)}2-1.}
$$

The improvement comes from making the [polar curve](../algebraic-geometry.md#polar-curve) tangent at a chosen nonsingular point, thereby reserving at least two of the available intersections.

<h3 id="1/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#1/d)

Let $d_i=\deg C_i$. Under the [reduced plane curve](../algebraic-geometry.md#reduced-plane-curve) convention, the distinct [irreducible components](../algebraic-geometry.md#irreducible-component) have $\sum_i d_i=d$. Away from intersections with other components, a point of $C_i$ is nonsingular on the union exactly when it is nonsingular on $C_i$. For example, a local equation of the union is the equation of $C_i$ multiplied by a nonvanishing factor. At an intersection of two components, differentiating their product makes every first [partial derivative](../multivariable-calculus.md#partial-derivative) vanish. Therefore

$$
\operatorname{Sing}(C)=\bigcup_i\operatorname{Sing}(C_i)\ \cup\ \bigcup_{i<j}(C_i\cap C_j).
$$

Part (b) bounds the number of [singular points](../algebraic-geometry.md#singular-point-of-an-algebraic-variety) of $C_i$ by $d_i(d_i-1)/2$. For each distinct pair of [irreducible components](../algebraic-geometry.md#irreducible-component), [Bézout's theorem](../algebraic-geometry.md#bezout-s-theorem) bounds the number of distinct intersections by $d_id_j$. The [cardinality](../cardinal-number.md#cardinality) of a union is at most the sum of the [cardinalities](../cardinal-number.md#cardinality), even if some points have been counted several times. Hence

$$
\begin{aligned}
\#\operatorname{Sing}(C)
&\leq\sum_i\frac{d_i(d_i-1)}2+\sum_{i<j}d_id_j\\
&=\frac12\left(\left(\sum_i d_i\right)^2-\sum_i d_i\right)
=\boxed{\frac{d(d-1)}2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The reduced convention matters. If one instead allows the equation $L^2=0$ as a nonreduced curve and uses its equation to test singularity, every point on its supporting line has vanishing first [partial derivatives](../multivariable-calculus.md#partial-derivative). A finite bound would then be false.

<h3 id="1/e">e</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/e/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [E](#1/e)

Take a union of $d$ distinct lines in [general position for projective lines](../projective-space.md#general-position-for-projective-lines), meaning that no three pass through one point. Each pair contributes one distinct [singular point](../algebraic-geometry.md#singular-point-of-an-algebraic-variety), and every other point lies on just one nonsingular line. Thus the union has [degree of a projective curve](../algebraic-geometry.md#degree-of-a-projective-curve) $d$ and exactly

$$
\boxed{\binom d2=\frac{d(d-1)}2}
$$

distinct [singular points](../algebraic-geometry.md#singular-point-of-an-algebraic-variety). For $d=1$, this gives a line with zero [singular points](../algebraic-geometry.md#singular-point-of-an-algebraic-variety), as required.

For a concrete construction, choose distinct $t_1,\ldots,t_d\in\mathbb C$ and set

$$
L_i:\quad y=t_i x+t_i^2z,
\qquad
C:\quad\prod_{i=1}^d(y-t_ix-t_i^2z)=0.
$$

For $i\ne j$, the intersection is $[-(t_i+t_j):-t_it_j:1]$. Substitution into the equation of $L_k$ gives $-(t_k-t_i)(t_k-t_j)$, which is nonzero when $k$ is distinct from $i,j$. Thus there are no triple intersections, including at infinity.

↑ **Parent:** [2023](2023.md)

<h3 id="2/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#2/a)

A [nonsingular point of an algebraic curve](../algebraic-variety.md#nonsingular-point-of-an-algebraic-curve) $p$ is an [inflection point of an algebraic plane curve](../algebraic-geometry.md#inflection-point-of-an-algebraic-plane-curve), or [flex](../algebraic-geometry.md#inflection-point-of-an-algebraic-plane-curve), if its [projective tangent line](../algebraic-variety.md#tangent-line-to-a-projective-plane-curve) has contact of order at least three:

$$
I_p(C,T_pC)\geq3.
$$

Here the [projective tangent line](../algebraic-variety.md#tangent-line-to-a-projective-plane-curve) must not be a component of $C$. Ordinary tangency has contact order two.

Let $C=V(F)$, where $F$ is a [homogeneous polynomial](../algebra.md#homogeneous-polynomial) of degree $d\geq2$. Its [Hessian curve](../algebraic-geometry.md#hessian-curve-of-a-plane-curve) is defined by

$$
H_F=\det\begin{pmatrix}
F_{xx}&F_{xy}&F_{xz}\\
F_{yx}&F_{yy}&F_{yz}\\
F_{zx}&F_{zy}&F_{zz}
\end{pmatrix}=0.
$$

Each entry has degree $d-2$, so a nonzero $H_F$ has degree $3(d-2)$. By the [Hessian criterion for a flex](../algebraic-geometry.md#hessian-criterion-for-a-flex), the [flexes](../algebraic-geometry.md#inflection-point-of-an-algebraic-plane-curve) are exactly the points of $C\cap V(H_F)$. The stated no-common-component assumption and [Bézout's theorem](../algebraic-geometry.md#bezout-s-theorem) therefore give

$$
\boxed{\#\{\text{distinct flexes of }C\}\leq3d(d-2).}
$$

This counts distinct points; higher contact can use more than one unit of [intersection multiplicity](../algebraic-geometry.md#intersection-multiplicity) at a single point.

For completeness, the [Hessian criterion for a flex](../algebraic-geometry.md#hessian-criterion-for-a-flex) follows by putting $p=[0:0:1]$ and $T_pC=\{y=0\}$, with $F_y(p)\ne0$. The [Euler theorem for homogeneous functions](../real-analysis.md#euler-theorem-for-homogeneous-functions) gives $F_{xz}(p)=F_{zz}(p)=0$ and $F_{yz}(p)=(d-1)F_y(p)$, whence

$$
H_F(p)=-(d-1)^2F_y(p)^2F_{xx}(p).
$$

The restriction $F(x,0,1)$ already has vanishing constant and linear terms. Its quadratic term vanishes exactly when the [intersection multiplicity](../algebraic-geometry.md#intersection-multiplicity) with the tangent is at least three, proving the criterion.

For $d\geq3$ the bound is the actual [maximum number of flexes of a smooth plane curve](../algebraic-geometry.md#maximum-number-of-flexes-of-a-smooth-plane-curve): a general nonsingular curve has only contact of order three, and the corresponding intersections with its [Hessian curve](../algebraic-geometry.md#hessian-curve-of-a-plane-curve) are simple. One way to see genericity is to vary a point and a line through it, a three-dimensional family. Contact of order at least four imposes four independent linear conditions on the coefficients of a degree-$d$ equation for $d\geq3$. Thus the locus of equations with such contact is a proper exceptional locus. Locally, if the curve is $y=h(x)$, its [Hessian curve](../algebraic-geometry.md#hessian-curve-of-a-plane-curve) restricted to $C$ is a nonvanishing factor times $h''(x)$; contact exactly three gives a simple zero.

A nonsingular [plane conic](../algebraic-geometry.md#plane-conic) has no [flexes](../algebraic-geometry.md#inflection-point-of-an-algebraic-plane-curve), agreeing with the formula at $d=2$. A line is excluded from this formulation: its tangent is itself and its [Hessian matrix](../multivariable-calculus.md#hessian-matrix) is zero, so the no-common-component hypothesis fails. The formula is therefore intended for $d\geq2$, rather than giving the meaningless negative value at $d=1$.

<h3 id="2/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

For $F=x^3+y^3+3xz^2$, the first [partial derivatives](../multivariable-calculus.md#partial-derivative) are

$$
F_x=3(x^2+z^2),\qquad F_y=3y^2,\qquad F_z=6xz.
$$

Their simultaneous vanishing would force $y=0$, $xz=0$ and $x^2+z^2=0$, hence $x=y=z=0$, which is not a [projective point](../projective-space.md#projective-point). This also verifies that the given [plane cubic](../algebraic-geometry.md#plane-cubic) is nonsingular.

The [Hessian matrix](../multivariable-calculus.md#hessian-matrix) and its [determinant](../multilinear-algebra.md#determinant) are

$$
\operatorname{Hess}F=6\begin{pmatrix}x&0&z\\0&y&0\\z&0&x\end{pmatrix},
\qquad H_F=216y(x^2-z^2).
$$

Thus the [Hessian curve](../algebraic-geometry.md#hessian-curve-of-a-plane-curve) is the union of $y=0$, $x=z$ and $x=-z$. On $y=0$, the equation of $C$ reduces to $x(x^2+3z^2)=0$, giving

$$
\boxed{[0:0:1],\quad[\mathrm i\sqrt3:0:1],\quad[-\mathrm i\sqrt3:0:1].}
$$

On $x=z$ one must have $z\ne0$, so put $z=x=1$ and obtain $y^3=-4$. On $x=-z$ similarly put $z=1$, $x=-1$, obtaining $y^3=4$. Choose $r\in\mathbb C$ with $r^3=4$ and let $\omega=\exp(2\pi\mathrm i/3)$. The remaining points are

$$
\boxed{[1:-r\omega^j:1],\quad[-1:r\omega^j:1],\qquad j=0,1,2.}
$$

All nine listed [projective points](../projective-space.md#projective-point) are distinct. They exhaust the intersection with the [Hessian curve](../algebraic-geometry.md#hessian-curve-of-a-plane-curve), so the [Hessian criterion for a flex](../algebraic-geometry.md#hessian-criterion-for-a-flex) proves that these, and only these, are the [flexes](../algebraic-geometry.md#inflection-point-of-an-algebraic-plane-curve) in this example. There are none at infinity: $z=0$ and $H_F=0$ would force $x=y=0$ on $C$.

<h3 id="2/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#2/c)

First make the invertible linear change of [homogeneous coordinates](../projective-space.md#homogeneous-coordinate)

$$
U=-y,\qquad V=\sqrt3\,z,\qquad W=x.
$$

The original equation becomes

$$
V^2W=U^3-W^3=(U-W)(U-\omega W)(U-\omega^2W),
\qquad \omega=\exp(2\pi\mathrm i/3).
$$

This takes the [flex](../algebraic-geometry.md#inflection-point-of-an-algebraic-plane-curve) $[0:0:1]$ to the point at infinity $[0:1:0]$ of a [Weierstrass model](../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#weierstrass-equation-of-an-elliptic-curve).

Put $h=\omega-1$ and choose $s\in\mathbb C$ with $s^2=h^3$. Both are nonzero. Define

$$
X=\frac{U-W}{h},\qquad Y=\frac V{s},\qquad Z=W.
$$

Since $U=hX+Z$, the three factors on the right are $hX$, $h(X-Z)$ and $h(X-\lambda Z)$, where

$$
\boxed{\lambda=\frac{\omega^2-1}{\omega-1}=1+\omega=\frac{1+\mathrm i\sqrt3}{2}.}
$$

The resulting equation is precisely

$$
\boxed{Y^2Z-X(X-Z)(X-\lambda Z)=0,}
$$

the [Legendre form of an elliptic curve](../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#legendre-form-of-an-elliptic-curve). In particular, $\lambda\ne0,1$.

Combining the two changes gives an explicit [projective transformation](../projective-space.md#projective-linear-transformation) from the original coordinates:

$$
\begin{pmatrix}X\\Y\\Z\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}
-1/h&-1/h&0\\
0&0&\sqrt3/s\\
1&0&0
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\\z\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Its [determinant](../multilinear-algebra.md#determinant) is $-\sqrt3/(hs)\ne0$, so this is a valid transformation of the whole [projective plane](../projective-space.md#projective-plane), including the points where an affine coordinate would be undefined.

<h3 id="2/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#2/d)

Let $(r_0,r_1,r_2)$ be any ordering of the three distinct numbers $0,1,\lambda$. Set $h=r_1-r_0$ and choose $s\ne0$ with $s^2=h^3$. The [projective transformation](../projective-space.md#projective-linear-transformation)

$$
\begin{pmatrix}X\\Y\\Z\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}
1/h&0&-r_0/h\\
0&1/s&0\\
0&0&1
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\\z\end{pmatrix}
$$

has the required matrix form. As $x=hX+r_0Z$ and $y=sY$, substitution gives

$$
Y^2Z=X(X-Z)(X-\widetilde\lambda Z),
\qquad
\boxed{\widetilde\lambda=\frac{r_2-r_0}{r_1-r_0}.}
$$

The choice $s^2=h^3$ is exactly what cancels the coefficient introduced by the change in $x$.

The six orderings give the [Legendre parameter orbit](../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#legendre-parameter-orbit):

$$
\begin{array}{c|c}
(r_0,r_1,r_2)&\widetilde\lambda\\\hline
(0,1,\lambda)&\lambda\\
(1,0,\lambda)&1-\lambda\\
(0,\lambda,1)&1/\lambda\\
(\lambda,0,1)&(\lambda-1)/\lambda\\
(1,\lambda,0)&1/(1-\lambda)\\
(\lambda,1,0)&\lambda/(\lambda-1)
\end{array}
$$

Every entry is different from $0,1$ when $\lambda\ne0,1$.

There are no further possibilities for a matrix of the stated form. Its [determinant](../multilinear-algebra.md#determinant) is $acd$, so $a,c,d$ must be nonzero. On $z=1$ it induces the affine change $u\mapsto(a/d)u+b/d$ and scales $v=y/z$ by $c/d$. It preserves the line $y=0$, whose three intersections with the [plane cubic](../algebraic-geometry.md#plane-cubic) have $u$-coordinates $0,1,\lambda$. Their images must have coordinates $0,1,\widetilde\lambda$. Choosing the two roots sent to $0,1$ determines the affine change and leaves exactly the six orderings above.

For general $\lambda$ the six values are distinct. They coincide in some special cases: $\lambda\in\{-1,2,1/2\}$ gives three values, and $\lambda^2-\lambda+1=0$ gives two. In particular, for the value obtained in part (c), the two possible values are $(1\pm\mathrm i\sqrt3)/2$. This does not contradict the paper's qualification that there are six for general $\lambda$.

<h3 id="3/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

A [divisor on an algebraic curve](../algebraic-geometry.md#divisor-on-an-algebraic-curve) is a finite formal sum

$$
D=\sum_{p\in C}n_pp,\qquad n_p\in\mathbb Z.
$$

Its [degree of a divisor](../algebraic-geometry.md#degree-of-a-divisor) is $\deg D=\sum_pn_p$. It is an [effective divisor](../cartier-divisor.md#effective-cartier-divisor), written $D\geq0$, when every $n_p\geq0$.

A [meromorphic differential on a Riemann surface](../riemann-surfaces.md#meromorphic-differential-on-a-riemann-surface) is a one-form represented in every local [holomorphic](../holomorphic-function.md) coordinate $t$ by $a(t)\,dt$, where $a$ is a [meromorphic function](../isolated-singularity.md#meromorphic-function). Under a coordinate change $t=t(u)$, its coefficient becomes $a(t(u))t'(u)$, so these expressions agree on overlapping charts. Since $t'(u)$ is a nonvanishing [holomorphic function](../holomorphic-function.md) for a valid coordinate change, the order of a zero or [pole](../isolated-singularity.md#pole) of the differential is independent of coordinate. Equivalently, for a fixed nonconstant [meromorphic function](../isolated-singularity.md#meromorphic-function) $h$, any such differential can be written $f\,dh$ with $f$ meromorphic.

A [canonical divisor](../algebraic-geometry.md#canonical-divisor) is the [divisor on an algebraic curve](../algebraic-geometry.md#divisor-on-an-algebraic-curve)

$$
K=(\eta)=\sum_{p\in C}\operatorname{ord}_p(\eta)p
$$

of a nonzero [meromorphic differential on a Riemann surface](../riemann-surfaces.md#meromorphic-differential-on-a-riemann-surface) $\eta$. If $\eta'=f\eta$, then $(\eta')=(\eta)+(f)$, so all [canonical divisors](../algebraic-geometry.md#canonical-divisor) are related by [linear equivalence of divisors](../algebraic-geometry.md#linear-equivalence-of-divisors). Their degree is

$$
\boxed{\deg K=2g-2.}
$$

The [Riemann-Roch theorem](../algebraic-geometry.md#riemann-roch-theorem) states that, for every [divisor on an algebraic curve](../algebraic-geometry.md#divisor-on-an-algebraic-curve) $D$ and any [canonical divisor](../algebraic-geometry.md#canonical-divisor) $K$ on the nonsingular [projective curve](../projective-space.md#projective-curve) $C$ of [genus](../topological-surface.md#genus-of-a-surface) $g$,

$$
\boxed{\ell(D)-\ell(K-D)=\deg D+1-g,}
\qquad
\ell(D)=\dim_{\mathbb C}\mathcal L(D).
$$

The term $\ell(K-D)$ measures the failure of the naive count of conditions on [meromorphic functions](../isolated-singularity.md#meromorphic-function).

<h3 id="3/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

Choose a nonzero [meromorphic differential on a Riemann surface](../riemann-surfaces.md#meromorphic-differential-on-a-riemann-surface) $\eta$ and write $K=(\eta)$. Multiplication defines a linear map

$$
\mathcal L(K)\longrightarrow\operatorname{HD}(C),\qquad f\longmapsto f\eta.
$$

It takes values in the [holomorphic differential forms](../complex-geometry.md#holomorphic-differential-form) because $(f\eta)=(f)+K\geq0$. It is injective since $\eta\ne0$. It is surjective because, for any [holomorphic differential form](../complex-geometry.md#holomorphic-differential-form) $\omega$, the ratio $\omega/\eta$ is a [meromorphic function](../isolated-singularity.md#meromorphic-function) and has $(\omega/\eta)+K=(\omega)\geq0$. Thus this map identifies $\operatorname{HD}(C)$ with the [canonical Riemann-Roch space](../algebraic-geometry.md#canonical-riemann-roch-space).

Furthermore, $\mathcal L(0)=\mathbb C$: a [meromorphic function](../isolated-singularity.md#meromorphic-function) with no [poles](../isolated-singularity.md#pole) is [holomorphic](../holomorphic-function.md) on the [compact Riemann surface](../riemann-surfaces.md#compact-riemann-surface) $C$, and hence constant by the [maximum modulus principle](../complex-analysis.md#maximum-modulus-principle). Applying the [Riemann-Roch theorem](../algebraic-geometry.md#riemann-roch-theorem) with $D=0$ gives

$$
1-\ell(K)=1-g.
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{\dim_{\mathbb C}\operatorname{HD}(C)=\ell(K)=g.}
$$

<h3 id="3/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#3/c)

The generator $\eta=f\,dh$ is a nonzero [holomorphic differential form](../complex-geometry.md#holomorphic-differential-form). Its [canonical divisor](../algebraic-geometry.md#canonical-divisor) $(\eta)$ is an [effective divisor](../cartier-divisor.md#effective-cartier-divisor), since there are no [poles](../isolated-singularity.md#pole). In [genus](../topological-surface.md#genus-of-a-surface) one, the [degree of a divisor](../algebraic-geometry.md#degree-of-a-divisor) formula in part (a) gives

$$
\deg(\eta)=2g-2=0.
$$

An [effective divisor](../cartier-divisor.md#effective-cartier-divisor) of degree zero has every coefficient zero. Thus

$$
\boxed{(\eta)=0,\qquad f\,dh\text{ has no zeros anywhere on }C.}
$$

This proves [nonvanishing of holomorphic differentials in genus one](../complex-geometry.md#nonvanishing-of-holomorphic-differentials-in-genus-one). It concerns the differential itself: a zero or [pole](../isolated-singularity.md#pole) of the particular coefficient $f$ may be cancelled by the local behavior of $dh$.

<h3 id="3/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#3/d)

An explicit answer, initially written where $yz\ne0$, is the [meromorphic differential on a Riemann surface](../riemann-surfaces.md#meromorphic-differential-on-a-riemann-surface)

$$
\boxed{\eta=\frac{d(x/z)}{y/z}=\frac{z\,dx-x\,dz}{yz}.}
$$

The ratios $x/z$ and $y/z$ are functions on $C$, so the expression does not depend on the representative of its [homogeneous coordinates](../projective-space.md#homogeneous-coordinate). It is a scalar multiple of an [invariant differential on an elliptic curve](../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#invariant-differential-on-an-elliptic-curve).

On the [complex torus](../complex-geometry.md#complex-torus) $\mathbb C/\Lambda$, the differential $dw$ descends from $\mathbb C$ because translation by any element of the [period lattice](../complex-analysis.md#period-lattice) leaves $dw$ unchanged. It is [holomorphic](../holomorphic-function.md) and nowhere zero. The given [biholomorphism](../riemann-surfaces.md#biholomorphism) satisfies, wherever the displayed quotient is initially defined,

$$
\Phi^*\eta=\frac{d\wp(w)}{\wp'(w)}=dw.
$$

Consequently, $\eta=(\Phi^{-1})^*(dw)$ extends across every apparent denominator zero and across $[0:1:0]$ as a nonzero [holomorphic differential form](../complex-geometry.md#holomorphic-differential-form) on all of $C$.

One can also check the apparent exceptional points directly. On $z=1$, put $P(X)=4\prod_i(X-e_i)$, so $Y^2=P(X)$. At $Y=0$ the roots are distinct, and differentiating gives

$$
\frac{dX}{Y}=\frac{2\,dY}{P'(X)},
$$

which is regular and nonvanishing there. At $[0:1:0]$, use $u=x/y$, $v=z/y$. The equation is $v=4\prod_i(u-e_iv)$, so $u$ is a [local parameter on a smooth algebraic curve](../projective-space.md#local-parameter-on-a-smooth-algebraic-curve) and $v=4u^3+O(u^5)$. Hence

$$
\eta=v\,d(u/v)=du-\frac{u}{v}\,dv=(-2+O(u^2))\,du,
$$

also regular and nonvanishing.

Only distinctness of the $e_i$ is needed. The paper additionally writes $e_i\ne0$, which is not true for every [period lattice](../complex-analysis.md#period-lattice): for $\Lambda=\mathbb Z+\mathrm i\mathbb Z$, the symmetry $\wp(\mathrm iw)=-\wp(w)$ makes the half-period value at $w=(1+\mathrm i)/2$ zero, since $\mathrm iw\equiv w\pmod\Lambda$. This unnecessary restriction has no effect on the answer.

<h3 id="3/e">e</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/e/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [E](#3/e)

First fix distinct $q_1,\ldots,q_k$ and let $Q=q_1+\cdots+q_k$. A [negative-degree divisor has no sections](../algebraic-geometry.md#negative-degree-divisor-has-no-sections): if $0\ne f\in\mathcal L(D)$, then $(f)+D$ is an [effective divisor](../cartier-divisor.md#effective-cartier-divisor) of degree $\deg D$, since a [principal divisor](../algebraic-geometry.md#principal-divisor-on-an-algebraic-curve) has degree zero, so $\deg D$ cannot be negative. As $\deg K=0$ and $\deg(K-Q)=-k<0$, the [Riemann-Roch theorem](../algebraic-geometry.md#riemann-roch-theorem) therefore gives

$$
\boxed{\ell(Q)=\deg Q+1-g+\ell(K-Q)=k.}
$$

Now choose distinct $p_1,\ldots,p_k$ outside the support of $Q$, and put $P=p_1+\cdots+p_k$. A function with the required zeros and [poles](../isolated-singularity.md#pole) must be a nonzero element of $\mathcal L(Q-P)$. Conversely, if $0\ne f\in\mathcal L(Q-P)$, then $(f)+Q-P$ is an [effective divisor](../cartier-divisor.md#effective-cartier-divisor) of degree zero, hence is zero. It follows that $(f)=P-Q$, giving precisely the required simple zeros and simple [poles](../isolated-singularity.md#pole), with no others. Thus the existence question is exactly whether $\mathcal L(Q-P)\ne0$.

Take a [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) $f_1,\ldots,f_k$ of the [Riemann-Roch space](../algebraic-geometry.md#riemann-roch-space) $\mathcal L(Q)$. Evaluation at the $p_i$ defines the [linear map](../vector-space.md#linear-map)

$$
E_P:\mathcal L(Q)\longrightarrow\mathbb C^k,
\qquad f\longmapsto(f(p_1),\ldots,f(p_k)),
$$

whose [kernel](../kernel-of-a-linear-map.md) is $\mathcal L(Q-P)$. The [evaluation criterion for a prescribed principal divisor](../algebraic-geometry.md#evaluation-criterion-for-a-prescribed-principal-divisor) becomes

$$
\mathcal L(Q-P)\ne0
\iff
\Delta(p_1,\ldots,p_k):=\det(f_j(p_i))_{1\leq i,j\leq k}=0.
$$

The crucial point is that this [determinant](../multilinear-algebra.md#determinant) does not vanish identically; a count of unknowns and equations alone would not prove this.

To verify it, begin with $V_0=\mathcal L(Q)$, of [dimension](../vector-space.md#dimension-vector-space) $k$. Whenever $V_{j-1}\ne0$, choose a nonzero $f\in V_{j-1}$. Its zeros are finite. Choose $p_j$ outside those zeros, the support of $Q$ and the previously chosen points. Evaluation at $p_j$ is then a nonzero linear functional on $V_{j-1}$, so

$$
V_j=\{h\in V_{j-1}:h(p_j)=0\},
\qquad \dim V_j=\dim V_{j-1}-1.
$$

After $k$ steps, $V_k=0$. Thus there exists a tuple of distinct points with $\Delta\ne0$.

The entries of this [determinant](../multilinear-algebra.md#determinant) vary [holomorphically](../holomorphic-function.md) away from the $q_i$, and are [rational functions](../isolated-singularity.md#rational-function) on the nonsingular [projective curve](../projective-space.md#projective-curve). Its vanishing is therefore a proper closed exceptional locus on this configuration space; its complement is open and dense in the [Zariski topology](../algebraic-geometry.md#zariski-topology). For a generic choice of the $p_i$, $E_P$ is invertible and

$$
\boxed{\ell(Q-P)=0,\quad\text{so no function with the prescribed zeros and poles exists}.}
$$

This holds for every fixed tuple of distinct $q_i$, which proves the assertion for generic choices of both sets of points. At $k=1$, it is especially transparent: $\mathcal L(q_1)=\mathbb C$, so no nonzero member can vanish at a distinct $p_1$.
