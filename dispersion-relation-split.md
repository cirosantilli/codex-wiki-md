# Dispersion relation

↑ **Parent:** [Wave equation](wave-equation-split.md)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dispersion_relation)

A [dispersion relation](dispersion-relation-split.md) specifies which [angular frequencies](angular-frequency.md) and [wavevectors](wavevector.md) a linear wave model admits. Fix the [plane wave](plane-wave.md) convention

$$
u(\mathbf x,t)=\operatorname{Re}\!\left\{a e^{i(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x-\omega t)}\right\},\qquad D(\omega,\mathbf k)=0.
$$

Here $a$ is a constant [complex amplitude](complex-amplitude.md), $\mathbf k$ is the [wavevector](wavevector.md), and $\omega$ is the [angular frequency](angular-frequency.md). Substitution into a constant-coefficient [linear partial differential equation](linear-partial-differential-equation.md) replaces $\partial_t$ by $-i\omega$ and $\nabla$ by $i\mathbf k$, turning the wave problem into an algebraic condition. A system can have several roots; $\omega=\omega(\mathbf k)$ means one chosen branch, rather than a promise that the full [dispersion relation](dispersion-relation-split.md) is single-valued. For real $\mathbf k$, $\operatorname{Im}\omega>0$ means temporal growth in this convention, while $\operatorname{Im}\omega<0$ means [damping](damping.md). The real-branch discussion of [phase velocity](phase-velocity.md) and [group velocity](group-velocity.md) below assumes negligible loss.

For the one-dimensional [wave equation](wave-equation-split.md) $u_{tt}=c^2u_{xx}$, the [dispersion relation](dispersion-relation-split.md) is $\omega^2=c^2k^2$. The branch $\omega=ck$ carries right-moving [travelling waves](travelling-wave.md) for $c>0$; both its [phase velocity](phase-velocity.md) and [group velocity](group-velocity.md) are $c$. One may instead use the positive-[angular frequency](angular-frequency.md) branch $\omega=c|k|$, with right-moving components at $k>0$ and left-moving components at $k<0$. A real wave pairs each $(k,\omega)$ with $(-k,-\omega)$; that conjugate pair represents the same travelling pattern. Branch and sign choices therefore matter when interpreting a [dispersion diagram](dispersion-diagram.md).

A particularly tangible [dispersive](wave-dispersion.md) example is a small-amplitude [surface gravity wave](surface-gravity-wave.md) on still water of uniform depth $h$. Neglect [surface tension](surface-tension-split.md) and [viscosity](dynamic-viscosity-split.md), assume [incompressible flow](incompressible-flow.md) and [irrotational flow](irrotational-flow.md), and impose a flat impermeable bottom. With $k>0$ and positive [angular frequency](angular-frequency.md),

$$
\omega^2=gk\tanh(kh),\qquad g>0.
$$

Here $g$ is [gravitational acceleration](gravitational-acceleration.md) and $\tanh$ is the [hyperbolic tangent](hyperbolic-tangent.md). Deep water means $kh\gg1$: $\omega\simeq\sqrt{gk}$ and longer [wavelengths](wavelength.md) have larger [phase speed](phase-speed.md). Shallow water means $kh\ll1$: $\omega\simeq\sqrt{gh}\,k$, an approximately [nondispersive wave](nondispersive-wave.md). Thus depth relative to [wavelength](wavelength.md), rather than depth alone, determines whether crests and [wave packets](wave-packet.md) travel together.[equation 3.2](free-surface-water-waves-13-012-hydrodynamics-for-ocean-engineers-reading-5-by-a-h-techet.md)

The [dispersion diagram](dispersion-diagram.md) below also includes the ideal deep-water [capillary wave](capillary-wave.md) limit $\omega=\sqrt{\gamma/\rho}\,k^{3/2}$, where $\gamma$ is [surface tension](surface-tension-split.md) and $\rho$ is [mass density](density.md). This limit requires the [surface tension](surface-tension-split.md) restoring term to dominate [gravity](gravity.md); it reverses the trend of the [surface gravity wave](surface-gravity-wave.md), giving larger [phase speed](phase-speed.md) at shorter [wavelength](wavelength.md). The combined [gravity-capillary wave](gravity-capillary-wave.md) has $\omega^2=gk+(\gamma/\rho)k^3$.[equation 9.7](pulse-propagation-by-richard-fitzpatrick.md)

![](phase-group-dispersion-slopes.png)

**[Figure 1](#image-analytical-dispersion-diagrams-origin-to-point-slope-gives-phase-velocity-tangent-slope-gives-group-velocity). Analytical dispersion diagrams: origin-to-point slope gives phase velocity, tangent slope gives group velocity**.

The curves use dimensionless normalizations chosen separately for each model; they are analytical diagrams, not experimental data. Their beauty is that a single [dispersion relation](dispersion-relation-split.md) encodes several observable behaviours: its value gives the [angular frequency](angular-frequency.md), its origin-to-point slope gives the one-dimensional [phase velocity](phase-velocity.md), its tangent slope gives the [group velocity](group-velocity.md), and variation of that tangent slope predicts distortion of a [wave packet](wave-packet.md). A straight line through the origin describes a [nondispersive wave](nondispersive-wave.md); an affine branch $\omega=ck+b$ with $b\ne0$ has constant [group velocity](group-velocity.md) but [phase velocity](phase-velocity.md) $c+b/k$, so those properties must be distinguished.
