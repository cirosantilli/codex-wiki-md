<h1 id="3/c/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [C](../c.md)

Randomly oriented crystallites supply planes with their normals in each required scattering-vector direction. Of the five families considered, **$(111)$, $(200)$ and $(220)$ can therefore give accessible [copper](../../../../../copper.md) $K_\alpha$ powder peaks; $(300)$ remains absent and $(640)$ remains beyond a roughly $130^\circ$ detector limit.** Other allowed families also appear if their [Bragg angles](../../../../../bragg-angle.md) lie inside the instrument range; orientation averaging does not restrict the pattern to these five examples.

Only a small fraction of randomly oriented [crystal grains](../../../../../grain-in-a-polycrystal.md) satisfy the Bragg condition for a given detector setting, and each family scatters around a cone rather than concentrating all its intensity into one single-crystal spot. A narrow detector aperture therefore receives a relatively weak signal from a small powder specimen compared with a well-oriented large [single crystal](../../../../../single-crystal.md). Adequate [crystal grain](../../../../../grain-in-a-polycrystal.md) statistics, longer counting, suitable powder packing and detector acceptance matter. The odd-parity peaks additionally involve the difference of ionic scattering factors. No absolute single-crystal/powder intensity ratio follows without illuminated [volumes](../../../../../volume.md), absorption and instrumental geometry.
