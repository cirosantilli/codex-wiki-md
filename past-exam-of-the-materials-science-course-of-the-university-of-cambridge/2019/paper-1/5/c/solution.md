<h1 id="5/c/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [C](../c.md)

Initially, one [slip system](../../../../../slip-system.md) carries most of the deformation. Easy [dislocation glide](../../../../../dislocation-glide.md) gives the nearly flat stage-I response, although lattice rotation and gradual [dislocation](../../../../../dislocation.md) interactions can already change the required tensile [stress](../../../../../stress.md). In tension the loading direction expressed in the rotating crystal approaches the primary slip direction. Its path has the form $(5+t,1+t,4)$ until it reaches the symmetry boundary $y=z$, at $[844]=[211]$.

At that orientation, **$(1\bar11)[110]$ and $(11\bar1)[101]$** have equal [Schmid factors](../../../../../schmid-factor.md), $1/\sqrt6$. Conjugate slip therefore becomes competitive. Intersections between the two [dislocation](../../../../../dislocation.md) families generate obstacles, junctions and [forest hardening](../../../../../forest-hardening.md), giving the sharp stage-II rise in flow [stress](../../../../../stress.md). This is the ideal single-slip-to-conjugate-slip construction; real activation stresses can be shifted by latent hardening and imperfections.
