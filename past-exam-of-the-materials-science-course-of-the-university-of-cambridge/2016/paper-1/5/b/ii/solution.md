<h1 id="5/b/ii/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](../ii.md)

For FCC [aluminium](../../../../../../aluminium.md), consider $\{111\}\langle110\rangle$ slip. Normalize the tensile direction and one candidate system:

$$
\mathbf l_0=\frac{(1,2,4)}{\sqrt{21}},\qquad
\mathbf n=\frac{(-1,1,1)}{\sqrt3},\qquad
\mathbf s=\frac{(1,0,1)}{\sqrt2}.
$$

Here $\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf s=0$. Comparing all twelve systems gives the largest [Schmid factor](../../../../../../schmid-factor.md) on $(\overline111)[101]$:

$$
m=|\mathbf l_0\cdot\mathbf n|\,|\mathbf l_0\cdot\mathbf s|
=\frac{25}{21\sqrt6}=0.48601.
$$

[Schmid's law](../../../../../../schmid-s-law.md) therefore gives

$$
\boxed{\sigma_y=\frac{0.78}{0.48601}=1.605\,\mathrm{MPa}.}
$$

During ideal single slip, the tensile direction expressed in crystal coordinates rotates **towards $[101]$**, along the great-circle path joining the original axis to that slip direction. It reaches a symmetry boundary where another [slip system](../../../../../../slip-system.md) becomes equally favored. This [single-slip tensile-axis rotation](../../../../../../single-slip-tensile-axis-rotation.md) concerns the crystal relative to the externally held loading direction, rather than a rotation of the laboratory loading apparatus.
