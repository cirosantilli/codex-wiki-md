<h1 id="2/b/i/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [I](../i.md)

An unstressed [glass](../../../../../../glass.md) is optically isotropic, so it does not split incident light into differently phased in-plane components; it remains dark between crossed polars. A natural explanation of the colored regions after heating is [devitrification](../../../../../../devitrification.md): anisotropic crystallites have nucleated in the [glass](../../../../../../glass.md). Edges and surfaces provide [heterogeneous nucleation](../../../../../../heterogeneous-nucleation.md) sites, making their greater concentration there plausible.

Each approximately uniform crystallite can give a nearly uniform [interference colour](../../../../../../interference-colour.md). Regions with similar thickness and effective [birefringence](../../../../../../birefringence.md) have similar retardation and therefore similar spectral colour, while differing azimuths multiply the intensity by $\sin^2(2\alpha)$ and change brightness. Identical hue does not prove identical three-dimensional crystallographic orientation.

Stress-induced [birefringence](../../../../../../birefringence.md) is a possible alternative in a heat-treated [glass](../../../../../../glass.md). The observations alone do not uniquely prove crystallinity; rotating-stage extinction, morphology or diffraction could distinguish the explanations. The crystallite interpretation is the straightforward phase-transformation explanation, without inventing a composition or heat-treatment schedule.
