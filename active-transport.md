# Active transport

↑ **Parent:** [Membrane transport](membrane-transport.md)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_transport)

[Active transport](active-transport.md) moves a substance uphill in its [electrochemical gradient](electrochemical-gradient.md) by coupling the transfer to a downhill process. Some [carrier proteins](carrier-protein.md) use [ATP](adenosine-triphosphate.md) [hydrolysis](hydrolysis.md); others couple uphill transfer of one [solute](solute.md) to downhill transfer of another [solute](solute.md), often an [ion](ion.md). The latter mechanism uses a stored [electrochemical gradient](electrochemical-gradient.md) and need not consume [ATP](adenosine-triphosphate.md) directly at that [carrier protein](carrier-protein.md). Maintaining the driving [electrochemical gradient](electrochemical-gradient.md) still requires a source of [energy](energy-split.md). See [Alberts et al.: carrier proteins and active membrane transport](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26896/).

For the neutral 8:2 example, B-to-A transfer costs $RT\ln4\simeq1.38629RT$ per mole. As an explicitly assumed bookkeeping example, suppose a tightly coupled cycle supplies a downhill [free energy](thermodynamic-free-energy.md) change of $-2RT$ per mole of transferred [solute](solute.md). The total is then $\Delta G_{\mathrm{cycle}}=RT\ln4-2RT\simeq-0.61371RT$. This makes forward cycling energetically possible under those assumptions; it specifies neither a molecular mechanism nor a rate. Merely adding a [carrier protein](carrier-protein.md) cannot supply the missing [free energy](thermodynamic-free-energy.md). Conversely, as the transported [concentration](concentration.md) ratio rises, the required uphill cost rises, so a fixed driving process has a finite thermodynamic limit.
