<h1 id="3/c/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [C](../c.md)

In the simple transport model, $\mu_e=e\tau_e/m_e^*$ and $\mu_h=e\tau_h/m_h^*$. Conduction bands in many common semiconductors have lighter transport masses than the dominant valence hole bands. For comparable momentum-relaxation times, this gives larger [electron mobility](../../../../electron-mobility.md). It is a common trend, not a theorem: band anisotropy, light and heavy hole populations and different scattering times can alter the comparison. The hole's [mass](../../../../mass.md) is not the [mass](../../../../mass.md) of an atom, and crystal translational symmetry does not make its mobility infinite.

For the intrinsic [germanium](../../../../germanium.md) sample, [electrons](../../../../electron.md) and holes both contribute positive conventional conductivity:

$$
\sigma=e(n\mu_e+p\mu_h)=en_i(\mu_e+\mu_h),\qquad\sigma=1/\rho=2\,\mathrm{S\,m^{-1}}.
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{n=p=n_i=\frac1{(0.5)e(0.36+0.18)}
=2.31\times10^{19}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}.}
$$

This is the density of each species; the combined carrier [number density](../../../../number-density.md) is $2n_i$. Assigning separate densities from the two mobilities would violate intrinsic charge neutrality.
