<h1 id="2/c/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [C](../c.md)

Replacing X-rays by neutrons of the same [wavelength](../../../../wavelength.md) keeps the geometric Bragg positions, but replaces electron-density amplitudes by nuclear coherent scattering lengths. For Al all sites still carry the same nonzero amplitude, so its six reflections remain. For Si the identical two-site basis still has the same destructive-interference conditions, so its four reflections remain. Isotopic randomness can add diffuse/incoherent scattering but does not create a new periodic basis with different systematic Bragg absences.

K and Cl, however, have different nuclear amplitudes despite their isoelectronic ionic [electron](../../../../electron.md) clouds. For natural isotopic composition, $b_K=3.67$ fm and $b_{\rm Cl}=9.577$ fm. The all-odd/all-even basis-intensity ratio becomes

$$
\frac{|b_K-b_{\rm Cl}|^2}{|b_K+b_{\rm Cl}|^2}=0.199,
$$

which is no longer the strong X-ray cancellation. The two odd rings should therefore be observable with suitable sensitivity, giving

$$
\boxed{N_{\rm Al}=6,\qquad N_{\rm KCl}=6,\qquad N_{\rm Si}=4}.
$$

The numerical contrast uses the NIST bound coherent lengths.[Complete table, natural-element K and Cl rows: coherent b in femtometres; also Al and Si rows](../../../../neutron-scattering-lengths-and-cross-sections-by-nist.md) Counts here mean allowed and reasonably observable rings in the specified range, assuming sufficient angular coverage and resolution; no theory alone fixes an instrument's intensity threshold. These nonmagnetic examples use nuclear elastic scattering, not magnetic scattering.
