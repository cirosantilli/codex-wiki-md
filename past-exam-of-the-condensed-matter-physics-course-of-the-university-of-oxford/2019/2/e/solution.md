<h1 id="2/e/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [E](../e.md)

For one Cartesian displacement, the harmonic potential is $m_{\mathrm{Ar}}\omega_E^2x^2/2$. At $T\gg T_E$, the [equipartition theorem](../../../../equipartition-theorem.md) assigns $k_BT/2$ to this quadratic potential term. Hence

$$
\boxed{\langle x^2\rangle=\frac{k_BT}{m_{\mathrm{Ar}}\omega_E^2}=\frac{\hbar^2T}{m_{\mathrm{Ar}}k_BT_E^2}.}
$$

This is a one-coordinate [mean squared displacement](../../../../mean-squared-displacement.md), not the total three-dimensional displacement variance, which is three times larger in the isotropic model.

For comparison, the exact [Einstein mean-square displacement](../../../../einstein-mean-square-displacement.md) is

$$
\langle x^2\rangle=\frac{\hbar}{2m_{\mathrm{Ar}}\omega_E}\coth\left(\frac{T_E}{2T}\right).
$$

Using $\coth y\simeq1/y$ recovers the requested high-temperature result. Its nonzero zero-temperature limit is the displacement contribution of the [zero-point energy](../../../../zero-point-energy.md).
