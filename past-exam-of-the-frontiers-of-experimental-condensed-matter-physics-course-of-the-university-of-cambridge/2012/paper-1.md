<h4 id="1/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1/a)

Both [neutron diffraction](../../neutron.md#neutron-diffraction) and [X-ray diffraction](../../quantum-scattering.md#x-ray-diffraction) use [interference](../../physical-optics.md#interference-wave-propagation) from atomic arrangements. [Elastic scattering](../../quantum-scattering.md#elastic-scattering) gives reciprocal-space peaks when the [momentum transfer](../../quantum-scattering.md#momentum-transfer) matches a [reciprocal lattice vector](../../quantum-scattering.md#reciprocal-lattice-vector). For a basis with sites $\mathbf r_j$, the structural amplitude is $F(\mathbf Q)=\sum_jb_j(\mathbf Q)e^{i\mathbf Q\cdot\mathbf r_j}$, and peak intensities constrain positions, occupancies and thermal displacement as well as lattice symmetry. Powder averaging loses some directional information that a suitable single-crystal measurement retains.

X-rays couple primarily to the [electron](../../physics.md#electron) distribution. Their atomic form factors generally favor heavier atoms and decrease with increasing scattering angle; the [wavelength](../../wave-equation.md#wavelength) can also be tuned near an [absorption edge](../../electromagnetic-radiation.md#absorption-edge) for element-sensitive anomalous contrast. Neutrons scatter from nuclei with scattering lengths that vary irregularly with element and [isotope](../../chemical-substance.md#isotope). This can make light atoms visible beside heavy ones, and [isotope](../../chemical-substance.md#isotope) substitution can change contrast without changing the nominal chemical element. In particular, replacing [hydrogen](../../chemical-substance.md#hydrogen) with [deuterium](../../chemical-substance.md#deuterium) changes the balance of coherent and incoherent neutron scattering.

Neutron magnetic scattering also couples to electronic magnetic moments, allowing [magnetic order](../../electromagnetism.md#magnetic-order) to produce peaks distinct from the nuclear structure. X-ray magnetic scattering is possible too, especially under suitable resonant conditions; it is incorrect to say X-rays can never probe [magnetism](../../electromagnetism.md#magnetism). Neutron experiments often require larger samples and dedicated sources, whereas X-ray scattering is available in laboratories as well as synchrotrons. Absorption, multiple scattering, texture and instrumental resolution affect both methods. Their complementarity comes from different contrast weights and magnetic sensitivity, rather than from two identical pictures of the same density.

<h4 id="1/b/solution">Solution</h4>

In [photoemission electron microscopy](../../optics.md#photoemission-electron-microscopy), illuminate a surface with ultraviolet light or X-rays and form an electron-optical image of the emitted [electrons](../../physics.md#electron). An extraction field accelerates them away from the sample; an immersion objective and projection lenses magnify their spatial distribution onto an [electron](../../physics.md#electron) detector. Unlike a scanned probe, this is a full-field image. Surface preparation and [vacuum](../../physics.md#vacuum) matter because the emitted low-energy [electrons](../../physics.md#electron) are strongly affected by contamination and local [electric fields](../../electric-field.md).

Contrast can come from work-function differences, composition, occupied electronic states, illumination and topography. Near threshold, a small work-function change can produce a large emission-yield change. Therefore brightness is not simply geometrical height. An [energy filter](../../optics.md#energy-filter) can select an emitted-electron [energy](../../energy.md) interval; scanning the selection yields spatially resolved spectra. Sweeping the [photon](../../quantum-mechanics.md#photon) [energy](../../energy.md) across an [absorption edge](../../electromagnetic-radiation.md#absorption-edge) can add element and chemical-state sensitivity. Appropriate polarization-dependent measurements can probe magnetic domains.[instrument definition and optional energy-filter imaging](../../physics-bibliography.md#photo-emission-electron-microscope-by-jeol)

Resolution depends on electron-lens aberrations, the accepted angular and [energy](../../energy.md) spread, extraction geometry, sample flatness, space charge, mechanical stability and detector sampling. Rejecting [electrons](../../physics.md#electron) improves some aberrations at a cost in signal. An unfiltered [photoelectron](../../atomic-physics.md#photoelectron) image and a chemically selected image therefore answer different questions even when they use the same microscope.

In [X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy](../../physical-chemistry.md#x-ray-photoelectron-spectroscopy), the [electron](../../physics.md#electron) [binding energy](../../energy.md#binding-energy) relative to the chosen reference follows $E_B=h\nu-E_K-\phi_{\rm analyzer}$. A [core-level chemical shift](../../physical-chemistry.md#core-level-chemical-shift) is a change in a core peak's [binding energy](../../energy.md#binding-energy) between environments. Initial-state contributions include charge redistribution, bonding, the local electrostatic potential and chemical-potential changes. Final-state contributions include relaxation and screening of the newly created core hole. The measured shift contains both; it is not a direct meter of an integer oxidation state.

For a related chemical series, [electron](../../physics.md#electron) withdrawal often raises a core [binding energy](../../energy.md#binding-energy), but screening and referencing can change that trend. A [surface core-level shift](../../physical-chemistry.md#surface-core-level-shift) can separate surface atoms from bulk atoms because coordination and screening differ. Adsorption can introduce another component for chemically bound surface atoms. Spin-orbit doublets, multiplet or satellite features must be distinguished from separate chemical environments.

Reliable comparison needs a consistent Fermi-level or other justified [energy](../../energy.md) reference, compensation or diagnosis of sample charging, adequate [energy](../../energy.md) resolution and a stated line-shape/background model. Angle or photon-energy variation changes surface weighting through [electron](../../physics.md#electron) escape depth, helping distinguish surface and bulk assignments. A shifted, broader or overlapping peak alone does not uniquely identify a chemical species or its concentration.

<h3 id="2/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

The large rises mark the opening of Ga and As K-shell [photoionization](../../atomic-physics.md#photoionization) channels at their respective thresholds. Above each edge, the outgoing [photoelectron](../../atomic-physics.md#photoelectron) interferes with waves backscattered by neighboring atoms; that [interference](../../physical-optics.md#interference-wave-propagation) modulates the absorption transition probability. Near-edge resonances and multiple scattering require a different level of treatment from the simple high-energy single-scattering formula. Ga has four As nearest neighbors, while As has four Ga nearest neighbors, so the two edges sample different central atoms and scattering factors even though they probe the same nearest-neighbor bond lengths.

Write $k=\sqrt{2m_e(E-E_0)}/\hbar$ and $\chi=(\mu-\mu_0)/\mu_0$ relative to a smooth atomic background. The phase $2kR_j+\phi_j(k)$ includes the outward-and-return propagation distance and atomic [phase shifts](../../wave.md#phase-shift). The factor $R_j^{-2}$ describes geometrical spreading; the backscattering amplitude depends on the neighbor species. The exponential $e^{-2R_j/\lambda(k)}$ accounts for loss of coherent [photoelectron](../../atomic-physics.md#photoelectron) propagation. Averaging over Gaussian bond-length fluctuations gives $\langle e^{2ik\delta R}\rangle=e^{-2k^2\sigma_j^2}$.

The printed [polarization](../../wave.md#polarization-waves) factor needs a notation correction: for K-shell dipole absorption it is proportional to $(\widehat{\mathbf R}_j\cdot\boldsymbol\epsilon)^2=\cos^2\theta_j$, not literally the cosine of a dimensional vector dot product. Four equivalent tetrahedral bonds have an isotropic sum of these projection weights. If the sum is over shells rather than individual neighbors, it also needs the [coordination number](../../ionic-bonding.md#coordination-number). A common conventional amplitude contains $S_0^2f_j(k)/(kR_j^2)$; omitted normalization and $1/k$ factors must be absorbed in the printed $f_B$ convention rather than silently changing its dimensions.

These are the ingredients of [extended X-ray absorption fine structure](../../physics.md#extended-x-ray-absorption-fine-structure). A shell fit uses calculated or reference scattering amplitudes and phases, and refines bond distances, coordination and mean-square relative disorder together with an edge-energy correction. Fourier transformation separates approximate distance contributions, but the uncorrected peak position is shifted by the scattering phase and is not an exact bond length.[Chapter 3, equations 3.9–3.11 and subsequent mean-free-path correction; Chapter 6, shell fitting](../../physics-bibliography.md#fundamentals-of-xafs-by-matthew-newville)

**Sample A has the larger Ga–As bond length.** Its oscillations advance slightly faster with $k$: at higher $k$ its corresponding maxima lie to the left of B's. Reading several full cycles gives approximate periods $\Delta k_A\simeq1.47\,\mathrm{\mathring A^{-1}}$ and $\Delta k_B\simeq1.50\,\mathrm{\mathring A^{-1}}$. In the constant/common-phase-slope estimate, $R\propto1/\Delta k$, so

$$
\boxed{\frac{R_A-R_B}{R_B}\approx\frac{\Delta k_B}{\Delta k_A}-1\sim0.02.}
$$

This is a **rough percent-level difference**, not a precise fitted strain. Nonconstant atomic [phase shifts](../../wave.md#phase-shift) affect the absolute distance inferred from a period and also limit this fractional estimate. The graph does not establish which alloy has which [indium](../../chemical-substance.md#indium) concentration or how much its bulk lattice parameter changed. Local Ga–As relaxation need not follow a uniform bulk strain exactly.

Low [temperature](../../temperature.md) reduces thermal relative motion, preserving high-$k$ oscillations and separating small phase changes more clearly. It does not remove [zero-point motion](../../quantum-harmonic-oscillator.md#zero-point-motion) or static alloy disorder. To obtain an illustrative disorder estimate, take the high-$k$ envelope of the plotted $k\chi(k)$ to fall from about $0.19$ at $k=7.5\,\mathrm{\mathring A^{-1}}$ to $0.12$ at $k=10.4\,\mathrm{\mathring A^{-1}}$. If other envelope factors vary slowly over this interval,

$$
\sigma^2\approx\frac{\log(0.19/0.12)}{2(10.4^2-7.5^2)}\,\mathrm{\mathring A^2}
\simeq\boxed{4\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{\mathring A^2}},\qquad \sigma\sim0.06\,\mathrm{\mathring A}.
$$

The value is only an order-of-magnitude, model-dependent reading: the backscattering amplitude, [mean free path](../../ideal-gas.md#mean-free-path) and normalization have not been independently removed. In particular, $\sigma^2$ is the **bond-projected mean-square relative displacement**,

$$
\sigma^2=\langle[(\mathbf u_{\rm As}-\mathbf u_{\rm Ga})\cdot\widehat{\mathbf R}]^2\rangle,
$$

including [correlations](../../variance.md#pearson-correlation-coefficient) and static disorder. It is not uniquely either atom's mean-square displacement. Equal uncorrelated projected displacements would each contribute $\sigma^2/2$, but that extra assumption is not supplied by the figure.

In [scanning tunneling microscopy](../../quantum-mechanics.md#scanning-tunneling-microscopy), $I\propto e^{-2\kappa z}\int_{E_F}^{E_F+eV}\rho_s(\mathbf r,E)\,dE$ under simplified constant-tip-density assumptions, with $\kappa\simeq\sqrt{2m\phi}/\hbar$. A constant-current height map mixes actual surface height with local electronic density, barrier/work-function changes and tip structure. Changing bias can change contrast without moving atoms. [Scanning tunneling spectroscopy](../../quantum-mechanics.md#scanning-tunneling-spectroscopy) instead records [energy](../../energy.md) dependence, often through $dI/dV$; tip states, matrix elements and thermal or modulation broadening still affect its interpretation.

In [atomic force microscopy](../../engineering.md#atomic-force-microscopy), contrast comes from tip-sample [forces](../../force.md) or their gradients: short-range chemical bonding and repulsion, dispersion [forces](../../force.md), electrostatics and sometimes [magnetism](../../electromagnetism.md#magnetism) or capillary [forces](../../force.md). Contact deflection, oscillation amplitude and [frequency](../../wave.md#frequency) shift are different observables. A force-gradient image can therefore differ from a geometrical-height image, and changing the tip termination can strongly change atomic contrast.

STM's exponential distance sensitivity can give excellent vertical sensitivity; usable resolution also depends on current noise, feedback, vibration and drift. Lateral contrast depends on the apex orbitals and electronic spatial extent, not merely the nominal tip radius. In AFM, broad-tip geometrical convolution broadens larger features; a sharp terminal atom and short-range [forces](../../force.md) can produce much finer atomic contrast. [Cantilever](../../elasticity-physics.md#cantilever) stiffness, amplitude, [frequency](../../wave.md#frequency) noise, [force](../../force.md) range, [thermal fluctuations](../../statistical-physics.md#thermal-fluctuation) and feedback influence height sensitivity. In both techniques, detecting a small displacement is distinct from resolving two neighboring objects.

An example is the [Cu(111) surface-state standing-wave experiment](../../quantum-mechanics.md#cu-111-surface-state-standing-wave-experiment). Low-temperature STM and spatially resolved spectroscopy connect atomic steps or defects in a surface map with surrounding energy-dependent electronic [interference](../../physical-optics.md#interference-wave-propagation) patterns. Their period changes with bias, whereas a fixed structural corrugation would not acquire that electronic dispersion. The result shows why spectroscopic maps reveal surface-electron scattering beyond what a height image alone identifies.[1993, observation of Cu(111) local-density-of-states standing waves](../../physics-bibliography.md#imaging-standing-waves-in-a-two-dimensional-electron-gas-by-m-f-crommie-and-others) The apparent fringes are not a second set of equally spaced surface atoms.

<h4 id="3/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

[Transmission electron microscopy](../../optics.md#transmission-electron-microscopy) accelerates [electrons](../../physics.md#electron) through a thin sample, then uses [electron](../../physics.md#electron) lenses to form an image or diffraction pattern. [Elastic scattering](../../quantum-scattering.md#elastic-scattering) encodes projected structure and potential; inelastic scattering carries excitation and chemical information. Thin specimens reduce unwanted multiple scattering and absorption, although coherent dynamical scattering can still matter in a crystal. High-resolution phase-contrast images depend on defocus and the transfer function as well as the specimen; they are not direct height maps.

The relativistic [wavelength](../../wave-equation.md#wavelength) is $\lambda=h/\sqrt{2m_e eV[1+eV/(2m_ec^2)]}$. A shorter [wavelength](../../wave-equation.md#wavelength) permits fine resolution but does not guarantee it. A schematic probe/image blur budget contains diffraction $\sim\lambda/\alpha$, [spherical aberration](../../geometrical-optics.md#spherical-aberration) $\sim C_s\alpha^3$ and [chromatic aberration](../../geometrical-optics.md#chromatic-aberration) $\sim C_c\alpha\Delta E/E$, plus source size, instabilities, specimen motion and [radiation damage](../../biology.md#radiation-damage). Optimizing the aperture angle balances these terms; increasing accelerating voltage can also alter damage and contrast.

Bright-field imaging accepts the central transmitted beam or its neighborhood. Regions that scatter [electrons](../../physics.md#electron) outside that acceptance can appear dark. Conventional dark-field imaging selects a diffracted beam. [Annular dark-field electron imaging](../../optics.md#annular-dark-field-electron-imaging) in a scanning TEM instead collects a range of scattered angles while a probe is scanned. A sufficiently high-angle detector often gives strong atomic-number sensitivity and more nearly incoherent contrast; thickness, channeling and detector geometry prevent a universal conversion of intensity to composition.

[Electron energy loss spectroscopy](../../physics.md#electron-energy-loss-spectroscopy) measures [energy](../../energy.md) lost by transmitted [electrons](../../physics.md#electron). Core-ionization thresholds identify elements, while near-edge fine structure probes bonding and unoccupied states. At successive probe positions, one can record a spectrum image or form a map from a selected edge after background subtraction. Plural scattering, edge overlap, delocalization, specimen thickness and counting statistics limit the interpretation. Chemical specificity comes from excitation energies and spectral structure, not from brightness alone.

A development already established before this 2012 paper is [aberration-corrected transmission electron microscopy](../../optics.md#aberration-corrected-transmission-electron-microscopy): multipole correctors compensate leading lens aberrations, allowing useful larger angular acceptance and finer probes. Correction does not remove every chromatic, stability or specimen limit. A concrete application was atom-by-atom annular-dark-field analysis of monolayer boron nitride with substitutional defects, resolving chemical differences between sites in a nonuniform lattice.[2010, low-voltage aberration-corrected STEM of monolayer boron nitride](../../physics-bibliography.md#atom-by-atom-structural-and-chemical-analysis-by-annular-dark-field-electron-microscopy-by-o-l-krivanek-and-others) Combining structural maps with spatially resolved core-loss spectra supplies another route to distinguish composition and bonding at interfaces. Both exploit a calibrated imaging or spectroscopy response rather than assuming every visible spot is chemically self-identifying.
