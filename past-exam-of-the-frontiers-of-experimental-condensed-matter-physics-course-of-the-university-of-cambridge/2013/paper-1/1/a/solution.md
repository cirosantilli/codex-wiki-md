[X-ray diffraction](../../../../../x-ray-diffraction.md) arises predominantly from electromagnetic scattering by the electronic charge distribution. For elastic scattering, contributions from different unit cells interfere constructively at a [reciprocal lattice](../../../../../reciprocal-lattice.md) vector, while the unit-cell [structure factor](../../../../../structure-factor.md) weights the different atoms and their positions. The atomic X-ray scattering factor generally decreases with increasing [momentum](../../../../../momentum-split.md) transfer because an extended [electron](../../../../../electron.md) cloud scatters out of phase with itself. Heavy atoms often scatter more strongly, making a light atom difficult to locate beside a heavy one; near an absorption edge, anomalous dispersion changes the contrast and can provide additional chemical sensitivity.

In [neutron diffraction](../../../../../neutron-diffraction.md), the strong interaction with atomic nuclei supplies a short-range nuclear scattering amplitude. Its coherent scattering length depends on isotope and does not vary monotonically with atomic number; isotope substitution can therefore give useful contrast, and some light elements can be prominent. A neutron's magnetic moment also couples to magnetic moments in a specimen, so magnetic [diffraction](../../../../../diffraction.md) measures ordered spin structures. Absorption and incoherent scattering depend strongly on isotope as well. The nuclear scattering length is approximately independent of [momentum](../../../../../momentum-split.md) transfer in the usual thermal-neutron regime, whereas magnetic scattering has a form factor reflecting the electronic magnetization distribution.

[Electron diffraction](../../../../../electron-diffraction.md) probes the electrostatic potential of both nuclei and [electrons](../../../../../electron.md). The [electron](../../../../../electron.md)'s charge makes scattering strong, allowing thin specimens or surfaces to be studied with intense signals. Strong interaction also makes multiple, dynamical scattering more important, so [electron](../../../../../electron.md) [diffraction](../../../../../diffraction.md) intensities often cannot be interpreted as a simple single-scattering structure factor squared. Transmission geometry needs electron-transparent material; reflected low-energy [electrons](../../../../../electron.md) provide a surface-sensitive geometry. All three methods use phase-sensitive [interference](../../../../../interference-wave-propagation.md) to reveal periodic structure, but their scattering interactions, penetration, sample requirements and contrast differ.
