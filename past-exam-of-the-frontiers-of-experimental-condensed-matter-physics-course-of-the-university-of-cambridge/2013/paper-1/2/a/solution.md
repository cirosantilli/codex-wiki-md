<h1 id="2/a/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [A](../a.md)

In [near-edge X-ray absorption fine structure](../../../../../near-edge-x-ray-absorption-fine-structure.md), a [photon](../../../../../photon.md) promotes a core [electron](../../../../../electron.md) into an unoccupied molecular or solid-state level. Near an oxygen K-edge this is principally an O $1s$ excitation. Bound or resonant final states, orbital symmetry, local bonding and multiple scattering shape the edge. A monochromatic tunable X-ray beam can measure transmission through a thin specimen, or fluorescence or [electron](../../../../../electron.md) yield as an absorption proxy. In [scanning transmission X-ray microscopy](../../../../../scanning-transmission-x-ray-microscopy.md), a focused spot is scanned while transmitted intensity is recorded; [energy](../../../../../energy-split.md) stacks across an edge distinguish regions with different chemistry even when their thicknesses are similar. A zone plate or another suitable X-ray optic sets spatial resolution, while [energy](../../../../../energy-split.md) resolution and reference spectra control chemical discrimination.

The dipole transition amplitude contains $\mathbf e\cdot\mathbf r$, so the incident electric-field direction selects final-orbital orientations. For an approximately free diatomic molecule with axis $\mathbf n$, the axial $\sigma^*$ transition has strength proportional to $|\mathbf e\cdot\mathbf n|^2$. Summing the two perpendicular $\pi^*$ directions gives strength proportional to $1-|\mathbf e\cdot\mathbf n|^2$, with separate radial matrix-element factors for the two resonance families. This [NEXAFS orientation dependence of a diatomic molecule](../../../../../nexafs-orientation-dependence-of-a-diatomic-molecule.md) explains the complementary [polarization](../../../../../polarization-waves.md) behavior; the molecular axis is not itself the direction of a $\pi^*$ orbital.

In the supplied geometry, normal incidence has [electric field](../../../../../electric-field-split.md) parallel to the surface, whereas near-grazing incidence with the illustrated [polarization](../../../../../polarization-waves.md) has a large surface-normal field component. The single-layer curves show a stronger $\pi^*$ resonance and much weaker $\sigma^*$ structure in the latter geometry. This is consistent with oxygen axes lying largely parallel to the surface: a normal [electric field](../../../../../electric-field-split.md) is perpendicular to those axes and excites $\pi^*$ efficiently, while suppressing the axial $\sigma^*$ transition. For flat molecules with random in-plane azimuths, the ideal axial factor is $1/2$ for a tangential field and zero for a normal field.

The multilayer curves instead have a strong $\pi^*$ peak at normal incidence and substantially stronger relative $\sigma^*$ response at grazing incidence. Their axes therefore have a substantial surface-normal component, consistent with the proposed tilted, more upright packing. The distinction supports a different orientation distribution in the multilayer and monolayer, rather than one isotropically oriented molecular population in both.

**[Figure 1](#2/a/image-ideal-dipole-orientation-factors-for-flat-and-tilted-diatomic-molecules). Ideal dipole orientation factors for flat and tilted diatomic molecules**.

For illustration, a molecular polar tilt $\beta$ and random azimuth give $\langle|\mathbf e\cdot\mathbf n|^2\rangle=\cos^2\beta\cos^2\alpha+\tfrac12\sin^2\beta\sin^2\alpha$, where $\alpha$ is the electric-field angle to the surface normal. The original diagram plots these ideal factors for two chosen tilts; it is not a reconstruction or fit of the supplied spectra. Quantitative tilt extraction would additionally need intensity normalization, [polarization](../../../../../polarization-waves.md) purity, azimuthal distribution and substrate-induced changes of the resonances. The spectra alone do not determine unique bond lengths or every adsorption site.
