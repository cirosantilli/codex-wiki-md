## 1

↑ **Parent:** [2011](2011.md)

<h3 id="1/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1/a)

The [Bessel process](../brownian-motion.md#bessel-process) is stopped in the compact interval $[\alpha,\beta]$, where its drift and diffusion coefficients are bounded. In particular its stopped stochastic integral has expectation zero on every finite time interval.

For $k>1$, taking expectations in the stopped equation gives

$$
\mathbb E Y_t-1=\frac{k-1}{2}\mathbb E\int_0^{t\wedge\tau}\frac{ds}{y_s}
\geq\frac{k-1}{2\beta}\mathbb E(t\wedge\tau).
$$

Since $Y_t\leq\beta$, this bounds $\mathbb E(t\wedge\tau)$ uniformly in $t$. For $k<1$, the same argument with $1-\mathbb E Y_t$ gives $\mathbb E(t\wedge\tau)\leq2\beta(1-\alpha)/(1-k)$.

If $k=1$, the stopped process is a stopped Brownian motion. [Itô's formula](../stochastic-calculus.md#ito-s-lemma) gives $\mathbb E(Y_t-1)^2=\mathbb E(t\wedge\tau)$, which is at most $\max\{(1-\alpha)^2,(\beta-1)^2\}$. Thus in every case the [monotone convergence theorem](../measure-theory.md#monotone-convergence-theorem) gives $\mathbb E\tau<\infty$, so $\tau<\infty$ almost surely. By path continuity its value at exit is one of the two endpoints. They are disjoint possibilities, hence

$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(y_\tau=\alpha)+\mathbb P(y_\tau=\beta)=1.}
$$

<h3 id="1/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

For a twice continuously differentiable function, [Itô's formula](../stochastic-calculus.md#ito-s-lemma) in this stopped diffusion reads

$$
f(Y_t)=f(1)+\int_0^{t\wedge\tau}f'(y_s)\,dW_s+
\frac12\int_0^{t\wedge\tau}\left(f''(y_s)+\frac{k-1}{y_s}f'(y_s)\right)ds.
$$

For $f(x)=x^{2-d}$,

$$
f'(x)=(2-d)x^{1-d},\qquad
\frac12\left(f''(x)+\frac{k-1}{x}f'(x)\right)=\frac{(2-d)(k-d)}2x^{-d}.
$$

Setting $d=k$ eliminates the drift. Moreover $f'$ is bounded on $[\alpha,\beta]$, so the stochastic integral is square-integrable on each finite horizon by the [Itô isometry](../stochastic-calculus.md#ito-isometry). Hence **$f(Y_t)$ is a true martingale**, not merely a local one. The process itself is bounded on the stopped interval. For $k=2$, this particular function is the constant one; the logarithmic scale function will supply the nontrivial hitting identity.

<h3 id="1/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#1/c)

Let $p=\mathbb P(y_\tau=\alpha)$. For $k\ne2$, the bounded stopped martingale from part (b) converges to $y_\tau^{2-k}$, so the [dominated convergence theorem](../measure-theory.md#dominated-convergence-theorem) gives

$$
1=p\alpha^{2-k}+(1-p)\beta^{2-k}.
$$

Therefore the [boundary hitting probability from a diffusion scale function](../stochastic-calculus.md#boundary-hitting-probability-from-a-diffusion-scale-function) is

$$
\boxed{p=\frac{1-\beta^{2-k}}{\alpha^{2-k}-\beta^{2-k}}\quad(k\ne2).}
$$

For $k=2$, applying [Itô's formula](../stochastic-calculus.md#ito-s-lemma) to $\log Y_t$ gives a bounded stopped martingale instead, yielding

$$
\boxed{p=\frac{\log\beta}{\log\beta-\log\alpha}\quad(k=2).}
$$

If $k>2$ and $\beta>1$ is fixed, the first expression tends to zero as $\alpha\downarrow0$. A continuous path that reaches zero before it reaches $\beta$ must first reach every such $\alpha$ before $\beta$, so that event has probability zero. Any path hitting zero in finite time has a finite maximum up to that time, and therefore belongs to one of these zero-probability events with an integer upper boundary $\beta>1$. Their countable union still has probability zero. Thus **the process does not hit zero almost surely**. This is the relevant part of the [Hitting-zero classification for a Bessel process](../brownian-motion.md#hitting-zero-classification-for-a-bessel-process).

## 2

↑ **Parent:** [2011](2011.md)

<h3 id="2/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#2/a)

The given [Doob L2 maximal inequality](../martingale.md#doob-l2-maximal-inequality) gives

$$
\mathbb E[(M_t^*)^2]\leq4\mathbb E|M_t|^2<4\,2^{-2n}=2^{-2(n-1)}.
$$

Apply [Markov's inequality](../probability-inequality.md#markov-s-inequality) to the nonnegative variable $(M_t^*)^2$, at the threshold printed in the question:

$$
\boxed{\mathbb P\bigl((M_t^*)^2\geq2^{-(n-1)}\bigr)
\leq\frac{2^{-2(n-1)}}{2^{-(n-1)}}=2^{-(n-1)}.}
$$

The corresponding threshold for $M_t^*$ itself is $2^{-(n-1)/2}$.

<h3 id="2/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

Set $A_n=\{\sup_{s\leq t}|M_s^{(n)}|\geq2^{-(n-1)/2}\}$. Part (a) gives $\sum_n\mathbb P(A_n)<\infty$. The [Borel-Cantelli first lemma](../probability-theory.md#borel-cantelli-first-lemma) implies that, almost surely, only finitely many $A_n$ occur. On the complementary full-probability event, all sufficiently late terms satisfy

$$
\sup_{s\leq t}|M_s^{(n)}|<2^{-(n-1)/2}.
$$

These deterministic bounds form a convergent geometric series. The finitely many earlier suprema are finite by continuity on the compact time interval. Hence

$$
\boxed{\sum_n\sup_{s\leq t}|M_s^{(n)}|<\infty\quad\text{almost surely}.}
$$

The series therefore converges absolutely and uniformly on $[0,t]$ by the [Weierstrass M-test](../probability-and-statistics.md#weierstrass-m-test), and its sample-path limit is continuous.

<h3 id="2/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#2/c)

Work modulo [indistinguishability of stochastic processes](../stochastic-process.md#indistinguishability-of-stochastic-processes), with norm $\|M\|=(\mathbb E|M_t|^2)^{1/2}$. It is a genuine norm: zero terminal value implies $M_s=\mathbb E[M_t\mid\mathcal F_s]=0$ at every fixed time, and continuity extends equality from countably many rational times to all times simultaneously.

Let $M^j$ be a Cauchy sequence in this norm. Choose a subsequence with $\|M^{j_{n+1}}-M^{j_n}\|<2^{-n}$. Part (b) applies to these difference martingales, so their telescoping series converges uniformly almost surely. Its limit has continuous paths. At each time $s$, conditional-expectation contractivity gives

$$
\|M^j_s-M^k_s\|_2\leq\|M^j_t-M^k_t\|_2,
$$

so the fixed-time limit also exists in $L^2$ and agrees with the pathwise limit. It has an adapted version obtained by taking the finite pointwise subsequence limit when it exists, and zero otherwise. Each such definition is measurable at its own time, and the almost surely uniform limit gives continuous paths. Passing to the $L^2$ limit in $\mathbb E[M^j_v\mid\mathcal F_s]=M^j_s$ shows that the limit is a martingale. The original Cauchy sequence converges to it in terminal norm. Thus **the space is complete**.

Part (b) supplies what fixed-time $L^2$ completeness alone does not: a single full-probability set on which a subsequence converges uniformly in time, preserving path continuity. This is [completeness of continuous square-integrable martingales](../martingale.md#completeness-of-continuous-square-integrable-martingales).

<h3 id="2/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#2/d)

For simple predictable integrands, the [Itô integral](../stochastic-calculus.md#ito-integral) is a continuous square-integrable martingale and the [Itô isometry](../stochastic-calculus.md#ito-isometry) gives

$$
\left\|\int_0^t(H_s-K_s)\,dW_s\right\|_2^2=\mathbb E\int_0^t|H_s-K_s|^2ds.
$$

Approximate a general square-integrable predictable integrand by simple ones. The resulting integral martingales are Cauchy in terminal $L^2$ norm. [Completeness of continuous square-integrable martingales](../martingale.md#completeness-of-continuous-square-integrable-martingales) guarantees a unique continuous martingale limit, independent of the approximation, which defines the integral on the completed integrand space.

## 3

↑ **Parent:** [2011](2011.md)

<h3 id="3/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

The bounded integrand makes $X_s=\int_0^sY_u\,dW_u$ a square-integrable martingale, with [quadratic variation](../stochastic-calculus.md#quadratic-variation)

$$
\langle X\rangle_s=\int_0^sY_u^2du\leq C^2t\qquad(s\leq t).
$$

Thus

$$
\mathbb E\exp\bigl(\tfrac12\langle X\rangle_t\bigr)\leq e^{C^2t/2}<\infty.
$$

[Novikov's condition](../stochastic-calculus.md#novikov-s-condition) applies to the [stochastic exponential](../stochastic-calculus.md#doleans-dade-exponential) $Z_s=\exp(X_s-\langle X\rangle_s/2)$. Hence **$Z$ is a true martingale with $\mathbb E Z_s=Z_0=1$**. It is strictly positive because its exponent is finite almost surely.

<h3 id="3/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

Define $\mathbb Q$ on $\mathcal F_t$ by density $Z_t$. Nonnegativity and [countable additivity](../measure.md#countable-additivity) of the integral make it a measure, and $\mathbb Q(\Omega)=\mathbb E_PZ_t=1$. Strict positivity of $Z_t$ also makes the two measures equivalent. The [Radon-Nikodym derivative](../measure-theory.md#radon-nikodym-derivative) identity gives $\mathbb E_Q[\mathbf1_AY]=\mathbb E_P[\mathbf1_AYZ_t]$, first for simple variables and then for every Q-integrable $Y$ by approximation.

Write $U=\mathbb E_Q[Y\mid\mathcal F_s]$. The density on $\mathcal F_s$ is $Z_s=\mathbb E_P[Z_t\mid\mathcal F_s]$. For every $A\in\mathcal F_s$,

$$
\mathbb E_P[\mathbf1_AZ_sU]=\mathbb E_Q[\mathbf1_AU]
=\mathbb E_Q[\mathbf1_AY]=\mathbb E_P[\mathbf1_AYZ_t].
$$

Moreover $Z_sU$ is P-integrable because $\mathbb E_P[Z_s|U|]=\mathbb E_Q|U|<\infty$. Uniqueness in the definition of [conditional expectation](../measure-theory.md#conditional-expectation) proves

$$
\boxed{Z_s\mathbb E_Q[Y\mid\mathcal F_s]=\mathbb E_P[YZ_t\mid\mathcal F_s].}
$$

This is [conditional expectation under a martingale density](../measure-theory.md#conditional-expectation-under-a-martingale-density).

<h3 id="3/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#3/c)

Boundedness makes each $N_u$ Q-integrable. For $s\leq u\leq t$, the density formula and conditioning first on $\mathcal F_u$ give

$$
\mathbb E_Q[N_u\mid\mathcal F_s]
=\frac{\mathbb E_P[N_uZ_t\mid\mathcal F_s]}{Z_s}
=\frac{\mathbb E_P[N_uZ_u\mid\mathcal F_s]}{Z_s}
=\frac{N_sZ_s}{Z_s}=N_s.
$$

The middle equality uses that $N_u$ is $\mathcal F_u$-measurable and $Z$ is a martingale; the next uses the assumed martingale property of $NZ$. Thus **$N$ is a Q-martingale**.

<h3 id="3/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#3/d)

The initial value can first be subtracted: it is $\mathcal F_0$-measurable and has the same integrability under both measures, since the density has conditional expectation one on $\mathcal F_0$. Put $N=M-\langle M,X\rangle$. Since $dZ=Z\,dX$, the [Itô product rule](../stochastic-calculus.md#ito-product-rule) and the [quadratic covariation](../stochastic-calculus.md#quadratic-covariation) identity $d\langle M,Z\rangle=Z\,d\langle M,X\rangle$ give

$$
d(ZN)=N\,dZ+Z\,dM-Z\,d\langle M,X\rangle+d\langle M,Z\rangle
=N\,dZ+Z\,dM.
$$

This is a local P-martingale. Stop $N$ at increasing bounded levels; the resulting products with $Z$ are true martingales, since they are local martingales dominated at stopping times by a constant times the uniformly integrable density process. Part (c) then shows that the stopped $N$ processes are Q-martingales. Consequently

$$
\boxed{M-\langle M,X\rangle\text{ is a continuous local Q-martingale}.}
$$

The unqualified true-martingale assertion printed in this part needs an additional integrability hypothesis. One sufficient version is a square-integrable P-martingale $M$. Indeed $Z_t\in L^2(P)$ by boundedness of $Y$, and the covariation bound gives

$$
\sup_{s\leq t}|\langle M,X\rangle_s|\leq C\sqrt t\,\langle M\rangle_t^{1/2}.
$$

The [Doob L2 maximal inequality](../martingale.md#doob-l2-maximal-inequality), $\mathbb E_P\langle M\rangle_t<\infty$, and [Cauchy-Schwarz inequality](../probability-and-statistics.md#cauchy-schwarz-inequality) therefore give $\mathbb E_Q\sup_{s\leq t}|N_s|<\infty$. Its local martingale identities pass to the limit, making it a true Q-martingale. This is [Girsanov compensation with square-integrable input](../stochastic-calculus.md#girsanov-compensation-with-square-integrable-input).

Here is a counterexample to the broader printed assertion. On $[0,1]$, take $X_s=W_s$, so $Y_s\equiv1$ and $Z_s=e^{W_s-s/2}$. Put $a=1/2$ and

$$
H=\frac{e^{W_a^2}}{1+W_a^2},\qquad
M_s=\begin{cases}0,&s\leq a,\\H(W_s-W_a),&s>a.\end{cases}
$$

Under P, $W_a$ has density $\pi^{-1/2}e^{-x^2}$, so $\mathbb E_PH<\infty$. Independence of future Brownian increments proves $M$ is a continuous true P-martingale. Under Q, $W_s^Q=W_s-s$ is Brownian and the density of $W_a$ is multiplied by $e^{x-1/4}$, so $\mathbb E_QH=\infty$. The compensated process after $a$ is

$$
N_s=H(W_s^Q-W_a^Q).
$$

The future Q-Brownian increment is independent of $H$ and has positive finite expected absolute value. Thus $\mathbb E_Q|N_s|=\infty$ for every $s>a$, so it cannot be a true martingale. This proves that [Bounded Girsanov drift does not preserve true martingales under compensation](../stochastic-calculus.md#bounded-girsanov-drift-does-not-preserve-true-martingales-under-compensation).

For the requested application, the Brownian driver does satisfy the needed conditions. To distinguish the state from the density-driving integral, call the state $U$. Set $Y_s=b(U_s)/a(U_s)$; the stated coefficient bounds make this integrand bounded. Applying the local result to $M=W$ gives

$$
\widetilde W_s=W_s-\int_0^s\frac{b(U_u)}{a(U_u)}du.
$$

It is a continuous local Q-martingale with quadratic variation $s$. The [Lévy characterization of Brownian motion](../brownian-motion.md#levy-characterization-of-brownian-motion) therefore makes it a Q-Brownian motion. Substituting $dW_s=d\widetilde W_s+[b(U_s)/a(U_s)]ds$ into the original state equation gives

$$
\boxed{dU_s=a(U_s)\,d\widetilde W_s+b(U_s)\,ds\quad\text{under Q}.}
$$

The Brownian driver after the change is $\widetilde W$; writing it as $W$ again in the last printed equation amounts to renaming that driver.
