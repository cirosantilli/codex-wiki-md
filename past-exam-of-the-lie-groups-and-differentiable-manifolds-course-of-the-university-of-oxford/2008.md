<h4 id="a/1/a">a</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#a/1)

<h5 id="a/1/a/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a/1/a)

For a [Matrix Lie group](../lie-theory.md#matrix-lie-group), $T_gG$ consists of derivatives $\gamma'(0)$ of differentiable curves in $G$ with $\gamma(0)=g$, viewed inside $M_n(\mathbb C)$ as a real vector space. At the identity, multiplying two such curves adds their derivatives, and reparametrizing a curve multiplies its derivative by any real scalar. Thus $L(G)=T_IG$ is a real vector subspace.

If $Y\in L(G)$ and $g\in G$, conjugating a curve with derivative $Y$ proves $gYg^{-1}\in L(G)$. Let $g(s)$ have $g(0)=I$ and $g'(0)=X\in L(G)$. Differentiating the curve $g(s)Yg(s)^{-1}$ inside the finite-dimensional, hence closed, vector subspace $L(G)$ gives

$$
XY-YX\in L(G).
$$

The matrix [commutator](../lie-algebra.md#commutator) is real bilinear, antisymmetric, and satisfies the [Jacobi identity](../lie-algebra.md#jacobi-identity) by associativity of matrix multiplication. This proves that **$L(G)$ is a real [Lie algebra](../lie-algebra.md)**.

The exponential-map theorem for a linear [Lie group](../lie-theory.md#lie-group) says $\exp X\in G$ whenever $X\in L(G)$. Applying it to every real multiple of $X$ gives $\exp(tX)\in G$ for every real $t$. Conversely that curve has derivative $X$ at zero, so $X\in L(G)$. Hence

$$
\boxed{X\in L(G)\iff\exp(tX)\in G\ \text{for every }t\in\mathbb R}.
$$

Here the [orthogonal group](../linear-algebra.md#orthogonal-group) is the real [group](../group.md) $O_n(\mathbb R)$. Differentiating $g(t)^Tg(t)=I$ at zero gives $X^T+X=0$. Conversely a real [skew-symmetric matrix](../linear-algebra.md#skew-symmetric-matrix) satisfies $(e^{tX})^Te^{tX}=e^{-tX}e^{tX}=I$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{L(O_n)=\{X\in M_n(\mathbb R):X^T=-X\},\qquad\dim_{\mathbb R}L(O_n)=n(n-1)/2}.
$$

For $n\ge2$ this is not a complex Lie subalgebra of $M_n(\mathbb C)$: multiplying a nonzero real member by $i$ leaves this real subspace. For $n=1$ the zero algebra is the trivial complex [Lie algebra](../lie-algebra.md) as well. The complex [orthogonal group](../linear-algebra.md#orthogonal-group) would be a different [group](../group.md) from the real [orthogonal group](../linear-algebra.md#orthogonal-group) used here.

<h5 id="a/1/b/ii">ii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#a/1/b)

<h6 id="a/1/b/ii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#a/1/b/ii)

Differentiating the affine block form gives

$$
\boxed{L(\operatorname{Aff}_n(\mathbb C))=
\left\{\begin{pmatrix}B&u\\0&0\end{pmatrix}:B\in M_n(\mathbb C),\ u\in\mathbb C^n\right\}}.
$$

Conversely the exponential of such a block matrix has bottom row $(0,1)$ and invertible upper-left block $e^{tB}$, so it remains in the [affine group](../lie-theory.md#affine-group). Its [commutator](../lie-algebra.md#commutator) corresponds to

$$
[(B,u),(C,v)]=([B,C],Bv-Cu).
$$

The space is closed under multiplication by $i$ and its bracket is complex bilinear. Thus it is a **complex [Lie algebra](../lie-algebra.md) of dimension $n^2+n$**, and its real dimension is $2n^2+2n$.

<h6 id="a/2/a/i/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#a/2/a/i)

The [matrix exponential](../linear-operator-theory.md#matrix-exponential) series differentiates term by term on bounded intervals and gives $(e^{tX})'=Xe^{tX}$ and $e^{0X}=I$, proving existence. If $a$ is another differentiable solution, then

$$
\frac d{dt}(e^{-tX}a(t))=-Xe^{-tX}a(t)+e^{-tX}Xa(t)=0.
$$

Its initial value is $I$, so $e^{-tX}a(t)=I$, giving **$a(t)=e^{tX}$ uniquely**.

<h5 id="a/2/a/ii">ii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a/2/a)

<h6 id="a/2/a/ii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#a/2/a/ii)

The exponential satisfies the initial conditions and $e^{(s+t)X}=e^{sX}e^{tX}$ because the two matrix exponents commute. Conversely, differentiating $a(s+t)=a(s)a(t)$ with respect to $s$ at zero gives $a'(t)=Xa(t)$. Even if only differentiability at zero was initially specified, the identity $a(t+h)=a(h)a(t)$ supplies differentiability at every $t$. The uniqueness result just proved yields $\boxed{a(t)=e^{tX}}$.

<h4 id="a/2/b">b</h4>

<h5 id="a/2/b/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#a/2/b)

For fixed $X\in L(G)$, set $b(t)=\phi(e^{tX})$. The [homomorphism](../algebra.md#homomorphism) property gives $b(s+t)=b(s)b(t)$ and $b(0)=I$. Its initial derivative is $L(\phi)(X)=D\phi_I(X)$. The [one-parameter subgroup](../lie-theory.md#one-parameter-subgroup) uniqueness theorem gives

$$
b(t)=e^{tL(\phi)(X)},\qquad\boxed{\phi(e^X)=e^{L(\phi)(X)}}.
$$

For the [Adjoint representation](../lie-algebra.md#adjoint-representation-of-a-lie-algebra), $\operatorname{Ad}_gY=gYg^{-1}$. Thus

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{ad}_XY=\left.\frac d{dt}(e^{tX}Ye^{-tX})\right|_{t=0}=XY-YX=[X,Y]}.
$$

The differential $L(\phi)$ is a real linear map of Lie algebras; the bracket identity follows by differentiating the compatibility of conjugation with $\phi$.

<h4 id="a/2/c">c</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#a/2)

<h5 id="a/2/c/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#a/2/c)

Write $\mathfrak g_{\mathbb C}=L(G_{\mathbb C})$ and $\mathfrak h=L(H)$. A root is a nonzero weight $\alpha\in\mathfrak h^*$ of the adjoint action. Its [root space](../semisimple-lie-algebra.md#root-space) is

$$
\mathfrak g_\alpha=\{Y\in\mathfrak g_{\mathbb C}:[Z,Y]=\alpha(Z)Y\text{ for all }Z\in\mathfrak h\}.
$$

At the [group](../group.md) level these weights come from nontrivial characters of the Cartan [subgroup](../group.md#subgroup) acting by conjugation. The Cartan [root-space decomposition](../semisimple-lie-algebra.md#root-space-decomposition) is

$$
\mathfrak g_{\mathbb C}=\mathfrak h\oplus\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Phi}\mathfrak g_\alpha.
$$

The zero-[weight space](../semisimple-lie-algebra.md#weight-space) is $\mathfrak h$; for the complexification of a [compact](../topology.md#compact-space) [group](../group.md) the algebra is reductive, with any centre contained in this zero-[weight space](../semisimple-lie-algebra.md#weight-space).

For the product in question,

$$
G_{\mathbb C}=SL_2(\mathbb C)\times SL_2(\mathbb C),\qquad
\mathfrak g_{\mathbb C}=\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb C)\oplus\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb C).
$$

Take the diagonal [Cartan subalgebra](../semisimple-lie-algebra.md#cartan-subalgebra) generated by $H_1=(\operatorname{diag}(1,-1),0)$ and $H_2=(0,\operatorname{diag}(1,-1))$. On $Z=xH_1+yH_2$, define $\alpha_1(Z)=2x$ and $\alpha_2(Z)=2y$. The roots are exactly

$$
\boxed{\Phi=\{\alpha_1,-\alpha_1,\alpha_2,-\alpha_2\}}.
$$

If $E=E_{12}$ and $F=E_{21}$, their four root spaces are respectively $\mathbb C(E,0)$, $\mathbb C(F,0)$, $\mathbb C(0,E)$, and $\mathbb C(0,F)$. These four lines and the two-dimensional diagonal subalgebra form the full six-dimensional decomposition. There are no mixed roots: the two direct summands commute. This is the [root system](../semisimple-lie-algebra.md#root-system) $A_1\times A_1$.

<h4 id="a/3/a">a</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#a/3)

<h5 id="a/3/a/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a/3/a)

With the convention applicable also to disconnected [compact](../topology.md#compact-space) groups, the [Weyl group](../semisimple-lie-algebra.md#weyl-group) is $W=N_G(T)/T$, where the [normalizer](../group-action.md#normalizer) consists of elements taking $T$ to itself by conjugation. Put $\mathfrak t=L(T)$ and $\Lambda=\ker(\exp:\mathfrak t\to T)$. This is a full [lattice](../mathematical-logic.md#lattice) with $T\cong\mathfrak t/\Lambda$.

If $\psi$ is a [torus](../topological-surface.md#torus) automorphism, exponential compatibility gives $\psi(\exp X)=\exp(D\psi_I X)$. Therefore $D\psi_I$ sends $\Lambda$ into itself; applying the inverse proves it sends the [lattice](../mathematical-logic.md#lattice) onto itself. In a [lattice](../mathematical-logic.md#lattice) [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) this derivative is an element of $GL_r(\mathbb Z)$.

Conjugation by a [connected](../geometry-and-topology.md#connected-space) [subgroup](../group.md#subgroup) $Q\subset N_G(T)$ induces a [continuous](../calculus.md#continuous-function) family of these [integer](../integer.md) matrices. Since $GL_r(\mathbb Z)$ is discrete, this family is constant and equals its value at the identity. Its action on $\mathfrak t$ is therefore trivial, and surjectivity of the [torus](../topological-surface.md#torus) exponential shows that **$Q$ centralizes $T$**.

Apply this to the [identity component](../geometry-and-topology.md#identity-component) $N_G(T)^0$. It lies in $G^0$ and centralizes $T$. The allowed maximal-abelian property of $T$ in $G^0$ gives $N_G(T)^0\subseteq T$, while the [connected](../geometry-and-topology.md#connected-space) [group](../group.md) $T$ lies in that [identity component](../geometry-and-topology.md#identity-component). Thus $N_G(T)^0=T$. The [normalizer](../group-action.md#normalizer) is a closed [compact](../topology.md#compact-space) Lie [subgroup](../group.md#subgroup), so its component quotient is discrete and [compact](../topology.md#compact-space), hence finite. Consequently **$W$ is finite and discrete**. For disconnected $G$, its action on the [lattice](../mathematical-logic.md#lattice) need not be faithful; the component argument is essential.

The distinction is recorded in [Weyl group of a disconnected compact Lie group](../semisimple-lie-algebra.md#weyl-group-of-a-disconnected-compact-lie-group).

A finite discrete [group](../group.md) has trivial [identity component](../geometry-and-topology.md#identity-component), so its only [torus](../topological-surface.md#torus) is $T=\{1\}$. Thus $N_G(T)=G$ and, under the definition $N_G(T)/T$,

$$

The alternative quotient by the [centralizer](../group-action.md#centralizer) would give a different answer for a disconnected [group](../group.md); it is not the convention used here.

A [maximal torus](../lie-theory.md#maximal-torus) is $T=SO_2$, which is normal of index two in $O_2$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{W=O_2/SO_2\cong C_2}.

<h5 id="a/3/b/iii">iii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#a/3/b)

<h6 id="a/3/b/iii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#a/3/b/iii)

Use the given [torus](../topological-surface.md#torus) of independent rotations in $n$ mutually [orthogonal](../inner-product.md#orthogonal-vectors) two-planes and a fixed final axis. A [normalizer](../group-action.md#normalizer) element permutes the two-plane rotation characters, and on each plane either preserves or reverses its [orientation](../algebraic-topology.md#orientation-of-a-simplex). Its induced action therefore permutes the $n$ angle coordinates and changes their signs independently.

Every such signed permutation is realized. Permutations of two-dimensional blocks have [determinant](../multilinear-algebra.md#determinant) one. A reflection in any chosen plane reverses that angle; reflecting the remaining one-dimensional axis as necessary restores total [determinant](../multilinear-algebra.md#determinant) one. The kernel consists precisely of independent plane rotations: commuting with a generic [torus](../topological-surface.md#torus) element forces preservation of its distinct rotation planes, and the [determinant](../multilinear-algebra.md#determinant) condition then fixes the final axis. Thus the kernel is $T$ and

$$
\boxed{W\cong(C_2)^n\rtimes S_n,\qquad |W|=2^nn!}.
$$

This is the signed permutation [group](../group.md), the [Weyl group](../semisimple-lie-algebra.md#weyl-group) of type $B_n$.

<h4 id="a/4/a">a</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#a/4)

<h5 id="a/4/a/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a/4/a)

As usual here, representations are finite-dimensional and [continuous](../calculus.md#continuous-function). Start with any positive definite [Hermitian form](../linear-algebra.md#hermitian-form) $h_0$ and normalized [Haar measure](../measure.md#haar-measure) $\mu$ on the [compact](../topology.md#compact-space) [group](../group.md). Define

$$
h(v,w)=\int_Gh_0(gv,gw)\,d\mu(g).
$$

This is Hermitian, sesquilinear, and positive definite: for $v\ne0$ its [continuous](../calculus.md#continuous-function) integrand $h_0(gv,gv)$ is strictly positive. Bi-invariance of [Haar measure](../measure.md#haar-measure) shows $h(av,aw)=h(v,w)$ for every $a\in G$.

If $U\subset V$ is invariant, then so is its [orthogonal complement](../hilbert-space.md#orthogonal-complement): for $v\perp U$ and $u\in U$, $h(av,u)=h(v,a^{-1}u)=0$. Hence $V=U\oplus U^\perp$ as a representation. Induct on dimension, splitting whenever a nontrivial proper [invariant subspace](../representation-theory.md#invariant-subspace) exists. This proves **every such representation is a direct sum of irreducible representations**.

<h4 id="a/4/b">b</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#a/4)

<h5 id="a/4/b/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#a/4/b)

Schur [orthogonality](../inner-product.md#orthogonal-vectors) for irreducible complex characters of a [compact](../topology.md#compact-space) [group](../group.md), with normalized [Haar measure](../measure.md#haar-measure), is

$$
\langle\chi_\alpha,\chi_\beta\rangle:=\int_G\chi_\alpha(g)\overline{\chi_\beta(g)}\,d\mu(g)=\delta_{\alpha\beta}.
$$

Equivalent representations clearly have equal characters, since trace is invariant under conjugation. Conversely complete reducibility gives $\chi_V=\sum_\alpha m_\alpha\chi_\alpha$, where the nonnegative [integer](../integer.md) multiplicity is recovered by $m_\alpha=\langle\chi_V,\chi_\alpha\rangle$. Equal characters thus give equal multiplicities of every irreducible constituent, hence **equivalent representations**.

<h4 id="a/4/c">c</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#a/4)

<h5 id="a/4/c/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#a/4/c)

The usual [Weyl character formula](../semisimple-lie-algebra.md#weyl-character-formula) requires **a [compact](../topology.md#compact-space) [connected](../geometry-and-topology.md#connected-space) [Lie group](../lie-theory.md#lie-group)**. Choose a [maximal torus](../lie-theory.md#maximal-torus) $T$, a positive [root system](../semisimple-lie-algebra.md#root-system) $\Phi^+$, [Weyl group](../semisimple-lie-algebra.md#weyl-group) $W$, and $\rho=\frac12\sum_{\alpha\in\Phi^+}\alpha$. Irreducible representations are indexed by dominant integral highest weights $\lambda$ belonging to the [character](../representation-theory.md#character-of-a-representation) [lattice](../mathematical-logic.md#lattice) of the actual [group](../group.md) $T$. For $X\in L(T)$ such that $\exp X$ is regular,

$$
\boxed{\chi_\lambda(\exp X)=
\frac{\sum_{w\in W}\det(w)e^{w(\lambda+\rho)(X)}}
{\sum_{w\in W}\det(w)e^{w\rho(X)}}}.
$$

Weights are written as linear functionals on the complexified [torus](../topological-surface.md#torus) algebra; their values on the [compact](../topology.md#compact-space) real [torus](../topological-surface.md#torus) algebra are imaginary. The denominator is $e^{\rho(X)}\prod_{\alpha>0}(1-e^{-\alpha(X)})$. If $\rho$ is not itself a [character](../representation-theory.md#character-of-a-representation) of $T$, the individual expressions are interpreted on its covering algebra; their quotient is single-valued and descends to $T$. The apparent singularities at nonregular elements are removable, and conjugacy into a [maximal torus](../lie-theory.md#maximal-torus) determines the [character](../representation-theory.md#character-of-a-representation) on the whole [connected](../geometry-and-topology.md#connected-space) [group](../group.md).

The formula explicitly determines all irreducible characters from their highest weights, and its limit at the identity gives the dimension formula

$$
\dim V_\lambda=\prod_{\alpha>0}\frac{\langle\lambda+\rho,\alpha^\vee\rangle}{\langle\rho,\alpha^\vee\rangle}.
$$

[Character](../representation-theory.md#character-of-a-representation) multiplication and [orthogonality](../inner-product.md#orthogonal-vectors) then compute tensor-product multiplicities. For $SU_2$, highest weights are integers $m\ge0$, and at $\operatorname{diag}(z,z^{-1})$,

$$
\chi_m=\frac{z^{m+1}-z^{-m-1}}{z-z^{-1}}=z^m+z^{m-2}+\cdots+z^{-m},\qquad\dim V_m=m+1.
$$

[Connectedness](../geometry-and-topology.md#connected-space) cannot simply be omitted from this version. For a nontrivial finite [group](../group.md) the [maximal torus](../lie-theory.md#maximal-torus) is trivial, yet characters can differ away from the identity. The root data on that [torus](../topological-surface.md#torus) alone cannot supply the characters of all disconnected [compact](../topology.md#compact-space) groups.

<h3 id="b/5">5</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b)

<h4 id="b/5/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#b/5)

One definition of $T_xM$ is the space of derivations at $x$: linear maps $v:C^\infty(M)\to\mathbb R$ satisfying $v(fh)=f(x)v(h)+h(x)v(f)$. In local coordinates $u^1,\ldots,u^n$, each has the unique form $v=\sum_iv^i\partial/\partial u^i|_x$, identifying $T_xM$ with $\mathbb R^n$. Equivalently it is the space of velocities of curves through $x$, identifying curves with the same coordinate derivative.

For a chart $\phi:U\to\mathbb R^n$, give $TM|_U$ coordinates $(x,v)\mapsto(\phi(x),D\phi_xv)$. If $F=\psi\circ\phi^{-1}$ is a base transition, the induced transition is

$$
(u,v)\longmapsto(F(u),DF_u v).
$$

This is a smooth bijection with smooth inverse coming from the reverse chart. The resulting bundle atlas makes $TM$ a [smooth manifold](../smooth-manifold.md) of dimension $2n$. These local product charts also give the usual [Hausdorff](../topology.md#hausdorff-space) and second-countable [topology](../topology.md), inherited from a countable base atlas and its fibre coordinates.

For $n\ge1$, identify nonzero vectors in each fibre up to nonzero real scalar multiplication. Locally this gives $P(TM)|_U\cong U\times\mathbb{RP}^{n-1}$. In a projective chart where $v^j\ne0$, use the ratios $v^i/v^j$ for $i\ne j$. Under a base change, the fibre line maps by $[v]\mapsto[DF_uv]$; its projective coordinates are ratios of smooth linear expressions with nonzero denominator on the overlap. This defines a [smooth manifold](../smooth-manifold.md) of dimension $n+(n-1)=2n-1$.

For the sphere, a point is a pair $(x,L)$ with $|x|=1$ and an unoriented line $L\subset x^\perp$. Choose a unit $v\in L$. The triple $(x,v,x\times v)$ is a positively oriented orthonormal frame. Given a second pair, the unique [orientation](../algebraic-topology.md#orientation-of-a-simplex)-preserving linear map between chosen frames lies in $SO_3$ and carries the first pair to the second. Thus the action is transitive.

A rotation stabilizing $(x,L)$ fixes $x$, so rotates its tangent plane. To preserve the unoriented line it must act by $v\mapsto v$ or $v\mapsto-v$, giving exactly the identity and a half-turn about $x$. Hence **the [stabilizer](../group-action.md#stabilizer-subgroup) has order two**, and $P(TS^2)\cong SO_3/C_2$.

This is an example of the [projectivized tangent bundle](../vector-bundle.md#projectivized-tangent-bundle).

<h3 id="b/6">6</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b)

<h4 id="b/6/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#b/6)

In a [coordinate chart](../differential-geometry.md#manifold-chart), define the [exterior derivative](../differential-form.md#exterior-derivative) by

$$
d\left(\sum_Ia_I\,dx^{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{i_r}\right)
=\sum_Ida_I\wedge dx^{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{i_r}.
$$

The chain rule makes this independent of coordinates. It is real linear, increases form degree by one, agrees with the differential on functions, satisfies $d^2=0$, and obeys the [graded Leibniz rule](../commutative-algebra.md#graded-leibniz-rule)

$$
d(\alpha\wedge\beta)=d\alpha\wedge\beta+(-1)^r\alpha\wedge d\beta.
$$

It also commutes with [pullback](../category.md#pullback-category-theory) by a [smooth map](../smooth-manifold.md#smooth-map-between-manifolds). These properties characterize it locally and hence globally.

[de Rham cohomology](../differential-form.md#de-rham-cohomology) is the quotient of closed forms by exact forms. If $d\alpha=d\beta=0$, Leibniz gives $d(\alpha\wedge\beta)=0$. Changing $\alpha$ to $\alpha+d\eta$ changes the product by $d(\eta\wedge\beta)$; changing $\beta$ to $\beta+d\xi$ changes it by $(-1)^rd(\alpha\wedge\xi)$. Thus

$$
\boxed{[\alpha][\beta]=[\alpha\wedge\beta]}
$$

is independent of representatives. This gives the graded-commutative [de Rham cohomology](../differential-form.md#de-rham-cohomology) ring.

<h4 id="b/6/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#b/6)

<h5 id="b/6/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#b/6/i)

The nonvanishing top-degree form $\omega^m$ is a [volume form](../differential-form.md#volume-form). Declare an ordered tangent [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) positive when its value under $\omega^m$ is positive. Smooth nonvanishing ensures these choices agree on overlapping oriented charts and vary continuously. Hence **a [symplectic manifold](../symplectic-geometry.md#symplectic-manifold) is orientable**; this is its symplectic [orientation](../algebraic-topology.md#orientation-of-a-simplex).

<h4 id="b/6/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#b/6)

<h5 id="b/6/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#b/6/ii)

Use the standard convention that the [compact](../topology.md#compact-space) [manifold](../topological-manifold.md) is nonempty and has no boundary. Orient it by $\omega^m$. Since $d\omega=0$, every power $\omega^k$ is closed. If $\omega^k=d\eta$ for some $1\le k\le m$, then

$$
\omega^m=d(\eta\wedge\omega^{m-k}),
$$

again by Leibniz and closedness. [Stokes theorem](../multivariable-calculus.md#stokes-theorem) would give $\int_M\omega^m=0$. But this integral is strictly positive in the symplectic [orientation](../algebraic-topology.md#orientation-of-a-simplex), a contradiction. Therefore

$$
\boxed{[\omega^k]\ne0\in H^{2k}_{\mathrm{dR}}(M)\quad(1\le k\le m)}.
$$

The boundaryless hypothesis matters: a [compact](../topology.md#compact-space) disk has a symplectic area form but no nonzero ordinary second cohomology class.

<h4 id="b/6/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#b/6)

<h5 id="b/6/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#b/6/iii)

Let the two oriented surfaces carry positive area forms $\alpha$ and $\beta$, obtainable from oriented Riemannian metrics. A top-degree form on a surface is automatically closed. On their product set

$$
\omega=\pi_1^*\alpha+\pi_2^*\beta.
$$

It is closed, and

$$
\omega^2=2\pi_1^*\alpha\wedge\pi_2^*\beta
$$

is a nowhere-vanishing four-form; the two squared terms vanish because each comes from a two-dimensional factor. Thus **the product is symplectic**, with no [compactness](../topology.md#compact-space) assumption needed.

<h3 id="b/7">7</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b)

<h4 id="b/7/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [7](#b/7)

For [compact](../topology.md#compact-space) [connected](../geometry-and-topology.md#connected-space) oriented $n$-manifolds without boundary, the degree is characterized by

$$
\int_MF^*\eta=\deg(F)\int_N\eta
$$

for every smooth top-degree form $\eta$. Equivalently, at any [regular value](../differential-geometry.md#regular-value) $y$,

$$
\deg(F)=\sum_{x\in F^{-1}(y)}\operatorname{sign}\det D F_x.
$$

The [preimage](../set-theory.md#preimage) is finite by [compactness](../topology.md#compact-space) and the [inverse function theorem](../multivariable-calculus.md#inverse-function-theorem). Taking a form supported in a small neighbourhood of a [regular value](../differential-geometry.md#regular-value) and applying change of variables proves the signed-count formula. The top [de Rham cohomology](../differential-form.md#de-rham-cohomology) of a [compact](../topology.md#compact-space) [connected](../geometry-and-topology.md#connected-space) oriented [manifold](../topological-manifold.md) is one-dimensional, with integration giving its coordinate; [pullback](../category.md#pullback-category-theory) and [Stokes theorem](../multivariable-calculus.md#stokes-theorem) make the integral characterization independent of the chosen normalized form. Thus this signed count is independent of the [regular value](../differential-geometry.md#regular-value). In particular a nonzero degree forces surjectivity, and the number of points in any regular fibre is at least the absolute degree. Degree is invariant under [homotopy](../homotopy.md), as follows by applying [Stokes theorem](../multivariable-calculus.md#stokes-theorem) to the [pullback](../category.md#pullback-category-theory) on $M\times[0,1]$.

For the [polynomial](../polynomial.md) assertion take $k\ge1$. The map $P:\mathbb C\to\mathbb C$ extends smoothly to a map of the [Riemann sphere](../riemann-surfaces.md#riemann-sphere) fixing infinity: in reciprocal coordinates its target coordinate is

$$
\frac1{P(1/\zeta)}=\frac{\zeta^k}{1+a_1\zeta+\cdots+a_k\zeta^k}.
$$

The [homotopy](../homotopy.md) $P_t(z)=z^k+t(a_1z^{k-1}+\cdots+a_k)$ extends smoothly in the same way. The map $z\mapsto z^k$ has $k$ distinct inverse images of the [regular value](../differential-geometry.md#regular-value) one, each [orientation](../algebraic-topology.md#orientation-of-a-simplex)-preserving, since the real Jacobian of multiplication by a nonzero complex derivative is its squared absolute value. Its degree is $k$, so $\deg P=k\ne0$. It is onto the sphere and must attain zero at a finite point. This proves **every nonconstant complex [polynomial](../polynomial.md) has a root**, without assuming the result to count roots of a general [polynomial](../polynomial.md).

For a proper smooth $F:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^n$, choose a compactly supported $n$-form $\eta$ with $\int\eta=1$ and define

$$
\deg F=\int_{\mathbb R^n}F^*\eta.
$$

Properness makes its [pullback](../category.md#pullback-category-theory) compactly supported. If $\eta'$ is another choice, the given result gives $\eta-\eta'=d\beta$ with [compact](../topology.md#compact-space) support. The difference of the defining integrals is $\int d(F^*\beta)=0$, proving [independence](../random-variable.md#independent-random-variables). Sard's theorem supplies a [regular value](../differential-geometry.md#regular-value). A [proper map](../cohomology.md#proper-map) has finitely many preimages of it, and a sufficiently small neighbourhood has no additional inverse sheets outside the chosen inverse-[function](../function.md) neighbourhoods: otherwise a sequence of extra preimages would have a convergent subsequence by properness and limit in that fibre. A normalized form there therefore gives the same finite signed-count formula, proving that the degree is an [integer](../integer.md).

Finally $x\mapsto x^k$ is proper for $k\ge1$. For odd $k$, a positive [regular value](../differential-geometry.md#regular-value) has one inverse image with positive derivative. For positive even $k$, it has two inverse images with opposite derivative signs. Consequently

$$
\boxed{\deg(x\mapsto x^k)=\begin{cases}1,&k\ge1\text{ odd},\\0,&k\ge2\text{ even}.\end{cases}}
$$

For $k=0$ it is the constant map one, whose inverse image of the [compact](../topology.md#compact-space) singleton $\{1\}$ is all of $\mathbb R$. It is **not proper**, so its degree is not defined by the proper-map construction requested here.

<h3 id="b/8">8</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b)

<h4 id="b/8/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [8](#b/8)

A [Riemannian metric](../riemannian-metric.md) is a smooth positive definite symmetric bilinear form $g_q$ on each [tangent space](../tangent-vector.md#tangent-space). The inverse form on the cotangent space defines the kinetic [Hamiltonian](../hamiltonian-mechanics.md#hamiltonian)

$$
H(q,p)=\tfrac12g_q^{-1}(p,p)=\tfrac12g^{ij}(q)p_ip_j.
$$

It is smooth and satisfies $H(q,ap)=a^2H(q,p)$. With the canonical [symplectic form](../symplectic-geometry.md#symplectic-form) $\Omega=\sum_i dq^i\wedge dp_i$, define $\iota_{X_H}\Omega=dH$. This gives Hamilton's equations

$$
\dot q^i=\partial_{p_i}H=g^{ij}p_j,\qquad\dot p_i=-\partial_{q^i}H.
$$

Thus $p_i=g_{ij}\dot q^j$. Differentiating the inverse metric yields $\dot p_i=\tfrac12\partial_i g_{jk}\dot q^j\dot q^k$. Differentiating $p_i=g_{ij}\dot q^j$ and substituting gives

$$
\ddot q^i+\Gamma^i_{jk}\dot q^j\dot q^k=0.
$$

Therefore the projections of the [Hamiltonian](../hamiltonian-mechanics.md#hamiltonian) integral curves are affinely parametrized geodesics, and lifting a [geodesic](../riemannian-geometry.md#geodesic) by its metric covector gives a [Hamiltonian](../hamiltonian-mechanics.md#hamiltonian) integral curve.

For the [Killing vector field](../killing-vector-field.md) define the fibrewise linear [function](../function.md) $f(q,p)=p(Y_q)=p_iY^i(q)$. Along the lifted [geodesic](../riemannian-geometry.md#geodesic) it equals $g(\dot q,Y)$. Metric compatibility and the [geodesic equation](../riemannian-geometry.md#geodesic-equation) give

$$
\frac d{dt}g(\dot q,Y)=g(\dot q,\nabla_{\dot q}Y)=\tfrac12(\mathcal L_Yg)(\dot q,\dot q)=0.
$$

Thus **$f$ is conserved**. Equivalently the cotangent lift of the [isometry](../riemannian-geometry.md#isometry) flow preserves $H$, and its generator has [Hamiltonian](../hamiltonian-mechanics.md#hamiltonian) $f$, so their [Poisson bracket](../hamiltonian-mechanics.md#poisson-bracket) vanishes.

For the final two metrics write $s=\ell(x)+m(y)$, restricting to an [open set](../topology.md#open-set) where the printed tensors are smooth nondegenerate metrics. Their inverse forms give

$$
H_1=\frac{p_x^2+p_y^2}{2s},\qquad
H_2=\frac{m(y)p_x^2-\ell(x)p_y^2}{2s}=m(y)H_1-\frac12p_y^2.
$$

Along the $H_1$ flow, $H_1$ is constant, while

$$
\dot y=p_y/s,\qquad \dot p_y=\frac{m'(y)}{2s^2}(p_x^2+p_y^2)=\frac{m'(y)H_1}{s}.
$$

Hence

$$
\boxed{\frac{dH_2}{dt}=m'(y)\frac{p_y}{s}H_1-p_y\frac{m'(y)H_1}{s}=0}.
$$

This is the [Liouville metric geodesic integral](../riemannian-geometry.md#liouville-metric-geodesic-integral), a quadratic conserved quantity. It does not require a [Killing vector field](../killing-vector-field.md); it arises from the separated metric coefficients. When both printed metrics are Riemannian, their positivity in particular requires $s>0$, $m>0$ and $\ell<0$; the calculation also applies wherever the corresponding inverse tensors are defined.
