# Equilibrium constant

↑ **Parent:** [Chemical equilibrium](chemical-equilibrium.md)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equilibrium_constant)

For a specified [chemical reaction](chemical-reaction-split.md) and [standard state](standard-state.md) convention, the dimensionless [equilibrium constant](equilibrium-constant.md) is the equilibrium value of the [reaction quotient](reaction-quotient.md) $Q=\prod_i a_i^{\nu_i}$, where $a_i$ denotes [thermodynamic activity](thermodynamic-activity.md) and $\nu_i$ a signed [stoichiometric vector](stoichiometric-vector.md) entry. [Thermodynamic activities](thermodynamic-activity.md) incorporate the reference state and departures from ideal behavior. See [IUPAC Gold Book, equilibrium constant](https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/E02177).

In the ideal concentration model for A converting to B, use $a_{\mathrm A}=[\mathrm A]/c^\circ$ and $a_{\mathrm B}=[\mathrm B]/c^\circ$ with the same reference $c^\circ=1\,\mathrm M$. Their ratio gives $Q=[\mathrm B]/[\mathrm A]$ and $K=0.75/0.25=3$. Equality $K=k_f/k_r$ applies to this elementary first-order pair; it is not a universal ratio of two measured [rate constants](rate-constant.md) for an arbitrary multistep [chemical reaction](chemical-reaction-split.md).

At a specified constant [temperature](temperature-split.md), with consistent [standard states](standard-state.md), the standard molar reaction [Gibbs free energy](gibbs-free-energy.md) is

$$
\Delta_rG^\circ=\sum_i\nu_i\mu_i^\circ=-RT\ln K,
\qquad \Delta_rG=\Delta_rG^\circ+RT\ln Q=RT\ln(Q/K).
$$

Here $\Delta_rG$ is the local slope per [mole](mole-unit.md) of [extent of reaction](extent-of-reaction.md) at fixed [temperature](temperature-split.md) and [pressure](pressure-split.md), rather than the finite total [Gibbs free energy](gibbs-free-energy.md) change between two mixtures. At equilibrium $\Delta_rG=0$, while $\Delta_rG^\circ$ generally remains nonzero. For $K=3$ at $298\,\mathrm K$, it is about $-2.72\,\mathrm{kJ/mol}$. For positive activities, $Q<K$ favors forward conversion and $Q>K$ favors reverse conversion; neither sign supplies a [reaction rate](reaction-rate.md). See [MIT 5.111, Lecture 18, reaction free energy and equilibrium](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/5-111sc-principles-of-chemical-science-fall-2014/ec12f084d867a5e5532b12ee99bdc767_MIT5_111F14_Lec18.pdf).
