<h3 id="a/1">1</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a)

<h4 id="a/1/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#a/1)

For an [irreducible polynomial](../polynomial.md#irreducible-polynomial) $f\in K[X]$, take $L=K[X]/(f)$ and let $\alpha$ be the class of $X$. The quotient is a [field](../algebra.md#field), is generated by $\alpha$ over $K$, and satisfies $f(\alpha)=0$. Its degree is $\boxed{[L:K]=\deg f}$, with [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) $1,\alpha,\ldots,\alpha^{\deg f-1}$.

A [splitting field](../galois-theory.md#splitting-field) is a [field](../algebra.md#field) generated over $K$ by all roots of the [polynomial](../polynomial.md) and in which the [polynomial](../polynomial.md) is a product of linear factors. Prove existence and the factorial bound together by induction on the degree. Constant nonzero [polynomials](../polynomial.md) and linear [polynomials](../polynomial.md) require no extension. For a reducible degree-$n$ [polynomial](../polynomial.md) write $g=hk$ with positive degrees $r,n-r$. Split $h$ by induction, then split $k$ over that [field](../algebra.md#field). The resulting [field](../algebra.md#field) is generated by all roots of $g$, and its degree over $K$ is at most $r!(n-r)!\le n!$. If $g$ is irreducible, adjoin one root in degree $n$, divide out its linear factor, and split the degree-$(n-1)$ quotient over that extension. The tower formula gives degree at most $n(n-1)!=n!$. This also works in inseparable [characteristic](../algebra.md#characteristic-of-a-field) and proves the required bound without assuming Galoisness.

For the sextic with constant term three, [Eisenstein's criterion](../commutative-algebra.md#eisenstein-criterion) at three gives degree six. Choose a root $\alpha$, so $\alpha^3$ is a square root of $-3$. Then

$$
\zeta=\frac{1+\alpha^3}{2}
$$

satisfies $\zeta^2-\zeta+1=0$, and is a primitive sixth [root of unity](../algebra.md#root-of-unity): $\zeta^3=-1$ and $\zeta\ne-1$. The six distinct roots are $\alpha\zeta^j$ for $0\le j<6$, all in $\mathbb Q(\alpha)$. Hence the simple extension is itself the [splitting field](../galois-theory.md#splitting-field) and $\boxed{[L:\mathbb Q]=6}$.

For the constant term two, Eisenstein at two gives $[\mathbb Q(\gamma):\mathbb Q]=6$ for a root $\gamma$. Its [splitting field](../galois-theory.md#splitting-field) is $\mathbb Q(\gamma,\zeta_6)$. The supplied noncontainment of a root of $X^2+3$ means that adjoining $\zeta_6=(1+\sqrt{-3})/2$ has degree two. Thus

$$
\boxed{[M:\mathbb Q]=12.}
$$

<h3 id="a/2">2</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a)

<h4 id="a/2/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#a/2)

Every [automorphism](../algebra.md#automorphism) fixes zero and one, and preserves sums, products, negatives and multiplicative inverses of nonzero elements. Consequently elements fixed by every member of $G$ are closed under these operations and form a [field](../algebra.md#field). [Artin fixed-field theorem](../galois-theory.md#artin-fixed-field-theorem) gives $\boxed{[L:L^G]=|G|}$ for a finite [automorphism](../algebra.md#automorphism) [group](../group.md).

Here $\mathbb Q(t)$ is the fraction [field](../algebra.md#field) of $\mathbb Q[t]$, with $t$ transcendental; its elements are fractions of rational-coefficient [polynomials](../polynomial.md), with nonzero denominator and the usual equality by cross-multiplication. Substitution of $1-t$ or $1/t$ defines an [automorphism](../algebra.md#automorphism), each its own inverse. With $\sigma\tau$ meaning first apply $\tau$ to a [field](../algebra.md#field) element and then $\sigma$, its image on $t$ is $1/(1-t)$. Iterating gives $(t-1)/t$ and then $t$, so its order is three. The relations reduce every word to at most six possibilities, and the six resulting images are

$$
t,\quad1-t,\quad1/t,\quad1/(1-t),\quad(t-1)/t,\quad t/(t-1).
$$

They are distinct [rational functions](../isolated-singularity.md#rational-function), so the [group](../group.md) has six elements. Its faithful [permutation](../combinatorics.md#permutation) action on $\{0,1,\infty\}$ identifies it with $\boxed{S_3}$.

For the first [fixed field](../galois-theory.md#fixed-field) put $u=t(1-t)$. It is fixed by $\sigma$, and $t$ satisfies $X^2-X+u=0$ over $\mathbb Q(u)$. The degree is exactly two because the nontrivial [automorphism](../algebra.md#automorphism) $\sigma$ fixes that [field](../algebra.md#field). The fixed-[field](../algebra.md#field) degree is also two, so

$$
\boxed{\mathbb Q(t)^{\langle\sigma\rangle}=\mathbb Q(t(1-t)).}
$$

For the inversion put $v=t+1/t$. It is fixed and $t$ satisfies $X^2-vX+1=0$. The same degree argument gives

$$
\boxed{\mathbb Q(t)^{\langle\tau\rangle}=\mathbb Q(t+1/t).}
$$

The source requests [polynomial](../polynomial.md) generators in both cases. The second requirement is impossible. If a nonconstant [polynomial](../polynomial.md) $p(t)=a_dt^d+\cdots+a_0$ were fixed by inversion, $p(t)=p(1/t)$ would equate a [Laurent polynomial](../polynomial.md#laurent-polynomial) with highest exponent $d>0$ to one with no positive exponent, a contradiction. Thus the only [polynomial](../polynomial.md) invariants of inversion are constants, which cannot generate its nonconstant [fixed field](../galois-theory.md#fixed-field). The [fixed field of inversion in a rational function field](../algebra.md#fixed-field-of-inversion-in-a-rational-function-field) therefore has the second displayed rational-function generator.

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a)

<h4 id="a/3/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#a/3)

A finite extension is Galois when it is both normal and separable; over $\mathbb Q$ separability is automatic, so it is equivalently a [splitting field](../galois-theory.md#splitting-field) of a [polynomial](../polynomial.md) with distinct roots. Its [Galois group](../galois-theory.md#galois-group) consists of the [field](../algebra.md#field) [automorphisms](../algebra.md#automorphism) fixing $\mathbb Q$. If $G=\operatorname{Gal}(L/\mathbb Q)$, the [Fundamental theorem of Galois theory](../galois-theory.md#fundamental-theorem-of-galois-theory) gives mutually inverse inclusion-reversing bijections

$$
H\longmapsto L^H,\qquad K\longmapsto\operatorname{Gal}(L/K).
$$

They satisfy $[L:L^H]=|H|$ and $[L^H:\mathbb Q]=[G:H]$. The intermediate extension $K/\mathbb Q$ is Galois exactly when $H=\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)$ is normal, and then $\boxed{\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb Q)\cong G/H}$ via restriction.

Every [permutation](../combinatorics.md#permutation) of the four roots permutes the three unordered partitions into two pairs and hence permutes the three $\beta_j$. Thus each coefficient of $g(X)=\prod_{j=1}^3(X-\beta_j)$ is a [symmetric polynomial](../polynomial.md#symmetric-polynomial) in the four roots. The [Fundamental theorem of symmetric polynomials](../polynomial.md#fundamental-theorem-of-symmetric-polynomials) expresses it in the [elementary symmetric functions](../polynomial.md#elementary-symmetric-polynomial), which are rational coefficients of $f$. Hence $g\in\mathbb Q[X]$. Each product $\alpha_i\alpha_j$, $i<j$, occurs exactly twice in $\beta_1+\beta_2+\beta_3$, so that sum is $2q$. Therefore the quadratic coefficient is $\boxed{-2q}$.

For the given cubic write $g(X)=(X-2)^3+12$. The translated cubic $Y^3+12$ is Eisenstein at three, hence irreducible. It has one real root and a nonreal conjugate pair. Its [Galois group](../galois-theory.md#galois-group) is transitive on three roots, so its order is divisible by three; [complex conjugation](../complex-analysis.md#complex-conjugation) is a [transposition](../combinatorics.md#transposition-permutation). A [subgroup](../group.md#subgroup) of $S_3$ with both a three-cycle and a [transposition](../combinatorics.md#transposition-permutation) is all of $S_3$. Hence

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb Q)\cong S_3,\qquad[K:\mathbb Q]=6.}
$$

Let $G=\operatorname{Gal}(L/\mathbb Q)\le S_4$. Irreducibility of the quartic makes the root action transitive, so four divides $|G|$. The cubic [splitting field](../galois-theory.md#splitting-field) $K$ lies inside $L$ by the definitions of the $\beta_j$, and restriction onto its [Galois group](../galois-theory.md#galois-group) gives a quotient of order six. Thus six also divides $|G|$. Since $|G|$ divides 24, it is 12 or 24. If it were 12, the supplied index-two result would give $G=A_4$. The restriction kernel would then be a [normal subgroup](../group-theory.md#normal-subgroup) of $A_4$ of index six, contrary to the other supplied result. Thus

$$
\boxed{G=S_4.}
$$

<h4 id="a/4/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#a/4)

A [straightedge and compass construction](../straightedge-and-compass-construction.md) uses finitely many steps drawing lines through known points and circles with known centres and constructible radii, and adjoining their intersection points. The coordinates obtained by line–line intersections require only [field](../algebra.md#field) operations; line–circle and circle–circle intersections require at most a [quadratic extension](../algebra.md#quadratic-extension) after eliminating a linear equation. Thus all coordinates lie in a tower of [quadratic extensions](../algebra.md#quadratic-extension) of the initial coordinate [field](../algebra.md#field). The degree of any finite intermediate coordinate [field](../algebra.md#field) containing that initial [field](../algebra.md#field) divides a [power of two](../integer.md#power-of-two) and is therefore $\boxed{2^r}$ for some $r\ge0$.

For the source's notation this assumes $K\supseteq K_0$, as is customary when previously constructed points are retained. If only the coordinates of a final subset are called $K$, replace it by the compositum $K_0K$; otherwise the expression $[K:K_0]$ need not be defined. The power-of-two condition is necessary and is not being asserted as sufficient for an arbitrary algebraic number.

Starting from a unit cube, [doubling its volume](../straightedge-and-compass-construction.md#impossibility-of-doubling-the-cube-by-straightedge-and-compass) would construct a length $\sqrt[3]2$. Its [polynomial](../polynomial.md) $X^3-2$ is Eisenstein at two, so its degree over $\mathbb Q$ is three, which cannot divide a [power of two](../integer.md#power-of-two). Thus **doubling the cube is impossible by ruler and compass**.

Set $c=\cos\theta$, $s=\sin\theta$. The real part of $(c+is)^5$ is $c^5-10c^3s^2+5cs^4$. Replacing $s^2$ by $1-c^2$ gives

$$
\boxed{\cos5\theta=16c^5-20c^3+5c.}
$$

Choose the given angle to have cosine $5/6$. It is constructible, since its [unit circle](../complex-analysis.md#complex-unit-circle) point has coordinates $(5/6,\sqrt{11}/6)$. If a fifth of that angle were constructible and had cosine $c$, the identity would imply

$$
96c^5-120c^3+30c-5=0.
$$

This [polynomial](../polynomial.md) is Eisenstein at five: all nonleading coefficients are divisible by five and the constant term is not divisible by 25, while 96 is not divisible by five. Hence $[\mathbb Q(c):\mathbb Q]=5$. But the initial angle [field](../algebra.md#field) is $\mathbb Q(\sqrt{11})$, itself of degree two, so adjoining any finite collection of constructible coordinates to it would still give total degree over $\mathbb Q$ a [power of two](../integer.md#power-of-two). It could not contain a degree-five element. Thus **[general angle quintisection is impossible with straightedge and compass](../straightedge-and-compass-construction.md#general-angle-quintisection-is-impossible-with-straightedge-and-compass)**.

<h4 id="b/5/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#b/5)

For the [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) $w_1,\ldots,w_d$, its [discriminant](../polynomial.md#discriminant) is

$$
\operatorname{disc}(B)=\det\big(\operatorname{Tr}_{K/\mathbb Q}(w_iw_j)\big)_{i,j}.
$$

Equivalently it is the square of the [determinant](../multilinear-algebra.md#determinant) of the [matrix](../vector-space.md#matrix) of the $d$ embeddings applied to the [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) vectors. It is nonzero because [number fields](../algebraic-number-theory.md#number-field) are separable. An [integral basis](../algebraic-number-theory.md#integral-basis) is a [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) of the [ring of integers](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ring-of-integers) as a [free abelian group](../group-theory.md#free-abelian-group); in particular it is a rational [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) of the [field](../algebra.md#field) and expresses every [algebraic integer](../algebraic-number-theory.md#algebraic-integer) uniquely with [integer](../integer.md) coefficients.

To prove existence, start with any rational [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) and multiply each vector by a suitable nonzero [integer](../integer.md) to make it integral. This is possible by clearing coefficients in its monic rational [minimal polynomial of an algebraic element](../galois-theory.md#minimal-polynomial-of-an-algebraic-element) and rescaling the variable. Let $M$ be the [lattice](../group-theory.md#free-abelian-group) spanned over $\mathbb Z$ by these integral vectors, and let $D\ne0$ be its [discriminant](../polynomial.md#discriminant). For any [algebraic integer](../algebraic-number-theory.md#algebraic-integer) $\alpha=\sum_jc_jw_j$, each trace $\operatorname{Tr}(\alpha w_i)$ is an [integer](../integer.md). The resulting [linear system](../linear-algebra.md#system-of-linear-equations) has integral coefficient [matrix](../vector-space.md#matrix) with [determinant](../multilinear-algebra.md#determinant) $D$. Its [adjugate matrix](../linear-algebra.md#adjugate-matrix) shows $c_j\in D^{-1}\mathbb Z$. Thus

$$
M\subseteq\mathcal O_K\subseteq D^{-1}M.
$$

A [subgroup](../group.md#subgroup) of a finitely generated [free abelian group](../group-theory.md#free-abelian-group) is free and finitely generated. Here its rank is $d$, since it contains $M$ and is contained in a rank-$d$ [lattice](../group-theory.md#free-abelian-group). Its free generators are the required [integral basis](../algebraic-number-theory.md#integral-basis).

For squarefree $d\ne1$, the quadratic [integral basis](../algebraic-number-theory.md#integral-basis) is

$$
\boxed{\{1,(1+\sqrt d)/2\}\quad(d\equiv1\pmod4),\qquad\{1,\sqrt d\}\quad(d\equiv2,3\pmod4).}
$$

The source allows squarefree nonzero [integers](../integer.md), so the degenerate value $d=1$ gives $K=\mathbb Q$ and [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) $\{1\}$ instead of a two-element [basis](../vector-space.md#basis).

In the biquadratic [field](../algebra.md#field) define the three [automorphisms](../algebra.md#automorphism) by changing respectively the sign of $\sqrt r$, the sign of $\sqrt2$, and both signs. Their actions are

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sigma_1(\mu)&=a+b\sqrt2-c\sqrt r-d\sqrt{2r},\\
\sigma_2(\mu)&=a-b\sqrt2+c\sqrt r-d\sqrt{2r},\\
\sigma_3(\mu)&=a-b\sqrt2-c\sqrt r+d\sqrt{2r}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Hence the three sums are $2a+2b\sqrt2$, $2a+2c\sqrt r$ and $2a+2d\sqrt{2r}$. If $\mu$ is integral, each sum is integral and lies in its displayed quadratic subfield. Their radicands are squarefree and respectively congruent to two, three and two modulo four. The quadratic [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) formula therefore forces all their two coefficients to be [integers](../integer.md), proving

$$
\boxed{2a,2b,2c,2d\in\mathbb Z.}
$$

<h3 id="b/6">6</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b)

<h4 id="b/6/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#b/6)

The [Dedekind factorization theorem](../algebraic-number-theory.md#dedekind-factorization-theorem) says: if $K=\mathbb Q(\theta)$ with $\theta$ integral, [minimal polynomial of an algebraic element](../galois-theory.md#minimal-polynomial-of-an-algebraic-element) $f\in\mathbb Z[X]$, and the rational prime $p$ does not divide $[\mathcal O_K:\mathbb Z[\theta]]$, factor $\bar f=\prod_i\bar f_i^{e_i}$ into distinct monic irreducibles over $\mathbb F_p$. Then the distinct [prime ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) above $p$ are $P_i=(p,f_i(\theta))$, with [integer](../integer.md) lifts $f_i$, and

$$
\boxed{(p)=\prod_iP_i^{e_i},\qquad N(P_i)=p^{\deg\bar f_i}.}
$$

The index condition is part of the theorem and must be checked before applying it at a ramified prime.

For this cubic, $X^3-6$ is Eisenstein and has [discriminant](../polynomial.md#discriminant) $-972$. We first verify $\mathcal O_K=\mathbb Z[\theta]$. The square of the index divides 972, so only two and three can divide the index. If either prime $p$ divided it, the finite quotient [group](../group.md) would contain an element of order $p$, giving an integral element $(a+b\theta+c\theta^2)/p$ with digits $0\le a,b,c<p$, not all zero. Its [field norm](../algebraic-number-theory.md#field-norm) is

$$
\frac{a^3+6b^3+36c^3-18abc}{p^3}.
$$

For $p=2$, the numerator has valuation zero if $a=1$, valuation one if $a=0,b=1$, and valuation two if $a=b=0,c=1$. For $p=3$ the same three cases, according to the first nonzero digit, give valuations zero, one and two. None gives an [integer](../integer.md) [field norm](../algebraic-number-theory.md#field-norm) after dividing by $p^3$, contradicting integrality. Thus the index is one.

Multiplication by $a+b\theta$ in the [power basis](../algebra.md#power-basis), or the product over the three conjugates, gives $\boxed{N(a+b\theta)=a^3+6b^3}$. Define

$$
\begin{aligned}
P_2&=(2,\theta),&P_3&=(3,\theta),\\
P_5&=(5,\theta-1),&Q_5&=(5,\theta^2+\theta+1),\\
P_{7,r}&=(7,\theta-r)&& (r=3,5,6).
\end{aligned}
$$

Reduction of the cubic gives $X^3$ at two and three, $(X-1)(X^2+X+1)$ at five, and $(X-3)(X-5)(X-6)$ at seven. The quadratic factor at five is irreducible since its [discriminant](../polynomial.md#discriminant) is two, a nonsquare modulo five. Hence

$$
\boxed{(2)=P_2^3,\quad(3)=P_3^3,\quad(5)=P_5Q_5,\quad(7)=P_{7,3}P_{7,5}P_{7,6}.}
$$

The [norms](../normed-vector-space.md#norm) are respectively $2,3,5,25,7,7,7$. [Norms](../normed-vector-space.md#norm) and reduction of the elements at those primes give

$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
(\theta)&=P_2P_3,&(\theta-1)&=P_5,&(\theta-2)&=P_2,\\
(\theta-3)&=P_3P_{7,3},& (\theta+4)&=P_2P_5P_{7,3}.
\end{aligned}}
$$

For example their absolute element [field norms](../algebraic-number-theory.md#field-norm) are $6,5,2,21,70$; each indicated prime divides the element by substitution in its [residue field](../commutative-algebra.md#residue-field), and the product of the indicated [ideal norms](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ideal-norm) already equals that absolute [field norm](../algebraic-number-theory.md#field-norm). Thus no additional factor or exponent is possible.

In the [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) of the specified quotient every prime exponent cancels:

$$
(\mu)=\frac{P_2^2(P_3P_{7,3})P_5}{(P_2P_3)(P_2P_5P_{7,3})}=\mathcal O_K.
$$

Equality with the [unit ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#unit-ideal) implies that both $\mu$ and its inverse are integral, so $\boxed{\mu\text{ is a unit}}$. For the real root, $1<\theta<2$. The numerator is negative and has absolute value less than two, while the denominator is greater than five. Thus $-2/5<\mu<0$, proving $\boxed{\mu\ne\pm1}$. Its [field norm](../algebraic-number-theory.md#field-norm) is $-1$, consistent with the [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) calculation.

<h3 id="b/7">7</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b)

<h4 id="b/7/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [7](#b/7)

Choose a nonzero [integral ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#integral-ideal) $B$ whose class is $[A]^{-1}$; one can multiply the fractional inverse $A^{-1}$ by a suitable nonzero [integer](../integer.md) to obtain such a $B$. By the given bound choose $0\ne\beta\in B$ with $|N(\beta)|\le c_KN(B)$. Put $I_A=(\beta)B^{-1}$. Since $(\beta)\subseteq B$, multiplication by $B^{-1}$ makes $I_A$ an [integral ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#integral-ideal). Its class is $[B]^{-1}=[A]$, and multiplicativity of [ideal norms](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ideal-norm) gives

$$
\boxed{N(I_A)=\frac{|N(\beta)|}{N(B)}\le c_K.}
$$

This proves the class representative bound without accidentally constructing a representative of the inverse class instead.

To determine the [Ideal class group of Q of square root minus thirty-four](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ideal-class-group-of-q-of-square-root-minus-thirty-four), write $\eta=\sqrt{-34}$. The given [integral basis](../algebraic-number-theory.md#integral-basis) has [discriminant](../polynomial.md#discriminant) $-136$, so the imaginary-quadratic bound is $4\sqrt{34}/\pi<8$. Every class therefore has a representative of [ideal norm](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ideal-norm) at most seven, and factoring such representatives shows that the [ideal class group](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ideal-class-group) is generated by [prime ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) of [ideal norm](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ideal-norm) at most seven.

Two ramifies: let $P_2=(2,\eta)$, with $P_2^2=(2)$. Three is inert because $X^2+34\equiv X^2+1$ is irreducible modulo three, so its [prime ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) [ideal norm](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ideal-norm) is nine and supplies no small generator. At five and seven the roots are $\pm1$, so the primes of [ideal norm](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ideal-norm) five and seven are conjugate pairs. Set $P_5=(5,\eta-1)$ and $P_7=(7,\eta+1)$. Their conjugates have inverse [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) classes. No new [prime ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) has [ideal norm](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ideal-norm) four, since the only prime above two has [residue degree](../non-archimedean-analysis.md#residue-degree) one; six is not a [prime power](../prime-number.md#prime-power). Thus $[P_2],[P_5],[P_7]$ generate the [ideal class group](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ideal-class-group).

The element [field norms](../algebraic-number-theory.md#field-norm) $N(4+\eta)=50$ and $N(1+\eta)=35$, together with reduction at the split primes, give

$$
(4+\eta)=P_2P_5^2,\qquad(1+\eta)=\bar P_5P_7.
$$

The [ideal norm](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ideal-norm) products verify both exponents. Consequently $[P_5]^2=[P_2]$ and $[P_7]=[P_5]$, so the whole [group](../group.md) is generated by $[P_5]$ and its order divides four. The class $[P_2]$ is not trivial: a generator would be an element of the [ring of integers](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ring-of-integers) $a+b\eta$ of [field norm](../algebraic-number-theory.md#field-norm) two, but $a^2+34b^2=2$ has no [integer](../integer.md) solution. Since $[P_2]^2=1$, its order is exactly two. Hence $[P_5]$ has order four and

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Cl}(\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-34}))\cong C_4.}
$$

<h3 id="b/8">8</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b)

<h4 id="b/8/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [8](#b/8)

For [integral solutions of x squared minus two equals y cubed](../number-theory.md#integral-solutions-of-x-squared-minus-two-equals-y-cubed), the equation implies $y\ge-1$, so $y+2>0$. If $y$ were even, then $x$ would be even and $x^2-2$ would be two or six modulo eight, whereas $y^3$ would be zero modulo eight. Thus $y$ and $x$ are odd. Also $3\nmid x$: otherwise $x^2-2\equiv7\pmod9$, which is not a cubic residue modulo nine. Reducing modulo three then gives $y\equiv2\pmod3$. Consequently

$$
\boxed{\gcd(y+2,6)=1.}
$$

Put $\eta=\sqrt{-6}$ and $\alpha=x+(y+1)\eta$. Its [field norm](../algebraic-number-theory.md#field-norm) is $(y+2)^3$. The [principal ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal) $(\alpha)$ and $(\bar\alpha)$ are coprime. Indeed a common [prime ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) has underlying rational prime $p$ dividing $y+2$. The displayed gcd excludes two and three, so both two and $\eta$ are invertible modulo that [prime ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal). The difference $\alpha-\bar\alpha=2(y+1)\eta$ therefore implies $p\mid y+1$, contradicting $p\mid y+2$.

Their product is $(y+2)^3$, so [unique factorization of ideals in a number field](../algebraic-number-theory.md#unique-factorization-of-ideals-in-a-number-field) and coprimality imply $(\alpha)=A^3$ for an [integral ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#integral-ideal) $A$. In the given [ideal class group](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ideal-class-group) of order two, $[A]^3=1$ implies $[A]=1$. Hence $A=(u+v\eta)$ with [integers](../integer.md) $u,v$. Equality of [principal ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal) gives $\alpha=\varepsilon(u+v\eta)^3$. The only [units](../algebra.md#unit-in-a-ring) are $\pm1$, and both are cubes, so the sign can be absorbed into $u,v$. Taking [norms](../normed-vector-space.md#norm), and using $y+2>0$, gives

$$
\boxed{x+(y+1)\eta=(u+v\eta)^3,\qquad u^2+6v^2=y+2.}
$$

The coefficient of $\eta$ gives $y+1=3u^2v-6v^3$. Eliminating $y$ with the [field norm](../algebraic-number-theory.md#field-norm) identity yields

$$
(3v-1)u^2=6v^3+6v^2-1,\qquad u^2=\frac{6v^3+6v^2-1}{3v-1}.
$$

The denominator is nonzero for integral $v$. [Polynomial](../polynomial.md) division makes the integrality obstruction especially clear:

$$
\boxed{9u^2=18v^2+24v+8-\frac1{3v-1}.}
$$

If $u$ is integral, the reciprocal must be an [integer](../integer.md), forcing $3v-1=\pm1$. The only integral possibility is $v=0$. Then $u^2=1$, the [field norm](../algebraic-number-theory.md#field-norm) gives $y=-1$, and the real coefficient in the cube gives $x=u^3=\pm1$. Both pairs directly satisfy the original equation. Therefore all integral solutions are

$$
\boxed{(x,y)=(1,-1),\ (-1,-1).}
$$
