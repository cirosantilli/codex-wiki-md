<h4 id="a/1/a">a</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#a/1)

<h5 id="a/1/a/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a/1/a)

The [alternating polynomial](../polynomial.md#alternating-polynomial) given by the Vandermonde product is alternating: interchanging two variables changes its sign. One proof writes it as a [Vandermonde determinant](../galois-theory.md#vandermonde-determinant), up to a fixed sign, and interchanging two variables interchanges the corresponding determinant rows. Since [transpositions](../combinatorics.md#transposition-permutation) generate $S_n$,

$$
\boxed{\sigma(\delta)=\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\delta,\qquad\sigma(\Delta)=\Delta.}
$$

Thus every even [permutation](../combinatorics.md#permutation) fixes $\delta$, while a [transposition](../combinatorics.md#transposition-permutation) negates the nonzero formal [polynomial](../polynomial.md), so it is not invariant under all of $S_n$. This uses $n\ge2$ and [characteristic](../algebra.md#characteristic-of-a-field) different from two, as in the subsequent rational setting. For $n=1$ both [groups](../group.md) are trivial and the last distinction does not arise.

<h4 id="a/1/b">b</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#a/1)

<h5 id="a/1/b/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#a/1/b)

The [discriminant](../polynomial.md#discriminant) is a [symmetric polynomial](../polynomial.md#symmetric-polynomial) in the four roots, so the [Fundamental theorem of symmetric polynomials](../polynomial.md#fundamental-theorem-of-symmetric-polynomials) expresses it polynomially in the [elementary symmetric polynomial](../polynomial.md#elementary-symmetric-polynomial). For this depressed quartic they are $e_1=e_2=0$, $e_3=-r$, $e_4=s$. Scaling each root by $t$ multiplies the [discriminant](../polynomial.md#discriminant) by $t^{12}$, while $r,s$ scale by $t^3,t^4$. Thus only weighted-degree-twelve monomials can appear, giving $\Delta_f=Ar^4+Bs^3$.

For $r=0$, use $f'=4X^3$ and the monic [discriminant](../polynomial.md#discriminant) identity $\Delta_f=(-1)^{n(n-1)/2}\prod_if'(\alpha_i)$. At $n=4$ this gives $4^4(\prod_i\alpha_i)^3=256s^3$, so $B=256$. For $s=0$, the roots are zero together with those of $g=X^3+r$. The cubic's [discriminant](../polynomial.md#discriminant) is $-\prod g'(\beta_i)=-27r^2$. The extra squared differences from zero multiply this by $(\prod\beta_i)^2=r^2$, giving $-27r^4$. Hence

$$
\boxed{\Delta_f=256s^3-27r^4.}
$$

These computations are [polynomial](../polynomial.md) identities, so they extend to parameter values with repeated roots as well.

<h4 id="a/1/c">c</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#a/1)

<h5 id="a/1/c/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#a/1/c)

For a [polynomial](../polynomial.md) with $n$ distinct roots, the criterion is

$$
\boxed{G_f\subseteq A_n\iff\Delta_f\text{ is a nonzero square in }\mathbb Q.}
$$

Write $\delta=\prod_{i<j}(\alpha_i-\alpha_j)\ne0$. Part (a) gives $\sigma\delta=\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\delta$. If every Galois [permutation](../combinatorics.md#permutation) is even, $\delta$ is fixed by the entire [Galois group](../galois-theory.md#galois-group) and therefore belongs to its [fixed field](../galois-theory.md#fixed-field) $\mathbb Q$. Thus $\Delta_f=\delta^2$ is a rational square. Conversely, if $\Delta_f=q^2$ with $q\in\mathbb Q^\times$, then $\delta^2=q^2$ in a [field](../algebra.md#field) implies $\delta=\pm q$. Every [automorphism](../algebra.md#automorphism) fixes this nonzero element, so its [permutation](../combinatorics.md#permutation) sign must be one.

The distinct-root hypothesis is necessary in the statement's intended degree-$n$ [permutation](../combinatorics.md#permutation) action. The source says “arbitrary degree” without stating that hypothesis. If repeated roots were allowed, zero [discriminant](../polynomial.md#discriminant) would carry no sign information: $(X-1)^2(X^2-2)$ has [discriminant](../polynomial.md#discriminant) zero, while its nontrivial [automorphism](../algebra.md#automorphism) swaps $\sqrt2,-\sqrt2$ and fixes the two rational-root positions, an odd [permutation](../combinatorics.md#permutation). The criterion above applies to the [squarefree polynomial](../polynomial.md#square-free-polynomial), or to the squarefree part with its degree and root action adjusted accordingly.

<h4 id="a/2/a">a</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#a/2)

<h5 id="a/2/a/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a/2/a)

A [splitting field](../galois-theory.md#splitting-field) is an extension in which the [polynomial](../polynomial.md) factors into linear factors and which is generated over the base [field](../algebra.md#field) by its roots. Prove existence together with the factorial divisibility by strong induction on $d$, with degrees zero and one immediate for a nonzero [polynomial](../polynomial.md).

If $f$ is reducible, write $f=gh$ with $1\le e=\deg g<d$, $\deg h=d-e$. By induction first split $g$ in $K_1/K$ with degree dividing $e!$, then split $h$ over $K_1$ with degree dividing $(d-e)!$. The resulting [field](../algebra.md#field) is generated over $K$ by all roots of $f$, hence is a [splitting field](../galois-theory.md#splitting-field). Its degree divides $e!(d-e)!$, which divides $d!$ because the binomial coefficient $d!/[e!(d-e)!]$ is an [integer](../integer.md).

If $f$ is irreducible, adjoin a root $\alpha$, obtaining degree $[K(\alpha):K]=d$. Over this [field](../algebra.md#field) divide out $X-\alpha$ and split the degree-$(d-1)$ quotient by induction. That [splitting field](../galois-theory.md#splitting-field) together with the base already contains every root and has degree over $K$ dividing $d(d-1)!=d!$. Thus

$$
\boxed{[L:K]\mid d!.}
$$

The reducible/irreducible distinction proves divisibility, not just an upper bound on the degree, and works without assuming separability.

<h4 id="a/2/b">b</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#a/2)

<h5 id="a/2/b/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#a/2/b)

In the distinct-root convention, a [polynomial](../polynomial.md) is separable if it has no repeated roots in its [splitting field](../galois-theory.md#splitting-field), equivalently $\gcd(f,f')=1$. A common factorwise convention instead requires each distinct irreducible factor to have no repeated roots; repeated occurrences of an already separable factor do not affect the [splitting field](../galois-theory.md#splitting-field). Under either separability convention, the [splitting field](../galois-theory.md#splitting-field) is a finite normal separable extension and

$$
\boxed{|\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)|=[L:K].}
$$

For failure in the inseparable case, take $K=\mathbb F_p(t)$ and $f=X^p-t$. It is irreducible, for example by Eisenstein at the prime $t$ in $\mathbb F_p[t]$. Its [splitting field](../galois-theory.md#splitting-field) is $L=K(t^{1/p})$ of degree $p$, but the [polynomial](../polynomial.md) has just one distinct root. Every $K$-[automorphism](../algebra.md#automorphism) fixes that root, so the [Galois group](../galois-theory.md#galois-group) is trivial rather than having order $p$.

<h6 id="a/2/c/i/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#a/2/c/i)

Factor $f_1=(X^2-3)(X^2+2)$. Its [splitting field](../galois-theory.md#splitting-field) is $L=\mathbb Q(\sqrt3,\sqrt{-2})$. The first [quadratic field](../algebraic-number-theory.md#quadratic-field) is real, so it cannot contain the nonreal $\sqrt{-2}$. Thus adjoining the second square root doubles the degree again. Independent sign changes of the two square roots give all four [automorphisms](../algebra.md#automorphism), so

$$
\boxed{[L:\mathbb Q]=4,\qquad\operatorname{Gal}(L/\mathbb Q)\cong C_2\times C_2.}
$$

<h5 id="a/2/c/ii">ii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#a/2/c)

<h6 id="a/2/c/ii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#a/2/c/ii)

[Eisenstein's criterion](../commutative-algebra.md#eisenstein-criterion) at two makes $X^5-4X+2$ irreducible: all nonleading coefficients are divisible by two and the constant is not divisible by four. Its [Galois group](../galois-theory.md#galois-group) is therefore transitive on five roots, and its order is divisible by five, so [Cauchy's theorem for finite groups](../finite-group-theory.md#cauchy-theorem-for-groups) supplies a five-cycle.

The [derivative](../calculus.md#derivative) is $5X^4-4$, with real critical points $\pm c$, $c=(4/5)^{1/4}$. The values are $f(-c)=2+16c/5>0$ and $f(c)=2-16c/5<0$. The [derivative](../calculus.md#derivative) is positive outside these two points and negative between them, so there are exactly three real roots and one nonreal conjugate pair. [Complex conjugation](../complex-analysis.md#complex-conjugation) therefore acts as a single [transposition](../combinatorics.md#transposition-permutation).

A five-cycle together with any [transposition](../combinatorics.md#transposition-permutation) generates $S_5$: conjugating that [transposition](../combinatorics.md#transposition-permutation) by powers of the cycle gives edges joining labels with a fixed nonzero difference modulo five. These edges form a connected graph, and [transpositions on a connected graph generate the symmetric group](../finite-group-theory.md#transpositions-on-a-connected-graph-generate-the-symmetric-group). Hence

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Gal}(L/\mathbb Q)=S_5,\qquad[L:\mathbb Q]=120.}
$$

The equality of degree and [group](../group.md) order uses separability in [characteristic](../algebra.md#characteristic-of-a-field) zero.

<h5 id="a/2/c/iii">iii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#a/2/c)

<h6 id="a/2/c/iii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#a/2/c/iii)

The nonzero cubes modulo thirteen are $\{1,5,8,12\}$, which excludes three. Thus this cubic has no root in its base [field](../algebra.md#field) and is irreducible. For a root $\alpha$, the extension $\mathbb F_{13}(\alpha)$ has degree three. The base [field](../algebra.md#field) already contains the primitive cube [root of unity](../algebra.md#root-of-unity) $3$, since $3^3=1$ and $3\ne1$. Hence the other two roots are $3\alpha$ and $9\alpha$, already in this extension, making it the full [splitting field](../galois-theory.md#splitting-field).

The [Frobenius endomorphism](../galois-theory.md#frobenius-endomorphism) satisfies $\alpha^{13}=\alpha(\alpha^3)^4=3\alpha$, so it cycles all three roots. Therefore

$$
\boxed{[L:\mathbb F_{13}]=3,\qquad\operatorname{Gal}(L/\mathbb F_{13})\cong C_3.}
$$

<h4 id="a/3/a">a</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#a/3)

<h5 id="a/3/a/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a/3/a)

A [finite group](../group.md#finite-group) is solvable if its [derived series](../group-theory.md#derived-series), defined by $G^{(0)}=G$ and $G^{(j+1)}=[G^{(j)},G^{(j)}]$, reaches the identity after finitely many steps. Equivalently it has a [subnormal series](../group-theory.md#subnormal-series) with abelian factor [groups](../group.md). The factors need not all have prime order for this definition.

<h4 id="a/3/b">b</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#a/3)

<h5 id="a/3/b/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#a/3/b)

Let $V_4=\{1,(12)(34),(13)(24),(14)(23)\}$. This is an [abelian subgroup](../group.md#abelian-subgroup), and conjugation preserves its nonidentity cycle type, so it is normal in $S_4$ and hence in $A_4$. The series

$$
1\trianglelefteq V_4\trianglelefteq A_4\trianglelefteq S_4
$$

has factors $V_4\cong C_2\times C_2$, $A_4/V_4\cong C_3$, and $S_4/A_4\cong C_2$, all abelian. Thus $\boxed{S_4\text{ is solvable}}$.

<h4 id="a/3/d">d</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#a/3)

<h5 id="a/3/d/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#a/3/d)

Let $\alpha=p^{1/p}$ and let $\zeta$ be a primitive $p$th [root of unity](../algebra.md#root-of-unity). The roots are $\alpha\zeta^j$, so $L=\mathbb Q(\alpha,\zeta)$. Eisenstein gives $[\mathbb Q(\alpha):\mathbb Q]=p$, while the prime cyclotomic extension has degree $[\mathbb Q(\zeta):\mathbb Q]=p-1$. Both degrees divide $[L:\mathbb Q]$, and they are coprime. Conversely adjoining $\alpha$ to the [cyclotomic field](../galois-theory.md#cyclotomic-field) has degree at most $p$. These two bounds give

$$
\boxed{[L:\mathbb Q]=p(p-1).}
$$

Restriction to $E=\mathbb Q(\zeta)$ gives a [normal subgroup](../group-theory.md#normal-subgroup) $N=\operatorname{Gal}(L/E)$ of order $p$, hence cyclic, with quotient $\operatorname{Gal}(E/\mathbb Q)\cong C_{p-1}$. Restriction is onto because embeddings of the intermediate [field](../algebra.md#field) extend in the finite Galois [splitting field](../galois-theory.md#splitting-field). Thus $1\trianglelefteq N\trianglelefteq G$ has abelian factors, proving $\boxed{G\text{ is solvable}}$. For $p=2$ the [cyclotomic field](../galois-theory.md#cyclotomic-field) is just $\mathbb Q$, and the same argument gives degree two and a [cyclic group](../group.md#cyclic-group).

<h2 id="b">B</h2>

↑ **Parent:** [2009](2009.md)

<h3 id="b/4">4</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b)

<h4 id="b/4/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#b/4)

The set $\mathcal O_K$ consists of the elements of $K$ integral over $\mathbb Z$, meaning roots of monic polynomials in $\mathbb Z[X]$.

First prove the [finite-module criterion for integrality](../commutative-algebra.md#finite-module-criterion-for-integrality) in a slightly stronger form, for any nonzero finitely generated $\mathbb Z$-submodule $M\subseteq K$ stable under multiplication by $\alpha$. Choose generators $m_1,\ldots,m_r$ and write $\alpha m_i=\sum_j a_{ij}m_j$ with [integer](../integer.md) coefficients. Multiply the vector equation $(\alpha I-A)m=0$ by its [adjugate matrix](../linear-algebra.md#adjugate-matrix). This gives $\det(\alpha I-A)m_i=0$ for every generator. Some generator is nonzero and $K$ is a [field](../algebra.md#field), so $\det(\alpha I-A)=0$. The [polynomial](../polynomial.md) $\det(XI-A)$ is monic with [integer](../integer.md) coefficients, proving integrality of $\alpha$. This implication needs no prior closure theorem for $\mathcal O_K$.

Conversely, if $\alpha$ satisfies a [monic polynomial](../polynomial.md#monic-polynomial) of degree $d$, then $M=\mathbb Z[\alpha]$ is generated by $1,\alpha,\ldots,\alpha^{d-1}$ and is stable under $\alpha$. Every $\beta\in M$ also maps $M$ into itself because it is an [integer](../integer.md) [polynomial](../polynomial.md) in $\alpha$; the [finite-module criterion for integrality](../commutative-algebra.md#finite-module-criterion-for-integrality) just proved makes every such $\beta$ integral. Thus this $M$ really lies in $\mathcal O_K$, proving the stated equivalence without circularity.

For two integral elements $\alpha,\beta$, the module spanned by $\alpha^i\beta^j$ for $i<d_\alpha$, $j<d_\beta$ contains one, is finitely generated, and is stable under both elements by their monic equations. It is therefore stable under $\alpha+\beta$ and $\alpha\beta$. The [finite-module criterion for integrality](../commutative-algebra.md#finite-module-criterion-for-integrality) proves that both are integral. Negatives follow by replacing a multiplication matrix by its negative, and $0,1$ are integral. Hence $\boxed{\mathcal O_K\text{ is a ring}}$.

The [integral basis of the cubic field of discriminant minus 140](../algebraic-number-theory.md#integral-basis-of-the-cubic-field-of-discriminant-minus-140) can be obtained as follows. For the specified [field](../algebra.md#field), Eisenstein at two makes $X^3+2X+2$ irreducible, and $\theta$ is integral because the [polynomial](../polynomial.md) is monic. The [discriminant](../polynomial.md#discriminant) of a monic cubic $X^3+rX+s$ is $-4r^3-27s^2$, so

$$
\boxed{\Delta^2(1,\theta,\theta^2)=-4\cdot2^3-27\cdot2^2=-140.}
$$

Here $\Delta^2$ is the printed notation for the signed [discriminant](../polynomial.md#discriminant), namely the square of a Vandermonde product of the conjugates; that product need not be real.

Suppose $\eta=(a+b\theta+c\theta^2)/2$ is integral with the prescribed binary coefficients. The root traces give $\operatorname{Tr}(1)=3$, $\operatorname{Tr}(\theta)=0$, $\operatorname{Tr}(\theta^2)=-4$. Thus $\operatorname{Tr}(\eta)=(3a-4c)/2$ must be an [integer](../integer.md), forcing $a=0$. If $b=0$, nonzeroness of the coefficient triple forces $c=1$, giving $\eta=\theta^2/2$. If $b=1$, multiply by $\theta$ and use $\theta^3=-2\theta-2$:

$$
\theta\eta=\theta^2/2-c\theta-c.
$$

Closure of the [ring](../commutative-algebra.md#ring) then again gives $\theta^2/2\in\mathcal O_K$.

But $N_{K/\mathbb Q}(\theta)=-2$, so the [field norm](../algebraic-number-theory.md#field-norm) of $\theta^{-1}$ is $-1/2$. The [field norm](../algebraic-number-theory.md#field-norm) of an [algebraic integer](../algebraic-number-theory.md#algebraic-integer) is an [integer](../integer.md), proving $\theta^{-1}\notin\mathcal O_K$. Dividing the defining cubic by $2\theta$ gives $\theta^2/2=-1-\theta^{-1}$. Thus $\theta^2/2$ cannot be integral either.

Finally the [discriminant-index formula for an integral lattice](../algebraic-number-theory.md#discriminant-index-formula-for-an-integral-lattice) says $-140=[\mathcal O_K:\mathbb Z[\theta]]^2\operatorname{disc}(K)$. The square of the [integer](../integer.md) index divides $140$, so its only possibilities are one and four, giving index one or two. If the index were two, a nonzero coset would supply an integral element $(a+b\theta+c\theta^2)/2$ after reducing coefficients modulo two, with a nonzero binary triple. The preceding argument would force the impossible integrality of $\theta^2/2$. Hence

$$
\boxed{\mathcal O_K=\mathbb Z[\theta],\qquad\{1,\theta,\theta^2\}\text{ is an integral basis}.}
$$

<h3 id="b/5">5</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b)

<h4 id="b/5/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#b/5)

[ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) are coprime when $A+B=\mathcal O_K$, equivalently $1=a+b$ for some $a\in A$, $b\in B$. Always $AB\subseteq A\cap B$. If $x\in A\cap B$, then $x=xa+xb\in AB$, giving $\boxed{AB=A\cap B}$ in the coprime case. For nonzero integral [ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal), the [Chinese remainder theorem](../mathematics.md#chinese-remainder-theorem) gives $\mathcal O_K/(AB)\cong\mathcal O_K/A\times\mathcal O_K/B$, and taking finite cardinalities yields $\boxed{N(AB)=N(A)N(B)}$.

For a nonzero [prime ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal), the chain of finite-index inclusions gives

$$
N(P^e)=[\mathcal O_K:P]\prod_{i=1}^{e-1}[P^i:P^{i+1}]=N(P)^e
$$

by the given hypothesis. Distinct nonzero [prime ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) are maximal and coprime, as are their positive powers. [Unique factorization of ideals in a number field](../algebraic-number-theory.md#unique-factorization-of-ideals-in-a-number-field) therefore gives $N(A)=\prod_PN(P)^{v_P(A)}$. Exponents add under [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) multiplication, proving multiplicativity for arbitrary nonzero integral [ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal). This is the standard domain of the finite [ideal norm](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ideal-norm) used here.

For the [Dedekind factorization theorem](../algebraic-number-theory.md#dedekind-factorization-theorem), choose an integral primitive element $\theta$ of $K$ with monic [minimal polynomial of an algebraic element](../galois-theory.md#minimal-polynomial-of-an-algebraic-element) $f\in\mathbb Z[X]$, and assume the rational prime $p$ does not divide the index $[\mathcal O_K:\mathbb Z[\theta]]$. Factor

$$
\bar f=\prod_{i=1}^g\bar f_i^{e_i}
$$

over $\mathbb F_p$, with distinct monic irreducible factors. For [integer](../integer.md) [polynomial](../polynomial.md) lifts $f_i$, the distinct [prime ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) above $p$ are $P_i=(p,f_i(\theta))$, and

$$
\boxed{(p)=\prod_iP_i^{e_i},\quad\mathcal O_K/P_i\cong\mathbb F_p[X]/(\bar f_i),\quad N(P_i)=p^{\deg\bar f_i}.}
$$

Thus the [residue degree](../non-archimedean-analysis.md#residue-degree) is $\deg\bar f_i$, the [ramification index](../non-archimedean-analysis.md#ramification-index) is $e_i$, and $\sum_i e_i\deg\bar f_i=[K:\mathbb Q]$. The index restriction is essential to this direct [factorization](../algebra.md#factorization) formula.

For $K=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-2})$, the [ring of integers](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ring-of-integers) is $\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-2}]$, so the index is one and the theorem applies to every prime. Modulo two, $X^2+2=X^2$, giving one [prime ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) of [ideal norm](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ideal-norm) two. For an odd rational prime, a [prime ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) has [ideal norm](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ideal-norm) $p$ if the [polynomial](../polynomial.md) splits, and [ideal norm](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ideal-norm) $p^2$ if it is irreducible. In the former case the supplied condition forces $p\equiv1$ or $3\pmod8$; in the latter case every odd square is one modulo eight. Consequently

$$
\boxed{N(P)=2\quad\text{or}\quad N(P)\equiv1,3\pmod8.}
$$

The odd part of any [ideal norm](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ideal-norm) is thus one or three modulo eight, because $3^2\equiv1$. Multiplication by a power of two leaves possible residues $1,3$ for exponent zero, $2,6$ for exponent one, $4$ for exponent two, and $0$ for exponent at least three. Hence $\boxed{N(I)\not\equiv5,7\pmod8}$ for every nonzero [integral ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#integral-ideal).

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b)

<h4 id="b/6/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#b/6)

For nonzero [ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal), [unique factorization of ideals in a number field](../algebraic-number-theory.md#unique-factorization-of-ideals-in-a-number-field) converts $AB=C^3$ to $v_P(A)+v_P(B)=3v_P(C)$. Coprimality says at most one of the first two exponents is positive, so each is divisible by three. Dividing its exponents by three constructs [ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) whose cubes are $A,B$. If a zero [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) is allowed, coprimality forces the other [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) to be the whole [ring](../commutative-algebra.md#ring) and the conclusion is immediate with the zero [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) as its own cube.

To compute the [Ideal class group of Q of square root minus thirty](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ideal-class-group-of-q-of-square-root-minus-thirty), put $\theta=\sqrt{-30}$. The [ring of integers](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ring-of-integers) is $\mathbb Z[\theta]$, with [discriminant](../polynomial.md#discriminant) $-120$. The given [Minkowski bound for ideal classes](../algebraic-number-theory.md#minkowski-s-bound) is $2\sqrt{120}/\pi=4\sqrt{30}/\pi<7$. Hence the [ideal class group](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ideal-class-group) is generated by [prime ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) with [ideal norm](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ideal-norm) below seven. The rational primes two, three and five all ramify, giving unique [prime ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) $P_2=(2,\theta)$, $P_3=(3,\theta)$, $P_5=(5,\theta)$ with $P_i^2=(i)$ and [ideal norm](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ideal-norm) $i$. There is no additional [prime ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) of [ideal norm](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ideal-norm) four because two ramifies with [residue degree](../non-archimedean-analysis.md#residue-degree) one; six is not a [prime power](../prime-number.md#prime-power). Thus these three classes generate and each has order at most two.

As $\theta^2=-30$, [unique factorization of ideals in a number field](../algebraic-number-theory.md#unique-factorization-of-ideals-in-a-number-field) gives $(\theta)=P_2P_3P_5$, so their three classes multiply to one. None is principal: a generator of one would be an integral element $a+b\theta$ of [field norm](../algebraic-number-theory.md#field-norm) $a^2+30b^2$ equal to two, three or five, and none of those [field norm](../algebraic-number-theory.md#field-norm) equations has [integer](../integer.md) solutions. The three nontrivial classes are therefore distinct, with the third the product of the first two. Hence

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Cl}(\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-30}))\cong C_2\times C_2.}
$$

To rule out [integer](../integer.md) solutions, suppose $x^2+30=y^3$. Necessarily $y>0$. If a prime $p\mid30$ divided $y$, then reduction modulo $p$ would force $p\mid x$. Because thirty is squarefree, the valuation of $x^2+30$ at that prime would be exactly one, while that of $y^3$ is a positive multiple of three, a contradiction. Thus $\gcd(y,30)=1$.

If a [prime ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) divided both $(x+\theta)$ and $(x-\theta)$, it would divide their difference $(2\theta)$ and their product $(y)^3$. Its underlying rational prime would divide $N(2\theta)=120$, hence divide thirty, and would also divide $y$. This contradicts the gcd just proved, so the two [ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) are coprime. Their product is $(y)^3$, so the first part gives $(x+\theta)=A^3$ for an [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) $A$.

In a [ideal class group](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ideal-class-group) of exponent two, $[A]^3=1$ forces $[A]=1$. Thus $A=(a+b\theta)$ with $a,b\in\mathbb Z$. Equality of principal [ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) gives $x+\theta=\varepsilon(a+b\theta)^3$ for a [unit](../algebra.md#unit-in-a-ring) $\varepsilon$. The only units are $\pm1$, since $a^2+30b^2=1$ forces $b=0$, $a=\pm1$; both signs are cubes and can be absorbed into the generator. Comparing the coefficient of $\theta$ after cubing now gives

$$
1=3a^2b-30b^3=3b(a^2-10b^2),
$$

which is impossible modulo three. Therefore

$$
\boxed{x^2+30=y^3\text{ has no solutions in }\mathbb Z^2.}
$$
