# Lagrange multiplier

↑ **Parent:** [Mathematical optimization](mathematical-optimization-split.md)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrange_multiplier)

For [continuously differentiable functions](continuously-differentiable-function.md) $f,g$ on an [open subset](open-set.md) of $\mathbb R^n$, suppose $z_*$ is a local [extremum](maximum-and-minimum.md) of $f$ subject to $g(z)=b$, with $\nabla g(z_*)\ne0$. Then a [real number](real-number.md) $\lambda$, the [Lagrange multiplier](lagrange-multiplier.md), satisfies

$$
\nabla f(z_*)=\lambda\nabla g(z_*).
$$

The nonzero [gradient](gradient.md) assumption excludes singular points of the [optimization constraint](optimization-constraint.md). With several equality [optimization constraints](optimization-constraint.md), require their [gradients](gradient.md) to be [linearly independent](linear-independence.md); the [gradient](gradient.md) of $f$ is then a [linear combination](linear-combination.md) of them. These are necessary conditions, not automatic certificates of a [global maximum](global-maximum.md) or [global minimum](global-minimum.md).

Why can the [gradient](gradient.md) be nonzero at an optimum? In [unconstrained optimization](unconstrained-optimization.md), every direction is available at an interior local [extremum](maximum-and-minimum.md), so all [directional derivatives](directional-derivative.md) vanish and $\nabla f=0$. On a regular equality [feasible set](feasible-region.md), only directions in its [tangent space](tangent-space.md) are available to first order. The [gradient](gradient.md) of $f$ must be perpendicular to those directions, so it is parallel to the [gradient](gradient.md) of $g$. In two dimensions this explains the tangency of their [level sets](level-set.md). See [MIT, Lagrange multipliers and contour geometry](https://ocw.mit.edu/ans7870/18/18.02/f07/tools/LagrangeMultipliersTwoVariables.html).

For the product example, set $g(x,y)=x+y$ and use the [optimization Lagrangian](optimization-lagrangian.md)

$$
L(x,y,\lambda)=xy-\lambda(x+y-10).
$$

Its [partial derivatives](partial-derivative.md) give $y-\lambda=0$, $x-\lambda=0$, and $x+y=10$. Hence $x=y=\lambda=5$. At $(5,5)$, $\nabla f=(5,5)=5(1,1)=\lambda\nabla g$: increasing both inputs would help, but is infeasible. Along the allowed direction $(1,-1)$, the [directional derivative](directional-derivative.md) is zero. The endpoints must still be checked because of the nonnegativity [optimization constraints](optimization-constraint.md); [completing the square](completing-the-square.md) establishes the unique [global maximum](global-maximum.md).

The sign convention matters: writing $L=xy+\mu(x+y-10)$ instead gives $\mu=-5$. Neither equality [Lagrange multiplier](lagrange-multiplier.md) has a universal nonnegativity requirement. Also, the displayed $L$ is unbounded above along $x=y=t$, so stationarity here does not meet the global hypothesis of the [Lagrangian sufficiency theorem](lagrange-sufficiency-theorem.md).

The [Lagrange multiplier](lagrange-multiplier.md) also measures the value of extra resources. For a positive budget $b$, the optimizer is $(b/2,b/2)$, the optimum value is $V(b)=b^2/4$, and $V'(b)=b/2=\lambda$. At $b=10$, an extra $0.1$ units predicts a gain of $0.5$; the exact gain is $V(10.1)-V(10)=0.5025$. This is the [envelope theorem](envelope-theorem.md) in a computed [sensitivity analysis](sensitivity-analysis.md): the multiplier translates a binding [budget constraint](budget-constraint.md) into a marginal benefit.
