## 1

↑ **Parent:** [2011](2011.md)

<h3 id="1/i">i</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/i/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#1/i)

Write the [lower central series of a Lie algebra](../lie-algebra.md#lower-central-series-of-a-lie-algebra) as $\mathfrak g^1=\mathfrak g$ and $\mathfrak g^{j+1}=[\mathfrak g,\mathfrak g^j]$. Since the [nilpotent Lie algebra](../lie-algebra.md#nilpotent-lie-algebra) is nonzero, there is a last nonzero term $\mathfrak g^c$, with $\mathfrak g^{c+1}=0$. Thus $[\mathfrak g,\mathfrak g^c]=0$, so

$$
0\ne\mathfrak g^c\subseteq Z(\mathfrak g).
$$

Consequently **the centre is nonzero**. This argument works over every [field](../algebra.md#field) and includes an abelian algebra, for which $c=1$.

<h3 id="1/ii">ii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/ii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#1/ii)

Fix $x$ with $x^N=0$ as a [matrix](../vector-space.md#matrix). On the full [matrix](../vector-space.md#matrix) algebra define $L_x(y)=xy$ and $R_x(y)=yx$. These endomorphisms commute and each has $N$th power zero. The [Adjoint representation of a Lie algebra](../lie-algebra.md#adjoint-representation-of-a-lie-algebra) is $\operatorname{ad}(x)=L_x-R_x$. The binomial theorem gives

$$
(\operatorname{ad}x)^m(y)=\sum_{j=0}^{m}(-1)^j\binom mjx^{m-j}yx^j.
$$

For $m=2N-1$, each summand has either $j\ge N$ or $m-j\ge N$, and hence vanishes. Therefore $\operatorname{ad}(x)$ is nilpotent on the full [matrix](../vector-space.md#matrix) algebra and on its [invariant subspace](../representation-theory.md#invariant-subspace) $\mathfrak g$.

[Engel's theorem](../lie-algebra.md#engel-s-theorem), in its adjoint form, states that a finite-dimensional [Lie algebra](../lie-algebra.md) is nilpotent if every $\operatorname{ad}(x)$ is a [nilpotent endomorphism](../linear-operator-theory.md#nilpotent-linear-map). Applying it to all $x\in\mathfrak g$ proves

$$
\boxed{\mathfrak g\text{ is nilpotent}.}
$$

This is distinct from assuming that an arbitrary [representation](../representation-theory.md#group-representation) of a nilpotent algebra must act by nilpotent matrices, which is false.

<h3 id="1/iii">iii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/iii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#1/iii)

If $\mathfrak g$ is nilpotent, every [Lie subalgebra](../lie-algebra.md#lie-subalgebra) $\mathfrak a$ is nilpotent, since $\mathfrak a^j\subseteq\mathfrak g^j$ in the respective [lower central series of a Lie algebra](../lie-algebra.md#lower-central-series-of-a-lie-algebra). A two-dimensional nilpotent algebra has nonzero centre by part (i). If $0\ne z$ is central, complete it to a [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) $(z,u)$; every bracket of these [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) elements is zero. Thus $\mathfrak a$ is abelian.

Conversely, suppose $\mathfrak g$ is not nilpotent. By [Engel's theorem](../lie-algebra.md#engel-s-theorem), some $\operatorname{ad}(x)$ is not nilpotent. Since the [field](../algebra.md#field) is algebraically closed, it has an [eigenvalue](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) $\lambda\ne0$ with [eigenvector](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvector) $y\ne0$. Thus

$$
[x,y]=\lambda y.
$$

The vectors $x,y$ are independent: otherwise their bracket would be zero. Their span is therefore a two-dimensional [Lie subalgebra](../lie-algebra.md#lie-subalgebra) and it is nonabelian. This contradicts the hypothesis. Hence

$$
\boxed{\mathfrak g\text{ is nilpotent}\iff
\text{every two-dimensional subalgebra is abelian}.}
$$

<h3 id="1/iv">iv</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/iv/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#1/iv)

Suppose $\operatorname{ad}(x)$ has a nonzero [eigenvalue](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) $\lambda$ and corresponding vector $y$. Then $[x,y]=\lambda y$, so

$$
\operatorname{ad}(y)x=-\lambda y\ne0,\qquad
(\operatorname{ad}y)^2x=0.
$$

For a [diagonalizable](../linear-operator-theory.md#diagonalizable-matrix) [endomorphism](../algebra.md#endomorphism) $T$, its [eigenspace](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenspace) decomposition shows $\ker T^2=\ker T$. The displayed equations therefore contradict diagonalizability of $\operatorname{ad}(y)$.

Thus no $\operatorname{ad}(x)$ has a nonzero [eigenvalue](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue). Each is [diagonalizable](../linear-operator-theory.md#diagonalizable-matrix) over the [algebraically closed field](../algebra.md#algebraically-closed-field), so each is the zero [endomorphism](../algebra.md#endomorphism). Hence $[x,z]=0$ for every $x,z\in\mathfrak g$, proving

$$
\boxed{\mathfrak g\text{ is abelian}.}
$$

## 2

↑ **Parent:** [2011](2011.md)

<h3 id="2/i">i</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/i/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#2/i)

By the [classification of finite-dimensional sl2 representations](../semisimple-lie-algebra.md#classification-of-finite-dimensional-sl2-representations) over $\mathbb C$, every finite-dimensional [module](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics) is a [direct sum](../vector-space.md#direct-sum) of simple modules $L_m$, indexed by integers $m\ge0$. The [module](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics) $L_m$ has [dimension](../vector-space.md#dimension-vector-space) $m+1$ and a [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) $v_0,\ldots,v_m$ with

$$
\begin{aligned}
hv_j&=(m-2j)v_j,\\
ev_j&=jv_{j-1},\\
fv_j&=(m-j)v_{j+1},
\end{aligned}
$$

with out-of-range vectors interpreted as zero. In particular $v_0$ is a [highest weight](../semisimple-lie-algebra.md#highest-weight-of-a-representation) vector of weight $m$. The weights are $m,m-2,\ldots,-m$, each with multiplicity one. The multiplicities of the $L_m$ in a finite [module](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics) determine that [module](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics) up to isomorphism.

<h3 id="2/ii">ii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/ii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#2/ii)

In the [simple module](../module-theory.md#irreducible-module) $L_m$, weight $0$ occurs once if $m$ is even and never if $m$ is odd. Weight $1$ occurs once if $m$ is odd and never if $m$ is even. Thus each simple summand contributes exactly one to $\dim V_0+\dim V_1$.

[Complete reducibility of semisimple Lie algebra representations](../semisimple-lie-algebra.md#weyl-s-theorem-on-complete-reducibility) writes $V=\bigoplus_mL_m^{\oplus a_m}$. Taking the two [weight spaces](../semisimple-lie-algebra.md#weight-space) commutes with this finite [direct sum](../vector-space.md#direct-sum), so

$$
\boxed{\dim V_0+\dim V_1=\sum_ma_m.}
$$

The right side counts irreducible summands **with multiplicity**, independently of a chosen decomposition.

<h3 id="2/iii">iii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/iii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#2/iii)

Every $2\times2$ [matrix](../vector-space.md#matrix) has the unique decomposition

$$
A=\frac{\operatorname{tr}A}{2}I+\left(A-\frac{\operatorname{tr}A}{2}I\right).
$$

Both summands define invariant subspaces for the [Adjoint representation of a Lie algebra](../lie-algebra.md#adjoint-representation-of-a-lie-algebra). Scalar matrices commute with everything, so $\mathbb CI$ is $L_0$. The traceless matrices form $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ with [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) $e,h,f$ of weights $2,0,-2$. Their brackets satisfy $[e,f]=h$, $[h,e]=2e$, $[h,f]=-2f$; the raising and lowering operations connect all three weight lines. This is the [simple module](../module-theory.md#irreducible-module) $L_2$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\mathfrak{gl}_2(\mathbb C)\cong L_0\oplus L_2.}
$$

As a check, its zero-[weight space](../semisimple-lie-algebra.md#weight-space) has [dimension](../vector-space.md#dimension-vector-space) two and its one-[weight space](../semisimple-lie-algebra.md#weight-space) is zero, agreeing with part (ii).

<h3 id="2/iv">iv</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/iv/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#2/iv)

On a monomial $X^aY^b$, direct [differentiation](../calculus.md#differentiation) gives

$$
E(X^aY^b)=bX^{a+1}Y^{b-1},\quad
F(X^aY^b)=aX^{a-1}Y^{b+1},\quad
H(X^aY^b)=(a-b)X^aY^b.
$$

For example $(EF-FE)(X^aY^b)=[a(b+1)-b(a+1)]X^aY^b=(a-b)X^aY^b$. The same calculation with $H$ gives

$$
[H,E]=2E,\qquad[H,F]=-2F,\qquad[E,F]=H.
$$

These are exactly the brackets of $h,e,f$, so the [linear map](../vector-space.md#linear-map) $\rho$ respects every bracket and is a [Lie algebra representation](../lie-algebra.md#lie-algebra-representation).

For $m\ge0$, let $W_m$ be the space of [homogeneous polynomials](../algebra.md#homogeneous-polynomial) of [total degree](../polynomial.md#total-degree-of-a-polynomial) $m$. Each operator preserves [total degree](../polynomial.md#total-degree-of-a-polynomial). Every [polynomial](../polynomial.md) is a finite sum of homogeneous polynomials, so

$$
W=\bigoplus_{m=0}^{\infty}W_m.
$$

In $W_m$, the monomials $v_j=X^{m-j}Y^j$ have distinct weights $m-2j$ and exactly the actions described in part (i). To see simplicity directly, a nonzero [invariant subspace](../representation-theory.md#invariant-subspace) is invariant under $H$ and hence contains one of its weight components: [polynomial interpolation](../interpolation.md#polynomial-interpolation) in $H$ projects onto each of its distinct eigenspaces. Starting with a nonzero multiple of $v_j$, repeated application of $E$ reaches $v_0$, and repeated application of $F$ then reaches all $v_j$. All coefficients used are nonzero in characteristic zero. Therefore $W_m$ is simple and isomorphic to $L_m$.

Thus

$$
\boxed{\mathbb C[X,Y]=\bigoplus_{m\ge0}\operatorname{span}\{X^m,X^{m-1}Y,\ldots,Y^m\}
\cong\bigoplus_{m\ge0}L_m.}
$$

This is an algebraic [direct sum](../vector-space.md#direct-sum), not an infinite product or completion. Its simple submodules are precisely the $W_m$: they are pairwise nonisomorphic and each occurs once, so projection onto homogeneous degrees and [Schur's lemma](../representation-theory.md#schur-s-lemma) exclude diagonal copies between distinct degrees. Complete reducibility here follows from the explicit decomposition, rather than applying a finite-dimensional theorem directly to the infinite-dimensional $W$.

## 3

↑ **Parent:** [2011](2011.md)

<h3 id="3/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

Equip the finite-dimensional real vector space $V$ with a positive-definite [inner product](../inner-product.md). A reduced crystallographic [root system](../semisimple-lie-algebra.md#root-system) is a finite set $R\subset V\setminus\{0\}$ spanning $V$ such that, for every $\alpha\in R$,

$$
R\cap\mathbb R\alpha=\{\alpha,-\alpha\},\qquad
s_\alpha(R)=R,\qquad
\frac{2(\beta,\alpha)}{(\alpha,\alpha)}\in\mathbb Z\quad(\beta\in R),
$$

where $s_\alpha(v)=v-2(v,\alpha)\alpha/(\alpha,\alpha)$ is the [orthogonal reflection](../linear-algebra.md#reflection-in-a-hyperplane) in $\alpha^\perp$. The [Weyl group](../semisimple-lie-algebra.md#weyl-group) is $W(R)=\langle s_\alpha:\alpha\in R\rangle$. It permutes the finite set $R$. Its action is faithful because $R$ spans $V$: an element fixing every [root](../semisimple-lie-algebra.md#root-of-a-root-system) fixes every vector. Hence it embeds in the finite [symmetric group](../finite-group-theory.md#symmetric-group) of $R$ and **is finite**.

A [Cartan subalgebra](../semisimple-lie-algebra.md#cartan-subalgebra) is a nilpotent self-normalizing [Lie subalgebra](../lie-algebra.md#lie-subalgebra). For a finite-dimensional complex [semisimple Lie algebra](../semisimple-lie-algebra.md) it is equivalently a maximal toral subalgebra: a maximal abelian subalgebra whose elements have [diagonalizable](../linear-operator-theory.md#diagonalizable-matrix) adjoint actions. The commuting semisimple operators $\operatorname{ad}(h)$ give the [root-space decomposition](../semisimple-lie-algebra.md#root-space-decomposition)

$$
\mathfrak g=\mathfrak h\oplus\bigoplus_{\alpha\in R}\mathfrak g_\alpha,\qquad
\mathfrak g_\alpha=\{x:[h,x]=\alpha(h)x\text{ for every }h\in\mathfrak h\}.
$$

The nonzero weights $\alpha$ are the roots. Their real span inside the complex dual $\mathfrak h^*$, with the positive-definite real [inner product](../inner-product.md) induced by the [Killing form](../lie-algebra.md#killing-form), is the Euclidean space in which they form a reduced crystallographic [root system](../semisimple-lie-algebra.md#root-system). In particular one uses this real span, rather than treating all of $\mathfrak h^*$ as a real vector space of twice the [rank](../quadratic-form.md#rank-one-quadratic-form).

For the specified [Special orthogonal Lie algebra](../semisimple-lie-algebra.md#special-orthogonal-lie-algebra), put $\bar i=7-i$. The [matrix](../vector-space.md#matrix) relation is $x_{ij}=-x_{\bar j,\bar i}$. Thus its diagonal [Cartan subalgebra](../semisimple-lie-algebra.md#cartan-subalgebra) consists of

$$
H(t)=\operatorname{diag}(t_1,t_2,t_3,-t_3,-t_2,-t_1).
$$

Let $\varepsilon_i(H(t))=t_i$. The [matrix](../vector-space.md#matrix) units satisfy $[H,E_{ij}]=(d_i-d_j)E_{ij}$, where $d=(t_1,t_2,t_3,-t_3,-t_2,-t_1)$. A [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) of [root](../semisimple-lie-algebra.md#root-of-a-root-system) vectors is supplied, for $1\le i<j\le3$, by

$$
\begin{array}{c|c}
\text{root}&\text{root vector}\\\hline
\varepsilon_i-\varepsilon_j&E_{ij}-E_{\bar j,\bar i}\\
-\varepsilon_i+\varepsilon_j&E_{ji}-E_{\bar i,\bar j}\\
\varepsilon_i+\varepsilon_j&E_{i,\bar j}-E_{j,\bar i}\\
-\varepsilon_i-\varepsilon_j&E_{\bar j,i}-E_{\bar i,j}
\end{array}
$$

These are twelve independent off-diagonal matrices. Together with the three diagonal generators, they account for the full [dimension](../vector-space.md#dimension-vector-space) $6\cdot5/2=15$, so no further roots occur. Consequently

$$
\boxed{R=\{\pm\varepsilon_i\pm\varepsilon_j:1\le i<j\le3\},\qquad R\text{ has type }D_3.}
$$

Choose the simple roots

$$
\alpha_1=\varepsilon_1-\varepsilon_2,\qquad
\alpha_2=\varepsilon_2-\varepsilon_3,\qquad
\alpha_3=\varepsilon_2+\varepsilon_3.
$$

The six positive roots are $\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\alpha_3,\alpha_1+\alpha_2,\alpha_1+\alpha_3,\alpha_1+\alpha_2+\alpha_3$, and every other [root](../semisimple-lie-algebra.md#root-of-a-root-system) is their negative. In the standard orthonormal coordinates all roots have squared length $2$, and their [Cartan matrix](../semisimple-lie-algebra.md#cartan-matrix) is

$$
\begin{pmatrix}2&-1&-1\\-1&2&0\\-1&0&2\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Its [Dynkin diagram](../semisimple-lie-algebra.md#dynkin-diagram) is the connected chain $\alpha_2-\alpha_1-\alpha_3$. A decomposition into mutually orthogonal nonempty [root](../semisimple-lie-algebra.md#root-of-a-root-system) systems would separate this connected set of simple roots, which is impossible. Thus the [root system](../semisimple-lie-algebra.md#root-system) is irreducible. The chain is also the diagram $A_3$, which is the [root system](../semisimple-lie-algebra.md#root-system) of $\mathfrak{sl}_4(\mathbb C)$. The classification of complex semisimple Lie algebras by their [root](../semisimple-lie-algebra.md#root-of-a-root-system) systems yields the [Lie algebra isomorphism](../lie-algebra.md#lie-algebra-isomorphism)

$$
\boxed{\mathfrak{so}_6(\mathbb C)\cong\mathfrak{sl}_4(\mathbb C).}
$$

An explicit explanation of this coincidence uses the [exterior-power Lie algebra representation](../lie-algebra.md#exterior-power-lie-algebra-representation) of $\mathfrak{sl}_4$ on $\bigwedge^2\mathbb C^4$, a six-dimensional space. After choosing a volume form, $u\wedge v$ defines a nondegenerate [symmetric bilinear form](../bilinear-form.md#symmetric-bilinear-form) on that [exterior square](../multilinear-algebra.md#exterior-square). The induced action preserves it because a traceless [endomorphism](../algebra.md#endomorphism) acts trivially on the top exterior power. This gives a nonzero homomorphism $\mathfrak{sl}_4\to\mathfrak{so}_6$. Its [kernel](../kernel-of-a-linear-map.md) is an ideal in the simple algebra $\mathfrak{sl}_4$, so it is zero; equal dimensions $15$ then make it an isomorphism. Over $\mathbb C$ all nondegenerate symmetric forms in [dimension](../vector-space.md#dimension-vector-space) six are equivalent, including the form with [matrix](../vector-space.md#matrix) $J$.
