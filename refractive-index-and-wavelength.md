# Refractive index and wavelength

↑ **Parent:** [Refractive index](refractive-index.md)

For a linear stationary interface, [phase matching at a planar wave interface](phase-matching-at-a-planar-wave-interface.md) preserves [frequency](frequency.md): the incident and transmitted [electric fields](electric-field-split.md) must oscillate together at each boundary point. Their [phase velocities](phase-velocity.md) can differ, so their [wavelengths](wavelength.md) differ. Writing $\omega=2\pi f$, the definitions give

$$
\lambda_0=\frac{c}{f},\qquad \lambda=\frac{v_{\rm ph}}{f}=\frac{\lambda_0}{n},\qquad k=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}=n\frac{2\pi}{\lambda_0}.
$$

This explains the compressed [wavefront](wavefront.md) spacing in the figure without changing the time between crests at the interface. Moving interfaces or time-dependent material responses need not preserve [frequency](frequency.md). [The Feynman Lectures on Physics, Volume I, §31–1: boundary oscillations, vacuum and medium wavelengths, Fig. 31–2](the-feynman-lectures-on-physics-volume-i-by-richard-feynman-and-others/chapter-31-the-origin-of-the-refractive-index.md)

The notation $n(\lambda_0)$ labels [refractive index](refractive-index.md) by [vacuum](vacuum.md) [wavelength](wavelength.md); it does not mean the [wavelength](wavelength.md) inside the material. Dependence on $\lambda_0$, equivalently on [frequency](frequency.md), is [optical dispersion](dispersion-optics.md). The field driving the material induces oscillating [electric charges](electric-charge-split.md); their emitted [electromagnetic fields](electromagnetic-field.md) combine with the applied field to change the resulting [wave phase](phase-waves.md). This collective response motivates [refractive index](refractive-index.md), rather than a picture of independently delayed little pieces of [light](light.md). [The Feynman Lectures on Physics, Volume I by Richard Feynman and others; §31–1: superposition of the applied field and fields from driven charges; near-unity-index approximation](the-feynman-lectures-on-physics-volume-i-by-richard-feynman-and-others/chapter-31-the-origin-of-the-refractive-index.md)

To distinguish three speeds, track a [wavefront](wavefront.md) for [phase velocity](phase-velocity.md), a narrow [wave envelope](envelope-waves.md) for [group velocity](group-velocity.md), and the earliest newly introduced disturbance for [front velocity](front-velocity.md). For a transparent differentiable propagation branch,

$$
v_g=\left(\frac{dk}{d\omega}\right)^{-1}=\frac{c}{n+\omega\,dn/d\omega}=\frac{c}{n_g},\qquad n_g=n-\lambda_0\frac{dn}{d\lambda_0}.
$$

The denominator is the [group refractive index](group-refractive-index.md). It describes envelope transit when loss and reshaping are small. It need not equal $n$, and neither [phase velocity](phase-velocity.md) nor a reshaped pulse peak generally measures signal arrival. The [front velocity](front-velocity.md) article explains the separate [relativistic causality](relativistic-causality.md) constraint and why a detector threshold is not an exact causal-front measurement.
