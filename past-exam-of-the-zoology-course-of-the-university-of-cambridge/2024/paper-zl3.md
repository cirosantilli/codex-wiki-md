**[Animal signals](../../biology.md#animal-signal) are diverse because their functions, receivers, transmission environments and evolutionary incentives differ. Similar functions can also be achieved by different historical and developmental routes.** An [animal signal](../../biology.md#animal-signal) is shaped by selection for its effects on a receiver; an informative incidental feature can instead be a [biological cue](../../biology.md#biological-cue). Merely finding that another animal responds to a feature does not establish why it evolved. Diversity includes differences among [signal modalities](../../biology.md#signal-modality), differences among [species](../../biology.md#species) using the same modality, geographical variation, and variation among individuals or contexts within a population.

Different problems demand different information. [mate choice](../../biology.md#mate-choice) can favour information about identity, condition or compatibility; contests can favour assessment of fighting ability or motivation; offspring solicitation concerns need and likely returns to provisioning. Alarm communication can coordinate responses to danger, whereas recruitment can direct partners to resources. A single individual may therefore use several signals because the appropriate receiver and useful message change across activities. The efficiency of a signal is defined relative to a receiver's response and its consequences, not simply the amount of information that a human observer can extract.

Physical transmission strongly constrains [signal modality](../../biology.md#signal-modality). Light can provide fine spatial patterning but depends on illumination and line of sight. Sounds can travel around obstacles, yet propagation, reverberation and background noise alter what reaches a receiver. Chemical signals can persist after a signaller leaves, which is useful for some functions and poorly suited to rapid reversal in others. Substrate vibration and electrical stimulation provide additional routes where anatomy and environment permit them. The relevant comparison is the complete communication system: production, transmission, detection and action. A brightly coloured patch is ineffective if it is hidden by posture or cannot be distinguished by the intended receiver.

The environment can consequently drive divergence in signals and sensory systems. In [cichlids](../../organism.md#cichlid), variation in the light environment was associated with differences in visual pigments, male colours and female preferences, supporting a role for [sensory drive](../../biology.md#sensory-drive) in divergence. [Nature 455, pp. 620–626; light gradients, visual pigments, male colours and preferences](../../biology-bibliography.md#speciation-through-sensory-drive-in-cichlid-fish-by-ole-seehausen-and-others) The inference requires evidence for each link; a correlation between water colour and male colour alone would not establish evolved receiver tuning or its effect on mating. Reciprocal illumination treatments, receptor measurements and preference tests help distinguish sensory mechanisms from population history. Actual mating and [gene flow](../../biology.md#gene-flow) provide further evidence about consequences beyond laboratory attraction.

Background interference also changes which signals work. [great tits](../../organism.md#great-tit) in noisier urban locations sang with higher minimum frequencies in a classic field comparison. [Nature 424, p. 267, Fig. 1; song frequency and ambient urban noise](../../biology-bibliography.md#birds-sing-at-a-higher-pitch-in-urban-noise-by-hans-slabbekoorn-and-margriet-peet) This illustrates a plausible response to [acoustic masking](../../neuroscience.md#acoustic-masking), but the association by itself does not distinguish immediate adjustment, developmental [learning](../../biology.md#learning), habitat sorting and inherited change. Playback of controlled noise to the same birds, repeated recordings, and matched quiet sites would separate some of these explanations. Signal amplitude, distance to the microphone and recording conditions also require control. Diverse responses to the same disturbance can reflect different receiver sensitivities or different costs of modifying a signal.

Selection by intended receivers can oppose selection by unintended ones. Male [guppy](../../organism.md#guppy) colour patterns are associated with mating advantages but can also increase detection by [predators](../../ecology.md#predator). Experimental changes in [predation](../../ecology.md#predation) and visual background altered colour-pattern [evolution](../../biology.md#evolution), showing that ecological conditions can change the balance rather than imposing a single universally optimal ornament. [Evolution 34, pp. 76–91; predation and visual-background experiments](../../biology-bibliography.md#natural-selection-on-color-patterns-in-poecilia-reticulata-by-john-a-endler) The intended receiver and [predator](../../ecology.md#predator) may have different [colour vision](../../optics.md#color-vision), so conspicuousness should be modelled and tested separately for each. Increased mating attraction does not establish a net lifetime advantage if survival is substantially reduced. [signal eavesdropping](../../biology.md#signal-eavesdropping) thus helps explain why closely related populations can evolve different compromises.

Receiver psychology supplies further routes to diversity. A preference may originate in another task and subsequently be exploited by a new display, a process of [sensory exploitation](../../biology.md#sensory-exploitation). Alternatively, a signal and preference may coevolve through [sexual selection](../../biology.md#sexual-selection). These hypotheses have different historical predictions, requiring independent phylogenetic evidence about when the preference and display arose. Learned recognition can produce local conventions, while [social learning](../../biology.md#social-learning) can maintain differences without an initial genetic difference in the signal. A novel signal can be favoured when familiar ones lose effectiveness, but novelty is not always beneficial: unfamiliar warnings or mate signals may instead be ignored or mistaken for something else.

[signal honesty](../../biology.md#signal-honesty) also permits several designs. Reliability can follow aligned interests, physical links between production and an advertised property, or differences in the costs and benefits of expression. An [index signal](../../biology.md#index-signal) is constrained by its production mechanism; a condition-dependent signal may be easier for some individuals to sustain. In [costly signalling](../../biology.md#costly-signalling), an expense is relevant when it changes incentives to exaggerate. Showing that a display consumes energy is not sufficient: an equally expensive display could still be advantageous to an unreliable signaller. Tests should manipulate both the signal and the advertised property, then measure receiver responses and the resulting fitness effects. Different information problems need not share the same honesty mechanism.

Conflict can maintain deceptive alternatives alongside informative signals. [fork-tailed drongos](../../organism.md#fork-tailed-drongo) use varied alarm mimicry to obtain food from other foragers, and changes in signal type can restore responses when targets become less responsive to repetition. [Science 344, pp. 513–516; varied alarm mimicry and responses of foraging targets](../../biology-bibliography.md#deception-by-flexible-alarm-mimicry-in-an-african-bird-by-tom-p-flower-and-others) This demonstrates how receiver experience can favour behavioural variation rather than one permanently effective deception. To separate signal-specific [learning](../../biology.md#learning) from declining general responsiveness, experiments need controlled playback order, repeated genuine alarms and measurements of target state. Deception also depends on an otherwise useful response remaining sufficiently common; a response that never benefits its receiver is unlikely to be maintained indefinitely.

Finally, developmental mechanisms and evolutionary history limit the available solutions. Related [species](../../biology.md#species) inherit similar production structures and sensory systems, so some resemblance reflects [biological homology](../../biology.md#biological-homology). Different lineages may reach similar functional outcomes through [convergent evolution](../../biology.md#convergent-evolution). [multimodal signalling](../../biology.md#multimodal-signalling) can supply redundancy under variable conditions or combine different information, but additional components also carry production and detection costs. Comparisons should therefore measure both performance and history, account for [phylogenetic nonindependence](../../biology.md#phylogenetic-nonindependence), and distinguish within-individual flexibility from inherited divergence.

**Signal diversity is the outcome of several interacting filters: what benefits receivers, what signallers gain, what travels and can be perceived, and what inherited mechanisms can produce.** No single explanation such as mate attraction, environmental adaptation or honesty is expected to account for every component of that diversity.

**Parents share an interest in successful offspring but can differ over mating, fertilization, resource allocation and the costs of care. Resolution usually means a stable outcome under those competing incentives, not disappearance of the underlying conflict.** [sexual conflict](../../biology.md#sexual-conflict) is defined by differences in the outcomes favoured by selection, and can occur without overt aggression. It should be distinguished from [parent-offspring conflict](../../biology.md#parent-offspring-conflict), which compares parental and offspring interests rather than those of the two parents.

A fundamental source is the unequal return from additional mating or additional investment. [gamete](../../biology.md#gamete) production, gestation, lactation and care can impose different constraints on the sexes, but those constraints do not dictate one universal sex role. What matters is how changing an action affects lifetime [reproductive success](../../biology.md#reproductive-success), including future opportunities. A parent can favour more current investment by its partner while preferring to reserve its own resources for later reproduction. Conversely, both may benefit from increased joint effort when offspring survival requires contributions that neither can supply alone.

The conflict over mating begins before two animals become the parents of a particular brood. The mating rate that maximizes one sex's lifetime return can differ from that favoured by the other. Courtship persistence, resistance and ejaculate effects can then evolve through [interlocus sexual conflict](../../biology.md#interlocus-sexual-conflict). In [Drosophila melanogaster](../../organism.md#drosophila-melanogaster), comparisons involving males deficient in [sex peptide](../../biology.md#sex-peptide) connected a seminal component to costs of mating for females under the tested mating conditions. [Current Biology 15, pp. 316–321; sex-peptide deficient males and female lifetime performance](../../biology-bibliography.md#sex-peptide-causes-mating-costs-in-female-drosophila-melanogaster-by-stuart-wigby-and-tracey-chapman) The result is stronger than a correlation between mating frequency and shortened life, but it does not mean that the component is always harmful: effects depend on exposure, nutrition, remating and the offspring produced. Genetic-background controls and lifetime offspring counts are necessary when separating altered mating behaviour from direct physiological effects.

[antagonistic coevolution](../../biology.md#antagonistic-coevolution) can generate matching manipulative and resistant traits. Water-strider research combined experimental mating outcomes with comparative analyses of male grasping and female anti-grasping structures across [species](../../biology.md#species). [Nature 415, pp. 787–789; comparative and experimental analyses of water-strider mating armaments](../../biology-bibliography.md#antagonistic-coevolution-between-the-sexes-in-a-group-of-insects-by-goran-arnqvist-and-locke-rowe) Such evidence supports an arms-race interpretation, while retaining alternatives: correlated structures can reflect shared ancestry, ecology or mechanical compatibility. Demonstrating opposed effects on male and female lifetime fitness is more informative than labelling every elaborate genital structure a weapon. [evolution](../../biology.md#evolution) can reduce the current effect of a manipulation without removing the possibility of further counteradaptation.

After mating, the identity and number of offspring produced create further disagreements. A male can benefit from fertilizing a greater share of a brood, while a female's favoured allocation can depend on offspring quality, compatibility and future reproduction. Conflict over remating and fertilization should not be equated with conscious knowledge of parentage. [paternity certainty](../../biology.md#paternity-certainty) can influence the return to paternal care only through available information and feasible alternatives; population-level extra-pair paternity is not itself evidence that an individual detects a particular offspring's parentage. A male may still benefit from caring for a mixed brood when withholding care would sacrifice his own offspring as well.

The logic of conflict over [biparental care](../../biology.md#biparental-care) can be expressed with female effort $x$ and male effort $y$. Let offspring benefit be $B(x+y)$, with diminishing marginal returns, and let parental costs be $C_f(x)$ and $C_m(y)$. In a simple model, each parent values the same offspring benefit but pays only its own direct cost:

$$
W_f=B(x+y)-C_f(x),\qquad W_m=B(x+y)-C_m(y).
$$

At an interior best response with positive effort, each balances $B'$ against its own marginal cost. The outcome need not maximize the unweighted sum of the two payoffs, because that sum counts the shared offspring benefit twice. This is a transparent model of opposing incentives, not a complete prediction of care in a [species](../../biology.md#species). Different parentage, offspring dependence, survival and future mating opportunities alter both benefit and cost functions.

When $B''<0$ and $C_f''>0$, the female's local response to an imposed change in male effort satisfies:

$$
\frac{dx}{dy}=\frac{B''}{C_f''-B''},\qquad -1<\frac{dx}{dy}<0.
$$

Thus this particular model predicts [partial compensation in parental care](../../biology.md#partial-compensation-in-parental-care): reducing male effort increases female effort, but does not fully replace the lost care. Full compensation would give the reducing parent no immediate offspring penalty and could destabilize contributions. The slope is not a universal law, however. It assumes fixed information and benefit functions and does not apply unchanged when a partner's actions provide new information about brood demand.

Experiments on [great tits](../../organism.md#great-tit) used extra begging-call playback during one parent's nest visits to increase that parent's provisioning. The chicks' own begging did not increase, while the other parent also increased its effort. [Behavioral Ecology 17, pp. 6–12; partner provisioning manipulated independently of chick begging](../../biology-bibliography.md#negotiation-over-offspring-care-a-positive-response-to-partner-provisioning-rate-in-great-tits-by-camilla-a-hinde) Such matching can occur when partner activity reveals that offspring demand or expected returns are higher than previously estimated. It therefore need not refute all conflict over care; it challenges the assumption that each parent treats the partner's effort only as a substitutable input. Experiments should manipulate offspring signals and partner actions separately, measure actual food delivered rather than visit counts alone, and compare immediate responses with later care and reproductive consequences. Handicapping one parent can also change brood condition, so an apparent negotiation response may otherwise combine several causal pathways.

[parental negotiation](../../biology.md#parental-negotiation) can stabilize care through conditional adjustment, coordination and repeated interaction. Desertion opportunities, ecological dependence and availability of replacement partners can constrain unilateral withdrawal. Such constraints can align behaviour without aligning every preferred outcome. The observed result may involve alternating visits, task specialization or unequal contributions; equal time is not a necessary prediction. Conflict can also be reduced through sex-specific expression of a shared trait when its sex-specific optima differ, which concerns [intralocus sexual conflict](../../biology.md#intralocus-sexual-conflict) rather than manipulation between interacting loci.

Pregnancy extends the problem to allocation through offspring tissues. Under some patterns of relatedness, maternally and paternally inherited copies in offspring can favour different levels of maternal resource extraction. [kinship theory of genomic imprinting](../../molecular-genetics.md#kinship-theory-of-genomic-imprinting) concerns those copy-specific interests, not a claim that every father always favours larger offspring or every mother smaller ones. [Cell 64, pp. 1045–1046; original kinship-conflict interpretation of imprinted growth regulation](../../biology-bibliography.md#genomic-imprinting-and-the-strange-case-of-the-insulin-like-growth-factor-ii-receptor-by-david-haig-and-c-graham) Maternal survival, future offspring and mating structure determine the relevant inclusive-fitness effects. An expressed physiological compromise can therefore coexist with genetic conflict over its regulation.

**Conflict is resolved through the [evolution](../../biology.md#evolution) of resistance, constraints, conditional care and negotiated equilibria, often with continuing costs.** Establishing a mechanism requires comparing the parties' reproductive consequences and experimentally separating information, partner capacity and offspring demand; merely observing harmonious care or aggressive mating is insufficient.

**An organism contains genetic elements and cell lineages whose transmission interests need not coincide. Conflict arises when increasing an element's representation in descendants or within the body differs from maximizing the organism's [reproductive success](../../biology.md#reproductive-success).** Shared residence does not guarantee shared evolutionary interests, but neither does every difference between [genes](../../genetics.md#gene) constitute conflict. A convincing account identifies the competing lineages, their transmission routes, and the outcomes favoured by selection on each.

Ordinary [Mendelian segregation](../../genetics.md#mendelian-segregation) helps align the interests of unlinked nuclear loci: most depend on the same successful organism and receive an approximately equal chance of entering its [gametes](../../biology.md#gamete). [intragenomic conflict](../../biology.md#intragenomic-conflict) becomes possible when an element bypasses that shared route, biases transmission, replicates within the genome, or has a parent-specific effect on relatives. [mutation](../../genetics.md#mutation) supplies variation; [genetic linkage](../../genetics.md#genetic-linkage) can protect combinations involved in distortion, while [recombination](../../cosmology.md#recombination-cosmology) and selection at other loci can favour suppression. The fitness currency of a [selfish genetic element](../../biology.md#selfish-genetic-element) is therefore its own propagation, not the apparent health or fecundity of its carrier.

[meiotic drive](../../biology.md#meiotic-drive) provides a direct example. For a rare autosomal driver with transmission probabilities $t_f$ through heterozygous females and $t_m$ through heterozygous males, suppose, for illustration, that heterozygotes have the same viability factor $1-s$ in both sexes. With random mating and otherwise equal reproductive contributions, the rare-driver approximation is:

$$
p'\simeq(1-s)(t_f+t_m)p.
$$

A rare driver can increase when the multiplier exceeds one, despite a carrier cost. This accounting also shows why the sex in which drive occurs matters: with unbiased male transmission, $t_m=1/2$, not $t_m=t_f$. The approximation omits dominance in common [genotypes](../../genetics.md#genotype), sex-specific fertility, linkage and suppressors; it is an explanatory invasion calculation rather than a quantitative model of every natural driver.

Asymmetric female [meiosis](../../genetics.md#meiosis) creates one route to distortion because some products become eggs and others are discarded. In monkeyflowers, a centromere-associated [allele](../../genetics.md#allele) showed a strong female transmission advantage, with male fertility costs that expose a conflict between transmission and organismal performance. [Science 322, pp. 1559–1562; female transmission distortion and male fertility costs](../../biology-bibliography.md#centromere-associated-female-meiotic-drive-entails-male-fitness-costs-in-monkeyflowers-by-lila-fishman-and-arpiar-saunders) Reciprocal crosses and appropriate [genetic backgrounds](../../genetics.md#genetic-background) are important: distorted counts among surviving offspring can result from embryonic viability differences rather than biased segregation. Direct observation or genotyping of [gametes](../../biology.md#gamete) and early embryos helps identify the stage. [centromere drive](../../biology.md#centromere-drive) is consequently more specific than merely observing [segregation distortion](../../biology.md#segregation-distortion).

The evolutionary response can include [drive suppressors](../../biology.md#drive-suppressor) at other loci. Suppression is favoured when restoring fair transmission or reducing fertility costs improves the propagation of the rest of the genome. Its effect depends on linkage: a modifier tightly associated with the driver can experience different selection from an unlinked modifier. Arms races can maintain polymorphism and alter reproductive mechanisms; conversely, a successful suppressor can conceal an earlier conflict. Present-day Mendelian ratios therefore do not establish that transmission has always been conflict-free.

[Transposable elements](../../genetics.md#transposable-element) exploit another route: copying or moving genetic material within a lineage. McClintock's maize experiments established mobile controlling elements through genetic and cytological observations, providing foundational evidence that genomic components need not occupy fixed positions. [PNAS 36, pp. 344–355; genetic and cytological evidence for mobile controlling elements](../../biology-bibliography.md#the-origin-and-behavior-of-mutable-loci-in-maize-by-barbara-mcclintock) Mobility itself is not proof that every element is harmful or selfish in every context. The conflict hypothesis requires a replication or transmission advantage alongside consequences for other genomic functions. Host silencing through mechanisms such as [RNA interference](../../molecular-genetics.md#rna-interference) and [DNA methylation](../../molecular-genetics.md#dna-methylation) can constrain propagation, while some formerly mobile sequences acquire useful regulatory functions. Current usefulness and historical origin are different claims.

[genomic imprinting](../../molecular-genetics.md#genomic-imprinting) illustrates conflict through expression rather than transmission distortion. Maternally and paternally inherited copies can differ in their relatedness to individuals affected by resource allocation. If a mother's future offspring are less likely to share the current father, a paternally inherited copy in the current offspring can place relatively less weight on those future maternal offspring. [kinship theory of genomic imprinting](../../molecular-genetics.md#kinship-theory-of-genomic-imprinting) therefore predicts disagreement over resource extraction under specified mating and life-history assumptions. It does not predict opposed interests at every locus or in every mating system. [Cell 64, pp. 1045–1046; original kinship-conflict interpretation of imprinted growth regulation](../../biology-bibliography.md#genomic-imprinting-and-the-strange-case-of-the-insulin-like-growth-factor-ii-receptor-by-david-haig-and-c-graham)

Targeted disruption of the mouse [insulin-like growth factor 2](../../signal-transduction.md#insulin-like-growth-factor-2) [gene](../../genetics.md#gene) produced growth effects dependent on parental transmission, demonstrating parent-of-origin-dependent regulation of an important growth factor. [Cell 64, pp. 849–859; parental transmission of targeted Igf2 disruption and growth](../../biology-bibliography.md#parental-imprinting-of-the-mouse-insulin-like-growth-factor-ii-gene-by-thomas-m-dechiara-and-others) This is mechanistic evidence for imprinting. It is not, by itself, proof that parental genetic conflict caused the evolutionary origin of that regulatory pattern. Testing that explanation requires comparisons among related [species](../../biology.md#species) and mating systems, tissue-specific expression, effects on maternal and offspring fitness, and alternatives such as dosage regulation or coadaptation. The genetic copy's interest must not be confused with a father's conscious preference or with a universal claim that larger offspring are always better for one parent.

The broader category also includes conflicts between distinct genomes residing in one individual. [cytoplasmic inheritance](../../genetics.md#cytoplasmic-inheritance) can make an organelle or microbial symbiont depend mainly on female transmission, whereas nuclear loci are ordinarily transmitted through both sexes. A maternally inherited symbiont can consequently spread through changes to reproduction that do not benefit every host. In [Nasonia](../../organism.md#nasonia) wasps, reciprocal crossing, [chromosome](../../genetics.md#chromosome) observations and antibiotic removal connected cytoplasmic microorganisms to reproductive incompatibility and abnormal paternal [chromosome](../../genetics.md#chromosome) condensation. [Nature 346, pp. 558–560; reciprocal Nasonia crosses, chromosome condensation and antibiotic treatment](../../biology-bibliography.md#microorganisms-associated-with-chromosome-destruction-and-reproductive-isolation-between-two-insect-species-by-johannes-a-j-breeuwer-and-john-h-werren) This demonstrates a microbial cause of a barrier under those conditions, not necessarily the origin of every [species](../../biology.md#species) difference. Antibiotic effects on host condition and other microbes require controls, and reintroduction experiments would strengthen microbial specificity.

[somatic evolution](../../biology.md#somatic-evolution) is a related conflict at a different level. A mutant cell lineage can increase within a body by avoiding growth restraint or [cell death](../../biological-cell.md#cell-death) while damaging the organism. Analyses of [cancer](../../biology.md#cancer) genomes and normal somatic tissues identified patterns of selection on somatic [mutations](../../genetics.md#mutation), showing that cellular success is an evolutionary quantity distinct from organismal success. [Cell 171, pp. 1029–1041.e21; somatic mutation spectra, driver selection and tissue comparisons](../../biology-bibliography.md#universal-patterns-of-selection-in-cancer-and-somatic-tissues-by-inigo-martincorena-and-others) Not all cell proliferation is selfish, and some detected [mutations](../../genetics.md#mutation) are passengers. Mutation-rate heterogeneity, tissue composition and sequencing ascertainment must be modelled before an excess of [mutations](../../genetics.md#mutation) is interpreted as selection.

Such conflicts have consequences beyond immediate harm. They can favour fair-segregation mechanisms, genome defence, regulated cell proliferation and restrictions on which cell lineages produce offspring. A [single-cell bottleneck](../../organism.md#single-cell-bottleneck) and [germ-soma separation](../../organism.md#germ-soma-separation) can increase alignment among cellular descendants or limit transmission of within-body competitors, although neither prevents new [mutations](../../genetics.md#mutation). Conflict can also affect fertility, [sex ratios](../../population-genetics.md#sex-ratio), [reproductive isolation](../../biology.md#reproductive-isolation) and genome organization. Claims that it produced a particular major evolutionary change require historical and comparative evidence in addition to a plausible selective argument.

**Organismal integration is partly an evolved outcome of controlling conflict, not evidence that conflict is absent.** The strongest explanations connect a specific transmission asymmetry to an element's advantage, a measurable cost or opposed interest, and an experimentally or comparatively supported response.

**Stable social actions can reflect stable states or constraints, while flexible actions can follow an unchanged decision rule. The key distinction is between the rule, its inputs and the behaviour observed.** A [social decision rule](../../biology.md#social-decision-rule) maps perceived social information and internal state to an action. It need not involve conscious deliberation, and it need not change genetically whenever the action changes.

For example, an individual that helps when a cue exceeds a threshold follows a fixed conditional rule even if its daily actions vary. A simple [social reaction norm](../../genetics.md#social-reaction-norm) is:

$$
z_i(S)=a_i+b_iS,
$$

where $S$ describes a specified social condition, $a_i$ baseline behaviour and $b_i$ responsiveness. Individuals can differ in either parameter. A stable difference in average actions does not establish that their slopes are zero; conversely, changing actions after a partner changes does not establish that the underlying rule has been relearned. Experiments should vary social conditions within individuals and compare both baselines and responses.

Selection favours responsiveness when information predicts changes in the relative benefits of actions. A partner's willingness to cooperate, current rank, resource availability or the need for a particular task can all alter those returns. Flexibility is less useful when inputs are unreliable, conditions change faster than an individual can respond, or alternative actions cannot be produced. The [cost of behavioural flexibility](../../genetics.md#cost-of-behavioural-flexibility) can include sampling, neural processing and switching costs. A persistent environment may favour specialization, but environmental constancy alone is insufficient: social partners can still generate changing conditions.

Development and morphology can restrict the feasible alternatives. Body size, age, reproductive development and task-specific structures influence what an individual can do. Some specializations involve effectively irreversible developmental commitment, whereas others are reversible states. It is therefore necessary to measure capacity separately from willingness. An animal that remains in one role because it cannot perform another has a different explanation from an animal that could switch but currently gains more by staying. An individual's role can also be constrained by partner aggression or exclusion, so lack of observed switching need not reveal its preferred action.

[age polyethism](../../biology.md#age-polyethism) in [honeybees](../../organism.md#honeybee) illustrates both regularity and flexibility. Workers commonly change tasks with age, but rearing and transplant experiments showed that interactions with older workers alter behavioural development and its hormonal regulation. [PNAS 89, pp. 11726–11729; worker group rearing and older-worker transplant experiments](../../biology-bibliography.md#honeybee-colony-integration-worker-worker-interactions-mediate-hormonally-regulated-plasticity-in-division-of-labor-by-z-y-huang-and-gene-e-robinson) The pattern is thus not adequately described as an inflexible age clock. Changing the social composition can change when younger workers begin foraging. A convincing test distinguishes social information from nutrition, colony size, handling and brood demand, and measures task performance rather than relying only on age or a [hormone](../../signal-transduction.md#hormone) concentration. The colony, not every observation of a worker within it, is often the independent [experimental unit](../../design-of-experiments.md#experimental-unit).

Social opportunity can also change an adult's behavioural state rapidly. In [Astatotilapia burtoni](../../organism.md#astatotilapia-burtoni), removal of a dominant male allowed subordinate males to adopt dominance-related behaviour and coloration within minutes, accompanied by a localized immediate-early [gene](../../genetics.md#gene) response. Stable dominant and subordinate comparison groups helped distinguish new opportunity from simply performing dominance behaviour. [PLoS Biology 3, e363; removal of a dominant male and behavioural and neural responses](../../biology-bibliography.md#rapid-behavioral-and-genomic-responses-to-social-opportunity-by-sabrina-s-burmeister-and-others) The manipulation minimized disturbance to the focal fish. It did not establish that every component of reproductive physiology changed immediately: behaviour, [gene expression](../../molecular-genetics.md#gene-expression) and gonadal development operate on different timescales. A behaviourally fixed-looking subordinate can therefore possess latent flexibility that is expressed only when social constraints change.

Information acquisition and [learning](../../biology.md#learning) add another source of conditional behaviour. Repeated encounters can reveal partner quality, while experience can change the expected cost of aggression or [cooperation](../../ecology.md#cooperation). A [winner-loser effect](../../biology.md#winner-loser-effect) can reinforce existing social asymmetries, but prior contest outcomes are correlated with fighting ability unless experiments separate experience from pre-existing differences. Likewise, familiarity can alter interactions through recognition without requiring a complex representation of another's intentions. Testing partner-specific rules requires swapping partners or their cues while controlling location, experience and motivational state.

Stable individual differences can arise through inherited variation, persistent developmental experience or feedback between behaviour and state. An exploratory individual may gain information that makes further exploration advantageous; a dominant individual can gain resources that maintain its competitive advantage. Partners can adjust around these differences, producing [social niche specialization](../../biology.md#social-niche-specialization). Once complementary roles are established, unilateral switching can be costly even if an alternative allocation would have been possible initially. Such stability need not imply a separate genetically fixed strategy for every role.

[animal personality](../../biology.md#animal-personality) and [behavioural repeatability](../../biology.md#behavioural-repeatability) describe patterns that require explanation rather than supplying one. Repeatability can be high because individuals repeatedly encounter different environments. A [common garden experiment](../../genetics.md#common-garden-experiment), repeated measurement across social contexts and controlled developmental histories help distinguish persistent environmental effects from inherited contributions. Even a heritable baseline can coexist with a highly flexible response slope. Conversely, averaging across opposite responses can hide substantial flexibility or falsely suggest that all individuals follow the same rule.

Social specialization should also be distinguished from efficiency. In ants, separately measuring task specialization and individual task performance showed that specialization did not generally predict greater efficiency. [PLoS Biology 6, e285; separately measured task specialization, efficiency and work allocation](../../biology-bibliography.md#specialization-does-not-predict-individual-efficiency-in-an-ant-by-anna-dornhaus) Stable allocation can therefore reflect task availability, differences in response thresholds or social organization rather than practice-driven superiority at the chosen task. Manipulating task demand and group composition, then measuring both the individual's response and colony output, is more informative than assuming that a frequently observed specialist is the best performer.

An experimental programme should consequently manipulate the information available and the payoffs or constraints separately. Change the number or identity of partners, task demand, social rank or encounter history; use sham disturbances and within-individual comparisons where appropriate. Reversing the treatment tests whether a change is reversible, while a delayed assay tests whether it persists through [learning](../../biology.md#learning) or developmental modification. Pedigrees or controlled breeding can estimate inherited variation without equating it with immutability. Response latency, errors and fitness consequences matter alongside the final action, because a rule can be flexible yet too slow or inaccurate to be advantageous.

**Fixed and flexible social behaviour are outcomes of selection on conditional responses, information, state and feasible actions.** Strong evidence identifies which component changed. Behavioural consistency alone establishes neither genetic determination nor adaptation, and behavioural change alone establishes neither [learning](../../biology.md#learning) nor a changed social rule.

<h3 id="5/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#5)

**[interaction frequency](../../ecology.md#interaction-frequency) changes evolutionary incentives by altering immediate encounter benefits, [learning](../../biology.md#learning) opportunities and expected future returns. Its effect depends on who meets whom and what happens during an encounter; more interaction does not universally favour [cooperation](../../ecology.md#cooperation).** Three quantities must be separated: encounter rate per unit time, probability of meeting the same partner again, and the frequency of a behavioural or genetic variant in the population. They can change independently.

Repeated encounters can support [direct reciprocity](../../ecology.md#direct-reciprocity). In a simple [donation game](../../game-theory.md#donation-game), [cooperation](../../ecology.md#cooperation) costs its actor $c$ and supplies a partner benefit $b$, with $b>c>0$. Suppose both players initially cooperate and use [grim trigger](../../game-theory.md#grim-trigger), withdrawing [cooperation](../../ecology.md#cooperation) permanently after a defection. With effective continuation factor $0\leq\delta<1$, continued [cooperation](../../ecology.md#cooperation) returns:

$$
V_C=\frac{b-c}{1-\delta},
$$

whereas a one-time defector obtains $b$ and then receives zero thereafter against that rule. [cooperation](../../ecology.md#cooperation) is a best response when:

$$
\delta\geq\frac cb.
$$

The repeated-game analysis shows how a cost now can be offset by future returns. It does not show that this rule must evolve, that it can invade from arbitrarily low frequency, or that animals calculate the inequality. Conditional strategies, initial assortment, implementation errors and monitoring determine the evolutionary outcome. The classic theoretical and computational work on repeated [cooperation](../../ecology.md#cooperation) established the importance of future interaction without proving one universal mechanism in nature. [Science 211, pp. 1390–1396; repeated-game theory and strategy tournament](../../biology-bibliography.md#the-evolution-of-cooperation-by-robert-axelrod-and-william-d-hamilton)

Elapsed time matters as well as the number of rounds. For illustration, let further encounters with the same partner occur at rate $\lambda$, while the relationship ends irreversibly at independent rate $\mu$. Under these competing exponential waiting times:

$$
\delta=\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+\mu}.
$$

Faster recurrence can then make benefits arrive before the relationship ends. Combining the two results gives $\lambda\geq\mu c/(b-c)$ under these restrictive assumptions. If frequent encounters are instead with new unrecognizable partners, they need not increase the continuation value of any one relationship. Higher population density can therefore increase encounter count while making partner-specific reciprocity less important.

Intraspecific food sharing in [vampire bats](../../organism.md#vampire-bat) supplies relevant evidence. Controlled fasting induced opportunities to donate food among familiar individuals of mixed relatedness; food received predicted food given more strongly than relatedness, and donors often initiated transfers. [Proceedings B 280, 20122573; controlled fasting, mixed relatedness and directional food donations](../../biology-bibliography.md#food-sharing-in-vampire-bats-reciprocal-help-predicts-donations-more-than-relatedness-or-harassment-by-gerald-g-carter-and-gerald-s-wilkinson) This supports a role for mutual direct benefits beyond a purely kin-based or harassment explanation. It does not establish one-for-one repayment after every donation. Stable social relationships can jointly cause both directions of help, so prospective manipulations of partner availability and ability to reciprocate would provide stronger tests of particular contingency rules. Repeated records within a dyad must not be counted as independent relationships, and opportunities to give must be distinguished from willingness.

Repeated interaction can also support [cooperation](../../ecology.md#cooperation) between [species](../../biology.md#species). Cleaner fish can gain by consuming client mucus rather than the material whose removal benefits the client. In experiments using artificial clients, cleaners chose between preferred prawn and less preferred flake food; taking the preferred item caused plate removal or a brief chasing response in treatment groups. Cleaners subsequently changed their choices relative to controls. [Biology Letters 1, pp. 396–399; artificial-client learning with removal and chase responses](../../biology-bibliography.md#punishment-and-partner-switching-cause-cooperative-behaviour-in-a-cleaning-mutualism-by-redouan-bshary-and-alexandra-s-grutter) This tests a [learning](../../biology.md#learning) mechanism through which loss of access or an adverse response changes incentives. It does not directly measure a natural client's lifetime return or require a tit-for-tat rule. The controls against mere sequence-dependent preference change and the known initial food preference make the interpretation more precise than simply observing frequent cleaning.

[partner choice](../../ecology.md#partner-choice) adds a different effect of encounter structure. If clients can select among several cleaners, access to alternatives can reward good service through continued custom. A resident client with few alternatives may instead rely more on immediate control. Increasing the availability of partners can thus strengthen a [biological market](../../ecology.md#biological-market) while reducing fidelity to any single partner. The net outcome depends on information about service, switching costs and competition for clients. Opportunity for future exchange matters, but partner switching and repeated bilateral reciprocity are not the same process.

Some mutualisms rely on physiological coupling rather than repeated behavioural encounters. In the legume–rhizobium system, experimentally preventing nitrogen fixation altered host support and bacterial reproductive outcomes, providing evidence for conditional [partner sanctions](../../biology.md#partner-sanctions). [Nature 425, pp. 78–81; preventing nitrogen fixation and measuring host and rhizobial responses](../../biology-bibliography.md#host-sanctions-and-the-legume-rhizobium-mutualism-by-e-toby-kiers-and-others) A persistent association can make a partner's contribution affect its own future resources without recognition of discrete past exchanges. The experiment should be interpreted through host physiology and bacterial reproduction, not as evidence that a plant consciously punishes. Controls for the manipulated gas environment and nodule condition distinguish reduced service from incidental treatment damage.

More frequent interactions can equally intensify antagonism. In a simple well-mixed [predator](../../ecology.md#predator)–[prey](../../ecology.md#prey) model, encounters can scale with the product of [predator](../../ecology.md#predator) and [prey](../../ecology.md#prey) densities. However, successful attacks cannot rise without limit when handling takes time. An illustrative [Holling type II functional response](../../ecology.md#holling-type-ii-functional-response) is:

$$
f(N)=\frac{aN}{1+ahN},
$$

with [prey](../../ecology.md#prey) density $N$, encounter coefficient $a$ and handling time $h$. Increasing [prey](../../ecology.md#prey) availability changes the value of search and [prey](../../ecology.md#prey) choice, while saturation changes the benefit of further encounters. [The Canadian Entomologist 91, pp. 385–398; artificial predation experiments and search–handling models](../../biology-bibliography.md#some-characteristics-of-simple-types-of-predation-and-parasitism-by-c-s-holling) This is an ecological link to evolutionary incentives, not proof that all [predators](../../ecology.md#predator) have the same response. Spatial refuge, [prey](../../ecology.md#prey) switching and [learning](../../biology.md#learning) can alter it. Within [species](../../biology.md#species), frequent contests can instead increase injury, resource depletion or the value of stable dominance relationships.

The frequency of strategies supplies a further distinction. The return to aggression, cheating or a mating tactic depends on the alternatives encountered. A rare exploitative strategy may gain from common cooperative partners, but its advantage can decline as it depletes those opportunities. That is [frequency-dependent selection](../../biology.md#frequency-dependent-selection), even if each individual has exactly the same number of encounters per day. By contrast, multiplying every payoff and cost by the same encounter rate may only speed selection without changing the favoured strategy. Effects on equilibrium require a change in relative returns, timing, partner composition or constraints.

Empirically, studies should manipulate encounter rate separately from identity, density and total resource availability, then measure survival or reproduction as well as immediate behaviour. Repeated partners can be maintained with fewer total meetings, or shuffled while holding meeting number fixed. Matched feeding and handling treatments help separate social experience from nutritional state and disturbance. **Interaction frequency is therefore a mechanism-changing ecological variable, not a simple [cooperation](../../ecology.md#cooperation) switch: its importance lies in how it reshapes future consequences, [learning](../../biology.md#learning), assortment and the balance between mutual benefit and exploitation.**

**Mutual support is a major component of [evolution](../../biology.md#evolution), but its universal superiority to competition is neither a well-defined nor an established empirical claim. [cooperation](../../ecology.md#cooperation) and conflict often explain different levels of the same biological system.** The productive question is how support remains advantageous and stable when individuals or genetic elements can gain by contributing less, and how its ecological effects compare with the costs of competition in a specified setting.

In [Mutual Aid: A Factor of Evolution by Peter Kropotkin](../../biology-bibliography.md#mutual-aid-a-factor-of-evolution-by-peter-kropotkin), [Peter Kropotkin](../../biology.md#peter-kropotkin) emphasized animal associations and cooperative benefits that an exclusively combative picture of nature overlooks. [Introduction and Chapters I–II; historical argument about animal mutual support](../../biology-bibliography.md#mutual-aid-a-factor-of-evolution-by-peter-kropotkin) This is a historically important corrective, not a controlled survey assigning a universal effect size to helping. Its descriptive examples generate hypotheses about survival and reproduction. They cannot establish how common [cooperation](../../ecology.md#cooperation) is after correcting for which taxa, environments and conspicuous associations were observed. A claim about the entire organic world also exceeds a collection concerned principally with selected animal and human examples.

Definitions matter. [mutualism](../../ecology.md#mutualism) supplies benefits to both participants, whereas evolutionary [altruism](../../ecology.md#altruism) imposes a direct fitness cost on an actor while benefiting another. [cooperation](../../ecology.md#cooperation) can be mutually beneficial and require no sacrifice at all. Moreover, a short-term feeding cost can be part of a relationship that ultimately increases the donor's own lifetime [reproductive success](../../biology.md#reproductive-success). The biological categories depend on the stated comparison and timescale, not on whether an action appears generous. Conversely, physical aggression is not required for competition: individuals can reduce one another's reproduction by consuming the same limiting resource without meeting.

Some support is explained by immediate complementary returns. Group defence or joint foraging can be individually advantageous when combined actions achieve something inaccessible alone. Field work on groupers and giant moray eels documented recruitment and nonrandom associations during hunting, consistent with gains from their complementary ways of accessing [prey](../../ecology.md#prey). [PLoS Biology 4, e431; recruitment, nonrandom association and complementary hunting](../../biology-bibliography.md#interspecific-communicative-and-coordinated-hunting-between-groupers-and-giant-moray-eels-in-the-red-sea-by-redouan-bshary-and-others) The evidence concerns a particular interspecific association; it does not imply that all joint hunting is reciprocal [altruism](../../ecology.md#altruism). To estimate the benefit rigorously, compare [prey](../../ecology.md#prey) availability, effort, detection and success with and without the partner, accounting for selective association in especially profitable locations. Shared location alone would be weaker evidence than coordinated recruitment and complementary actions.

Support among relatives can evolve even when its actor pays a direct cost. In a simplified marginal accounting, let $C$ be the actor's loss, $B$ a recipient's gain, and $r$ the appropriate genetic association between them. An altruistic change is favoured when:

$$
rB>C.
$$

[inclusive fitness](../../biology.md#inclusive-fitness) theory makes clear why effects on other individuals can influence selection on an actor. [Journal of Theoretical Biology 7, pp. 1–16; original inclusive-fitness theory](../../biology-bibliography.md#the-genetical-evolution-of-social-behaviour-i-by-william-d-hamilton) The coefficients and effects require specification: relatedness is not simply physical proximity, benefits must be measured in relevant reproductive units, and local demographic competition can change their net value. The expression is not a licence to label every action benefiting relatives adaptive without measuring costs and alternatives.

Experiments with [siderophore](../../biology.md#siderophore) production in [Pseudomonas aeruginosa](../../organism.md#pseudomonas-aeruginosa) show why both association and competition matter. Manipulating relatedness and the scale of competition changed the success of cooperative production. [Nature 430, pp. 1024–1027; manipulated relatedness and scale of competition in siderophore production](../../biology-bibliography.md#cooperation-and-competition-in-pathogenic-bacteria-by-ashleigh-s-griffin-and-others) High relatedness can make benefits to neighbours genetically valuable, but the same neighbours may compete for the same limited opportunities. Under sufficiently [local competition](../../biology.md#local-competition), one relative's gain can replace another's rather than increase their collective contribution to future populations. Experimental mixing, replicated populations and controls for iron availability and bacterial growth help identify these mechanisms. The mere observation that producers grow better together than nonproducers would leave the evolutionary competition between the types unresolved.

Support among unrelated individuals has additional routes. [by-product mutualism](../../ecology.md#by-product-mutualism) can supply immediate returns; [partner fidelity feedback](../../ecology.md#partner-fidelity-feedback) makes a persistent partner's improved condition beneficial to the actor; [partner choice](../../ecology.md#partner-choice) can redirect future opportunities toward good partners. Conditional withdrawal of benefits can limit exploitation. These mechanisms do not require all participants to value the group's success independently of their own reproduction. Their presence also does not eliminate [evolutionary conflict](../../biology.md#evolutionary-conflict) over how the joint benefit is divided. A [mutualism](../../ecology.md#mutualism) can remain beneficial to both participants while each favours a different exchange rate.

Even highly integrated societies contain competition over reproduction. In [honeybees](../../organism.md#honeybee), workers can produce male eggs, and experiments showed discrimination against worker-laid eggs, supporting [worker policing](../../biology.md#worker-policing). [Nature 342, pp. 796–797; discriminatory removal of worker-laid male eggs](../../biology-bibliography.md#worker-policing-in-the-honeybee-by-francis-l-w-ratnieks-and-p-kirk-visscher) Relatedness under multiple mating can contribute to the incentive to suppress worker reproduction, while effects on colony productivity also require consideration. The observed collective organization is therefore maintained partly by controlling opportunities for individual reproduction. Egg transfer experiments, controls for egg age and handling, and tests of recognition cues distinguish policing from generalized removal of damaged eggs. A peaceful-looking colony need not be free of conflict, and aggressive enforcement can help maintain collective functioning.

The same logic extends to integration across biological levels. A multicellular organism depends on coordinated cell activities, but [somatic evolution](../../biology.md#somatic-evolution) can favour cell lineages that undermine organismal reproduction. A [single-cell bottleneck](../../organism.md#single-cell-bottleneck), regulation of [cell division](../../biological-cell.md#cell-division) and restrictions on reproductive lineages can reduce such conflicts. These are candidate mechanisms for alignment, not evidence that every part of the organism has always had identical interests. [Major evolutionary transitions](../../biology.md#major-evolutionary-transition) are important partly because [cooperation](../../ecology.md#cooperation) becomes stable enough to form new reproductive units; suppression of lower-level competition can be part of that success rather than its conceptual opposite.

[multilevel selection](../../biology.md#multilevel-selection) provides another accounting of these processes. Groups with more contributors can produce more descendants while noncontributors increase within mixed groups. Whether contribution spreads depends on the strength of both effects and on how groups form and reproduce. Simply demonstrating that a group performs well does not show that its costly contributing trait resists invasion. In appropriate models, inclusive-fitness and multilevel descriptions can track the same underlying change from different partitions, so changing vocabulary alone does not supply a different biological mechanism.

Assessing relative importance requires an explicit metric. One might compare effects on population persistence, individual lifetime reproduction, biomass production or the rate of a major transition. Those answers need not agree. A short interval of severe competition can determine the outcome despite long periods of [cooperation](../../ecology.md#cooperation); alternatively, cooperative defence can be essential during a rare environmental crisis. Support within a group can also improve that group's capacity to compete with others, transferring rather than eliminating antagonism. Neither the proportion of observations that look peaceful nor the number of visibly aggressive events measures total selective importance.

**The defensible conclusion is that mutual support is pervasive and often indispensable, while evolutionary competition and conflict help explain when that support evolves, how it is regulated and where it breaks down.** Mechanistic and comparative tests support this joint account more strongly than a universal ranking of support over struggle.
