**Sleep can protect newly acquired information, actively reorganize it, and restore the capacity for subsequent learning.** These are distinct claims. [Memory consolidation](../../../memory-consolidation.md) concerns changes after acquisition; better learning the next morning concerns encoding capacity; less interference during an unoccupied interval need not imply an active sleep mechanism. The strongest explanation therefore combines behavioural comparisons with recordings and selective interventions.

A basic retention experiment compares later performance after sleep and waking, ideally relative to an immediate baseline. Yet a night-versus-day comparison also changes [circadian phase](../../../circadian-phase.md), interference and alertness at testing. Sleep deprivation adds [physiological stress](../../../physiological-stress.md) and attentional impairment. Matching acquisition, testing at comparable times, measuring vigilance, and comparing sleep with quiet waking make the inference sharper. A retention advantage after sleep remains compatible with both passive protection and active processing; distinguishing them requires manipulating what happens during the interval.

The [active systems consolidation hypothesis](../../../active-systems-consolidation-hypothesis.md) provides an active mechanism. Rapid relational encoding by the [hippocampus](../../../hippocampus.md) permits later [memory reactivation](../../../memory-reactivation.md) to modify distributed [cerebral cortex](../../../cerebral-cortex.md) representations. During [NREM sleep](../../../non-rapid-eye-movement-sleep.md), cortical [sleep slow oscillations](../../../sleep-slow-oscillation.md) can organize [sleep spindles](../../../sleep-spindle.md) and [hippocampal sharp-wave ripples](../../../hippocampal-sharp-wave-ripple.md), creating coordinated windows for [synaptic plasticity](../../../synaptic-plasticity.md). This is a timing hypothesis about communication, not proof that a complete memory is copied from one anatomical container to another. [Systems consolidation](../../../systems-consolidation.md) and [synaptic consolidation](../../../synaptic-consolidation.md) can proceed together, and some detailed [episodic memories](../../../episodic-memory.md) may continue to require hippocampal contributions.

An important recording experiment monitored ensembles of hippocampal [place cells](../../../place-cell.md) in rats during spatial behaviour and during sleep before and after it. Cells active together at particular locations became more likely to fire together in subsequent slow-wave sleep; the pre-experience recording supplied a valuable baseline. This demonstrates experience-related [memory reactivation](../../../memory-reactivation.md). Pairwise coactivation alone does not establish ordered [neural replay](../../../neural-replay.md), nor does a [correlation](../../../pearson-correlation-coefficient.md) show that the events improve retention. An apparently memory-related pattern might otherwise be an incidental consequence of prior activity.[Abstract and pp. 676–679: simultaneous ensemble recordings and pre-/post-experience comparison](../../../reactivation-of-hippocampal-ensemble-memories-during-sleep-by-matthew-a-wilson-and-others.md)

A stronger causal test interrupted [hippocampal sharp-wave ripples](../../../hippocampal-sharp-wave-ripple.md) during post-training sleep in rats learning a spatial task. An online electrical recording system detected ripple events and triggered brief disruption; a delayed-stimulation comparison helped control stimulation without abolishing the same events. Spatial performance was impaired, while gross sleep architecture was not detectably changed. This supports a functional role for ripple-associated activity beyond simply obtaining sleep. Nevertheless, disruption affects an event and its associated activity, not an experimentally isolated memory message; it does not identify the exact information transferred or establish that all [memory consolidation](../../../memory-consolidation.md) requires the same mechanism.[pp. 1222–1223; Figures 1–2: event-triggered disruption, delayed control and behaviour](../../../selective-suppression-of-hippocampal-ripples-impairs-spatial-memory-by-gabrielle-girardeau-and-others.md)

Human [targeted memory reactivation](../../../targeted-memory-reactivation.md) offers complementary evidence. Participants learned object locations with an odour present, then received that odour again during polysomnographically identified slow-wave sleep. Retention improved compared with an odourless condition. Crucially, the benefit did not appear when the odour had been absent during learning, or when it was presented during waking or [REM sleep](../../../rapid-eye-movement-sleep.md); procedural finger-tapping did not show the same benefit. The cue–learning relationship and sleep state therefore mattered. The associated hippocampal activation is consistent with [memory reactivation](../../../memory-reactivation.md), although activation does not reveal all the represented content. This is stronger evidence for selective processing than an undifferentiated sleep/wake advantage.[pp. 1426–1429; experiments I–IV: object-location learning, unpaired odour and sleep-state controls](../../../odor-cues-during-slow-wave-sleep-prompt-declarative-memory-consolidation-by-bjorn-rasch-and-others.md)

Sleep could also benefit memory by regulating the substrate for learning. The [synaptic homeostasis hypothesis](../../../synaptic-homeostasis-hypothesis.md) proposes that predominantly waking-associated strengthening requires compensatory renormalization, limiting saturation, energy costs and interference. Selective preservation can improve the contrast of important connections even if average strength of [synapses](../../../synapse.md) falls. Serial electron-microscopy reconstructions of mouse cortical synapses found smaller axon–spine interfaces after sleep, with an average decrease of roughly 18%; the largest synapses were relatively spared. The comparison included spontaneous and enforced waking. These are structural measurements consistent with selective scaling, not a direct measurement of every synapse's efficacy or proof that the measured changes caused a particular memory benefit.[pp. 507–510; Figures 1–3: three-dimensional reconstructions and size-dependent changes](../../../ultrastructural-evidence-for-synaptic-scaling-across-the-wake-sleep-cycle-by-luisa-de-vivo-and-others.md)

Renormalization and selective strengthening are not mutually exclusive. A learning-related ensemble could be selectively stabilized while less useful background connections weaken. [Memory reactivation](../../../memory-reactivation.md) could help select what survives, and improved capacity after sleep could support the next episode rather than merely preserve the last one. An adequate theory must explain both specificity and restoration, rather than equate more firing or larger synapses with better memory everywhere.

The interpretation must remain task-specific. A cueing effect for [declarative memory](../../../declarative-memory.md) does not assign every [procedural memory](../../../procedural-memory.md) to [REM sleep](../../../rapid-eye-movement-sleep.md), and a null cueing result in one stage does not show that stage has no mnemonic function. Sleep-stage manipulations can alter other stages or awaken subjects. Rodent spatial performance provides mechanistic access but does not by itself establish human autobiographical experience. Replicated behavioural effects, adequate controls and selective circuit perturbations are most persuasive when they converge.

Thus sleep is important because it provides a state in which information can be selectively re-expressed and reorganized while conditions at [synapses](../../../synapse.md) are regulated. Evidence available by April 2022 supports active contributions in addition to passive protection; it does not establish a universal sleep-only route for every kind of learning.
