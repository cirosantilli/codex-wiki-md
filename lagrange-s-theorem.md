<h1 id="lagrange-s-theorem">Lagrange's theorem</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [Group theory](group-theory-split.md)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrange's_theorem_(group_theory))

For a [finite group](finite-group.md) $G$ and a [subgroup](subgroup.md) $H$, the [group order](order-of-a-finite-group.md) of $H$ divides the [group order](order-of-a-finite-group.md) of $G$:

$$
|G|=[G:H]|H|.
$$

Here $[G:H]$ is the [index of a subgroup](index-of-a-subgroup.md), the number of distinct left [cosets](coset.md). It is also the number of distinct right [cosets](coset.md): inversion gives a [bijection](bijection.md) $gH\mapsto Hg^{-1}$ between the two collections. No [normal subgroup](normal-subgroup.md) assumption is needed for [Lagrange's theorem](lagrange-s-theorem.md). The [normal subgroup](normal-subgroup.md) condition matters when constructing a [quotient group](quotient-group.md), rather than when counting a [partition of a set](partition-of-a-set.md).

The beauty of [Lagrange's theorem](lagrange-s-theorem.md) is that an arithmetic restriction follows from a structural picture. A [group](group-split.md) can be tiled by equal-sized [cosets](coset.md) of any [subgroup](subgroup.md). To see the count, choose one representative from each left [coset](coset.md). Every [group element](group-element.md) has exactly one expression $g_i h$ for these chosen representatives and $h\in H$: different rows do not overlap, and multiplication by $g_i$ is a [bijection](bijection.md) from $H$ to its row. Counting rows times row size gives the formula. See [MIT's symmetries notes, section 2](https://math.mit.edu/~djk/18.310/Lecture-Notes/symmetries_2007.html).

The additive [cyclic group](cyclic-group.md) $\mathbb Z/6\mathbb Z$ above gives $6=3\times2$ from $H=\{0,3\}$. Another [subgroup](subgroup.md), $K=\{0,2,4\}$, has [cosets](coset.md) $K$ and $1+K=\{1,3,5\}$, giving $6=2\times3$. In $S_3$, the [subgroup](subgroup.md) $\{e,(12)\}$ also gives $6=3\times2$, despite its distinct left and right [coset](coset.md) partitions. Thus equal row sizes persist in a [nonabelian group](non-abelian-group.md). A proposed [subgroup](subgroup.md) of size four in either [group](group-split.md) is impossible: four does not divide six. This filters possible [subgroups](subgroup.md) before any search for [group generators](generator-of-a-group.md).

Apply [Lagrange's theorem](lagrange-s-theorem.md) to the [cyclic subgroup](cyclic-subgroup.md) generated by a [group element](group-element.md) $g$. Its size is the [order of a group element](order-of-a-group-element.md), so $\operatorname{ord}(g)\mid|G|$ and $g^{|G|}=e$. In the additive example, $2$ has [order of a group element](order-of-a-group-element.md) $3$, since $2+2+2=0$ modulo six; the corresponding multiplicative statement becomes $6\cdot2=0$. A [group](group-split.md) of [prime](prime-number-split.md) [group order](order-of-a-finite-group.md) is consequently a [cyclic group](cyclic-group.md): any nonidentity [group element](group-element.md) generates a [subgroup](subgroup.md) whose size is greater than one and divides that [prime](prime-number-split.md), forcing it to be the whole [group](group-split.md). See [MIT's cyclic-group notes](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-703-modern-algebra-spring-2013/50c134275caf32dbf4430ab097185157_MIT18_703S13_pra_l_4.pdf). The same counting idea underlies the [orbit-stabilizer theorem](orbit-stabilizer-theorem.md): the [orbit of a group action](orbit-group-theory.md) is parametrized by [cosets](coset.md) of its [stabilizer subgroup](stabilizer-subgroup.md).

Historically, [Lagrange's theorem](lagrange-s-theorem.md) grew from investigations of [permutations](permutation.md) of [roots of a polynomial](root-of-a-polynomial.md); see [St Andrews' account of Lagrange's early work](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Lagrange/). The modern statement about arbitrary [finite groups](finite-group.md) should not be read back into the early work. Carmichael's 1921 review explicitly cautioned against attributing the full result to Lagrange; see [Carmichael's historical note, reproduced by St Andrews](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Extras/Carmichael_Hardy_1/). Finally, [Lagrange's theorem](lagrange-s-theorem.md) supplies a necessary size condition; the [failure of the converse to Lagrange's theorem](failure-of-the-converse-to-lagrange-s-theorem.md) below explains its limit.
