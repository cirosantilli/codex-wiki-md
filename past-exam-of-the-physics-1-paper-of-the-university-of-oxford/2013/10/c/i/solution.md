<h1 id="10/c/i/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [I](../i.md)

Interpret the one-light-day separation $D$ as measured in the station frame at capsule launch, and retain the ship's initial inertial [rest frame](../../../../../rest-frame.md) until reception at the station. The phrase does not explicitly name a frame; an alternative is given below. The ship-frame simultaneous separation is $D'=D/\gamma_s$. The capsule moves at $0.9c$ towards a station approaching at $0.85c$, so their separation decreases at $1.75c$ in this frame. This is a separation rate, not either object's [speed](../../../../../speed.md). Hence the elapsed ship-frame coordinate [time](../../../../../time-in-physics.md) is

$$
\boxed{\Delta t_s=\frac{D}{\gamma_s(0.9+0.85)c}\simeq0.301018\,\mathrm{day}\simeq7.2244\,\mathrm h.}
$$

It is the interval assigned using synchronized clocks in the ship frame to the launch and remote arrival. A single onboard clock does not directly mark the remote arrival event; observing its arrival signal would add a light-travel delay. If the stated one-light-day separation were instead measured in the ship frame, the answer would be $\Delta t_s=(1/1.75)\,\mathrm{day}=0.571429\,\mathrm{day}$.
