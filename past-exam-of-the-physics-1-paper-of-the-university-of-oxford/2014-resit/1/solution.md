<h1 id="1/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [1](../1.md)

The [parallel axis theorem](../../../parallel-axis-theorem.md) relates the [moment of inertia](../../../moment-of-inertia.md) about an axis through the [centre of mass](../../../center-of-mass.md) to that about a parallel axis displaced by perpendicular distance $d$:

$$
\boxed{I=I_G+Md^2.}
$$

The reference axis must pass through the [centre of mass](../../../center-of-mass.md). To see why, write the displacement from it as $\mathbf r=\mathbf R+\mathbf d$ in the plane perpendicular to the axes. Expanding $\int|\mathbf R+\mathbf d|^2\,dm$ gives $I_G+Md^2$ because $\int\mathbf R\,dm=0$.

For a thin planar [mass distribution](../../../mass-distribution.md), let $x,y$ be perpendicular axes in its plane and $z$ the normal axis through **their same intersection**. The [perpendicular axis theorem](../../../perpendicular-axis-theorem.md) states

$$
\boxed{I_z=I_x+I_y.}
$$

Indeed, $I_z=\int(x^2+y^2)\,dm$, while $I_x=\int y^2\,dm$ and $I_y=\int x^2\,dm$. Their common intersection need not be the [centre of mass](../../../center-of-mass.md), but an arbitrary three-dimensional body does not satisfy the planar equality.
