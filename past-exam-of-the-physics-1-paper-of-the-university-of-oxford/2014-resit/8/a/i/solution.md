<h1 id="8/a/i/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [I](../i.md)

Take $\theta$ from the downward vertical. Under the conventional light-rod idealization, only the endpoint [point mass](../../../../../point-mass.md) contributes appreciably. Its speed is $l\dot\theta$, and its gravitational [potential energy](../../../../../potential-energy.md) is $-mgl\cos\theta$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\mathcal L=\frac12ml^2\dot\theta^2+mgl\cos\theta.}
$$

An additive constant in the [potential energy](../../../../../potential-energy.md) has no effect on the [Euler-Lagrange equation](../../../../../euler-lagrange-equation.md).

**Rod-mass qualification:** “thin” and “uniform” do not imply negligible [mass](../../../../../mass.md). If the uniform rod has a finite unspecified [mass](../../../../../mass.md) $M_r$, its pivot [moment of inertia](../../../../../moment-of-inertia.md) is $M_rl^2/3$ and its [centre of mass](../../../../../center-of-mass.md) lies $l/2$ from the pivot. For a [pendulum with a uniform rod and an endpoint mass](../../../../../pendulum-with-a-uniform-rod-and-an-endpoint-mass.md), the general answer is

$$
\mathcal L=\frac12I\dot\theta^2+A\cos\theta,\qquad
I=l^2\left(m+\frac{M_r}{3}\right),\qquad
A=gl\left(m+\frac{M_r}{2}\right).
$$

This formula covers either convention without inventing a rod [mass](../../../../../mass.md). A result involving only the specified $m,l,g$ uses $M_r=0$ unless an additional relation for $M_r$ is stated.
