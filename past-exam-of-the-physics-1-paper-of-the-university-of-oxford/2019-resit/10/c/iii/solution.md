<h1 id="10/c/iii/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](../iii.md)

For the initial radius $r_0=l/2$ to be circular, the radial equation requires

$$
\frac{mv_0^2}{r_0}=Mg,\qquad
v_0^2=\frac{Mgl}{2m}.
$$

Consider a small radial perturbation at fixed conserved [angular momentum](../../../../../angular-momentum.md) $J$. Using $J^2/(mr_0^3)=Mg$ and $r=r_0+\epsilon$ in the radial equation gives

$$
(M+m)\ddot\epsilon
=Mg\left[\left(1+\frac\epsilon{r_0}\right)^{-3}-1\right]
=-\frac{3Mg}{r_0}\epsilon+O(\epsilon^2).
$$

Consequently the linearized radial motion is [simple harmonic motion](../../../../../simple-harmonic-motion.md):

$$
\boxed{\ddot\epsilon+\omega_r^2\epsilon=0,\qquad
\omega_r=\sqrt{\frac{3Mg}{(M+m)r_0}}=\sqrt{\frac{6Mg}{(M+m)l}}.}
$$

The positive curvature of the [effective potential](../../../../../effective-potential.md) establishes radial stability. This is the small-amplitude limit, not an exact finite-amplitude sinusoid. A perturbation that changes $J$ shifts the circular radius; its radial oscillations are about the new radius to the same order. A radial displacement or radial impulse preserving $J$ gives the stated oscillations about $l/2$.
