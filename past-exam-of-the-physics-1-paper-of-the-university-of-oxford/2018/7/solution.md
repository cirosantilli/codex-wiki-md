<h1 id="7/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [7](../7.md)

The [parallel axis theorem](../../../parallel-axis-theorem.md) relates the [moment of inertia](../../../moment-of-inertia.md) about an axis through the [centre of mass](../../../center-of-mass.md) to that about a parallel axis a perpendicular distance $d$ away:

$$
\boxed{I=I_{\mathrm{CM}}+M_{\mathrm{tot}}d^2.}
$$

To see the cross term vanish, write each perpendicular displacement as its displacement from the [centre of mass](../../../center-of-mass.md) plus the fixed offset. In the defining mass [integral](../../../integral-split.md), the first moment about the [centre of mass](../../../center-of-mass.md) is zero.

For a thin planar [rigid body](../../../rigid-body-dynamics-split.md) in the $xy$ plane, the [perpendicular axis theorem](../../../perpendicular-axis-theorem.md) says

$$
\boxed{I_z=I_x+I_y,}
$$

where all three axes pass through the same point, $x,y$ lie in the plane, and $z$ is perpendicular to it. This follows directly from $x^2+y^2$ being the squared distance from the $z$ axis. The thin-planar condition and common intersection point are essential; the formula is not a general identity for three-dimensional bodies.
