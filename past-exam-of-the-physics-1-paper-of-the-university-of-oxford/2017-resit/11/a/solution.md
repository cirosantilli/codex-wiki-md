<h1 id="11/a/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [A](../a.md)

For a planar lamina in the $xy$-plane and three mutually perpendicular axes through one common point, the [perpendicular axis theorem](../../../../perpendicular-axis-theorem.md) is $I_z=I_x+I_y$. Indeed $I_z=\int(x^2+y^2)\,dm$, while $I_x=\int y^2\,dm$ and $I_y=\int x^2\,dm$. Planarity and the common axis intersection are essential.

For two parallel axes, one through the [centre of mass](../../../../center-of-mass.md) $G$ and one a perpendicular distance $d$ away, the [parallel axis theorem](../../../../parallel-axis-theorem.md) is

$$
\boxed{I=I_G+Md^2,}
$$

where $M$ is the total [mass](../../../../mass.md). Expanding the squared distance in the defining [moment of inertia](../../../../moment-of-inertia.md) integral leaves a cross term proportional to $\int\mathbf r_G\,dm=0$. The second axis need not pass through the body, and uniform [density](../../../../density.md) is not required, but the reference axis for $I_G$ must pass through its [centre of mass](../../../../center-of-mass.md).
