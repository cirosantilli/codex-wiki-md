<h1 id="6/b/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [B](../b.md)

Use the usual dumbbell geometry: the uniform thin rod joins the disc centres and is perpendicular to both disc faces. The required axis through the rod midpoint lies in a direction parallel to a diameter of each disc. For either disc, [rotational symmetry](../../../../rotational-symmetry.md) and the [perpendicular axis theorem](../../../../perpendicular-axis-theorem.md) give

$$
I_{\rm diameter}=\frac12I_{\rm normal}=\frac14Ma^2.
$$

Each disc centre lies a distance $l/2$ from the requested axis. The [parallel axis theorem](../../../../parallel-axis-theorem.md) therefore gives a disc contribution $Ma^2/4+M(l/2)^2$. The rod contributes

$$
I_{\rm rod}=\frac{m_0}{l}\int_{-l/2}^{l/2}x^2\,dx
=\frac1{12}m_0l^2.
$$

Add the two disc contributions and the rod's [moment of inertia](../../../../moment-of-inertia.md):

$$
\boxed{I=\frac1{12}m_0l^2+\frac12Ml^2+\frac12Ma^2.}
$$

Using $Ma^2/2$ for each disc's central contribution would instead use an axis perpendicular to its face, which is parallel to the rod and is not the requested axis.
