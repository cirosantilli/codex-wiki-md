<h1 id="frequency-form-of-wien-s-displacement-law">Frequency form of Wien's displacement law</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [Wien's displacement law](wien-s-displacement-law.md)

For [Planck's law](planck-s-law.md) per unit [frequency](frequency.md), setting $x=h\nu/(k_BT)$ leaves a positive constant times $x^3/(e^x-1)$. Its [logarithmic derivative](logarithmic-derivative.md) gives

$$
x_\nu=3(1-e^{-x_\nu}),\qquad x_\nu=2.8214393721\ldots,\qquad
\frac{\nu_{\max}}T=\frac{k_Bx_\nu}{h}\simeq5.878925758\times10^{10}\,\mathrm{Hz\,K^{-1}}.
$$

The same uniqueness argument as in the [Derivation of Wien's displacement law](derivation-of-wien-s-displacement-law.md), with $5$ replaced by $3$, selects the positive root. The maxima differ because corresponding positive spectral intervals obey

$$
B_\lambda\,d\lambda=B_\nu\,|d\nu|,\qquad
B_\lambda(\lambda)=B_\nu(c/\lambda)\frac{c}{\lambda^2}.
$$

The nonconstant [Jacobian determinant](jacobian-determinant.md) $c/\lambda^2$ changes density heights. Consequently $c/\nu_{\max}=(x_\lambda/x_\nu)\lambda_{\max}\simeq1.75978\lambda_{\max}$. A peak is a property of a specified density, rather than an invariant point of the spectrum. For equal logarithmic intervals, the corresponding densities satisfy $\lambda B_\lambda=\nu B_\nu$ and have matching converted peak positions; this is a third convention, distinct from either linear-interval density.
