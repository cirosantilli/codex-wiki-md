**Fluoride favors geminal disubstitution:** after the first substitution, the second F replaces the other chloride at that same phosphorus, giving a $\mathrm{PF_2}$ unit and two unchanged $\mathrm{PCl_2}$ units. The first highly electronegative F withdraws [electron](../../../../../../electron.md) density inductively, maintaining or increasing the [electrophilicity](../../../../../../electrophilicity.md) of its phosphorus and activating the neighboring P–Cl bond toward another [nucleophile](../../../../../../nucleophile.md). The small fluoride ion also has little [steric hindrance](../../../../../../steric-hindrance.md) to attack there. In this context [geminal substitution](../../../../../../geminal-substitution.md) means both incoming substituents on one phosphorus; it does not describe the relative positions of different ring [nitrogen](../../../../../../nitrogen.md) atoms. Electronic activation at the substituted phosphorus competes with effects elsewhere in the ring, so the argument predicts the preferred pathway rather than an unconditional exclusive product.
