↑ **Parent:** [D](../d.md)

The CO trans to a [phosphine ligand](../../../../../phosphine-ligand.md) competes for metal pi donation with a weaker pi acceptor than CO itself, whereas a cis CO has another CO trans to it. The trans-to-phosphine carbonyl can therefore benefit from stronger M–CO [pi backbonding](../../../../../pi-backbonding.md); loss of one of the four cis carbonyls is commonly favored. Capture of the incoming phosphine at that vacancy gives **the cis bis(phosphine) product**. The fourfold statistical availability of cis sites reinforces an electronic preference but does not establish it by itself.

This is the carbonyl [cis effect](../../../../../cis-effect.md), rather than the square-planar [trans effect](../../../../../trans-effect.md) applied without qualification. A fuller explanation compares the five-coordinate dissociation [transition states](../../../../../transition-state.md) and their ligand site preferences. Product geometry alone cannot prove which CO first departed, because the intermediate can rearrange before capture. Isotopically labeled CO exchange is a more discriminating experiment; the classic studies explicitly separate this issue from product stereochemistry.[1976, pp. 3160–3161, isotope exchange, intermediate rearrangement and limitations of inferring dissociation sites from substitution products](../../../../../cis-labilization-of-ligand-dissociation-3-survey-of-group-6-and-7-six-coordinate-carbonyl-compounds-the-site-preference-model-for-ligand-labilization-effects-by-jim-d-atwood-and-theodore-l-brown.md)
