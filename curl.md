# Curl

↑ **Parent:** [Vector calculus](vector-calculus.md)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curl)

The curl of $F$ has components

$$
(\nabla\times F)_i=\epsilon_{ijk}\partial_jF_k.
$$

For a continuously differentiable [vector field](vector-field.md) in three-dimensional [Euclidean space](euclidean-norm.md), the component of its curl along a [unit normal vector](unit-normal.md) is the limiting circulation per unit area around a small loop in the perpendicular plane. The loop direction and the normal obey the [right-hand rule](right-hand-rule.md). [Stokes theorem](stokes-theorem.md) gives the corresponding relation for a finite surface.

For the rigidly rotating [velocity field](velocity-field.md) $G=(-\Omega y,\Omega x,0)$, direct differentiation gives $\nabla\times G=(0,0,2\Omega)$. A circle of radius $R$ has tangential speed $\Omega R$ and circumference $2\pi R$, so its circulation is $2\pi\Omega R^2$, twice the [angular velocity](angular-velocity.md) times the enclosed area. The factor of two distinguishes [vorticity](vorticity-split.md) from rigid-body angular velocity.

Curved [integral curves](integral-curve-of-a-vector-field.md) alone do not demonstrate nonzero curl. The field $H=(x,-y,0)$ has zero curl, yet its trajectories satisfy $x=x_0e^t$, $y=y_0e^{-t}$ and hence $xy=x_0y_0$: away from the axes they are [hyperbolas](hyperbola.md). The curl measures local circulation of the field, not the curvature of an individual trajectory.
