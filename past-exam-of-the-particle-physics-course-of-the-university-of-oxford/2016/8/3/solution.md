<h1 id="8/3/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [3](../3.md)

The geometric rms [beam emittance](../../../../beam-emittance.md) measures transverse [phase space](../../../../phase-space.md) area. In one plane,

$$
\epsilon_x=\sqrt{\langle x^2\rangle\langle x'^2\rangle-\langle xx'\rangle^2},\qquad\sigma_x^2=\epsilon_x\beta_x.
$$

The [betatron amplitude function](../../../../betatron-amplitude-function.md) $\beta_x(s)$ describes how the beam envelope varies around the lattice. At a waist, $\langle xx'\rangle=0$ and $\langle x'^2\rangle=\epsilon_x/\beta_x$. This [betatron amplitude function](../../../../betatron-amplitude-function.md) is unrelated to either the relativistic velocity ratio $v/c$ or a renormalization-group [beta function](../../../../beta-function.md).

With the [beam emittances](../../../../beam-emittance.md) and bunch populations held fixed, reducing each interaction-point amplitude function to one third makes both transverse rms widths smaller by $\sqrt3$. Hence their product is smaller by three, and

$$
\boxed{\mathcal L_{\rm new}=3\mathcal L_{\rm old}}.
$$

The change is not a factor of nine: widths are proportional to the square root of the amplitude function. The transverse angular spread increases by $\sqrt3$. In a real machine, stronger final focusing, finite bunch length, crossing angle, aperture and beam-beam limits can reduce the nominal gain; the question's ideal symmetric head-on approximation neglects these effects.
