<h1 id="7/3/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [3](../3.md)

For $N_p$ [protons](../../../../proton.md), exposure $T$ and efficiency $\epsilon$, the expected count in this channel is $\mu=\epsilon N_p\Gamma_{\mu\pi^0}T$. A partial [decay rate](../../../../decay-width.md) at the quoted upper bound gives one event per year when

$$
N_p\simeq6.6\times10^{33},\qquad\boxed{M_p=N_pm_p\simeq1.10\times10^7\ \mathrm{kg}\simeq11\ \mathrm{kilotonnes}}.
$$

More generally, $M_p\simeq11\ \mathrm{kilotonnes}\,\mu/[\epsilon(T/\mathrm{yr})]$ at the limiting rate. This is an estimate of the required proton inventory, not necessarily the whole detector [mass](../../../../mass.md). For water, each molecule contains ten [protons](../../../../proton.md) and has [mass](../../../../mass.md) approximately $18$ atomic mass units; the corresponding ideal inventory is about $19.7\ \mathrm{kilotonnes}$ of water. Finite efficiency and a useful sample larger than one event increase the required exposure.

An upper limit is not a measured nonzero rate. The true rate could be much smaller, including zero, so no finite target guarantees a measurable signal. The estimate describes sensitivity to a rate near the existing limit.
