<h1 id="algebra/3/d/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [D](../d.md)

The zero [ideal](../../../../../ideal.md) is a [prime ideal](../../../../../prime-ideal.md) because $R$ is an [integral domain](../../../../../integral-domain.md). Each of $(2),(3),(5)$ is a [prime ideal](../../../../../prime-ideal.md): for $p$ among these three, reduction $a/b\mapsto\overline a\,\overline b^{-1}$ is a surjective [ring homomorphism](../../../../../ring-homomorphism.md) $R\to\mathbb F_p$ with [kernel of a ring homomorphism](../../../../../kernel-of-a-ring-homomorphism.md) $(p)$, so its [quotient ring](../../../../../quotient-ring.md) is a [field](../../../../../field.md).

Any other nonzero proper [ideal](../../../../../ideal.md) in (c) has generator $d=2^\alpha3^\beta5^\gamma$ with total exponent at least two. Choose one [prime factor](../../../../../prime-factor.md) $p$ and put $e=d/p$. Then $pe=d\in(d)$ but neither $p$ nor $e$ belongs to $(d)$, as seen from the required [prime](../../../../../prime-number-split.md) exponents. Such an [ideal](../../../../../ideal.md) is not [prime](../../../../../prime-number-split.md). Thus with the standard proper-[ideal](../../../../../ideal.md) convention the answer is

$$
\boxed{(0),(2),(3),(5).}
$$

**The definition printed in this paper omits properness.** Literally under that definition $R$ itself also satisfies the implication, so its literal complete list is the preceding four [ideals](../../../../../ideal.md) together with $\boxed{R}$. Both interpretations are thereby accounted for.
