<h3 id="algebra/1">1</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [Algebra](#algebra)

<h4 id="algebra/1/a">a</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#algebra/1)

<h5 id="algebra/1/a/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#algebra/1/a)

Write $n=\dim V$. If $Rv=\lambda v$ with $v\ne0$, then $0=R^av=\lambda^av$, hence $\lambda=0$. The [characteristic polynomial](../linear-operator-theory.md#characteristic-polynomial) splits over $\mathbb C$ and has degree $n$, so

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{spec}(R)=\{0\},\qquad\chi_R(t)=t^n.}
$$

There is indeed an [eigenvector](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvector) for the [eigenvalue](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) zero: a nonzero [vector](../vector-space.md#vector) in the last nonzero [image](../set-theory.md#image-of-a-function) of $R$ lies in its [kernel of a linear map](../kernel-of-a-linear-map.md). For the bound, choose $v$ with $R^{a-1}v\ne0$, using minimality. The vectors $v,Rv,\ldots,R^{a-1}v$ are [linearly independent](../vector-space.md#linear-independence). Otherwise choose the least $j$ with nonzero coefficient in a relation and apply $R^{a-1-j}$; only its nonzero multiple of $R^{a-1}v$ survives, a contradiction. Therefore $\boxed{a\le n}$.

<h4 id="algebra/1/b">b</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#algebra/1)

<h5 id="algebra/1/b/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#algebra/1/b)

Suppose $S^b=0$. Use the increasing chain of [vector subspaces](../vector-space.md#vector-subspace)

$$
0=\ker S^0\subseteq\ker S\subseteq\ker S^2\subseteq\cdots\subseteq\ker S^b=V.
$$

Choose a [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) successively, extending a [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) of each [kernel of a linear map](../kernel-of-a-linear-map.md) to one of the next [kernel of a linear map](../kernel-of-a-linear-map.md). If a [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) [vector](../vector-space.md#vector) first enters at level $j$, its [image](../set-theory.md#image-of-a-function) under $S$ belongs to $\ker S^{j-1}$, spanned entirely by earlier [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) vectors. Thus every [matrix](../vector-space.md#matrix) column has nonzero entries only above its diagonal: the [matrix](../vector-space.md#matrix) is [strictly upper triangular](../linear-algebra.md#strictly-upper-triangular-matrix).

Conversely, for such a [matrix](../vector-space.md#matrix) and its ordered [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) $e_1,\ldots,e_n$, one has $S\langle e_1,\ldots,e_j\rangle\subseteq\langle e_1,\ldots,e_{j-1}\rangle$. Iterating $n$ times kills every [vector](../vector-space.md#vector). Hence $\boxed{S^n=0}$, establishing both directions without assuming a Jordan form.

<h4 id="algebra/1/c">c</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#algebra/1)

<h5 id="algebra/1/c/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#algebra/1/c)

The [polynomials](../polynomial.md) $t^c$ and $(t-1)^d$ are [coprime](../divisibility.md#coprime-integers). [Bézout's identity](../algebra.md#bezout-s-identity) gives [polynomials](../polynomial.md) $u,v$ with $u(t)t^c+v(t)(t-1)^d=1$. Set

$$
V_0=\ker T^c,\qquad V_1=\ker(T-I)^d.
$$

For every $x$, the decomposition

$$
x=v(T)(T-I)^dx+u(T)T^cx
$$

has first summand in $V_0$ and second in $V_1$, since $T^c(T-I)^d=0$ and [polynomial](../polynomial.md) expressions in $T$ commute. If $x$ belongs to both [kernels](../kernel-of-a-linear-map.md), the same Bézout identity gives $x=0$. Thus $V=V_0\oplus V_1$. Both [vector subspaces](../vector-space.md#vector-subspace) are [invariant](../mathematics.md#invariant-mathematics) under $T$.

On $V_0$, the restriction of $T$ is [nilpotent](../linear-operator-theory.md#nilpotent-linear-map); on $V_1$, the restriction of $T-I$ is [nilpotent](../linear-operator-theory.md#nilpotent-linear-map). Apply (b) separately, then concatenate the two [bases](../vector-space.md#basis). This yields

$$
\boxed{[T]=\begin{pmatrix}A&0\\0&I+N\end{pmatrix},}
$$

where $A,N$ are [strictly upper triangular](../linear-algebra.md#strictly-upper-triangular-matrix). Empty blocks are allowed if one subspace is zero. In particular $C=I+N$ has precisely the required diagonal.

↑ **Parent:** [Algebra](#algebra)

<h4 id="algebra/2/a">a</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#algebra/2)

<h5 id="algebra/2/a/i">i</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#algebra/2/a)

<h6 id="algebra/2/a/i/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#algebra/2/a/i)

Let $D=T^*T-TT^*$. This operator is [self-adjoint](../linear-operator-theory.md#self-adjoint-operator), and

$$
\langle Dx,x\rangle=\|Tx\|^2-\|T^*x\|^2.
$$

If condition (iii) holds, this [quadratic form](../quadratic-form.md) vanishes. For a real [inner product](../inner-product.md), [polarization](../quadratic-form.md#polarization-identity) gives

$$
2\langle Dx,y\rangle=\langle D(x+y),x+y\rangle-\langle Dx,x\rangle-\langle Dy,y\rangle=0.
$$

Therefore $D=0$, which is condition (i). This supplies the nontrivial reverse implication needed for the equivalence.

<h5 id="algebra/2/a/ii">ii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#algebra/2/a)

<h6 id="algebra/2/a/ii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#algebra/2/a/ii)

By the definition of the [adjoint](../hilbert-space.md#adjoint-operator),

$$
\langle Tx,Ty\rangle-\langle T^*x,T^*y\rangle
=\langle x,(T^*T-TT^*)y\rangle.
$$

If (i) holds, the expression is zero for all $x,y$, giving (ii). Conversely, if this expression vanishes for every $x$, nondegeneracy of the [inner product](../inner-product.md) gives $(T^*T-TT^*)y=0$ for every $y$, so (ii) also implies (i).

<h5 id="algebra/2/a/iii">iii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#algebra/2/a)

<h6 id="algebra/2/a/iii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#algebra/2/a/iii)

Taking $y=x$ in (ii) gives (iii). Together with (iii)$\Rightarrow$(i) from part (i) and (i)$\Rightarrow$(ii) from part (ii), this completes

$$
\boxed{\text{(i)}\iff\text{(ii)}\iff\text{(iii)}.}
$$

<h4 id="algebra/2/b">b</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#algebra/2)

<h5 id="algebra/2/b/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#algebra/2/b)

For a [normal operator](../hilbert-space.md#normal-operator), (a) gives $\|Tx\|=\|T^*x\|$, so one side is zero exactly when the other is. Hence $\boxed{\ker T=\ker T^*}$.

For real $\lambda$, the [adjoint](../hilbert-space.md#adjoint-operator) of $T-\lambda I$ is $T^*-\lambda I$. Expanding both products shows that their difference is $T^*T-TT^*=0$. Thus $T-\lambda I$ is normal, and applying the [kernel of a linear map](../kernel-of-a-linear-map.md) conclusion to it gives

$$
\boxed{\ker(T-\lambda I)=\ker(T^*-\lambda I)\quad(\lambda\in\mathbb R).}
$$

This proves equality of each real [eigenspace](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenspace), hence the same [eigenvalues](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) and [eigenvectors](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvector) on the stated real [vector space](../vector-space.md). If neither has real [eigenvalues](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue), the conclusion is vacuous. Upon complexification one instead pairs $\lambda$ with $\overline\lambda$ for the [adjoint](../hilbert-space.md#adjoint-operator); no claim of the same complex [eigenvalues](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) is needed here.

<h4 id="algebra/2/c">c</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#algebra/2)

<h5 id="algebra/2/c/i">i</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#algebra/2/c)

<h6 id="algebra/2/c/i/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#algebra/2/c/i)

In fact the [adjoint](../hilbert-space.md#adjoint-operator) invariance follows from the [orthogonal decomposition](../hilbert-space.md#orthogonal-decomposition-by-a-closed-subspace) and the invariance under $T$, without using the [normal operator](../hilbert-space.md#normal-operator) property. For $x\in V_i$ and $y\in V_j$ with $j\ne i$,

$$
\langle T^*x,y\rangle=\langle x,Ty\rangle=0,
$$

since $Ty\in V_j$. Hence $T^*x$ is [orthogonal](../inner-product.md#orthogonal-vectors) to every other summand and therefore lies in $V_i$. Moreover, for $x,y\in V_i$, the [adjoint](../hilbert-space.md#adjoint-operator) identity restricted to that subspace shows

$$
\boxed{T_i^*=T^*|_{V_i}.}
$$

<h5 id="algebra/2/c/ii">ii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#algebra/2/c)

<h6 id="algebra/2/c/ii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#algebra/2/c/ii)

Restricting $T^*T=TT^*$ to $V_i$, using (i), gives $T_i^*T_i=T_iT_i^*$, so the global [normal operator](../hilbert-space.md#normal-operator) property implies the [normal operator](../hilbert-space.md#normal-operator) property of every restriction. Conversely, if the restrictions are normal and the [adjoint](../hilbert-space.md#adjoint-operator) preserves each summand, then for $x=\sum_i x_i$,

$$
(T^*T-TT^*)x=\sum_i(T_i^*T_i-T_iT_i^*)x_i=0.
$$

Thus $\boxed{T\text{ is normal}\iff\text{every }T_i\text{ is normal}}$, with condition (i) automatic under the stated decomposition.

<h3 id="algebra/3">3</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [Algebra](#algebra)

<h4 id="algebra/3/a">a</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#algebra/3)

<h5 id="algebra/3/a/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#algebra/3/a)

Interpret membership as having a representation with denominator [coprime](../divisibility.md#coprime-integers) to $30$, equivalently having this property in lowest terms. Clearly $0,1\in R$. If $a/b,c/d\in R$ with admissible denominators, then

$$
\frac ab-\frac cd=\frac{ad-bc}{bd},\qquad\frac ab\frac cd=\frac{ac}{bd}.
$$

The product $bd$ is still [coprime](../divisibility.md#coprime-integers) to $30$. These are elements of $R$, proving that $\boxed{R\text{ is a subring of }\mathbb Q}$.

<h4 id="algebra/3/b">b</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#algebra/3)

<h5 id="algebra/3/b/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#algebra/3/b)

Take a reduced fraction $a/b\in R$. If $a\ne0$, its only possible inverse in $\mathbb Q$ is $b/a$. Since $\gcd(a,b)=1$, this is in lowest terms up to sign and belongs to $R$ exactly when $\gcd(a,30)=1$. Zero is not invertible. Therefore

$$
\boxed{R^\times=\{a/b:\gcd(a,b)=1,\ \gcd(ab,30)=1\}.}
$$

Equivalently, these are the [rational numbers](../number-theory.md#rational-number) whose [valuations](../algebra.md#valuation) at $2,3,5$ are all zero.

<h4 id="algebra/3/c">c</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#algebra/3)

<h5 id="algebra/3/c/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#algebra/3/c)

The intersection $J=I\cap\mathbb Z$ is an additive subgroup of $\mathbb Z$, and $nj\in I\cap\mathbb Z$ for $n\in\mathbb Z$, $j\in J$. Thus it is an [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) of $\mathbb Z$, necessarily $d\mathbb Z$ for a unique $d\ge0$.

Because $d\in I$, one has $dR\subseteq I$. Conversely, if $a/b\in I$ with $b$ [coprime](../divisibility.md#coprime-integers) to $30$, multiplication by $b\in R$ gives $a\in I\cap\mathbb Z=d\mathbb Z$. Write $a=dk$; then $a/b=d(k/b)\in dR$. Thus $I=dR$.

Every positive [integer](../integer.md) factors as $d=2^\alpha3^\beta5^\gamma u$ with $\gcd(u,30)=1$. The factor $u$ is a [unit](../algebra.md#unit-in-a-ring), so it does not change the [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal). The complete distinct list is

$$
\boxed{(0),\qquad (2^\alpha3^\beta5^\gamma)_R\quad(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\ge0).}
$$

They are distinct because the intersection of the displayed nonzero [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) with $\mathbb Z$ is $2^\alpha3^\beta5^\gamma\mathbb Z$: admissible denominators cannot cancel any of these [prime](../prime-number.md) powers. The triple $(0,0,0)$ gives $R$ itself.

<h4 id="algebra/3/d">d</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#algebra/3)

<h5 id="algebra/3/d/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#algebra/3/d)

The zero [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) is a [prime ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) because $R$ is an [integral domain](../commutative-algebra.md#integral-domain). Each of $(2),(3),(5)$ is a [prime ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal): for $p$ among these three, reduction $a/b\mapsto\overline a\,\overline b^{-1}$ is a surjective [ring homomorphism](../commutative-algebra.md#ring-homomorphism) $R\to\mathbb F_p$ with [kernel of a ring homomorphism](../commutative-algebra.md#kernel-of-a-ring-homomorphism) $(p)$, so its [quotient ring](../commutative-algebra.md#quotient-ring) is a [field](../algebra.md#field).

Any other nonzero proper [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) in (c) has generator $d=2^\alpha3^\beta5^\gamma$ with total exponent at least two. Choose one [prime factor](../prime-number.md#prime-factor) $p$ and put $e=d/p$. Then $pe=d\in(d)$ but neither $p$ nor $e$ belongs to $(d)$, as seen from the required [prime](../prime-number.md) exponents. Such an [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) is not [prime](../prime-number.md). Thus with the standard proper-[ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) convention the answer is

$$
\boxed{(0),(2),(3),(5).}
$$

**The definition printed in this paper omits properness.** Literally under that definition $R$ itself also satisfies the implication, so its literal complete list is the preceding four [ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) together with $\boxed{R}$. Both interpretations are thereby accounted for.

## Analysis

<h3 id="analysis/4">4</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [Analysis](#analysis)

<h4 id="analysis/4/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#analysis/4)

<h5 id="analysis/4/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#analysis/4/i)

Write $f=u+iv$ and $z=x+iy$. The [complex derivative](../holomorphic-function.md#complex-derivative) computed along a real increment is $f'=u_x+iv_x$, while along an imaginary increment it is $f'=v_y-iu_y$. Equality gives

$$
\boxed{u_x=v_y,\qquad u_y=-v_x.}
$$

These are the [Cauchy–Riemann equations](../analysis.md#cauchy-riemann-equations) at every point where $f$ is [holomorphic](../holomorphic-function.md). The directional calculations also establish the existence of those [partial derivatives](../multivariable-calculus.md#partial-derivative). More explicitly, $f(z+h)-f(z)=f'(z)h+o(|h|)$ gives both real-direction formulas directly. If a converse is desired, [continuous](../calculus.md#continuous-function) first [partial derivatives](../multivariable-calculus.md#partial-derivative) together with these equations make the real differential multiplication by $u_x+iv_x$, hence give [complex differentiability](../holomorphic-function.md#complex-differentiability); the necessity above is what is requested.

<h4 id="analysis/4/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#analysis/4)

<h5 id="analysis/4/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#analysis/4/ii)

The allowed theorem makes $f'$ [holomorphic](../holomorphic-function.md) and hence [continuous](../calculus.md#continuous-function); applying it repeatedly gives [continuous](../calculus.md#continuous-function) [derivatives](../calculus.md#derivative) of every order. Since $u_x=\Re f'$, $v_x=\Im f'$, $u_y=-\Im f'$ and $v_y=\Re f'$, the real and imaginary parts have [continuous](../calculus.md#continuous-function) second [partial derivatives](../multivariable-calculus.md#partial-derivative). Differentiate the [Cauchy–Riemann equations](../analysis.md#cauchy-riemann-equations) and commute mixed [derivatives](../calculus.md#derivative):

$$
u_{xx}+u_{yy}=(v_y)_x-(v_x)_y=0,
\qquad
v_{xx}+v_{yy}=-(u_y)_x+(u_x)_y=0.
$$

Thus $\boxed{\Delta u=\Delta v=0}$, with the required $C^2$ regularity, proving harmonicity.

<h4 id="analysis/4/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#analysis/4)

<h5 id="analysis/4/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#analysis/4/iii)

Write $f=u+iv$ and evaluate all [derivatives](../calculus.md#derivative) of $\psi$ at $(u,v)$. The real [chain rule](../calculus.md#chain-rule) gives

$$
\Delta(\psi\circ f)=\psi_{uu}(u_x^2+u_y^2)+2\psi_{uv}(u_xv_x+u_yv_y)+\psi_{vv}(v_x^2+v_y^2)+\psi_u\Delta u+\psi_v\Delta v.
$$

The [Cauchy–Riemann equations](../analysis.md#cauchy-riemann-equations) give equal squared lengths $u_x^2+u_y^2=v_x^2+v_y^2=|f'|^2$ and zero cross term $u_xv_x+u_yv_y=0$. Part (ii) removes the last two terms. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\Delta(\psi\circ f)=|f'|^2(\Delta\psi)\circ f=0.}
$$

The composition is $C^2$, so it is [harmonic](../partial-differential-equation.md#harmonic-function). No assumption that $f'$ is nonzero is required.

<h4 id="analysis/4/iv">iv</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#analysis/4)

<h5 id="analysis/4/iv/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#analysis/4/iv)

The [complex exponential function](../calculus.md#complex-exponential-function) $E(z)=e^z$ maps the strip bijectively and holomorphically to the [upper half-plane](../complex-analysis.md#upper-half-plane-complex-analysis): its modulus is $e^{\Re z}>0$ and its argument is $\Im z\in(0,\pi)$. Its inverse is the [holomorphic](../holomorphic-function.md) [logarithm](../calculus.md#logarithm) $L(w)=\log|w|+i\operatorname{Arg}w$ with $\operatorname{Arg}w\in(0,\pi)$.

Part (iii) applies to both maps. The required inverse correspondences are

$$
\boxed{h\in\mathcal H(\Omega_2)\longmapsto h\circ E\in\mathcal H(\Omega_1),\qquad k\longmapsto k\circ L.}
$$

They compose to the identity because $L\circ E$ and $E\circ L$ do. This proves injectivity and surjectivity, not merely a map between the two sets.

<h3 id="analysis/5">5</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [Analysis](#analysis)

<h4 id="analysis/5/a">a</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#analysis/5)

<h5 id="analysis/5/a/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#analysis/5/a)

For any $0<r<1$, a [Laurent series](../analysis.md#laurent-series) converges uniformly on the circle $|z|=r$, so termwise [contour integration](../complex-analysis.md#contour-integration) is valid. Multiplication by $z^{-k-1}$ and integration gives, for every [integer](../integer.md) $k$,

$$
\boxed{a_k=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{|z|=r}\frac{f(z)}{z^{k+1}}\,dz.}
$$

Indeed, the integral of $z^{j-k-1}$ is $2\pi i$ for $j=k$ and zero otherwise. These coefficients depend only on $f$, so any two Laurent expansions must agree coefficient by coefficient. This proves uniqueness, including all terms of the [principal part](../complex-geometry.md#principal-part-of-a-meromorphic-function).

<h4 id="analysis/5/b">b</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#analysis/5)

<h5 id="analysis/5/b/i">i</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#analysis/5/b)

<h6 id="analysis/5/b/i/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#analysis/5/b/i)

A [removable singularity](../isolated-singularity.md#removable-singularity) means that $f$ has a [holomorphic](../holomorphic-function.md) extension to the full disk, assigning a finite value at zero. Equivalently its [Laurent series](../analysis.md#laurent-series) has no negative-power terms. One direction follows by the [Taylor expansion](../calculus.md#taylor-expansion) of the extension; the other by the convergent nonnegative-power series. Thus $\boxed{a_k=0\text{ for all }k<0}$ is the coefficient criterion.

<h5 id="analysis/5/b/ii">ii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#analysis/5/b)

<h6 id="analysis/5/b/ii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#analysis/5/b/ii)

A [pole](../isolated-singularity.md#pole) of order $n$ means $f(z)=z^{-n}g(z)$ with $g$ [holomorphic](../holomorphic-function.md) near zero and $g(0)\ne0$. Equivalently, the [principal part](../complex-geometry.md#principal-part-of-a-meromorphic-function) is finite and starts with $\boxed{a_{-n}z^{-n},\ a_{-n}\ne0}$, while $a_k=0$ for $k<-n$. In particular $n$ is the exact order, not merely an upper bound.

<h5 id="analysis/5/b/iii">iii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#analysis/5/b)

<h6 id="analysis/5/b/iii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#analysis/5/b/iii)

An [essential singularity](../isolated-singularity.md#essential-singularity) is an [isolated singularity](../isolated-singularity.md) that is neither removable nor a [pole](../isolated-singularity.md#pole). In terms of the [Laurent series](../analysis.md#laurent-series) this means $\boxed{\text{infinitely many negative-power coefficients are nonzero}}$: no finite multiplication by a power of $z$ removes the singularity.

<h4 id="analysis/5/c">c</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#analysis/5)

<h5 id="analysis/5/c/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#analysis/5/c)

For $|z|<1$, differentiate the [geometric series](../sequence-and-series.md#geometric-series) to obtain $(1-t)^{-2}=\sum_{k\ge0}(k+1)t^k$. Since $(z-i)^2=-(1-z/i)^2$,

$$
\boxed{\frac1{z(z-i)^2}=-\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(k+1)(-i)^kz^{k-1}=-\frac1z+2i+3z-4iz^2-5z^3+\cdots.}
$$

The convergence region is precisely $0<|z|<1$, the specified punctured disk; the other [pole](../isolated-singularity.md#pole) is on its boundary at $i$.

<h4 id="analysis/5/d">d</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#analysis/5)

<h5 id="analysis/5/d/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#analysis/5/d)

If $f=z^{-n}g$ with $g(0)\ne0$, [continuity](../calculus.md#continuous-function) gives a small disk on which $|g(0)|/2\le|g(z)|\le3|g(0)|/2$. Multiplying by $|z|^{-n}$ gives the two required bounds.

Conversely, set $g(z)=z^nf(z)$ on the punctured disk. The upper bound makes $g$ bounded, so the allowed removable-singularity theorem gives a [holomorphic](../holomorphic-function.md) extension. The lower bound gives $|g(0)|\ge C_1>0$ by [continuity](../calculus.md#continuous-function). Therefore $f=z^{-n}g$ has a [pole](../isolated-singularity.md#pole) of exact order $n$. The lower bound is essential: the upper bound alone only proves order at most $n$.

<h3 id="analysis/6">6</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [Analysis](#analysis)

<h4 id="analysis/6/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#analysis/6)

<h5 id="analysis/6/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#analysis/6/i)

On $D=\mathbb C\setminus[0,\infty)$ choose the [continuous](../calculus.md#continuous-function) argument $0<\arg z<2\pi$, and put

$$
\boxed{z^w=\exp\bigl(w(\log|z|+i\arg z)\bigr).}
$$

This is [holomorphic](../holomorphic-function.md) on the slit [plane](../geometry-and-topology.md#plane) for every fixed $w$. The upper boundary value at $x>0$ is $x^w$, whereas the lower boundary value is $e^{2\pi iw}x^w$. This difference is exactly what the [keyhole contour](../complex-analysis.md#keyhole-contour) measures.

<h4 id="analysis/6/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#analysis/6)

<h5 id="analysis/6/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#analysis/6/ii)

Use the branch in (i) and the positively oriented slit-annulus contour. Its upper ray runs from $r$ to $R$, its outer circle counterclockwise, its lower ray from $R$ back to $r$, and its inner circle clockwise. Initially keep their arguments at $\alpha$ and $2\pi-\alpha$ as in the figure, with $0<\alpha<\pi/2$; let $\alpha\downarrow0$ for fixed $0<r<1<R$. Both poles $i,-i$ lie inside.

Write $\sigma=\Re w\in(-1,1)$. On the outer circle the absolute integral is $O(R^{\sigma-1})$, since $|z^w|\le e^{2\pi|\Im w|}R^\sigma$ and $|z^2+1|\ge R^2-1$. On the inner circle it is $O(r^{\sigma+1})$. Both tend to zero. The real integral converges absolutely because its endpoint behaviors are $x^\sigma$ and $x^{\sigma-2}$.

The two residues, with the chosen arguments, are

$$
\frac{e^{i\pi w/2}}{2i},\qquad-\frac{e^{3i\pi w/2}}{2i}.
$$

The [residue theorem](../analysis.md#residue-theorem) and the two ray orientations therefore give

$$
(1-e^{2\pi iw})I(w)=\pi e^{i\pi w/2}(1-e^{i\pi w}),\qquad I(w)=\int_0^\infty\frac{x^w}{1+x^2}\,dx.
$$

For $w\ne0$ in this strip we may cancel $1-e^{i\pi w}$; its only possible zero here is $w=0$. Consequently

$$
\boxed{I(w)=\frac{\pi e^{i\pi w/2}}{1+e^{i\pi w}}=\frac\pi{2\cos(\pi w/2)}.}
$$

At $w=0$ the same result follows directly from $I(0)=\int_0^\infty(1+x^2)^{-1}dx=\pi/2$, or by [dominated convergence](../measure-theory.md#dominated-convergence-theorem). The denominator has no zero in the open strip.

<h4 id="analysis/6/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#analysis/6)

<h5 id="analysis/6/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#analysis/6/iii)

Set $w=i$, which satisfies $\Re w=0$, and take [real parts](../complex-analysis.md#real-part) of the absolutely convergent integral. Since $x^i=\cos(\log x)+i\sin(\log x)$ and $\cos(i\pi/2)=\cosh(\pi/2)$,

$$
\boxed{\int_0^\infty\frac{\cos(\log x)}{1+x^2}\,dx=\frac\pi{2\cosh(\pi/2)}.}
$$

Absolute integrability justifies moving the real-part operation through the integral.

## Differential Equations

↑ **Parent:** [2010](2010.md)

<h3 id="differential-equations/7">7</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [Differential Equations](#differential-equations)

<h4 id="differential-equations/7/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [7](#differential-equations/7)

If $Au+Bv=0$, evaluation at zero gives $A=0$ and differentiation at zero gives $B=0$. Thus the solutions are [linearly independent](../vector-space.md#linear-independence). For $w=uv'-u'v$,

$$
w'=uv''-u''v=-p(uv'-u'v)=-pw,\qquad w(0)=1,
$$

so $\boxed{w(x)=\exp(-\int_0^xp(s)\,ds)>0}$.

[Variation of parameters](../linear-ordinary-differential-equation.md#variation-of-parameters) sets $y=A(x)u+B(x)v$ with $A'u+B'v=0$, $A'u'+B'v'=f$. Solving gives $A'=-vf/w$ and $B'=uf/w$. Zero integration constants enforce both [initial conditions](../differential-equation.md#initial-condition). Therefore

$$
\boxed{y(x)=\int_0^x\frac{v(x)u(t)-u(x)v(t)}{w(t)}f(t)\,dt,\qquad K(x,t)=\frac{v(x)u(t)-u(x)v(t)}{w(t)}.}
$$

For fixed $t$, this [kernel](../green-s-function.md) is a [linear combination](../vector-space.md#linear-combination) of homogeneous solutions, so $K_{xx}+pK_x+qK=0$. On the diagonal $K(t,t)=0$ and $K_x(t,t)=(v'(t)u(t)-u'(t)v(t))/w(t)=1$. Differentiating the integral twice now verifies the forced equation as well as $y(0)=y'(0)=0$. Standard uniqueness of the linear [initial-value problem](../differential-equation.md#initial-value-problem) makes this the solution, for [continuous](../calculus.md#continuous-function) $f$.

If $u,v$ have [period](../function.md#periodic-function) $T>0$, their [derivatives](../calculus.md#derivative) do also, hence $w$ is [periodic](../function.md#periodic-function). The [kernel](../green-s-function.md) is [periodic](../function.md#periodic-function) in its first variable. With $f=v$, split the integral at $x$ and use this periodicity to get

$$
y(x+T)-y(x)=-u(x)\int_x^{x+T}\frac{v(t)^2}{w(t)}\,dt+v(x)\int_x^{x+T}\frac{u(t)v(t)}{w(t)}\,dt.
$$

At $x=0$, the right side is $-\int_0^T v(t)^2/w(t)\,dt<0$, because $w>0$ and $v'(0)=1$ ensures $v$ is not identically zero. Thus $\boxed{y(T)\ne y(0)}$, so $y$ cannot have [period](../function.md#periodic-function) $T$. This direct sign argument also rules out any apparent cancellation between the two terms.

<h3 id="differential-equations/8">8</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [Differential Equations](#differential-equations)

<h4 id="differential-equations/8/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [8](#differential-equations/8)

Use a parameter $t$. The characteristic equations are

$$
\boxed{\dot x=x+cy-bz,\quad\dot y=y+az-cx,\quad\dot z=z+bx-ay.}
$$

For a [graph of a function](../function.md#graph-of-a-function) $z=f(x,y)$ satisfying the equation, solve the first two equations after substituting $z=f(x,y)$. The [chain rule](../calculus.md#chain-rule) and the [partial differential equation](../partial-differential-equation.md) give $d f(x(t),y(t))/dt=f+bx-ay$, exactly the third equation. Uniqueness for the resulting [linear ordinary differential equation](../linear-ordinary-differential-equation.md) system shows that its characteristic through the initial point agrees with this curve on the [graph of a function](../function.md#graph-of-a-function). This proves invariance as long as the [graph of a function](../function.md#graph-of-a-function) remains defined along the curve. A claim about the entire curve additionally requires a [surface](../topological-surface.md) continued along that flow; a restricted [graph of a function](../function.md#graph-of-a-function) patch alone cannot imply global continuation.

Let $\mathbf k=(a,b,c)$, $\boldsymbol\ell=(l,m,n)$ and $\mathbf r=(x,y,z)$. Then $\dot{\mathbf r}=\mathbf r-\mathbf k\times\mathbf r$. Since $|\mathbf k|=1$,

$$
\frac d{dt}(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r)=\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r,\qquad\frac d{dt}|\mathbf r|^2=2|\mathbf r|^2.
$$

Along the characteristic starting from $s\boldsymbol\ell$ these give $\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r=e^ts(\mathbf k\cdot\boldsymbol\ell)$ and $|\mathbf r|=e^t|s|$. Consequently the characteristic [surface](../topological-surface.md) generated by the given line satisfies

$$
\boxed{|ax+by+cz|=|al+bm+cn|\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}.}
$$

To see that the whole displayed [cone](../euclidean-geometry.md#cone-geometry) is generated, not just contained in it, decompose $\boldsymbol\ell=\kappa\mathbf k+\mathbf v$ with $\mathbf v\perp\mathbf k$, $\kappa=\mathbf k\cdot\boldsymbol\ell$. The full solution is

$$
\mathbf r(s,t)=se^t\bigl(\kappa\mathbf k+\cos t\,\mathbf v-\sin t\,(\mathbf k\times\mathbf v)\bigr).
$$

When $0<|\kappa|<1$, variation of $t$ gives all azimuths about the axis, while the freely chosen $s$ supplies every positive radius on both halves. This is precisely the two-sided circular [cone](../euclidean-geometry.md#cone-geometry), with

$$
\boxed{\text{vertex }(0,0,0),\quad\text{axis }\mathbb R(a,b,c),\quad\text{semi-angle }\arccos|al+bm+cn|.}
$$

The edge cases matter: $\kappa=0$ gives the [plane](../geometry-and-topology.md#plane) $ax+by+cz=0$ (semi-angle $\pi/2$), and $|\kappa|=1$ generates only the axis (semi-angle zero), not a two-dimensional [surface](../topological-surface.md). In the latter case the initial line is itself characteristic, so it does not determine a unique integral [surface](../topological-surface.md). The nondegenerate [cone](../euclidean-geometry.md#cone-geometry) is smooth away from its vertex and can be used as a [graph of a function](../function.md#graph-of-a-function) only on patches whose [tangent plane](../tangent-vector.md#tangent-plane) is not vertical. Thus the printed geometric conclusion has its ordinary characteristic-[surface](../topological-surface.md) meaning in the nondegenerate case, with these domain and degeneracy qualifications; it cannot be asserted for every global smooth [graph of a function](../function.md#graph-of-a-function) without them.

<h3 id="differential-equations/9">9</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [Differential Equations](#differential-equations)

<h4 id="differential-equations/9/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [9](#differential-equations/9)

For an azimuth-independent product $u=R(r)\Theta(\theta)$, multiply [Laplace's equation](../partial-differential-equation.md#laplace-equation) by $r^2/(R\Theta)$ where the factors are nonzero. The radial and angular expressions must equal opposite constants:

$$
\boxed{r^2R''+2rR'=\lambda R,\qquad(\sin\theta\,\Theta')'=-\lambda\sin\theta\,\Theta.}
$$

The resulting differential identities extend across isolated zeros by [continuity](../calculus.md#continuous-function). Put $\rho=\cos\theta$. Then $\Theta'=-\sin\theta P'$ and

$$
(\sin\theta\,\Theta')'=\sin\theta\frac d{d\rho}\bigl((1-\rho^2)P'\bigr).
$$

This gives $((1-\rho^2)P')'+\lambda P=0$ on $(-1,1)$.

A nonzero angular factor must be regular at both poles: it must be bounded as $\rho\to\pm1$ and extend smoothly as an axisymmetric function on the [sphere](../euclidean-geometry.md#sphere). Equivalently one may use the regular endpoint solution at each end; the logarithmic endpoint solutions are excluded. These two endpoint conditions select $\boxed{\lambda=n(n+1),\ n=0,1,2,\ldots}$ and the multiples of $P_n$. It is not sufficient to impose no condition at the endpoints, since local solutions exist for arbitrary $\lambda$. One way to see the quantization is the regular Legendre series about one endpoint: unless it terminates at $\lambda=n(n+1)$, continuation to the other endpoint has a logarithmic singularity.

Direct differentiation verifies

$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
P&\lambda&R(r)\\\hline
1&0&A+B/r\\
\rho&2&Ar+Br^{-2}\\
(3\rho^2-1)/2&6&Ar^2+Br^{-3}
\end{array}
$$

Indeed $R=r^m$ gives $m(m+1)=\lambda$, so the two powers for degree $n$ are $r^n$ and $r^{-n-1}$.

For the shell assume $a>1$. The outer datum decomposes as $\rho^2=\tfrac13P_0(\rho)+\tfrac23P_2(\rho)$. Normalize each [radial solution](../partial-differential-equation.md#radial-function) to vanish at $1$ and equal $1$ at $a$; [superposition](../vector-space.md#superposition-principle) gives

$$
\boxed{u(r,\theta)=\frac13\frac{1-r^{-1}}{1-a^{-1}}+\frac23\frac{r^2-r^{-3}}{a^2-a^{-3}}P_2(\cos\theta).}
$$

Every term is [harmonic](../partial-differential-equation.md#harmonic-function) in the shell and regular at the angular poles; substitution at each boundary gives precisely the two prescribed data. For uniqueness among functions [harmonic](../partial-differential-equation.md#harmonic-function) in the shell and [continuous](../calculus.md#continuous-function) on its closure, subtract any two solutions. Their difference is [harmonic](../partial-differential-equation.md#harmonic-function), zero on both boundary spheres, and by the [maximum principle](../partial-differential-equation.md#maximum-principle) is both at most zero and at least zero on the bounded shell. It vanishes identically. Thus **the classical [Dirichlet solution](../analysis.md#dirichlet-problem) is unique**. The usual [continuity](../calculus.md#continuous-function) at the boundary is the interpretation of the stated [boundary conditions](../differential-equation.md#boundary-condition); allowing singular boundary behavior would remove that hypothesis.
