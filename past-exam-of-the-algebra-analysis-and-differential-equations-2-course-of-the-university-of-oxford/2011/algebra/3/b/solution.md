<h1 id="algebra/3/b/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [B](../b.md)

The set $\sqrt I$ contains zero. If $x^m\in I$, then $(-x)^m=(-1)^mx^m\in I$, giving closure under additive inverses; also $(rx)^m=r^mx^m\in I$ for every $r\in R$.

To prove closure under addition, let $x^m,y^n\in I$. Expand $(x+y)^{m+n-1}$ by the [binomial theorem](../../../../../binomial-theorem.md). In every monomial $x^jy^{m+n-1-j}$, either $j\geq m$ or $m+n-1-j\geq n$. Thus each term contains a factor $x^m$ or $y^n$ and belongs to $I$. Hence $(x+y)^{m+n-1}\in I$, so $x+y\in\sqrt I$. The additive subgroup and absorption properties are proved, giving

$$
\boxed{\sqrt I\text{ is an ideal of }R.}
$$

This [ideal](../../../../../ideal.md) is the [radical of an ideal](../../../../../radical-of-an-ideal.md). The finite exponent for a sum uses commutativity of the ring.
