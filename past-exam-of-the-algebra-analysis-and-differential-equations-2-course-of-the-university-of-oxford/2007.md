## 1

↑ **Parent:** [2007](2007.md)

<h3 id="1/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

Write $V^*=V'$ for the [dual space](../dual-space.md). Define $\theta(v)(f)=f(v)$. For fixed $v$, this is a [linear functional](../dual-space.md#linear-functional) on $V^*$; evaluation is also [linear](../vector-space.md#linearity) in $v$, so $\theta:V\to V^{**}$ is a [linear map](../vector-space.md#linear-map). If $v\ne0$, extend $v$ to a [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) and take its first coordinate [linear functional](../dual-space.md#linear-functional) $f$; then $f(v)=1$. Thus $\theta$ is [injective](../algebra.md#injective-function). A [dual basis](../dual-space.md#dual-basis) has the same number of elements as the original [basis](../vector-space.md#basis), so $\dim V=\dim V^*=\dim V^{**}$. The [rank-nullity theorem](../kernel-of-a-linear-map.md#rank-nullity-theorem) now makes $\theta$ an [linear isomorphism](../vector-space.md#linear-isomorphism).

The [annihilator of a vector subspace](../dual-space.md#annihilator-of-a-vector-subspace) of a [linear subspace](../vector-space.md#vector-subspace) $U\le V$ is

$$
U^\circ=\{f\in V^*: f(u)=0\text{ for every }u\in U\}.
$$

Extend a [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) $v_1,\ldots,v_k$ of $U$ to a [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) $v_1,\ldots,v_n$ of $V$, with [dual basis](../dual-space.md#dual-basis) $f_1,\ldots,f_n$. A [linear functional](../dual-space.md#linear-functional) belongs to $U^\circ$ exactly when its first $k$ coordinates in this [dual basis](../dual-space.md#dual-basis) vanish. Hence $U^\circ$ has [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) $f_{k+1},\ldots,f_n$, proving

$$
\boxed{\dim U+\dim U^\circ=\dim V.}
$$

The [dual map](../dual-space.md#transpose-of-a-linear-map) is $\alpha^*(h)=h\circ\alpha$; here this is the algebraic [dual map](../dual-space.md#transpose-of-a-linear-map), denoted $\alpha'$ in the source. If $v\in\ker\alpha$, then $(\alpha'h)(v)=h(\alpha v)=0$. Consequently $\operatorname{im}\alpha'\subseteq(\ker\alpha)^\circ$. Moreover,

$$
\theta(v)\in(\operatorname{im}\alpha')^\circ
\iff h(\alpha v)=0\text{ for every }h\in W^*
\iff\alpha v=0,
$$

because coordinate [linear functionals](../dual-space.md#linear-functional) separate [vectors](../vector-space.md#vector). This proves $(\operatorname{im}\alpha')^\circ=\theta(\ker\alpha)$, including the requested second inclusion. Taking [dimensions](../vector-space.md#dimension-vector-space) gives

$$
\dim\operatorname{im}\alpha'=\dim V-\dim\ker\alpha=\dim\operatorname{im}\alpha.
$$

The first inclusion has equal [dimensions](../vector-space.md#dimension-vector-space) on both sides, so

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{im}\alpha'=(\ker\alpha)^\circ,\qquad
(\operatorname{im}\alpha')^\circ=\theta(\ker\alpha).}
$$

For a [matrix](../vector-space.md#matrix) $A=(a_{ij})$ representing $\alpha$, the matrix of $\alpha'$ in the corresponding [dual bases](../dual-space.md#dual-basis) is $A^{\mathsf T}$: its $ji$ entry is $h_i(\alpha v_j)=a_{ij}$. Thus the [column rank](../vector-space.md#column-rank) of $A^{\mathsf T}$ is the [row rank](../vector-space.md#row-rank) of $A$, while the [column rank](../vector-space.md#column-rank) of $A$ is $\dim\operatorname{im}\alpha$. Applying the established equality to these two [linear maps](../vector-space.md#linear-map) proves **row rank equals column rank** over every [field](../algebra.md#field).

<h3 id="2/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

The [adjoint on an inner product space](../hilbert-space.md#adjoint-on-an-inner-product-space) $\alpha^*:V\to V$ is characterized by

$$
\langle\alpha v,w\rangle=\langle v,\alpha^*w\rangle\qquad(v,w\in V).
$$

Here is a finite-dimensional construction that supplies every required [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) fact. If $v_1,\ldots,v_n$ is any [basis](../vector-space.md#basis), its coordinate [linear functionals](../dual-space.md#linear-functional) form a [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) of $V^*$: a [linear functional](../dual-space.md#linear-functional) is uniquely specified by its values on these $n$ [vectors](../vector-space.md#vector). Hence $\dim V^*=n$. The [linear map](../vector-space.md#linear-map)

$$
J:V\to V^*,\qquad J(w)(v)=\langle v,w\rangle
$$

is [injective](../algebra.md#injective-function), because $J(w)=0$ implies $\langle w,w\rangle=0$, and [positive definiteness](../bilinear-form.md#positive-definiteness) implies $w=0$. Equal [dimensions](../vector-space.md#dimension-vector-space) make $J$ an [linear isomorphism](../vector-space.md#linear-isomorphism). If $\alpha'$ is the [dual map](../dual-space.md#transpose-of-a-linear-map), then

$$
\alpha^*=J^{-1}\alpha'J.
$$

This establishes existence and [linearity](../vector-space.md#linearity). If two possible [vectors](../vector-space.md#vector) $u,w$ represent the same [linear functional](../dual-space.md#linear-functional) through the [inner product](../inner-product.md), then $J(u-w)=0$, so $u=w$. Applied to every argument, this proves uniqueness of the [adjoint on an inner product space](../hilbert-space.md#adjoint-on-an-inner-product-space).

For [linear maps](../vector-space.md#linear-map) $S,T$, two applications of the defining [inner product](../inner-product.md) identity give

$$
\langle STv,w\rangle=\langle Tv,S^*w\rangle=\langle v,T^*S^*w\rangle.
$$

Uniqueness therefore gives $(ST)^*=T^*S^*$. Likewise $(S+T)^*=S^*+T^*$, $(cT)^*=cT^*$ for real $c$, and $I^*=I$. An [induction](../foundations-of-mathematics.md#mathematical-induction) gives $(\alpha^n)^*=(\alpha^*)^n$, and applying additivity to a [polynomial](../polynomial.md) with real coefficients gives

$$
\boxed{f(\alpha)^*=f(\alpha^*).}
$$

Symmetry of the real [inner product](../inner-product.md) also gives $(\alpha^*)^*=\alpha$. If $m$ is the [minimal polynomial](../linear-operator-theory.md#minimal-polynomial) of $\alpha$, the displayed identity gives $m(\alpha^*)=0$, so the [minimal polynomial](../linear-operator-theory.md#minimal-polynomial) of $\alpha^*$ divides $m$. Applying the same argument to $\alpha^*$ gives reverse divisibility. Both [polynomials](../polynomial.md) are [monic](../polynomial.md#monic-polynomial), hence **their minimal polynomials are equal**.

The distinct [eigenvalues](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) are exactly the roots of the [minimal polynomial](../linear-operator-theory.md#minimal-polynomial) in the chosen [field](../algebra.md#field). An [eigenvector](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvector) $v$ with [eigenvalue](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) $\lambda$ satisfies $m(\alpha)v=m(\lambda)v$, so $m(\lambda)=0$. Conversely, if $m(X)=(X-\lambda)q(X)$ and $\alpha-\lambda I$ were a [linear isomorphism](../vector-space.md#linear-isomorphism), then $m(\alpha)=0$ would imply $q(\alpha)=0$, contradicting minimal degree. Thus $\alpha-\lambda I$ is not [injective](../algebra.md#injective-function), giving an [eigenvector](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvector). It follows that $\alpha$ and $\alpha^*$ have **the same distinct eigenvalues**. This includes the complex [eigenvalues](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) after [complexification of a real vector space](../vector-space.md#complexification-of-a-real-vector-space); over the real [field](../algebra.md#field) it states equality of their real [eigenvalues](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue).

A [maximal ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#maximal-ideal) is a proper [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) $I$ of $R$ such that an [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) $J$ with $I\subseteq J\subseteq R$ is either $I$ or $R$. Suppose first that $I$ is a [maximal ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#maximal-ideal). If $a\notin I$, then $I+(a)=R$, so $1=i+ra$ for some $i\in I$ and $r\in R$. In the [quotient ring](../commutative-algebra.md#quotient-ring), $(r+I)(a+I)=1+I$. Every nonzero element of $R/I$ therefore has a [multiplicative inverse](../arithmetic.md#multiplicative-inverse), so $R/I$ is a [field](../algebra.md#field). Conversely, if $R/I$ is a [field](../algebra.md#field) and $J$ properly contains $I$, choose $a\in J\setminus I$. The element $a+I$ has a [multiplicative inverse](../arithmetic.md#multiplicative-inverse) $r+I$, whence $1-ra\in I\subset J$, giving $1\in J$ and $J=R$. Thus

$$
\boxed{I\text{ is maximal}\iff R/I\text{ is a field}.}
$$

The squares in $\mathbb F_7$ are $0,1,2,4$, which do not include $-1=6$. Hence $X^2+1$ has no root in $\mathbb F_7$. A [polynomial](../polynomial.md) of degree two or three over a [field](../algebra.md#field) is an [irreducible polynomial](../polynomial.md#irreducible-polynomial) exactly when it has no root: any proper [polynomial factorization](../polynomial.md#factorization-of-polynomials) of such a degree has a factor of degree one. The [polynomial ring](../commutative-algebra.md#polynomial-ring) over a [field](../algebra.md#field) is a [Euclidean domain](../commutative-algebra.md#euclidean-domain), so the [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) generated by an [irreducible polynomial](../polynomial.md#irreducible-polynomial) is a [maximal ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#maximal-ideal). It follows that

$$
F=\mathbb F_7[X]/(X^2+1)
$$

is a [field](../algebra.md#field). Every [coset](../group-theory.md#coset) has a unique representative $a+bX$, by [polynomial division](../polynomial.md#polynomial-division), so $|F|=7^2=49$.

Let $j$ be the [coset](../group-theory.md#coset) of $X$ in this [field](../algebra.md#field). Since $j^2=-1$, the map

$$
\phi:\mathbb Z[i]\to F,\qquad a+bi\longmapsto\overline a+\overline b\,j
$$

is a [ring homomorphism](../commutative-algebra.md#ring-homomorphism). It is [surjective](../algebra.md#surjective-function) by the representative description. Since $1,j$ are [linearly independent](../vector-space.md#linear-independence) over $\mathbb F_7$, its [kernel of a ring homomorphism](../commutative-algebra.md#kernel-of-a-ring-homomorphism) is

$$
\boxed{\ker\phi=7\mathbb Z[i]=(7).}
$$

There is also a [field](../algebra.md#field) with $343$ elements. The cubes in $\mathbb F_7$ are $0,1,-1$, so $X^3-2$ has no root and is an [irreducible polynomial](../polynomial.md#irreducible-polynomial). Therefore

$$
\boxed{\mathbb F_7[X]/(X^3-2)}
$$

is a [field](../algebra.md#field) whose unique representatives have degree below three. There are $7^3=343$ such representatives.

<h3 id="4/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#4)

Use the following boundary version of [Cauchy's integral theorem](../complex-analysis.md#cauchy-s-integral-theorem): if a function is [holomorphic](../holomorphic-function.md) on a neighborhood of a compact region bounded by finitely many piecewise smooth simple closed curves, its integral around the positively oriented boundary is zero. On an [annulus](../topology.md#annulus-mathematics) this means that its integrals around the two circles, both taken counterclockwise, are equal.

For $0<\rho<r$, the function $f(z)/(z-w)$ is [holomorphic](../holomorphic-function.md) on a neighborhood of $\rho\le|z-w|\le r$. The theorem therefore gives

$$
\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{|z-w|=r}\frac{f(z)}{z-w}\,dz
=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}f(w+\rho e^{it})\,dt.
$$

As $\rho\downarrow0$, continuity of $f$ makes the integrand converge uniformly to $f(w)$. The right side tends to $f(w)$, proving [Cauchy integral formula](../analysis.md#cauchy-integral-formula) without assuming that formula in an intermediate step.

Let $I$ denote the requested boundary integral. Parametrize the [unit circle](../complex-analysis.md#complex-unit-circle) by $z=e^{it}$. Since $dz=ie^{it}dt$,

$$
I=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}
\frac{\overline{f(e^{it})}}{1-ae^{-it}}\,dt.
$$

Taking [complex conjugates](../complex-analysis.md#complex-conjugate) and using $\overline{e^{it}}=e^{-it}$ gives

$$
\overline I=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}
\frac{f(e^{it})}{1-\overline a e^{it}}\,dt
=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{|z|=1}\frac{f(z)}{z(1-\overline a z)}\,dz.
$$

This is the [conjugated Cauchy integral on a circle](../analysis.md#conjugated-cauchy-integral-on-a-circle) identity. The function $h(z)=f(z)/(1-\overline a z)$ is [holomorphic](../holomorphic-function.md) on a neighborhood of the closed [unit disc](../topology.md#unit-disc), because $|a|<1$. Applying [Cauchy integral formula](../analysis.md#cauchy-integral-formula) at zero gives $\overline I=h(0)=f(0)$. Thus

$$
\boxed{I=\overline{f(0)}.}
$$

The [complex conjugates](../complex-analysis.md#complex-conjugate) in these formulas are essential; the boundary integrand contains $\overline{f(z)}$.

<h3 id="5/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#5)

For a [holomorphic function](../holomorphic-function.md) on $|z-a|<R$, the complex [Taylor theorem](../calculus.md#taylor-s-theorem) states

$$
f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}(z-a)^n\qquad(|z-a|<R),
$$

with uniform convergence on each smaller closed [disc](../topology.md#topological-disc). Here is a proof. Choose $r<\rho<R$. For $|z-a|\le r$ and $|\zeta-a|=\rho$, the [geometric series](../sequence-and-series.md#geometric-series)

$$
\frac1{\zeta-z}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(z-a)^n}{(\zeta-a)^{n+1}}
$$

converges uniformly. Substitute it into [Cauchy integral formula](../analysis.md#cauchy-integral-formula) and integrate term by term to get

$$
f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_n(z-a)^n,\qquad
c_n=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{|\zeta-a|=\rho}\frac{f(\zeta)}{(\zeta-a)^{n+1}}\,d\zeta.
$$

If $M_\rho=\max_{|\zeta-a|=\rho}|f(\zeta)|$, then $|c_n|\le M_\rho\rho^{-n}$. These bounds justify differentiating the [power series](../sequence-and-series.md#power-series) any fixed number of times on a smaller closed [disc](../topology.md#topological-disc), by comparison with convergent differentiated [geometric series](../sequence-and-series.md#geometric-series). Evaluation at $a$ gives $f^{(n)}(a)=n!c_n$. Since every $|z-a|<R$ belongs to some such smaller [disc](../topology.md#topological-disc), the formula holds throughout the stated [domain](../topology.md#domain-mathematical-analysis).

The [identity theorem](../complex-analysis.md#identity-theorem) says that two [holomorphic functions](../holomorphic-function.md) on a connected open subset of $\mathbb C$ are equal everywhere if their equality set has an accumulation point in that subset. Equivalently, a [holomorphic function](../holomorphic-function.md) with an interior accumulation point of zeros is identically zero there.

The proposed [Taylor coefficients](../calculus.md#taylor-coefficient) at $i$, including the specified value for $n=0$, give

$$
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^n(z-i)^n}{i^{n+1}}
=\frac{1/i}{1+(z-i)/i}=\frac1z\qquad(|z-i|<1).
$$

The function $1/z$ is [holomorphic](../holomorphic-function.md) on $\mathbb C\setminus\{0\}$ and has exactly the given [derivatives](../calculus.md#derivative), so it supplies existence. Any other admissible [holomorphic function](../holomorphic-function.md) has the same [Taylor series](../calculus.md#taylor-series) on $|z-i|<1$. The punctured plane is connected, so the [identity theorem](../complex-analysis.md#identity-theorem) gives uniqueness:

$$
\boxed{f(z)=\frac1z\quad(z\ne0).}
$$

On the [unit circle](../complex-analysis.md#complex-unit-circle), put $z=e^{i\theta}$. Then $d\theta=dz/(iz)$ and $\cos\theta=(z+z^{-1})/2$, so

$$
\frac{d\theta}{(2-\cos\theta)^2}
=\frac4i\frac{z\,dz}{(z^2-4z+1)^2}.
$$

Write $r=2-\sqrt3$ and $s=2+\sqrt3$. Only $r$ lies inside the [unit circle](../complex-analysis.md#complex-unit-circle); the [rational function](../isolated-singularity.md#rational-function) $h(z)=z/[(z-r)^2(z-s)^2]$ has a double [pole](../isolated-singularity.md#pole) there. Its [residue](../analysis.md#residue) is

$$
\operatorname{Res}_{z=r}h
=\left.\frac{d}{dz}\frac{z}{(z-s)^2}\right|_{z=r}
=\frac1{(r-s)^2}-\frac{2r}{(r-s)^3}
=-\frac{r+s}{(r-s)^3}=\frac1{6\sqrt3}.
$$

By the [residue theorem](../analysis.md#residue-theorem),

$$
\boxed{\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{d\theta}{(2-\cos\theta)^2}
=\frac4i\,2\pi i\frac1{6\sqrt3}=\frac{4\pi}{3\sqrt3}.}
$$

<h4 id="6/ii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#6/ii)

Choose the branch $\operatorname{Log}z=\log|z|+i\arg z$ with $-\pi/2<\arg z<3\pi/2$. It is [holomorphic](../holomorphic-function.md) off the downward imaginary ray and takes values $\log x$ on the positive real axis and $\log x+i\pi$ at $-x$ for $x>0$.

Take $R>2$ and $0<\varepsilon<2$. The positively oriented [contour](../complex-analysis.md#complex-integration-contour) consists of the real segments $[-R,-\varepsilon]$ and $[\varepsilon,R]$, the small upper semicircle from $-\varepsilon$ to $\varepsilon$ traversed clockwise, and the large upper semicircle from $R$ to $-R$ traversed counterclockwise. For $F(z)=\operatorname{Log}z/(4+z^2)$, the only enclosed [pole](../isolated-singularity.md#pole) is $2i$, with

$$
\operatorname{Res}_{z=2i}F=\frac{\log2+i\pi/2}{4i}.
$$

The integral over the large arc is $O(\log R/R)$, since its length is $\pi R$ and $|4+z^2|\ge R^2-4$. The small arc contributes $O(\varepsilon|\log\varepsilon|)$, since its length is $\pi\varepsilon$ and $|4+z^2|\ge4-\varepsilon^2$. Both tend to zero.

Let $J=\int_0^\infty\log x/(4+x^2)\,dx$; it converges because $|\log x|$ is integrable near zero and $\log x/x^2$ is integrable at infinity. The two real segments, after substituting $z=-x$ on the negative one, tend to

$$
2J+i\pi\int_0^\infty\frac{dx}{4+x^2}=2J+\frac{i\pi^2}{4}.
$$

The [residue theorem](../analysis.md#residue-theorem) therefore gives

$$
2J+\frac{i\pi^2}{4}
=2\pi i\frac{\log2+i\pi/2}{4i}
=\frac\pi2\log2+\frac{i\pi^2}{4}.
$$

Taking real parts yields

$$
\boxed{J=\frac\pi4\log2.}
$$

↑ **Parent:** [2007](2007.md)

<h3 id="7/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [7](#7)

Use [Picard iteration](../differential-equation.md#picard-iteration) on $[a-h,a+h]$:

$$
u_0(x)=b,\qquad u_{n+1}(x)=b+\int_a^x f(t,u_n(t))\,dt.
$$

By [induction](../foundations-of-mathematics.md#mathematical-induction), every iterate stays in the prescribed rectangle: if $u_n$ does, then $|u_{n+1}(x)-b|\le M|x-a|\le Mh\le k$. Continuity makes each next integral well defined. The [Lipschitz condition](../real-analysis.md#lipschitz-continuity) gives, for $n\ge0$,

$$
|u_{n+1}(x)-u_n(x)|\le\frac{MA^n|x-a|^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}.
$$

For $n=0$ this is the previous bound; integration of the bound multiplied by $A$ proves the next step, on either side of $a$.

The sum of these bounds converges uniformly on the entire interval. Thus $u_n$ converges uniformly to a continuous $u$, whose graph remains in the closed rectangle. The [Lipschitz condition](../real-analysis.md#lipschitz-continuity) also makes $f(t,u_n(t))$ converge uniformly to $f(t,u(t))$, so passage to the limit gives

$$
u(x)=b+\int_a^x f(t,u(t))\,dt.
$$

The [fundamental theorem of calculus](../calculus.md#fundamental-theorem-of-calculus) makes $u$ a continuously differentiable solution with $u(a)=b$.

For uniqueness, let $u,v$ be two solutions and put $D_0=\sup|u-v|$ on the interval. Repeated use of the [Lipschitz condition](../real-analysis.md#lipschitz-continuity) in their integral equations gives

$$
|u(x)-v(x)|\le D_0\frac{(A|x-a|)^n}{n!}
$$

for every $n$. Letting $n\to\infty$ gives $u=v$. The global bound $M$ prevents any competing solution whose graph lies in $D$ from exiting the rectangle through a horizontal boundary before the end of the interval. Therefore **a unique solution exists on the full stated interval**. No extra restriction $Ah<1$ is needed; the factorial estimates replace that restriction.

<h4 id="7/i/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#7/i)

At zero the right side is continuous but fails every local [Lipschitz condition](../real-analysis.md#lipschitz-continuity) in its second variable, since $\delta^{1/4}/\delta=\delta^{-3/4}\to\infty$. For every $c\ge0$, the function

$$
u_c(x)=\begin{cases}0,&x\le c,\\{}[3(x-c)/4]^{4/3},&x\ge c\end{cases}
$$

is continuously differentiable and satisfies the [differential equation](../differential-equation.md), including at the joining point where both one-sided [derivatives](../calculus.md#derivative) are zero. Each has $u_c(0)=0$. The identically zero function is another possibility. Thus **solutions exist but are not unique**, even locally: choosing $c=0$ and taking the zero function already gives two different solutions on every neighborhood of zero. These are instances of [waiting-time solutions of a sublinear Bernoulli equation](../differential-equation.md#waiting-time-solutions-of-a-sublinear-bernoulli-equation).

<h3 id="7/ii">ii</h3>

<h4 id="7/ii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#7/ii)

Near $y=1$, the right side is continuously differentiable in $y$, with [derivative](../calculus.md#derivative) $1/(4y^{3/4})$ bounded on, for example, $1/2\le y\le3/2$. It therefore satisfies a local [Lipschitz condition](../real-analysis.md#lipschitz-continuity); it is also bounded on an appropriate bounded neighborhood. The existence and uniqueness result applies there. [Separation of variables](../partial-differential-equation.md#separation-of-variables) gives

$$
u^{3/4}=1+\frac{3x}{4},\qquad
\boxed{u(x)=\left(1+\frac{3x}{4}\right)^{4/3}\quad(x>-4/3).}
$$

The unique extension to the whole real axis is $u(x)=[\max\{1+3x/4,0\}]^{4/3}$. It joins zero with zero [derivative](../calculus.md#derivative) at $x=-4/3$. To see uniqueness of this extension, $u'\ge0$ makes every solution nondecreasing. Before it reaches zero it cannot be positive, while a negative interval would have $u'=0$ and could not continuously reach zero. Thus it must be zero for $x\le-4/3$; the positive part is fixed by the separated formula.

<h3 id="7/iii">iii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [7](#7)

<h4 id="7/iii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#7/iii)

Translation in $x$ leaves this autonomous [differential equation](../differential-equation.md) unchanged. For every $c\ge1$, the same formula

$$
u_c(x)=\begin{cases}0,&x\le c,\\{}[3(x-c)/4]^{4/3},&x\ge c\end{cases}
$$

satisfies $u_c(1)=0$, and the zero function does too. Thus **solutions exist and are not unique**, exactly as for the initial point $(0,0)$. The relevant failure is the [Lipschitz condition](../real-analysis.md#lipschitz-continuity) at $y=0$; moving the initial value of $x$ does not remove it.

## 8

↑ **Parent:** [2007](2007.md)

<h3 id="8/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [8](#8)

First consider the usual local reduction with sufficiently smooth coefficients. The [hyperbolic partial differential equation](../partial-differential-equation.md#hyperbolic-partial-differential-equation) has two distinct characteristic directions because $S^2-RT>0$. Choose independent local [characteristic coordinates](../partial-differential-equation.md#characteristic-coordinate) $\xi,\eta$ whose gradients satisfy

$$
R\xi_x^2+2S\xi_x\xi_y+T\xi_y^2=0,\qquad
R\eta_x^2+2S\eta_x\eta_y+T\eta_y^2=0.
$$

Let $J=\xi_x\eta_y-\xi_y\eta_x\ne0$ and

$$
B=R\xi_x\eta_x+S(\xi_x\eta_y+\xi_y\eta_x)+T\xi_y\eta_y.
$$

The determinant of the transformed principal coefficient matrix gives $B^2=J^2(S^2-RT)>0$. By the [chain rule](../calculus.md#chain-rule), the left side of the original [partial differential equation](../partial-differential-equation.md) becomes

$$
2B\,u_{\xi\eta}+A_\xi u_\xi+A_\eta u_\eta,
\qquad A_\xi=R\xi_{xx}+2S\xi_{xy}+T\xi_{yy},
$$

with the analogous expression for $A_\eta$. The original first [derivatives](../calculus.md#derivative) are $z_x=u_\xi\xi_x+u_\eta\eta_x$ and $z_y=u_\xi\xi_y+u_\eta\eta_y$. Substitution and division by $2B$ yield the requested mixed-derivative canonical form. The construction is local, in a neighborhood where the two characteristic foliations give a [diffeomorphism](../geometry-and-topology.md#diffeomorphism).

There is a regularity qualification in the printed hypotheses: merely continuous coefficients need not give twice continuously differentiable [characteristic coordinates](../partial-differential-equation.md#characteristic-coordinate). Such coordinates are needed for the second-derivative [chain rule](../calculus.md#chain-rule) above. For example, take $R=0$, $S=1/2$ and $T=|x|$ near the origin. The characteristic equation factors as $\xi_y(\xi_x+|x|\xi_y)=0$. One family is $x=\text{constant}$ and the other has first integrals $\eta=h(y-x|x|/2)$. For an independent coordinate $h'\ne0$, but $\eta_{xx}$ has opposite one-sided limits at $x=0$. Hence no twice continuously differentiable characteristic coordinate system exists there. This illustrates that [continuous hyperbolic coefficients need not admit smooth characteristic coordinates](../partial-differential-equation.md#continuous-hyperbolic-coefficients-need-not-admit-smooth-characteristic-coordinates). The formal general argument requires additional smoothness, for example twice continuously differentiable coefficients. The concrete coefficients below are smooth, so this issue does not affect their solution.

For the concrete [partial differential equation](../partial-differential-equation.md), put

$$
\xi=\frac{x}{y^2},\qquad\eta=y\quad(y>0).
$$

The [Jacobian determinant](../multivariable-calculus.md#jacobian-determinant) is $1/y^2\ne0$. Direct use of the [chain rule](../calculus.md#chain-rule) gives

$$
z_{xx}=\frac{u_{\xi\xi}}{y^4},\qquad
z_{xy}=-\frac{2\xi u_{\xi\xi}}{y^3}+\frac{u_{\xi\eta}}{y^2}-\frac{2u_\xi}{y^3}.
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{u_{\xi\eta}=\frac2\eta u_\xi.}
$$

To impose the data efficiently, let $v=z_x$. Then $2xv_x+yv_y=0$, whose [characteristic curves](../transport-equation.md#characteristic-curve) preserve $x/y^2$, so $v=H(x/y^2)$. On the initial line $x=1$, the value $v(1,y)=y^2$ gives $H(\xi)=1/\xi$ for every $\xi>0$. Thus $z_x=y^2/x$, and integration in $x$ gives $z=y^2\log x+C(y)$. The remaining initial condition gives $C(y)=0$:

$$
\boxed{z(x,y)=y^2\log x,\qquad x>0,\ y>0.}
$$

Every point in this region is reached from the data by a characteristic with positive $x/y^2$. The line $x=0$ separates these characteristics from those with $x<0$, and the logarithmic divergence prevents a smooth extension of this solution through $x=0$. The original condition restricts us to $y>0$.

## 9

↑ **Parent:** [2007](2007.md)

<h3 id="9/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [9](#9)

A sufficient condition for existence of the [Laplace transform](../integral-transform.md#laplace-transform) is that $y$ be piecewise continuous on each bounded interval and have [exponential order](../integral-transform.md#exponential-order): $|y(t)|\le Ce^{at}$ for sufficiently large $t$. The defining integral then converges absolutely for real $p>a$, and more generally defines a [holomorphic function](../holomorphic-function.md) for complex $p$ with $\operatorname{Re}p>a$. The boundary $p=a$ may or may not be included, depending on $y$.

Write $Y(p)=\mathcal L[y](p)$. To solve the [differential equation](../differential-equation.md) by [Laplace transform](../integral-transform.md#laplace-transform), initially seek a sufficiently regular solution with $y(0)=1$ and finite $y'(0)=b$. The [Laplace transform of a derivative](../integral-transform.md#laplace-transform-of-a-derivative) gives

$$
\mathcal L[y']=pY-1,\qquad \mathcal L[y'']=p^2Y-p-b.
$$

Using $\mathcal L[ty]=-Y'$ from part (i), the transformed [differential equation](../differential-equation.md) becomes

$$
-(2pY+p^2Y'-1)-Y-pY'-(pY-1)-Y=0,
$$

or

$$
p(p+1)Y'+(3p+2)Y=2.
$$

After division by $p(p+1)$, an [integrating factor](../linear-ordinary-differential-equation.md#integrating-factor) is $p^2(p+1)$, since $(3p+2)/(p(p+1))=2/p+1/(p+1)$. Consequently

$$
\frac d{dp}\bigl[p^2(p+1)Y\bigr]=2p,\qquad
Y=\frac1{p+1}+\frac{C}{p^2(p+1)}.
$$

For a bounded $y$, $|Y(p)|\le\|y\|_\infty/p$ for $p>0$. Letting $p\downarrow0$ forces $C=0$, an instance of how [boundedness selects a Laplace transform at a singular endpoint](../integral-transform.md#boundedness-selects-a-laplace-transform-at-a-singular-endpoint). By the exponential formula in part (iii), this yields

$$
\boxed{y(t)=e^{-t}.}
$$

Direct substitution checks the [differential equation](../differential-equation.md), boundedness and the required limit.

To complete the uniqueness argument, no unproved endpoint regularity is needed. The two functions $e^{-t}$ and $t-1$ solve the homogeneous [linear differential equation](../differential-equation.md#linear-differential-equation) on $(0,\infty)$, and their [Wronskian](../linear-ordinary-differential-equation.md#wronskian) is $te^{-t}\ne0$. They form a [fundamental system of solutions](../linear-ordinary-differential-equation.md#fundamental-system-of-solutions) on that interval, so every classical solution is $Ae^{-t}+B(t-1)$. Boundedness forces $B=0$ and the limit at zero forces $A=1$. Thus **the bounded solution with the specified limit is unique**. The limit alone would not suffice: $e^{-t}+C(t-1+e^{-t})$ has the same limit for every $C$, but is unbounded when $C\ne0$.

<h3 id="9/i">i</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [9](#9)

<h4 id="9/i/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#9/i)

For $\operatorname{Re}p>a$, differentiation under the integral is justified by the [dominated convergence theorem](../measure-theory.md#dominated-convergence-theorem): on a smaller closed half-plane $\operatorname{Re}p\ge a+\delta$, the additional factor $t$ is controlled by $te^{-\delta t}$. Hence

$$
Y'(p)=\int_0^\infty -ty(t)e^{-pt}\,dt,
\qquad\boxed{\mathcal L[ty](p)=-Y'(p).}
$$

This is the multiplication rule for the [Laplace transform](../integral-transform.md#laplace-transform).

<h3 id="9/ii">ii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [9](#9)

<h4 id="9/ii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#9/ii)

For $\operatorname{Re}p>0$, the starting integral is $\int_0^\infty e^{-pt}\,dt=1/p$. Repeated [integration by parts](../calculus.md#integration-by-parts), with vanishing boundary terms, gives

$$
\mathcal L[t^n](p)=\frac np\mathcal L[t^{n-1}](p)
=\boxed{\frac{n!}{p^{n+1}}}\qquad(n\ge0).
$$

This also follows from the [gamma function](../complex-analysis.md#gamma-function). The stated open half-plane ensures absolute convergence.

<h3 id="9/iii">iii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [9](#9)

<h4 id="9/iii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#9/iii)

Integrating the exponential directly gives

$$
\mathcal L[e^{-nt}](p)=\int_0^\infty e^{-(p+n)t}\,dt
=\boxed{\frac1{p+n}},\qquad\operatorname{Re}p>-n.
$$

The inequality is the absolute-convergence domain of this [Laplace transform](../integral-transform.md#laplace-transform).
