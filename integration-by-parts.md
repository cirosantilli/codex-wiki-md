# Integration by parts

↑ **Parent:** [Calculus](calculus-split.md)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integration_by_parts)

[Integration by parts](integration-by-parts.md) transfers a [derivative](derivative.md) from one factor of a product to the other, while keeping an endpoint contribution. Its usefulness comes from choosing which factor becomes simpler when differentiated, and which has an accessible [antiderivative](antiderivative.md). The [integral](integral-split.md) is then replaced by a [boundary term](boundary-term.md) minus another [integral](integral-split.md).

For real [continuously differentiable functions](continuously-differentiable-function.md) $u,v$ on $[a,b]$, the precise [definite integral](definite-integral.md) formula is

$$
\int_a^b u(x)v'(x)\,dx=[u(x)v(x)]_a^b-\int_a^b u'(x)v(x)\,dx,
\qquad [uv]_a^b=u(b)v(b)-u(a)v(a).
$$

In the shorter notation, $du=u'(x)\,dx$ and $dv=v'(x)\,dx$, giving $\int u\,dv=uv-\int v\,du$ for [indefinite integrals](antiderivative.md), with a [constant of integration](constant-of-integration.md) understood. The [product rule](product-rule.md) says $(uv)'=u'v+uv'$; the [fundamental theorem of calculus](fundamental-theorem-of-calculus.md) turns its [integral](integral-split.md) into the endpoint difference. Thus the formula packages a differentiation rule into a tool for [integration](integral-split.md).[OpenStax, Calculus Volume 2, Section 3.1: Integration by parts](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-2/pages/3-1-integration-by-parts)

For example, choose $u=x$ and $dv=e^x\,dx$. Then $du=dx$ and $v=e^x$, since the [exponential function](exponential-function.md) is its own [derivative](derivative.md):

$$
\int_0^1 xe^x\,dx=[xe^x]_0^1-\int_0^1e^x\,dx=e-(e-1)=1.
$$

The [boundary term](boundary-term.md) is $e\approx2.718282$; the remaining [integral](integral-split.md) is $e-1\approx1.718282$. Their difference is exactly one. The endpoint product alone would overcount, because its [derivative](derivative.md) includes $u'v=e^x$ as well as the desired $uv'=xe^x$.

![](integration-by-parts-example.png)

**[Figure 28](#image-integration-by-parts-splits-the-accumulated-product-derivative-into-the-desired-integral-and-the-remaining-integral). Integration by parts splits the accumulated product derivative into the desired integral and the remaining integral**.

In the figure, the [area](surface-area.md) under $(xe^x)'=(1+x)e^x$ is the endpoint contribution $e$. Its lower band has [area](surface-area.md) $e-1$, and its upper band has thickness $xe^x$ and [area](surface-area.md) one. This decomposition makes the subtraction visible. The endpoint contribution is a difference of product values; it need not equal the [area](surface-area.md) under either factor separately.

The same choice repeatedly lowers the [degree of a polynomial](degree-of-a-polynomial.md) when integrating a [polynomial](polynomial-split.md) times an [exponential function](exponential-function.md), while choosing $u=\ln x$ gives $\int\ln x\,dx=x\ln x-x+C$ for $x>0$. More broadly, moving a [derivative](derivative.md) onto a convenient test function is the mechanism behind a [weak derivative](weak-derivative.md). Always evaluate the [boundary term](boundary-term.md): it disappears only when the relevant endpoint values justify doing so, often through a [boundary condition](boundary-condition.md).
