# Straightedge and compass construction

↑ **Parent:** [Euclidean geometry](geometry-and-topology.md#euclidean-geometry)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Straightedge_and_compass_construction)

A [compass-and-straightedge construction](straightedge-and-compass-construction.md) uses an unmarked straightedge to draw a [Euclidean line](geometry-and-topology.md#straight-line) through two already constructed points, and a compass to draw a [circle](topology.md#circle) with a constructed centre and radius. New points arise from intersections of these lines and circles. The allowed operations matter: an accurate numerical drawing need not supply such a construction.

## Constructible polygon

↑ **Parent:** [Straightedge and compass construction](straightedge-and-compass-construction.md)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constructible_polygon)

A [constructible polygon](#constructible-polygon) is a [polygon](geometry-and-topology.md#polygon) whose vertices can be obtained by a [compass-and-straightedge construction](straightedge-and-compass-construction.md) from the specified initial data. The classical question for a [regular polygon](geometry-and-topology.md#regular-polygon) asks which numbers of sides permit an exact construction from a given side or circumscribed [circle](topology.md#circle).

### Constructible regular polygon

↑ **Parent:** [Constructible polygon](#constructible-polygon)

A [constructible regular polygon](#constructible-regular-polygon) is a [regular polygon](geometry-and-topology.md#regular-polygon) admitting a [compass-and-straightedge construction](straightedge-and-compass-construction.md). The [Gauss-Wantzel theorem](#gauss-wantzel-theorem) classifies its possible numbers of sides.

#### Gauss-Wantzel theorem

↑ **Parent:** [Constructible regular polygon](#constructible-regular-polygon)

For $N\geq3$, a [regular polygon](geometry-and-topology.md#regular-polygon) with $N$ sides is a [constructible regular polygon](#constructible-regular-polygon) exactly when $N=2^a p_1\cdots p_r$, where $a$ is a [nonnegative integer](arithmetic.md#natural-number) and the $p_j$ are distinct [Fermat primes](number-theory.md#fermat-prime); the product may be empty. Thus seventeen and fifteen qualify, while nine does not. [Carl Friedrich Gauss](mathematics.md#carl-friedrich-gauss) established sufficiency, and [Pierre Wantzel](mathematics.md#pierre-wantzel) proved necessity in 1837.[slide 18](mathematics-bibliography.md#primality-testing-then-and-now-by-carl-pomerance)
