# Chain rule

↑ **Parent:** [Derivative](derivative.md)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chain_rule)

The [chain rule](chain-rule.md) calculates the [derivative](derivative.md) of a [function composition](function-composition.md) by combining local rates. If the inner [function](function-split.md) $f$ is [differentiable](differentiable-function.md) at $a$ and the outer [function](function-split.md) $g$ is [differentiable](differentiable-function.md) at $f(a)$, then

$$
(g\circ f)'(a)=g'(f(a))f'(a).
$$

The evaluation point matters: the outer [derivative](derivative.md) is evaluated at the inner output $f(a)$, not at $a$. A small input change first scales by $f'(a)$; the resulting intermediate change then scales by $g'(f(a))$. In terms of [linear approximations](linear-approximation.md), composing those two local descriptions multiplies their scale factors. [OpenStax, Calculus Volume 1: statement and proof of the chain rule](https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/3-6-the-chain-rule).

For example, $h(x)=\sin(x^2)$ applies a [quadratic function](quadratic-function.md) and then the [sine](sine.md). With $f(x)=x^2$ and $g(u)=\sin u$, the [chain rule](chain-rule.md) gives

$$
h'(x)=\cos(x^2)\,2x,\qquad h'(1)=2\cos1\approx1.08060461.
$$

Here all [angles](angle.md) are in [radians](radian.md). Near $x=1$, increasing the input by $\Delta x$ changes $x^2$ by about $2\Delta x$, and the [sine](sine.md) changes by about $\cos1$ times that intermediate change. For $\Delta x=0.01$, the [linear approximation](linear-approximation.md) predicts an output increase of $0.0108060$; the exact increase is $\sin(1.0201)-\sin1\approx0.0106894$.

**[Figure 24](#image-the-chain-rule-multiplies-the-local-rates-through-two-successive-functions). The chain rule multiplies the local rates through two successive functions**. The inner [quadratic function](quadratic-function.md) has [derivative](derivative.md) $2$ at $x=1$, and the outer [sine](sine.md) has [derivative](derivative.md) $\cos1$ at its input $u=1$. Their composite has [derivative](derivative.md) $2\cos1$. Each panel shows the corresponding [tangent line](tangent-line.md) and the exact output of the input change from $1$ to $1.01$. Tangents describe [linear approximations](linear-approximation.md), not exact finite changes.

The same rule handles several nested stages. For $h=g\circ f\circ k$, the [derivative](derivative.md) is $h'(x)=g'(f(k(x)))f'(k(x))k'(x)$ under the corresponding [differentiability](differentiability.md) assumptions. Writing each intermediate output prevents evaluating a [derivative](derivative.md) at the wrong stage. It also makes the [chain rule](chain-rule.md) useful for propagating sensitivities through a mathematical model, where one measured quantity influences another through several transformations.

For a quick proof idea, replace $f$ near $a$ by its [linear approximation](linear-approximation.md), then replace $g$ near $f(a)$ by its [linear approximation](linear-approximation.md). The composite leading term is their product; [differentiability](differentiability.md) ensures the remaining error is smaller than the original input change. This argument still applies when $f'(a)=0$, so the informal cancellation $dh/dx=(dh/du)(du/dx)$ should not be interpreted as division by a possibly zero finite change. The hypotheses are sufficient, but are not necessary for every composite to be [differentiable](differentiable-function.md), as the [nondifferentiable inner function with a differentiable nondegenerate composite](nondifferentiable-inner-function-with-a-differentiable-nondegenerate-composite.md) example shows.
