The [superconducting critical current](../../../../../superconducting-critical-current.md) depends on [temperature](../../../../../temperature-split.md), [magnetic field](../../../../../magnetic-field-split.md), sample geometry and the chosen dissipation criterion. For a magnet, a large [superconducting critical current density](../../../../../superconducting-critical-current-density.md) matters because it permits a useful winding current with manageable conductor [volume](../../../../../volume.md). The intrinsic depairing limit is an upper bound, but many practical type-II conductors become dissipative first through vortex motion.

A current exerts a transverse [force](../../../../../force-split.md) per unit vortex length $\mathbf f_L=\mathbf J\times\boldsymbol\Phi_0$. Moving flux produces an [electric field](../../../../../electric-field-split.md) and dissipation. [Flux pinning](../../../../../flux-pinning.md) holds vortices at energetically favorable defects, allowing a larger transport current before motion. Useful conductors therefore need pinning as well as a sufficiently high transition [temperature](../../../../../temperature-split.md) and upper critical field. Simply increasing disorder indefinitely is not a prescription: it can damage connectivity or superconductivity. High-angle grain boundaries in some copper-oxide materials also act as weak links, motivating controlled texture and connected current paths.

Conventional magnet conductors include [niobium-titanium](../../../../../niobium-titanium.md) and [niobium-tin](../../../../../niobium-tin.md); high-temperature coated conductors offer other operating regimes. Their performance must be compared on a [superconducting critical surface](../../../../../superconducting-critical-surface.md) in [temperature](../../../../../temperature-split.md), field and current density, with strain and thermal constraints also relevant. A high self-field current measured in a short sample does not by itself establish the current available throughout a high-field coil.

A [superconducting magnet](../../../../../superconducting-magnet.md) produces a strong, stable field without the normal winding's steady resistive power loss. Applications include [magnetic resonance imaging](../../../../../magnetic-resonance-imaging.md), [nuclear magnetic resonance](../../../../../nuclear-magnetic-resonance-split.md) and charged-particle beam steering or focusing in accelerators. A persistent-current loop is valuable when field stability matters. Refrigeration still requires power, and the magnet stores magnetic [energy](../../../../../energy-split.md), approximately $LI^2/2$ in a linear inductance model. A local transition to a resistive state can release that stored [energy](../../../../../energy-split.md) as heat, so [superconducting quench](../../../../../superconducting-quench.md) detection and protection are essential to operation. These requirements connect the microscopic vortex physics with useful engineering performance.
