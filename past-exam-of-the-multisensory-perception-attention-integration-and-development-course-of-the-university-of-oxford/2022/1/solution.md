**[Crossmodal correspondences](../../../crossmodal-correspondence.md) differ meaningfully in what is associated, how the association is acquired and where it affects performance. A matching preference, a response-compatibility effect and altered perceptual binding are distinct observations.** They need not have distinct underlying causes, and a single correspondence can have several causes.

A [crossmodal correspondence](../../../crossmodal-correspondence.md) is a systematic relation between attributes in different [sensory modalities](../../../sensory-modality.md), such as auditory [pitch](../../../pitch.md) and visual size. It does not require either stimulus to generate an extra conscious sensation. Thus it should not automatically be classified as developmental [synesthesia](../../../synesthesia.md), where stable inducer–concurrent experiences may be highly individual. Nor is correspondence equivalent to [multisensory integration](../../../multisensory-integration.md): judging that two attributes fit together does not establish that information from them was combined into one estimate.

Three useful explanatory distinctions concern sensory organization, environmental statistics and meaning. A [structural crossmodal correspondence](../../../structural-crossmodal-correspondence.md) would arise from shared coding or magnitude organization; this predicts a relation surviving changes in labels and response assignments. A [statistical crossmodal correspondence](../../../statistical-crossmodal-correspondence.md) would link features repeatedly generated by the same objects. For example, geometrically similar resonators with a fixed wave speed have characteristic [frequencies](../../../frequency.md) scaling approximately as inverse size: from $f\sim c/L$, increasing $L$ lowers $f$. This conditional physical relation can make [pitch](../../../pitch.md)–size coupling informative, but different materials, tensions or sound-production mechanisms can reverse a simple rule. A [semantic crossmodal correspondence](../../../semantic-crossmodal-correspondence.md) can link dimensions through [language](../../../language.md) or common concepts: “high” can describe [pitch](../../../pitch.md) and elevation. An [affective crossmodal correspondence](../../../affective-crossmodal-correspondence.md) could instead be mediated by shared pleasantness, which should be separated from a direct relation between sensory dimensions. These are explanatory hypotheses, not mutually exclusive boxes established by the appearance of a pairing.

Some correspondences are relative rather than absolute. A tone can be the higher member of one pair and the lower member of another. An experiment should therefore manipulate comparison range and context, rather than assuming that every participant has one fixed pitch-to-size conversion. Relations can also be asymmetric: an irrelevant sound might alter a visual response without a visual stimulus equivalently altering auditory judgment. Asymmetry can arise from task reliability or [attention](../../../attention.md), so it does not by itself establish a one-way anatomical connection.

A comparative experiment provides an important constraint. [Vera U. Ludwig](../../../vera-u-ludwig.md) and colleagues tested 33 humans and six chimpanzees on matching black and white squares while irrelevant high or low tones were played. Both groups performed better for high-pitch/white and low-pitch/black pairings; in chimpanzees the effect was expressed particularly in errors, whereas human errors were near floor and [reaction times](../../../reaction-time.md) were more informative. Both species used a touchscreen and loudspeaker arrangement.[PNAS 108, 20661–20665; Results, Apparatus and Procedure](../../../visuoauditory-mappings-between-high-luminance-and-high-pitch-are-shared-by-chimpanzees-and-humans-by-vera-u-ludwig-and-others.md) This makes a human-language-only account inadequate for that mapping. It does not distinguish inherited organization from every form of nonlinguistic [learning](../../../learning.md): the animals had substantial task experience, and shared environments can also generate shared associations.

A stronger test of perceptual consequences changes the dependent variable. [Cesare Valerio Parise](../../../cesare-valerio-parise.md) and [Charles Spence](../../../charles-spence.md) presented small/large visual stimuli with high/low tones and manipulated audiovisual timing or position. Participants judged temporal order or spatial discrepancy, rather than simply reporting which pairing they preferred. Matched pairings produced poorer access to the intersensory discrepancy, interpreted as stronger coupling.[PLoS ONE 4:e5664; Experiments 1–3](../../../when-birds-of-a-feather-flock-together-synesthetic-correspondences-modulate-audiovisual-integration-in-non-synesthetes-by-cesare-valerio-parise-and-charles-spence.md) This illustrates why increased binding need not improve every task: reducing conflict between estimates can make the conflict itself harder to discriminate. A changed discrimination threshold still needs controls for distraction, detectability and decision strategy; it does not uniquely identify a perceptual fusion mechanism.

To distinguish mechanisms experimentally, first obtain unisensory detection and discrimination measures and equate unintended salience differences. Then compare explicit matching, speeded classification, conflict discrimination and judgments of a common object. Counterbalance response keys and reverse stimulus–response mappings. A congruency effect that follows the response rule suggests compatibility at a decision or motor stage; persistence under orthogonal response rules constrains that explanation. Fit [psychometric functions](../../../psychometric-function.md) to conflict judgments and estimate bias, slope and lapses separately. A shift in subjective equality and a loss of sensitivity are different outcomes.

Next manipulate experience. Randomly assign participants to congruent, reversed or uncorrelated audiovisual training with equal exposures, and test untrained exemplars and delayed retention. A reversible mapping with transfer supports an experience-dependent contribution; failure to reverse it does not prove innateness, because training may be weak or poorly measured. Cross-language comparisons with nonverbal responses can test whether a shared word is necessary. Infant or nonhuman results can constrain linguistic explanations, but age differences, task accessibility and species-specific response demands remain possible confounds. For a pleasantness account, collect independent pleasantness ratings and select or manipulate stimuli so that hedonic matching varies separately from the sensory pairing. These are proposed discriminating designs, not asserted experimental results.

The most useful classification therefore combines origin, representational content and measurable consequence. **Correspondences are scientifically informative when their predicted transfer, context dependence and effects on sensitivity distinguish them from mere agreement about labels or response keys.**
