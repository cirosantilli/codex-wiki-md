<h1 id="2/b/iii/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](../iii.md)

**The third electron must enter the higher, degenerate and partly antibonding orbital pair rather than the already filled fully bonding orbital.** At an equilateral geometry the configuration becomes $(a_1')^2(e')^1$. This weakens the collective bonding and creates [orbital degeneracy](../../../../../../orbital-degeneracy.md), so a distortion can lower the singly occupied component and localize bonding. Along the ground-state [reaction coordinate](../../../../../../reaction-coordinate.md), formation of a normal two-electron H–H bond with a separated [hydrogen](../../../../../../hydrogen.md) atom becomes favourable.

The result is ground-state dissociation to $\mathrm{H_2+H}$, not loss of a [proton](../../../../../../proton.md) from the cation or formation of three isolated atoms. One should not claim that adding one electron exactly cancels two bonding [electrons](../../../../../../electron.md): a simple three-orbital count alone does not predict a zero [bond order](../../../../../../bond-order.md) or prove dissociation. The important qualitative changes are occupancy of an antibonding/degenerate level and access to a lower-energy dimer-plus-atom arrangement. A complete dissociation statement requires the potential-energy surface, including nuclear repulsion; electronically excited neutral H$_3$ states are a separate issue.
