<h3 id="1/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1/a)

A [commutative ring](../commutative-algebra.md#commutative-ring) $R$ is a [Noetherian ring](../algebra.md#noetherian-ring) if every [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) has a finite [generating set of an ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#generating-set-of-an-ideal). Equivalently, it satisfies the [ascending chain condition](../algebra.md#ascending-chain-condition) on [ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal). Indeed, the union of an ascending chain is an [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal), and finitely many generators all belong to one term, after which the chain stabilizes. Conversely, an [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) without a finite [generating set of an ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#generating-set-of-an-ideal) would allow us to choose successively $a_{n+1}\notin(a_1,\ldots,a_n)$, producing a strictly ascending chain of [ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal).

Let $\phi:R\to S$ be a [surjective](../algebra.md#surjective-function) [ring homomorphism](../commutative-algebra.md#ring-homomorphism), where $R$ is a [Noetherian ring](../algebra.md#noetherian-ring). For any [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) $J$ of $S$, its inverse image is an [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) of $R$, say $\phi^{-1}(J)=(a_1,\ldots,a_r)$. Taking images gives

$$
J=(\phi(a_1),\ldots,\phi(a_r)).
$$

Thus [homomorphic images of Noetherian rings are Noetherian](../algebra.md#homomorphic-images-of-noetherian-rings-are-noetherian). This also applies to the image of an arbitrary [ring homomorphism](../commutative-algebra.md#ring-homomorphism), by taking its image as the codomain.

The [Hilbert basis theorem](../algebra.md#hilbert-basis-theorem) states that if $R$ is a [Noetherian ring](../algebra.md#noetherian-ring), then the [polynomial ring](../commutative-algebra.md#polynomial-ring) $R[t]$ is a [Noetherian ring](../algebra.md#noetherian-ring). Repeating this gives the result for finitely many variables.

To prove it, fix an [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) $K\subseteq R[t]$. For $n\geq0$, define the [coefficient ideals of a polynomial ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#coefficient-ideals-of-a-polynomial-ideal)

$$
A_n=\{a\in R:\text{some }f\in K\text{ of degree at most }n\text{ has coefficient }a\text{ at }t^n\}.
$$

Here the zero [polynomial](../polynomial.md) is allowed. Each $A_n$ is an [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal): addition and multiplication by constants preserve the required degree bound. Multiplying a representing [polynomial](../polynomial.md) by $t$ gives $A_n\subseteq A_{n+1}$. The [ascending chain condition](../algebra.md#ascending-chain-condition) therefore supplies $N$ such that $A_n=A_N$ for every $n\geq N$.

For each $0\leq n\leq N$, choose a finite [generating set of an ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#generating-set-of-an-ideal) $a_{n1},\ldots,a_{nr_n}$ for $A_n$, omitting zero generators, and choose $f_{nj}\in K$ of [polynomial degree](../polynomial.md#degree-of-a-polynomial) $n$ with [leading coefficient](../polynomial.md#leading-coefficient-of-a-polynomial) $a_{nj}$. If $A_n=0$, choose no such [polynomials](../polynomial.md). We claim that the finitely many $f_{nj}$ generate $K$.

Take a nonzero $f\in K$ of [polynomial degree](../polynomial.md#degree-of-a-polynomial) $d$. Put $n=\min(d,N)$. Its [leading coefficient](../polynomial.md#leading-coefficient-of-a-polynomial) belongs to $A_d=A_n$, so write it as $\sum_jb_ja_{nj}$. The [polynomial](../polynomial.md)

$$
g=f-\sum_jb_jt^{d-n}f_{nj}
$$

belongs to $K$ and is either zero or has [polynomial degree](../polynomial.md#degree-of-a-polynomial) strictly below $d$. Induction on [polynomial degree](../polynomial.md#degree-of-a-polynomial) now expresses $f$ as a combination of the chosen $f_{nj}$; when $d=0$, the displayed difference is zero. Hence $K$ is finitely generated. Since $K$ was arbitrary, $R[t]$ is a [Noetherian ring](../algebra.md#noetherian-ring).

<h4 id="1/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

For $x\in X$, let $e_x$ be the [indicator function](../measure-theory.md#indicator-function) of $\{x\}$. In the [ring of functions](../commutative-algebra.md#ring-of-functions) $R=\mathbb R^X$, multiplication is pointwise, so

$$
e_xf=f(x)e_x.
$$

Suppose first that $X$ is finite, and let $J$ be an [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) of $R$. Define $S=\{x\in X:f(x)\ne0\text{ for some }f\in J\}$. If $x\in S$, choose such an $f$; multiplying it by $e_x/f(x)$ shows that $e_x\in J$. Every $f\in J$ satisfies

$$
f=\sum_{x\in S}f(x)e_x,
$$

where the real scalars are constant [functions](../function.md). Thus $J=(e_x:x\in S)$ has a finite [generating set of an ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#generating-set-of-an-ideal), so $R$ is a [Noetherian ring](../algebra.md#noetherian-ring). If $X$ is empty, $R$ is the [zero ring](../commutative-algebra.md#zero-ring), and the conclusion still holds.

If $X$ is infinite, consider the [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal)

$$
F=\{f\in\mathbb R^X:\{x:f(x)\ne0\}\text{ is finite}\}.
$$

It is an [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) because a sum has [support](../function.md#support) contained in the union of the two [supports](../function.md#support), and multiplication by any [function](../function.md) cannot enlarge [support](../function.md#support). If $f_1,\ldots,f_r$ generated $F$, their [supports](../function.md#support) would have a finite union $T$. Every element of $(f_1,\ldots,f_r)$ would vanish outside $T$, whereas $e_x\in F$ for any $x\in X\setminus T$. This contradiction proves

$$
\boxed{\mathbb R^X\text{ is Noetherian}\iff X\text{ is finite}.}
$$

**No.** Write $x_i=t_i+I$ in the [quotient ring](../commutative-algebra.md#quotient-ring) $R=L/I$. Its defining relations are

$$
x_1^2=0,\qquad x_i=x_{i+1}^2\quad(i\geq1).

The [principal ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal) satisfy

$$
(x_1)\subseteq(x_2)\subseteq(x_3)\subseteq\cdots.
$$

Every inclusion is strict. Otherwise $x_{i+1}=a x_i=a x_{i+1}^2$ for some $a\in R$, and hence

$$
x_{i+1}(1-a x_{i+1})=0.
$$

The fact that [one minus a nilpotent element is a unit](../algebra.md#one-minus-a-nilpotent-element-is-a-unit) applies: if $z^m=0$, then $(1-z)(1+z+\cdots+z^{m-1})=1$. Here $z=a x_{i+1}$ is [nilpotent](../commutative-algebra.md#nilpotent), so multiplication by this inverse would give $x_{i+1}=0$, a contradiction. The strictly ascending chain of [ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) violates the [ascending chain condition](../algebra.md#ascending-chain-condition). Therefore

$$
\boxed{L/I\text{ is not Noetherian}.}
$$

<h4 id="2/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#2/a)

The [radical of an ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#radical-of-an-ideal) $I$ is

$$
\boxed{\sqrt I=\operatorname{rad}(I)=\{a\in R:a^n\in I\text{ for some }n\geq1\}.}
$$

We first establish the properties needed below. If $a^m,b^n\in I$, every term of the [binomial theorem](../binomial-coefficient.md#binomial-theorem) expansion of $(a+b)^{m+n-1}$ contains either $a^m$ or $b^n$, so $a+b\in\sqrt I$. Also $(-a)^m\in I$ and $(ra)^m=r^ma^m\in I$. Thus $\sqrt I$ is an [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal). The definition gives $I\subseteq\sqrt I$ and shows that $I\subseteq J$ implies $\sqrt I\subseteq\sqrt J$. Finally, if $a^n\in\sqrt I$, then $(a^n)^m\in I$ for some $m$, so $a\in\sqrt I$. Hence $\sqrt{\sqrt I}=\sqrt I$; in other words, $\sqrt I$ is a [radical ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#radical-ideal).

We prove the stronger fact that the [radical of an ideal is the intersection of the prime ideals containing it](../commutative-algebra.md#radical-of-an-ideal-is-the-intersection-of-the-prime-ideals-containing-it):

$$
\sqrt I=\bigcap_{\substack{P\supseteq I\\P\text{ prime}}}P.
$$

If $a\in\sqrt I$ and $P\supseteq I$ is a [prime ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal), then $a^n\in P$ for some $n$, and repeated application of the definition of a [prime ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) gives $a\in P$. This proves one inclusion.

Conversely, fix $a\notin\sqrt I$. The [multiplicatively closed set](../commutative-algebra.md#multiplicatively-closed-set) $T=\{1,a,a^2,\ldots\}$ is disjoint from $I$. Consider the [ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) containing $I$ and disjoint from $T$. This collection is nonempty, and the union of any chain in it is another such [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal). By [Zorn's lemma](../set-theory.md#zorn-s-lemma), there is a maximal member $P$. It is proper because $1\in T$.

To prove that $P$ is a [prime ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal), suppose $bc\in P$ but $b,c\notin P$. Maximality forces both $P+(b)$ and $P+(c)$ to meet $T$. Thus, for some $u,v\geq0$,

$$
a^u=p+rb,\qquad a^v=q+sc,
\qquad p,q\in P,\quad r,s\in R.
$$

Multiplication gives $a^{u+v}\in P$, since every term lies in $P$, including $rsbc$. This contradicts disjointness from $T$. Therefore $P$ is a [prime ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) containing $I$ and avoiding $a$. This proves the other inclusion, including the required [prime ideal avoiding a multiplicative subset](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal-avoiding-a-multiplicative-subset) argument without using any unproved facts about the [nilradical](../commutative-algebra.md#nilradical).

If $I=\sqrt I$, the displayed intersection represents $I$ as required. Conversely, an [intersection of ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#intersection-of-ideals) that are all [prime ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) is a [radical ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#radical-ideal): if $a^n$ belongs to every one of them, then so does $a$. Consequently

$$
\boxed{I=\sqrt I\iff I\text{ is an intersection of prime ideals}.}
$$

The [unit ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#unit-ideal) $I=R$ is included by interpreting the empty intersection as $R$.

<h4 id="2/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

If $\sqrt I+\sqrt J=R$, write $1=a+b$ with $a\in\sqrt I$ and $b\in\sqrt J$. Choose $m,n\geq1$ such that $a^m\in I$ and $b^n\in J$. In the [binomial theorem](../binomial-coefficient.md#binomial-theorem) expansion

$$
1=(a+b)^{m+n-1},
$$

each term has either at least $m$ factors $a$, or at least $n$ factors $b$. Hence every term belongs to the [sum of ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#sum-of-ideals) $I+J$, and

$$
\boxed{\sqrt I+\sqrt J=R\ \Longrightarrow\ I+J=R.}
$$

Thus [comaximal ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#comaximal-ideals) can be recognized from their [radicals of ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#radical-of-an-ideal).

For the general identity, $I+J\subseteq\sqrt I+\sqrt J$ gives

$$
\sqrt{I+J}\subseteq\sqrt{\sqrt I+\sqrt J}.
$$

On the other hand, $I,J\subseteq I+J$ implies $\sqrt I,\sqrt J\subseteq\sqrt{I+J}$. Since the latter is an [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal), $\sqrt I+\sqrt J\subseteq\sqrt{I+J}$. Taking [radicals of ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#radical-of-an-ideal) and using the idempotence proved in [a](#2/a) yields

$$
\sqrt{\sqrt I+\sqrt J}\subseteq\sqrt{\sqrt{I+J}}=\sqrt{I+J}.
$$

Therefore the [radical of a sum of ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#radical-of-a-sum-of-ideals) satisfies

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{rad}(\operatorname{rad}(I)+\operatorname{rad}(J))=\operatorname{rad}(I+J).}
$$

<h3 id="2/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#2/c)

Fix $n\geq1$ and suppose, for a contradiction, that $I^n=I^{n+1}$. Set $M=I^n$. Since $R$ is a [Noetherian ring](../algebra.md#noetherian-ring), this [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) is a [finitely generated module](../module-theory.md#finitely-generated-module), say $M=(u_1,\ldots,u_r)$. It is nonzero: if $0\ne a\in I$, then $a^n\ne0$ because $R$ is an [integral domain](../commutative-algebra.md#integral-domain).

The equality $M=IM$ allows us to write

$$
u_i=\sum_{j=1}^r a_{ij}u_j,\qquad a_{ij}\in I.
$$

Let $A=(a_{ij})$ and $B=\operatorname{Id}_r-A$. Then $B(u_1,\ldots,u_r)^{\mathsf T}=0$. The [adjugate matrix](../linear-algebra.md#adjugate-matrix) identity $\operatorname{adj}(B)B=(\det B)\operatorname{Id}_r$ implies that $\det B$ annihilates every $u_i$. In the [determinant](../multilinear-algebra.md#determinant) expansion of $\operatorname{Id}_r-A$, the constant term is $1$ and every other term contains an entry of $A$, so

$$
\det B=1-b\quad\text{for some }b\in I.
$$

This is the [determinant trick](../module-theory.md#determinant-trick). Since some $u_i\ne0$, [multiplicative cancellation in an integral domain](../commutative-algebra.md#multiplicative-cancellation-in-an-integral-domain) gives $1-b=0$. But then $1=b\in I$, contradicting $I\ne R$. We have proved the [strict descent of powers of a nonzero proper ideal in a Noetherian domain](../algebra.md#strict-descent-of-powers-of-a-nonzero-proper-ideal-in-a-noetherian-domain):

$$
\boxed{I\supsetneq I^2\supsetneq I^3\supsetneq\cdots.}
$$

All these [powers of an ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#powers-of-an-ideal) have the same [radical of an ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#radical-of-an-ideal). Indeed, $I^n\subseteq I$ gives $\sqrt{I^n}\subseteq\sqrt I$. Conversely, if $x^m\in I$, then $x^{mn}\in I^n$, so $x\in\sqrt{I^n}$. Thus

$$
\boxed{\sqrt{I^n}=\sqrt I\quad(n\geq1),}
$$

and $J=I,I^2,I^3,\ldots$ gives infinitely many distinct [ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) with the prescribed [radical of an ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#radical-of-an-ideal). If the convention $0\in\mathbb N$ is used, the additional inequality $I^0=R\ne I$ follows immediately from properness.

<h4 id="2/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#2/d)

**No.** A [domain with a nonzero idempotent maximal ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#domain-with-a-nonzero-idempotent-maximal-ideal) is a counterexample. Consider

$$
R=\bigcup_{n\geq0}\mathbb Q[t^{1/2^n}],\qquad
u_n=t^{1/2^n},\qquad u_n=u_{n+1}^2.
$$

This can be realized concretely as a [ring of functions](../commutative-algebra.md#ring-of-functions) on $t>0$: its elements are finite sums of rational multiples of nonnegative [dyadic rational](../arithmetic.md#dyadic-rational) powers of $t$. Each stage $\mathbb Q[u_n]$ is a [polynomial ring](../commutative-algebra.md#polynomial-ring), because a nonzero [polynomial](../polynomial.md) cannot vanish for all $u_n>0$. Any two elements lie in one stage, which is an [integral domain](../commutative-algebra.md#integral-domain), so their product is nonzero if both are nonzero. Thus $R$ is an [integral domain](../commutative-algebra.md#integral-domain).

The constant term defines a [surjective](../algebra.md#surjective-function) [ring homomorphism](../commutative-algebra.md#ring-homomorphism) $\epsilon:R\to\mathbb Q$. It is well defined because replacing $u_n$ by $u_{n+1}^2$ preserves the constant term. Its [kernel of a ring homomorphism](../commutative-algebra.md#kernel-of-a-ring-homomorphism)

$$
\mathfrak m=\{f\in R:\text{the constant term of }f\text{ is }0\}
$$

is a [maximal ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#maximal-ideal), since $R/\mathfrak m\cong\mathbb Q$ is a [field](../algebra.md#field), by the [maximal ideal quotient criterion](../commutative-algebra.md#maximal-ideal-quotient-criterion). Also $u_0=t\in\mathfrak m$ is nonzero.

If $f\in\mathfrak m$, choose a stage in which $f=u_ng(u_n)$ for a [polynomial](../polynomial.md) $g\in\mathbb Q[z]$. Then

$$
f=u_{n+1}\bigl(u_{n+1}g(u_{n+1}^2)\bigr).
$$

Both factors belong to $\mathfrak m$, so $f\in\mathfrak m^2$. The reverse inclusion holds for every [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal), proving that $\mathfrak m$ is an [idempotent ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#idempotent-ideal). Hence

$$
\boxed{0\ne\mathfrak m\text{ is maximal},\qquad\mathfrak m^n=\mathfrak m^{n+1}=\mathfrak m\quad(n\geq1).}
$$

The [Noetherian ring](../algebra.md#noetherian-ring) hypothesis in [c](#2/c) is therefore essential even for a [maximal ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#maximal-ideal).

<h4 id="3/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

The [Krull dimension](../commutative-algebra.md#krull-dimension) of a [commutative ring](../commutative-algebra.md#commutative-ring) $R$ is

$$
\boxed{\dim R=\sup\{d:\text{there exist prime ideals }P_0\subsetneq P_1\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq P_d\}.}
$$

The length of a [prime chain](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-chain) counts strict inclusions, rather than the number of [prime ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal).

Let $R$ be a [principal ideal domain](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal-domain) that is not a [field](../algebra.md#field). We first show that every nonzero [prime ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) is a [maximal ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#maximal-ideal). Write such a [prime ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) as $P=(p)$ with $p\ne0$, and suppose $P\subseteq(a)$. Then $p=ab$ for some $b\in R$. Since $ab\in P$, either $a\in P$, in which case $(a)=P$, or $b\in P$. In the second case $b=pc$ for some $c$, and $p=apc$ gives $1=ac$ by [multiplicative cancellation in an integral domain](../commutative-algebra.md#multiplicative-cancellation-in-an-integral-domain). Thus $(a)=R$. This proves maximality.

Since $R$ is an [integral domain](../commutative-algebra.md#integral-domain), $(0)$ is a [prime ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal), and every other [prime ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) is maximal. Consequently every [prime chain](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-chain) has length at most one. For the opposite inequality, choose a nonzero [nonunit](../algebra.md#nonunit) $a$, which exists because $R$ is not a [field](../algebra.md#field). The proper [principal ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal) $(a)$ is contained in a [maximal ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#maximal-ideal) $\mathfrak m$; existence follows from [Zorn's lemma](../set-theory.md#zorn-s-lemma) applied to the proper [ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) containing $(a)$. A [maximal ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#maximal-ideal) is a [prime ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal), since its [quotient ring](../commutative-algebra.md#quotient-ring) is a [field](../algebra.md#field). Therefore $(0)\subsetneq\mathfrak m$ is a [prime chain](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-chain) of length one. Thus the [Krull dimension of a principal ideal domain](../commutative-algebra.md#krull-dimension-of-a-principal-ideal-domain) that is not a [field](../algebra.md#field) is

$$
\boxed{\dim R=1.}
$$

<h3 id="3/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

Let $P$ be a [prime ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) of $R$ disjoint from the [multiplicatively closed set](../commutative-algebra.md#multiplicatively-closed-set) $Y$. In the [localization](../commutative-algebra.md#localization-of-a-ring) $S=Y^{-1}R$, its [extension of an ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#extension-of-an-ideal) is

$$
SP=\{p/y:p\in P,\ y\in Y\}.
$$

Indeed, finite sums of such fractions have a common denominator in $Y$ and numerator in $P$, and every generator $p/1$ has this form.

We show that its [contraction of an ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#contraction-of-an-ideal) recovers $P$:

$$
\boxed{\{r\in R:r/1\in SP\}=P.}
$$

One inclusion is immediate. Conversely, if $r/1=p/y$ with $p\in P$ and $y\in Y$, equality of fractions in the [field of fractions](../commutative-algebra.md#field-of-fractions) of the [integral domain](../commutative-algebra.md#integral-domain) $R$ gives $yr=p\in P$. Since $y\notin P$ and $P$ is a [prime ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal), $r\in P$.

If $SP_1=SP_2$, contracting would therefore give $P_1=P_2$, contrary to the hypothesis. Hence

$$
\boxed{SP_1\ne SP_2.}
$$

We will also need the fact that $SP$ is a [prime ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal), part of the [prime ideal correspondence for localization](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal-correspondence-for-localization). It is proper by the contraction identity, since otherwise $1\in P$. If $(a/u)(b/v)\in SP$, multiplication by the denominator gives $ab/1\in SP$, and contraction gives $ab\in P$. Thus $a\in P$ or $b\in P$, so one of the two fractions belongs to $SP$.

<h4 id="3/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#3/c)

Suppose, for a contradiction, that $P_2\cap\mathbb Z=(0)$. Then $P_1\cap\mathbb Z=(0)$ too. Both [prime ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) are disjoint from the [multiplicatively closed set](../commutative-algebra.md#multiplicatively-closed-set) $Y=\mathbb Z\setminus\{0\}$. The [localization](../commutative-algebra.md#localization-of-a-ring) is

$$
Y^{-1}\mathbb Z[t]=\mathbb Q[t]:
$$

each fraction on the left has rational coefficients, and every [polynomial](../polynomial.md) with rational coefficients has a common nonzero integer denominator.

By [b](#3/b), the extended [prime ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) form a strict inclusion

$$
\mathbb Q[t]P_1\subsetneq\mathbb Q[t]P_2.
$$

The first is nonzero, because $P_1$ contains a nonzero [polynomial](../polynomial.md) and the map $\mathbb Z[t]\to\mathbb Q[t]$ is [injective](../algebra.md#injective-function).

The [polynomial ring](../commutative-algebra.md#polynomial-ring) $\mathbb Q[t]$ is a [principal ideal domain](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal-domain): in any nonzero [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal), a nonzero [polynomial](../polynomial.md) of least [polynomial degree](../polynomial.md#degree-of-a-polynomial) generates the [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal), since the [polynomial division algorithm](../polynomial.md#polynomial-division) gives a remainder of smaller [polynomial degree](../polynomial.md#degree-of-a-polynomial), which must be zero. By [a](#3/a), every nonzero [prime ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) of this [principal ideal domain](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal-domain) is maximal. The strict inclusion of extended [prime ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) is therefore impossible. We obtain

$$
\boxed{P_2\cap\mathbb Z\ne(0).}
$$

<h3 id="3/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#3/d)

For any [prime number](../prime-number.md) $p$, there is a [prime chain](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-chain)

$$
(0)\subsetneq(p)\subsetneq(p,t)
$$

in $\mathbb Z[t]$. These are [prime ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) because the respective [quotient rings](../commutative-algebra.md#quotient-ring) are $\mathbb Z[t]$, $\mathbb F_p[t]$, and $\mathbb F_p$, all [integral domains](../commutative-algebra.md#integral-domain). The inclusions are strict because $p\ne0$ and $t\notin(p)$. Hence $\dim\mathbb Z[t]\geq2$.

To prove the upper bound, suppose there were a [prime chain](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-chain) of length at least three. Since $\mathbb Z[t]$ is an [integral domain](../commutative-algebra.md#integral-domain), we could insert $(0)$ at its beginning if necessary and select a chain

$$
(0)\subsetneq P_1\subsetneq P_2\subsetneq P_3.
$$

By [c](#3/c), $P_2\cap\mathbb Z\ne(0)$. This intersection is a proper [prime ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) of $\mathbb Z$: if a product of integers lies in it, primality of $P_2$ puts one factor in it. Therefore $P_2\cap\mathbb Z=(p)$ for a [prime number](../prime-number.md) $p$, and $(p)\subseteq P_2$.

We need to check that $P_2\ne(p)$. Here [prime ideals below a principal prime](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideals-below-a-principal-prime) give the necessary restriction: no nonzero [prime ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) can lie strictly below $(p)$. If $0\ne P\subsetneq(p)$, then $p\notin P$. Choose $0\ne f\in P$ and factor out the largest common power of $p$ from its integer coefficients:

$$
f=p^kg,\qquad g\notin(p).
$$

Such a finite $k$ exists because $f$ has at least one nonzero integer coefficient. Since $p\notin P$ and $P$ is a [prime ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal), repeated primality gives $g\in P\subseteq(p)$, a contradiction. Applied to $P_1\subsetneq P_2$, this rules out $P_2=(p)$.

It follows that $P_2/(p)$ is a nonzero [prime ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) of

$$
\mathbb Z[t]/(p)\cong\mathbb F_p[t].
$$

As in [c](#3/c), the [polynomial division algorithm](../polynomial.md#polynomial-division) makes $\mathbb F_p[t]$ a [principal ideal domain](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal-domain), so its nonzero [prime ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) are maximal by [a](#3/a). Thus $P_2/(p)$ is maximal. The [ideal correspondence theorem](../commutative-algebra.md#correspondence-theorem-for-ideals) makes $P_2$ a [maximal ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#maximal-ideal) of $\mathbb Z[t]$, contradicting $P_2\subsetneq P_3$. Consequently

$$
\boxed{\dim\mathbb Z[t]=2.}
$$
