## 1

↑ **Parent:** [2023](2023.md)

<h3 id="1/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1/a)

Sort the observations into [order statistics](../probability-theory.md#order-statistic) $x_{(1)}\leq\cdots\leq x_{(n)}$. Choose [plotting positions](../probability-and-statistics.md#plotting-position) $p_i=(i-1/2)/n$ and calculate the [standard normal quantiles](../probability-distribution.md#standard-normal-quantile) $z_i=\Phi^{-1}(p_i)$. The [normal Q-Q plot](../probability-and-statistics.md#normal-q-q-plot) consists of the pairs $(z_i,x_{(i)})$.

If the [normal distribution](../probability-distribution.md#normal-distribution) model is appropriate, the points should lie approximately on a straight line with positive slope, because its population [quantile function](../probability-distribution.md#quantile-function) is

$$
Q(p)=\mu+\sigma\Phi^{-1}(p).
$$

The intercept is approximately $\mu$ and the slope approximately $\sigma$; neither needs to be known in advance. The reference need not be the identity line. One justification is the [probability integral transform](../probability-distribution.md#probability-integral-transform): for an independent normal [statistical sample](../statistical-inference.md#statistical-sample), $\Phi((X_{(i)}-\mu)/\sigma)$ are uniform [order statistics](../probability-theory.md#order-statistic), whose typical values are near their [plotting positions](../probability-and-statistics.md#plotting-position). The equality is approximate for a finite sample, particularly at the extremes. Systematic curvature, unusually heavy tails, or isolated extreme departures argue against the proposed distributional shape. A straight marginal [Q-Q plot](../probability-and-statistics.md#q-q-plot) does not itself establish [independence](../random-variable.md#independent-random-variables) of observations.

<h3 id="1/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

<h5 id="1/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#1/b/i)

Expanding the exponent, the [log-likelihood function](../maximum-likelihood-estimation.md#log-likelihood) is

$$
\ell(\theta)=C-\frac{\sum_i y_i}{2\theta^2}+\frac n\theta,
$$

where $C$ is independent of $\theta$. Its [score function](../statistical-modelling.md#informant-function) and second derivative are

$$
\ell'(\theta)=\frac{\sum_i y_i-n\theta}{\theta^3},\qquad
\ell''(\theta)=-\frac{3\sum_i y_i}{\theta^4}+\frac{2n}{\theta^3}.
$$

The first is positive below $\bar y$ and negative above it, so the unique [maximum-likelihood estimator](../maximum-likelihood-estimation.md#maximum-likelihood-estimator) is

$$
\boxed{\widehat\theta=\bar Y}.
$$

Using $\mathbb E\sum_iY_i=n\theta$, the one-observation [Fisher information](../statistical-modelling.md#fisher-information-matrix) is $I(\theta)=-n^{-1}\mathbb E\ell''(\theta)=\theta^{-3}$. The support does not depend on the interior parameter $\theta>0$, and the regular [asymptotic normality of a maximum likelihood estimator](../maximum-likelihood-estimation.md#asymptotic-normality-of-a-maximum-likelihood-estimator) gives

$$
\boxed{\sqrt n(\widehat\theta-\theta)\xrightarrow d N(0,\theta^3)},\qquad
\widehat\theta\ \mathrel{\dot\sim}\ N(\theta,\theta^3/n).
$$

This is [Inverse Gaussian mean estimation](../exponential-family.md#inverse-gaussian-mean-estimation) with known shape one. Equivalently, the [Inverse Gaussian distribution](../exponential-family.md#inverse-gaussian-distribution) has [variance](../variance.md) $\theta^3$, so the same result follows directly from the [central limit theorem](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#central-limit-theorem) applied to its [sample mean](../variance.md#sample-mean).

<h4 id="1/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

<h5 id="1/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#1/b/ii)

The estimated [standard error](../statistical-inference.md#standard-error) is $\widehat\theta^{3/2}/\sqrt n$. Using the central 95% [standard normal quantiles](../probability-distribution.md#standard-normal-quantile) $\pm1.96$, a [Wald confidence interval](../statistical-inference.md#wald-confidence-interval) is

$$
\boxed{\left[\widehat\theta-1.96\frac{\widehat\theta^{3/2}}{\sqrt n},\quad\widehat\theta+1.96\frac{\widehat\theta^{3/2}}{\sqrt n}\right]\cap(0,\infty)}.
$$

Consistency of $\widehat\theta$ and [Slutsky's theorem](../statistical-inference.md#slutsky-theorem) justify replacing the unknown scale $\theta^{3/2}$ by its estimate. The [coverage probability](../statistical-inference.md#coverage-probability) is asymptotic, not exact at finite $n$. Intersecting with the positive parameter space removes impossible values without changing whether the true positive parameter is covered.

<h3 id="1/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/c/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#1/c)

<h5 id="1/c/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#1/c/i)

Put $S=\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2$ and $q=S/\sigma_0^2$. Under the [null hypothesis](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#null-hypothesis), maximizing over the nuisance [mean](../expected-value.md) gives $\widehat\mu_0=\bar x$. The unrestricted [normal mean and variance maximum-likelihood estimators](../maximum-likelihood-estimation.md#normal-mean-and-variance-maximum-likelihood-estimators) are $\widehat\mu=\bar x$, $\widehat{\sigma^2}=S/n$. Thus, for $S>0$,

$$
\lambda(\boldsymbol x)=\frac{(2\pi\sigma_0^2)^{-n/2}e^{-S/(2\sigma_0^2)}}{(2\pi S/n)^{-n/2}e^{-n/2}}
=\boxed{\left(\frac qn\right)^{n/2}e^{(n-q)/2}}.
$$

The [likelihood-ratio test statistic](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#likelihood-ratio-test-statistic) is consequently

$$
\boxed{W=-2\log\lambda=q-n-n\log(q/n)}.
$$

The usual model assumes $n\geq2$. The event $S=0$ has [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability) zero under a nondegenerate [normal distribution](../probability-distribution.md#normal-distribution); at that observation the unrestricted likelihood is unbounded as the variance tends to zero, and the ratio has limiting value zero. This is the [likelihood-ratio test for a normal variance](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#likelihood-ratio-test-for-a-normal-variance) with unknown mean.

<h4 id="1/c/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#1/c)

<h5 id="1/c/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#1/c/ii)

The full parameter space has dimension two and the null space dimension one. The regular [Wilks theorem](../statistical-inference.md#wilks-theorem) therefore gives $W\xrightarrow d\chi_1^2$ under $H_0$ as $n\to\infty$. If $F_1$ denotes the [cumulative distribution function](../probability-distribution.md#cumulative-distribution-function) of the [chi-squared distribution](../probability-distribution.md#chi-squared-distribution) with one degree of freedom, the approximate size-$\alpha$ [rejection region](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#rejection-region) is

$$
\boxed{q-n-n\log(q/n)\geq F_1^{-1}(1-\alpha)}.
$$

The corresponding approximate [p-value](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#p-value) is

$$
\boxed{1-F_1\!\left(q_{\mathrm{obs}}-n-n\log(q_{\mathrm{obs}}/n)\right)}.
$$

The one degree of freedom here describes the large-sample [likelihood-ratio test statistic](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#likelihood-ratio-test-statistic); it is different from the exact residual degrees of freedom $n-1$ in the next part.

<h4 id="1/c/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#1/c)

<h5 id="1/c/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#1/c/iii)

The derivative of the log ratio is $\tfrac12(n/q-1)$, so $\lambda(q)$ increases from zero to one on $(0,n)$ and decreases to zero on $(n,\infty)$. Rejecting for a sufficiently small ratio therefore gives two tails with $0<q_1<n<q_2$.

Under $H_0$, [Cochran's theorem](../normal-linear-model.md#cochran-s-theorem) gives the exact [sampling distribution](../statistical-modelling.md#sampling-distribution) $Q=S/\sigma_0^2\sim\chi_{n-1}^2$, independently of the unknown mean. Writing $F_{n-1}$ for its [cumulative distribution function](../probability-distribution.md#cumulative-distribution-function), the two cutoffs are determined by

$$
\boxed{F_{n-1}(q_1)+1-F_{n-1}(q_2)=\alpha,\qquad n\log(q_2/q_1)=q_2-q_1}.
$$

The first equation calibrates the exact [size of a statistical test](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#size-of-a-statistical-test). The second is $\lambda(q_1)=\lambda(q_2)$, so both tails belong to one [likelihood ratio](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#likelihood-ratio) cutoff. Hence the exact [critical region](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#rejection-region) is $\boxed{\{q\leq q_1\}\cup\{q\geq q_2\}}$. Its two tail probabilities are not generally equal: equal-tailed chi-squared cutoffs give a different test.

<h3 id="2/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#2/a)

<h5 id="2/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#2/a/i)

For a [critical region](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#rejection-region) $C$, the [size of a statistical test](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#size-of-a-statistical-test) of the simple [null hypothesis](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#null-hypothesis) $P_0$ is $\alpha=P_0(\boldsymbol X\in C)$, the [Type I error](../information-theory.md#type-i-and-type-ii-errors) probability. Its [statistical power](../probability-and-statistics.md#statistical-power) against the simple [alternative hypothesis](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#alternative-hypothesis) $P_1$ is $P_1(\boldsymbol X\in C)$, the probability of correctly rejecting that null. For a randomized test function $\varphi(\boldsymbol x)\in[0,1]$, replace these by $\mathbb E_0\varphi$ and $\mathbb E_1\varphi$.

<h4 id="2/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#2/a)

<h5 id="2/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#2/a/ii)

The [Neyman-Pearson lemma](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#neyman-pearson-lemma) states that a test which rejects where the joint [likelihood ratio](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#likelihood-ratio) $L_1/L_0$ is largest is a [most powerful test](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#most-powerful-test) of its size: its power is at least that of any test with no larger size. More precisely, choose a threshold $k$ and reject if $L_1/L_0>k$, accept if it is smaller, and if necessary randomize on equality to attain the specified size.

For an independent and identically distributed [statistical sample](../statistical-inference.md#statistical-sample), take

$$
Y_i=\log\frac{f_0(X_i)}{f_1(X_i)}.
$$

The $Y_i$ are [independent and identically distributed random variables](../random-variable.md#independent-and-identically-distributed-random-variables) under either simple hypothesis, and the log joint ratio is their sum. Thus the nonrandomized threshold form can be written

$$
\boxed{\sum_{i=1}^nY_i\leq c}
$$

with $c=-\log k$, using either strict or non-strict inequality when the boundary has probability zero. The usual finite-log expression assumes positive common support; zero densities can instead be handled by the extended likelihood ratio on the relevant supports. Continuous observations alone do not ensure a continuous likelihood-ratio statistic: ties may require the boundary randomization in the lemma. This qualification preserves the exact-size formulation.

<h3 id="2/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

The joint [likelihood ratio](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#likelihood-ratio) in favor of the alternative is

$$
\frac{L_3}{L_2}=\left(\frac32\right)^n\prod_i x_i.
$$

It is increasing in the product, so the [Neyman-Pearson lemma](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#neyman-pearson-lemma) rejects for large product, equivalently small $S=-\sum_i\log x_i$. Under $H_0$, the transformation $Z_i=-\log X_i$ gives

$$
\mathbb P_0(Z_i>z)=\mathbb P_0(X_i<e^{-z})=e^{-2z},\qquad z\geq0.
$$

Therefore the $Z_i$ are [independent](../random-variable.md#independent-random-variables) [exponential random variables](../continuous-probability-distribution.md#exponential-distribution) of rate two, $S\sim\operatorname{Gamma}(n,\text{rate }2)$, and $4S\sim\chi_{2n}^2$. An exact size-$\alpha$ [critical region](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#rejection-region) is

$$
\boxed{S\leq c_\alpha},\qquad\boxed{F_{\chi_{2n}^2}(4c_\alpha)=\alpha}.
$$

Equivalently, reject when the [geometric mean](../arithmetic.md#geometric-mean) satisfies $g\geq e^{-c_\alpha/n}$. The boundary distribution is continuous, so no randomization is needed here.

<h3 id="2/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#2/c)

Under $H_0$, each $Z_i=-\log X_i$ has [expected value](../expected-value.md) $1/2$ and [variance](../variance.md) $1/4$. The [central limit theorem](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#central-limit-theorem) therefore approximates

$$
\frac{S-n/2}{\sqrt n/2}\ \mathrel{\dot\sim}\ N(0,1).
$$

For the supplied data this standardized value is

$$
\boxed{z_{\mathrm{obs}}=\frac{6.25-12.5}{2.5}=-2.5}.
$$

Using the provided approximation $\Phi(1.6)\approx0.95$, the lower-tail 5% rule rejects when $S\leq12.5-1.6(2.5)=8.5$. The observed $6.25$ is in this region, with approximate [p-value](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#p-value) $\Phi(-2.5)\approx0.005$. **Reject $H_0:\theta=2$ at the 5% level in favor of the specified alternative $\theta=3$.** The unusually large [geometric mean](../arithmetic.md#geometric-mean) is consistent with the direction of that alternative. This is evidence in this comparison, not a proof that the parameter is exactly three.

## 3

↑ **Parent:** [2023](2023.md)

<h3 id="3/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

<h5 id="3/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#3/a/i)

Given the observed data, a $100(1-\alpha)\%$ [credible interval](../bayesian-statistics.md#credible-interval) $[a,b]$ for the scalar parameter satisfies

$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(a\leq\theta\leq b\mid\boldsymbol x)=1-\alpha}.
$$

The probability refers to the [posterior distribution](../bayesian-statistics.md#bayesian-posterior), proportional to $\pi(\theta)\prod_i f(x_i\mid\theta)$ on the parameter space. It conditions on the observations; it is not the repeated-sampling [coverage probability](../statistical-inference.md#coverage-probability) definition of a [confidence interval](../statistical-inference.md#confidence-interval).

<h4 id="3/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

<h5 id="3/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#3/a/ii)

An [equal-tailed credible interval](../bayesian-statistics.md#equal-tailed-credible-interval) for a continuous [posterior distribution](../bayesian-statistics.md#bayesian-posterior) has probability $\alpha/2$ below its lower endpoint and $\alpha/2$ above its upper endpoint. If $F$ is the posterior [cumulative distribution function](../probability-distribution.md#cumulative-distribution-function), the endpoints are $F^{-1}(\alpha/2)$ and $F^{-1}(1-\alpha/2)$.

A [highest posterior density interval](../bayesian-statistics.md#highest-posterior-density-interval) is a [credible interval](../bayesian-statistics.md#credible-interval) consisting of points of highest [posterior density](../bayesian-statistics.md#posterior-density): every included point has density at least as high as every excluded point, apart from null-set and boundary choices. It is a posterior [highest density region](../statistical-inference.md#highest-density-region) when that region is an interval. For a continuous unimodal density with two interior endpoints, their densities agree. A mode at a support boundary can instead force one endpoint to that boundary; a multimodal posterior can have a disconnected highest-density region rather than one interval. Equal tails and highest density need not give the same interval.

<h3 id="3/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

<h5 id="3/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#3/b/i)

Writing $s=\sum_i x_i$, the [Poisson distribution](../discrete-probability-distribution.md#poisson-distribution) likelihood is proportional to $\theta^s e^{-n\theta}$. Multiplying by the [gamma distribution](../continuous-probability-distribution.md#gamma-distribution) prior gives

$$
\pi(\theta\mid\boldsymbol x)\propto\theta^{r+s-1}e^{-(\lambda+n)\theta}.
$$

This is [Poisson-gamma conjugacy](../bayesian-statistics.md#poisson-gamma-conjugacy), with the rate convention made explicit:

$$
\boxed{\theta\mid\boldsymbol x\sim\operatorname{Gamma}(r+s,\text{rate }\lambda+n)}.
$$

Its normalized density is $(\lambda+n)^{r+s}\theta^{r+s-1}e^{-(\lambda+n)\theta}/\Gamma(r+s)$ for $\theta>0$.

<h4 id="3/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

<h5 id="3/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#3/b/ii)

The [posterior mean](../bayesian-statistics.md#posterior-mean) is a [convex combination](../convex-set.md#convex-combination) of the [prior mean](../bayesian-statistics.md#prior-mean) and the usual [Poisson](../discrete-probability-distribution.md#poisson-distribution) [maximum-likelihood estimate](../maximum-likelihood-estimation.md#maximum-likelihood-estimator):

$$
\boxed{\mathbb E(\theta\mid\boldsymbol x)=\frac{r+s}{\lambda+n}=\frac\lambda{\lambda+n}\frac r\lambda+\frac n{\lambda+n}\bar x}.
$$

Both weights are positive and sum to one, so the mean lies between $r/\lambda$ and $\bar x$, strictly between them unless they coincide. If all counts are zero, $\bar x=0$ is the boundary estimate in the usual nonnegative Poisson parameter space; with a strictly positive parameter restriction it represents the likelihood supremum at that boundary. The displayed posterior-mean relation is valid in either interpretation.

<h3 id="3/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#3/c)

Let $M=\max\{b,y_1,\ldots,y_n\}$. The [continuous uniform distribution](../continuous-probability-distribution.md#continuous-uniform-distribution) likelihood contributes $\phi^{-n}\mathbf1_{\{\phi\geq\max_i y_i\}}$, while the [Pareto distribution](../continuous-probability-distribution.md#pareto-distribution) prior contributes $\phi^{-a-1}\mathbf1_{\{\phi\geq b\}}$. Their product proves [Uniform-Pareto conjugacy](../exponential-family.md#uniform-pareto-conjugacy):

$$
\boxed{p(\phi\mid\boldsymbol y)=(a+n)M^{a+n}\phi^{-(a+n+1)}\mathbf1_{\{\phi\geq M\}}}.
$$

Thus the [posterior distribution](../bayesian-statistics.md#bayesian-posterior) is Pareto with shape $a+n$ and lower bound $M$. Its density is strictly decreasing on its support, so the [highest posterior density interval](../bayesian-statistics.md#highest-posterior-density-interval) begins at $M$. Since the upper-tail probability is $(M/u)^{a+n}$, the required upper endpoint satisfies $(M/u)^{a+n}=0.05$. The [Pareto highest density interval](../continuous-probability-distribution.md#pareto-highest-density-interval) is therefore

$$
\boxed{[M,\ M\,20^{1/(a+n)}]}.
$$

<h3 id="3/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#3/d)

Write $m=r+s$ and $\beta=\lambda+n$. Conditional independence of the next count given $\theta$ allows us to average its [Poisson distribution](../discrete-probability-distribution.md#poisson-distribution) over the [posterior distribution](../bayesian-statistics.md#bayesian-posterior):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb P(X_{n+1}=k\mid\boldsymbol x)&=\int_0^\infty\frac{e^{-\theta}\theta^k}{k!}\frac{\beta^m}{\Gamma(m)}\theta^{m-1}e^{-\beta\theta}\,d\theta\\
&=\frac{\Gamma(k+m)}{\Gamma(m)k!}\frac{\beta^m}{(\beta+1)^{k+m}}\\
&=\binom{k+m-1}{k}\left(\frac\beta{\beta+1}\right)^m\left(\frac1{\beta+1}\right)^k.
\end{aligned}
$$

This [Poisson-gamma mixture](../discrete-probability-distribution.md#poisson-gamma-mixture) is the [posterior predictive distribution](../bayesian-statistics.md#posterior-predictive-distribution). In the stated [negative binomial distribution](../discrete-probability-distribution.md#negative-binomial-distribution) convention its parameters are

$$
\boxed{m=r+\sum_i x_i,\qquad p=\frac{\lambda+n}{\lambda+n+1}}.
$$

Its [expected value](../expected-value.md) $m/\beta$ equals the [posterior mean](../bayesian-statistics.md#posterior-mean) of $\theta$, while its [variance](../variance.md) $m/\beta+m/\beta^2$ includes both conditional Poisson variation and uncertainty in $\theta$.
