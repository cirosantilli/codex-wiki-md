<h3 id="1/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1/a)

<h5 id="1/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#1/a/i)

For the [beta distribution](../probability-distribution.md#beta-distribution) with parameters $(\theta,1)$, the [Euler beta function](../complex-analysis.md#beta-function) gives $B(\theta,1)=1/\theta$. Its [probability density function](../continuous-probability-distribution.md#probability-density-function) is therefore $\theta x^{\theta-1}$ on $(0,1)$. Direct [integration](../integral.md) gives the [raw moments](../expected-value.md#raw-moment)

$$
\mathbb E_\theta X^r=\theta\int_0^1x^{\theta+r-1}\,dx=\frac{\theta}{\theta+r},\qquad r> -\theta.
$$

In particular, its [mean](../expected-value.md) is $\mu(\theta)=\theta/(\theta+1)$. Equating this to the [sample mean](../variance.md#sample-mean) and rearranging gives

$$
\theta=\bar X(1+\theta),\qquad
\boxed{T_1=\frac{\bar X}{1-\bar X}}.
$$

The observations lie strictly between zero and one with [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability) one, so $0<\bar X<1$ and this [method of moments](../statistical-modelling.md#method-of-moments-statistics) [estimator](../statistical-modelling.md#estimator) is well defined and positive. This is an instance of [estimation of a beta shape parameter](../probability-distribution.md#estimation-of-a-beta-shape-parameter).

<h4 id="1/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1/a)

<h5 id="1/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#1/a/ii)

Using the [raw moments](../expected-value.md#raw-moment) from part (i), the [variance](../variance.md) of one observation is

$$
\operatorname{Var}_\theta(X)=\frac{\theta}{\theta+2}-\frac{\theta^2}{(\theta+1)^2}
=\frac{\theta}{(\theta+1)^2(\theta+2)}.
$$

The [central limit theorem](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#central-limit-theorem) for the independent [statistical sample](../statistical-inference.md#statistical-sample) gives

$$
\sqrt n(\bar X-\mu)\xrightarrow d
N\left(0,\frac{\theta}{(\theta+1)^2(\theta+2)}\right).
$$

For $g(x)=x/(1-x)$, its [derivative](../calculus.md#derivative) at the [mean](../expected-value.md) is $g'(\mu)=(1-\mu)^{-2}=(\theta+1)^2$. The [delta method](../statistical-inference.md#delta-method) therefore gives

$$
\boxed{\sqrt n(T_1-\theta)\xrightarrow d
N\left(0,\frac{\theta(\theta+1)^2}{\theta+2}\right)}.
$$

Equivalently, the large-sample [normal approximation](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#normal-approximation) is

$$
T_1\ \mathrel{\dot\sim}\ N\left(\theta,\frac{\theta(\theta+1)^2}{n(\theta+2)}\right),
\qquad v(T_1)=\frac{\theta(\theta+1)^2}{n(\theta+2)}.
$$

Here $v$ denotes the leading [asymptotic variance](../statistical-modelling.md#asymptotic-variance), rather than an assertion about an exact finite-sample [variance](../variance.md).

<h4 id="1/a/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1/a)

<h5 id="1/a/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#1/a/iii)

Write $A=-\sum_i\log X_i>0$, with $\log$ the [natural logarithm](../calculus.md#natural-logarithm). The [likelihood function](../maximum-likelihood-estimation.md#likelihood-function) and [log-likelihood function](../maximum-likelihood-estimation.md#log-likelihood) are

$$
L(\theta)=\theta^n\exp\{-(\theta-1)A\},\qquad
\ell(\theta)=n\log\theta-(\theta-1)A.
$$

The [score function](../statistical-modelling.md#informant-function) is $\ell'(\theta)=n/\theta-A$ and the second [derivative](../calculus.md#derivative) is $\ell''(\theta)=-n/\theta^2<0$. Thus the [log-likelihood function](../maximum-likelihood-estimation.md#log-likelihood) is strictly [concave](../real-analysis.md#concave-function), its stationary point is the unique global maximum, and the [maximum-likelihood estimator](../maximum-likelihood-estimation.md#maximum-likelihood-estimator) is

$$
\boxed{T_2=\widehat\theta=\frac nA=-\frac n{\sum_i\log X_i}}.
$$

The one-observation [Fisher information](../statistical-modelling.md#fisher-information-matrix) is $I(\theta)=1/\theta^2$. The [support of a probability distribution](../probability-distribution.md#support-of-a-probability-distribution) here is independent of $\theta>0$, so the regular [asymptotic normality of a maximum likelihood estimator](../maximum-likelihood-estimation.md#asymptotic-normality-of-a-maximum-likelihood-estimator) gives

$$
\boxed{\sqrt n(T_2-\theta)\xrightarrow dN(0,\theta^2)},\qquad
T_2\ \mathrel{\dot\sim}\ N(\theta,\theta^2/n),\qquad v(T_2)=\theta^2/n.
$$

There is also a direct check: $U_i=-\log X_i$ has an [exponential distribution](../continuous-probability-distribution.md#exponential-distribution) with rate $\theta$, since $\mathbb P(U_i>u)=\mathbb P(X_i<e^{-u})=e^{-\theta u}$ for $u>0$. Its [mean](../expected-value.md) and [variance](../variance.md) are $1/\theta$ and $1/\theta^2$. Apply the [central limit theorem](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#central-limit-theorem) to $A/n$ and the [delta method](../statistical-inference.md#delta-method) to $h(u)=1/u$ to obtain the same result.

<h4 id="1/a/iv">iv</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1/a)

<h5 id="1/a/iv/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#1/a/iv)

The specified [asymptotic relative efficiency](../statistical-inference.md#asymptotic-relative-efficiency) is

$$
\boxed{\frac{v(T_2)}{v(T_1)}
=\frac{\theta(\theta+2)}{(\theta+1)^2}
=1-\frac1{(\theta+1)^2}}.
$$

For every fixed $\theta>0$ this lies strictly between zero and one. **Prefer $T_2$ on grounds of asymptotic precision**: it has the smaller leading [asymptotic variance](../statistical-modelling.md#asymptotic-variance) and attains the inverse [Fisher information](../statistical-modelling.md#fisher-information-matrix). Both are [consistent estimators](../statistical-inference.md#consistency-statistics), by the [law of large numbers](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#law-of-large-numbers) and the [continuous mapping theorem](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#continuous-mapping-theorem) applied respectively to $\bar X$ and $A/n$. The efficiency approaches zero as $\theta\downarrow0$ and one as $\theta\to\infty$.

This preference is an asymptotic comparison. In particular, it does not assert that $T_2$ is an [unbiased estimator](../statistical-modelling.md#unbiased-estimator): since $A$ has a [gamma distribution](../continuous-probability-distribution.md#gamma-distribution) of shape $n$ and rate $\theta$, $\mathbb E T_2=n\theta/(n-1)$ for $n>1$.

<h4 id="1/a/v">v</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1/a)

<h5 id="1/a/v/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [V](#1/a/v)

The [standard error](../statistical-inference.md#standard-error) suggested by part (iii) is $\theta/\sqrt n$. Substituting the [consistent estimator](../statistical-inference.md#consistency-statistics) $T_2$ gives $T_2/\sqrt n$; [Slutsky's theorem](../statistical-inference.md#slutsky-theorem) gives

$$
\frac{\sqrt n(T_2-\theta)}{T_2}\xrightarrow dN(0,1).
$$

Using the central [standard normal quantiles](../probability-distribution.md#standard-normal-quantile) $\pm1.96$, an approximate 95% [Wald confidence interval](../statistical-inference.md#wald-confidence-interval) is

$$
\boxed{\left[T_2\left(1-\frac{1.96}{\sqrt n}\right),\quad
T_2\left(1+\frac{1.96}{\sqrt n}\right)\right]\cap(0,\infty)}.
$$

Its [coverage probability](../statistical-inference.md#coverage-probability) approaches 0.95 for each fixed $\theta>0$ as $n\to\infty$. The intersection enforces positivity and does not change whether the true positive [statistical parameter](../statistical-model.md#statistical-parameter) is covered. The value 1.64 would give the wrong central two-sided [coverage probability](../statistical-inference.md#coverage-probability).

<h3 id="1/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

<h5 id="1/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#1/b/i)

Put $A=-\sum_i\log x_i>0$, $B=-\sum_j\log y_j>0$, and $N=n+m$. The two independent [statistical samples](../statistical-inference.md#statistical-sample) give the joint [likelihood function](../maximum-likelihood-estimation.md#likelihood-function)

$$
L(\theta,\tau)=\theta^n\tau^m\exp\{-(\theta-1)A-(\tau-1)B\}.
$$

The unrestricted [maximum-likelihood estimators](../maximum-likelihood-estimation.md#maximum-likelihood-estimator) are $\widehat\theta=n/A$ and $\widehat\tau=m/B$. Under the [null hypothesis](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#null-hypothesis), the common [statistical parameter](../statistical-model.md#statistical-parameter) $\eta$ has [log-likelihood function](../maximum-likelihood-estimation.md#log-likelihood) $N\log\eta-(\eta-1)(A+B)$, maximized at $\widehat\eta=N/(A+B)$. With the [likelihood ratio](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#likelihood-ratio) defined as the null maximum divided by the unrestricted maximum,

$$
\lambda=\frac{(N/(A+B))^Ne^{-N+A+B}}
{(n/A)^n(m/B)^me^{-N+A+B}}
=\frac{N^N}{n^nm^m}\frac{A^nB^m}{(A+B)^N}.
$$

The statistic in the paper is $T=A/(A+B)$, since both original sums of [natural logarithms](../calculus.md#natural-logarithm) are negative. Hence

$$
\boxed{\lambda=c(n,m)T^n(1-T)^m,\qquad
c(n,m)=\frac{(n+m)^{n+m}}{n^nm^m}}.
$$

It has maximum one at $T=n/N$, corresponding to equal fitted rates. This is the [likelihood-ratio test for equality of beta shape parameters](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#likelihood-ratio-test-for-equality-of-beta-shape-parameters).

<h4 id="1/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

<h5 id="1/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#1/b/ii)

A [likelihood-ratio test](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#likelihood-ratio-test) rejects for small $\lambda$. The [derivative](../calculus.md#derivative) of the [natural logarithm](../calculus.md#natural-logarithm) of its variable factor is

$$
\frac d{dt}\{n\log t+m\log(1-t)\}
=\frac{n-(n+m)t}{t(1-t)}.
$$

Thus $t^n(1-t)^m$ increases up to $n/(n+m)$ and decreases thereafter, tending to zero at both endpoints. Every nontrivial lower [level set](../topology.md#level-set) of $\lambda$ therefore consists of two tails. For a prescribed [test size](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#size-of-a-statistical-test) $0<\alpha<1$, the exact [critical region](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#rejection-region) is

$$
\boxed{T<k_1\quad\text{or}\quad T>k_2},\qquad
0<k_1<\frac n{n+m}<k_2<1.
$$

Writing $F_{n,m}$ for the [cumulative distribution function](../probability-distribution.md#cumulative-distribution-function) of the [beta distribution](../probability-distribution.md#beta-distribution) with parameters $(n,m)$, the required two equations are

$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
k_1^n(1-k_1)^m&=k_2^n(1-k_2)^m,\\
F_{n,m}(k_1)+1-F_{n,m}(k_2)&=\alpha.
\end{aligned}}
$$

The first makes both cutoffs have the same [likelihood ratio](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#likelihood-ratio); the second calibrates the [null hypothesis](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#null-hypothesis) rejection [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability). Equivalently, the second left-hand side is

$$
\frac1{B(n,m)}\left\{\int_0^{k_1}t^{n-1}(1-t)^{m-1}\,dt+
\int_{k_2}^1t^{n-1}(1-t)^{m-1}\,dt\right\}.
$$

As the common [likelihood ratio](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#likelihood-ratio) threshold increases from zero to one, this [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability) increases continuously and strictly from zero to one; hence the equations determine unique cutoffs. **The two tail probabilities need not each equal $\alpha/2$** when $n\ne m$. Exact calibration holds for every common rate because the given [null distribution](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#null-distribution) of $T$ is independent of that rate. Boundary equalities have zero [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability).

↑ **Parent:** [A](#2/a)

<h5 id="2/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#2/a/i)

**False.** A [test size](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#size-of-a-statistical-test) constrains the [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability) of rejection conditional on the [null hypothesis](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#null-hypothesis) being true:

$$
\mathbb P_{H_0}(\text{reject }H_0)=\alpha
$$

for an exact size-$\alpha$ test of a [simple hypothesis](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#simple-hypothesis). It does not assign a [posterior probability](../bayesian-statistics.md#posterior-probability) to that [statistical hypothesis](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#statistical-hypothesis) after observing nonrejection. In the frequentist interpretation, the [statistical parameter](../statistical-model.md#statistical-parameter) is fixed and the data are random. Nonrejection can occur even when the [alternative hypothesis](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#alternative-hypothesis) is true; for example, it is particularly likely for an alternative against which the test has low [statistical power](../probability-and-statistics.md#statistical-power). A [posterior probability](../bayesian-statistics.md#posterior-probability) would require a [prior distribution](../bayesian-statistics.md#prior-probability) and a Bayesian model.

<h4 id="2/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#2/a)

<h5 id="2/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#2/a/ii)

**True, with the test's rejection rule fixed.** If its [rejection region](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#rejection-region) in the space of the [test statistic](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#test-statistic) is $C$, its [statistical power](../probability-and-statistics.md#statistical-power) against the specified [simple hypothesis](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#simple-hypothesis) $H_1$ is

$$
\boxed{\mathbb P_{H_1}(T\in C)}.
$$

Thus the [probability distribution](../probability-distribution.md) of $T$ under the [alternative hypothesis](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#alternative-hypothesis) determines the [statistical power](../probability-and-statistics.md#statistical-power) of this test. The [null distribution](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#null-distribution) is used to choose a rule with the desired [test size](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#size-of-a-statistical-test), but [statistical power](../probability-and-statistics.md#statistical-power) is evaluated under the [alternative hypothesis](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#alternative-hypothesis). Different rejection rules can have different [statistical power](../probability-and-statistics.md#statistical-power) even for the same [probability distribution](../probability-distribution.md) of $T$.

<h4 id="2/a/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#2/a)

<h5 id="2/a/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#2/a/iii)

**False.** For the test associated with a [p-value](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#p-value), reject at [significance level](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#significance-level) $\alpha$ when $p\leq\alpha$. <h3 id="2/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

<h5 id="2/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#2/b/i)

Let $S=\sum_iX_i$, which has a [binomial distribution](../discrete-probability-distribution.md#binomial-distribution) with parameters $(n,p)$. For $0<p_1<1/2$, the alternative-to-null [likelihood ratio](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#likelihood-ratio) of the independent [Bernoulli trials](../discrete-probability-distribution.md#bernoulli-trial) is

$$
R(S)=\frac{p_1^S(1-p_1)^{n-S}}{(1/2)^n}
=[2(1-p_1)]^n\left(\frac{p_1}{1-p_1}\right)^S.
$$

Since $p_1/(1-p_1)<1$, this is strictly decreasing in $S$. A threshold on large $R$ is therefore a lower-tail [likelihood-ratio test](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#likelihood-ratio-test), and a threshold between $R(2)$ and $R(1)$ gives the stated [critical region](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#rejection-region) $S<2$. The [Neyman-Pearson lemma](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#neyman-pearson-lemma) also proves it is a [most powerful test](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#most-powerful-test) at its attained [test size](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#size-of-a-statistical-test).

Using the [binomial probability mass](../discrete-probability-distribution.md#binomial-probability-mass), its [test size](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#size-of-a-statistical-test) and [statistical power](../probability-and-statistics.md#statistical-power) are respectively

$$
\boxed{\mathbb P_{1/2}(S<2)=\frac{1+n}{2^n}},\qquad
\boxed{\mathbb P_{p_1}(S<2)=(1-p_1)^n+np_1(1-p_1)^{n-1}}.
$$

The associated [power function](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#power-function-of-a-statistical-test) is $\pi(p)=(1-p)^n+np(1-p)^{n-1}$. These formulas hold for positive integer $n$; when $n=1$ the test always rejects and has size one. If the boundary value $p_1=0$ is admitted, all alternative [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability) is at $S=0$; the same test still has [statistical power](../probability-and-statistics.md#statistical-power) one and can be represented using a threshold with ties in the [likelihood ratio](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#likelihood-ratio).

<h4 id="2/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

<h5 id="2/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#2/b/ii)

For any fixed $p>1/4$, the alternative-to-null [likelihood ratio](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#likelihood-ratio) is

$$
R_p(S)=\left(\frac{1-p}{3/4}\right)^5
\left(\frac{3p}{1-p}\right)^S,
$$

which is strictly increasing in $S$ for $1/4<p<1$. Consequently the [Neyman-Pearson lemma](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#neyman-pearson-lemma) selects the same upper-tail ordering for every such [alternative hypothesis](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#alternative-hypothesis). At the [null hypothesis](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#null-hypothesis) value,

$$
\mathbb P_{1/4}(S\geq4)
=\binom54\left(\frac14\right)^4\frac34+\left(\frac14\right)^5
=\frac{15+1}{1024}=\frac1{64}.
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{\text{reject }H_0\text{ exactly when }S\geq4}
$$

is a [uniformly most powerful test](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#uniformly-most-powerful-test) of the required exact [test size](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#size-of-a-statistical-test). Its [power function](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#power-function-of-a-statistical-test) is $5p^4(1-p)+p^5=5p^4-4p^5$. At $p=1$ its [statistical power](../probability-and-statistics.md#statistical-power) is one, so including that boundary alternative preserves the conclusion.

<h4 id="2/b/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

<h5 id="2/b/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#2/b/iii)

Condition on the total of 400 complaints. Assume each complaint independently has the same [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability) $p$ of being received on Monday; in particular, repeated complaints from one incident must not create material dependence. The Monday count $S$ then has a [binomial distribution](../discrete-probability-distribution.md#binomial-distribution) with parameters $(400,p)$. Test $H_0:p=0.2$ against $H_1:p>0.2$, rejecting for large $S$ as suggested by the [monotone likelihood ratio](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#monotone-likelihood-ratio).

Under the [null hypothesis](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#null-hypothesis), $\mathbb E_0S=80$ and $\operatorname{Var}_0(S)=400(0.2)(0.8)=64$. Both expected successes and expected failures are large, so the [central limit theorem](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#central-limit-theorem) gives a suitable [normal approximation](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#normal-approximation):

$$
Z=\frac{S-80}{8}\ \mathrel{\dot\sim}\ N(0,1),\qquad
\boxed{z_{\rm obs}=\frac{100-80}{8}=2.5}.
$$

Using the paper's supplied rounded table, the upper-tail [p-value](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#p-value) is approximately

$$
\boxed{p_{\rm approx}=1-\Phi(2.5)\approx0.005}.
$$

A more accurate evaluation of the [standard normal distribution](../probability-distribution.md#standard-normal-distribution) [cumulative distribution function](../probability-distribution.md#cumulative-distribution-function) gives about 0.0062. For example, choose [significance level](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#significance-level) 0.05 before examining the data; the supplied upper 5% [standard normal quantile](../probability-distribution.md#standard-normal-quantile) is approximately 1.6, and $2.5>1.6$. **Reject $H_0$ at 5%, and also at 1%, using this approximation.** The observed [binomial proportion](../discrete-probability-distribution.md#binomial-proportion) is $100/400=0.25$, providing evidence that the Monday [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability) exceeds 0.2 under the sampling assumptions.

If applying a [continuity correction](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#continuity-correction), approximate $\mathbb P_0(S\geq100)$ by $1-\Phi((99.5-80)/8)=1-\Phi(2.4375)\approx0.0074$; this gives the same 5% and 1% conclusions. The uncorrected value 0.005 is approximate, so a decision exactly at the 0.5% boundary is not warranted by the supplied rounded table.

<h3 id="2/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#2/c)

Here the [gamma distribution](../continuous-probability-distribution.md#gamma-distribution) uses a rate [statistical parameter](../statistical-model.md#statistical-parameter) $\theta$, with known shape $\lambda$. Write $S=\sum_iX_i$ and $a=n\lambda$. For each fixed $\theta_1>\theta_0$, the alternative-to-null [likelihood ratio](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#likelihood-ratio) is

$$
\frac{L(\theta_1)}{L(\theta_0)}
=\left(\frac{\theta_1}{\theta_0}\right)^a
\exp\{-(\theta_1-\theta_0)S\},
$$

strictly decreasing in $S$. The [Neyman-Pearson lemma](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#neyman-pearson-lemma) therefore gives the same lower-tail [critical region](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#rejection-region) $S<c_\alpha$ for every larger rate. Since $S$ has a continuous [gamma distribution](../continuous-probability-distribution.md#gamma-distribution) under the [null hypothesis](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#null-hypothesis), there is a unique positive $c_\alpha$ satisfying

$$
\mathbb P_{\theta_0}(S<c_\alpha)=\alpha.
$$

Thus **reject when $S<c_\alpha$** is a [uniformly most powerful test](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#uniformly-most-powerful-test) of exact [test size](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#size-of-a-statistical-test) $\alpha$. If $G_a$ denotes the unit-rate [gamma distribution](../continuous-probability-distribution.md#gamma-distribution) [cumulative distribution function](../probability-distribution.md#cumulative-distribution-function) with shape $a$, then

$$
c_\alpha=\frac{G_a^{-1}(\alpha)}{\theta_0},\qquad
\pi(\theta)=G_a(\theta c_\alpha).
$$

This is the [uniformly most powerful test for a gamma rate](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#uniformly-most-powerful-test-for-a-gamma-rate).

When $\lambda=1/n$, the sum has shape one and hence an [exponential distribution](../continuous-probability-distribution.md#exponential-distribution) with rate $\theta$. Therefore

$$
1-e^{-\theta_0c_\alpha}=\alpha,\qquad
\boxed{c_\alpha=-\frac{\log(1-\alpha)}{\theta_0}},\qquad
\boxed{\pi(\theta)=1-(1-\alpha)^{\theta/\theta_0}}.
$$

In particular $\pi(\theta_0)=\alpha$, and the [power function](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#power-function-of-a-statistical-test) is strictly increasing, exceeding $\alpha$ for $\theta>\theta_0$ and tending to one as $\theta\to\infty$.

## 3

↑ **Parent:** [2021](2021.md)

<h3 id="3/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

<h5 id="3/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#3/a/i)

Set $S=\sum_i x_i$ and $N=nm$. Conditional [independence](../random-variable.md#independent-random-variables) of the [binomial distribution](../discrete-probability-distribution.md#binomial-distribution) observations gives the [likelihood function](../maximum-likelihood-estimation.md#likelihood-function)

$$
L(\theta)=\left\{\prod_{i=1}^n\binom m{x_i}\right\}
\theta^S(1-\theta)^{N-S}.
$$

The [prior distribution](../bayesian-statistics.md#prior-probability) is the [beta distribution](../probability-distribution.md#beta-distribution) with parameters $(1,\beta)$. By [Bayes' theorem](../probability-theory.md#bayes-theorem), multiplying the [prior density](../bayesian-statistics.md#prior-density) by the [likelihood function](../maximum-likelihood-estimation.md#likelihood-function) gives a [posterior density](../bayesian-statistics.md#posterior-density) proportional to

$$
\theta^S(1-\theta)^{N-S+\beta-1}.
$$

Recognizing and normalizing this [beta distribution](../probability-distribution.md#beta-distribution) gives

$$
\boxed{\theta\mid\boldsymbol x\sim\operatorname{Beta}(S+1,N-S+\beta)},\qquad
\boxed{\pi(\theta\mid\boldsymbol x)=
\frac{\theta^S(1-\theta)^{N-S+\beta-1}}{B(S+1,N-S+\beta)}}
$$

for $0<\theta<1$. This is [Beta-binomial conjugacy](../bayesian-statistics.md#beta-binomial-conjugacy); the original binomial coefficients cancel in the normalization because they are independent of $\theta$.

<h4 id="3/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

<h5 id="3/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#3/a/ii)

The [prior mean](../bayesian-statistics.md#prior-mean), [posterior mean](../bayesian-statistics.md#posterior-mean), and [maximum-likelihood estimate](../maximum-likelihood-estimation.md#maximum-likelihood-estimator) are

$$
\widetilde\theta_0=\frac1{\beta+1},\qquad
\widetilde\theta_1=\frac{S+1}{N+\beta+1},\qquad
\widehat\theta=\frac SN.
$$

For $0<S<N$, the last follows by maximizing $S\log\theta+(N-S)\log(1-\theta)$. At $S=0$ or $S=N$, use the usual [binomial likelihood](../discrete-probability-distribution.md#binomial-likelihood) convention that admits the endpoints $0$ and $1$ as [maximum-likelihood estimates](../maximum-likelihood-estimation.md#maximum-likelihood-estimator); if the parameter domain is strictly $(0,1)$, these are boundary suprema rather than attained maxima. The [posterior mean](../bayesian-statistics.md#posterior-mean) remains interior in all cases.

Taking

$$
\boxed{\lambda=\frac{\beta+1}{N+\beta+1}}
$$

gives

$$
\lambda\widetilde\theta_0+(1-\lambda)\widehat\theta
=\frac1{N+\beta+1}+\frac S{N+\beta+1}
=\boxed{\widetilde\theta_1}.
$$

Since $N=nm>0$ and $\beta+1>0$, **$0<\lambda<1$**. Thus the [posterior mean](../bayesian-statistics.md#posterior-mean) is a [convex combination](../convex-set.md#convex-combination) of the [prior mean](../bayesian-statistics.md#prior-mean) and observed [binomial proportion](../discrete-probability-distribution.md#binomial-proportion), with larger prior weight when $\beta+1$ is large relative to $N$.

<h4 id="3/a/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

<h5 id="3/a/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#3/a/iii)

The [prior mean](../bayesian-statistics.md#prior-mean) $1/(\beta+1)$ decreases as $\beta$ increases. More strongly, its [cumulative distribution function](../probability-distribution.md#cumulative-distribution-function) and upper-tail [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability) are

$$
F_\beta(t)=1-(1-t)^\beta,\qquad
\mathbb P_\beta(\theta>t)=(1-t)^\beta,\qquad 0<t<1.
$$

For every fixed $t$, that upper-tail [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability) decreases with $\beta$. The statistician increasingly favors smaller values of $\theta$; as $\beta\to\infty$, the [prior distribution](../bayesian-statistics.md#prior-probability) concentrates near zero. For $\beta>1$, its [variance](../variance.md) $\beta/[(\beta+1)^2(\beta+2)]$ also decreases. This is [concentration of a beta prior near zero](../probability-distribution.md#concentration-of-a-beta-prior-near-zero).

With the data fixed, the [derivatives](../calculus.md#derivative) are

$$
\boxed{\frac{d\lambda}{d\beta}=\frac{N}{(N+\beta+1)^2}>0},\qquad
\boxed{\frac{d\widetilde\theta_1}{d\beta}
=-\frac{S+1}{(N+\beta+1)^2}<0}.
$$

Thus **the prior weight increases and the posterior mean decreases**. Their limits are $\lambda\to1$ and $\widetilde\theta_1\to0$. Both the changing [prior mean](../bayesian-statistics.md#prior-mean) and the increasing weight contribute to the change in the [posterior mean](../bayesian-statistics.md#posterior-mean).

<h3 id="3/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

<h5 id="3/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#3/b/i)

Let $S_{xx}=\sum_i x_i^2$, $S_{xy}=\sum_i x_iy_i$, and $\bar y=n^{-1}\sum_i y_i$. The independent observations in the [normal linear model](../normal-linear-model.md) give

$$
L(\alpha,\beta)\propto
\exp\left\{-\frac1{2\sigma^2}\sum_i(y_i-\alpha-\beta x_i)^2\right\}.
$$

The centered design condition $\sum_i x_i=0$ removes the $\alpha\beta$ term:

$$
\sum_i(y_i-\alpha-\beta x_i)^2
=\sum_i y_i^2+n\alpha^2+S_{xx}\beta^2
-2n\bar y\alpha-2S_{xy}\beta.
$$

The stated [prior density](../bayesian-statistics.md#prior-density) factors into independent [normal distributions](../probability-distribution.md#normal-distribution) for the [regression intercept](../linear-regression.md#regression-intercept) $\alpha$ and [regression coefficient](../linear-regression.md#regression-coefficient) $\beta$. Multiplication by the [likelihood function](../maximum-likelihood-estimation.md#likelihood-function) therefore leaves separate quadratic expressions in the two coefficients. Define the positive posterior [variances](../variance.md)

$$
v_\alpha=\left(\frac n{\sigma^2}+\frac1{\tau_0^2}\right)^{-1},\qquad
v_\beta=\left(\frac{S_{xx}}{\sigma^2}+\frac1{\tau_1^2}\right)^{-1},
$$

and the [posterior means](../bayesian-statistics.md#posterior-mean)

$$
m_\alpha=v_\alpha\left(\frac{n\bar y}{\sigma^2}+\frac{\alpha_0}{\tau_0^2}\right),\qquad
m_\beta=v_\beta\left(\frac{S_{xy}}{\sigma^2}+\frac{\beta_0}{\tau_1^2}\right).
$$

By [completing the square](../polynomial.md#completing-the-square), the joint [posterior density](../bayesian-statistics.md#posterior-density) is

$$
\boxed{\pi(\alpha,\beta\mid\boldsymbol y)
=\frac1{2\pi\sqrt{v_\alpha v_\beta}}
\exp\left\{-\frac{(\alpha-m_\alpha)^2}{2v_\alpha}
-\frac{(\beta-m_\beta)^2}{2v_\beta}\right\}}.
$$

Equivalently,

$$
\boxed{\alpha\mid\boldsymbol y\sim N(m_\alpha,v_\alpha),\qquad
\beta\mid\boldsymbol y\sim N(m_\beta,v_\beta),\qquad
\alpha\perp\beta\mid\boldsymbol y}.
$$

This is [Gaussian conjugacy for a normal linear model](../normal-linear-model.md#gaussian-conjugacy-for-a-normal-linear-model). Each posterior [precision parameter](../statistical-modelling.md#precision-parameter) is the sum of its data and prior [precision parameters](../statistical-modelling.md#precision-parameter). The formulas remain valid when $S_{xx}=0$: all $x_i$ are then zero, so the [posterior distribution](../bayesian-statistics.md#bayesian-posterior) of $\beta$ equals its [prior distribution](../bayesian-statistics.md#prior-probability).

<h4 id="3/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

<h5 id="3/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#3/b/ii)

Put $M_0=\alpha+x_0\beta$. As a [linear combination of independent normal random variables](../probability-distribution.md#linear-combination-of-independent-normal-random-variables) under the [posterior distribution](../bayesian-statistics.md#bayesian-posterior),

$$
M_0\mid\boldsymbol y\sim N(m_0,v_0),\qquad
m_0=m_\alpha+x_0m_\beta,\qquad
v_0=v_\alpha+x_0^2v_\beta.
$$

The shortest 95% [credible interval](../bayesian-statistics.md#credible-interval) is

$$
\boxed{\left[m_\alpha+x_0m_\beta
-\Phi^{-1}(0.975)\sqrt{v_\alpha+x_0^2v_\beta},\quad
m_\alpha+x_0m_\beta
+\Phi^{-1}(0.975)\sqrt{v_\alpha+x_0^2v_\beta}\right]}.
$$

Here $\Phi$ is the [standard normal distribution](../probability-distribution.md#standard-normal-distribution) [cumulative distribution function](../probability-distribution.md#cumulative-distribution-function). To justify shortest length, consider all intervals of a fixed length $d$. If $f$ is this [normal distribution](../probability-distribution.md#normal-distribution) [posterior density](../bayesian-statistics.md#posterior-density), the interval probability $\int_a^{a+d}f(t)\,dt$ has [derivative](../calculus.md#derivative) $f(a+d)-f(a)$ with respect to $a$. The [normal distribution](../probability-distribution.md#normal-distribution) is symmetric and its density strictly decreases with distance from its [mean](../expected-value.md), so this probability is uniquely maximized when the midpoint is $m_0$. The centered interval can therefore attain 95% [posterior probability](../bayesian-statistics.md#posterior-probability) with the least length. It is both an [equal-tailed credible interval](../bayesian-statistics.md#equal-tailed-credible-interval) and a [highest posterior density interval](../bayesian-statistics.md#highest-posterior-density-interval).

The target is the experiment's [mean](../expected-value.md) at $x_0$. A [posterior predictive distribution](../bayesian-statistics.md#posterior-predictive-distribution) for a new noisy observation would instead have [variance](../variance.md) $v_0+\sigma^2$; that extra observation [variance](../variance.md) does not belong in the requested interval.

<h3 id="3/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#3/c)

An unknown [variance](../variance.md) requires a joint [prior distribution](../bayesian-statistics.md#prior-probability) for $(\alpha,\beta,\sigma^2)$. **The analysis is not determined until a prior for the unknown variance, and its relation to the coefficients, is specified.** A [maximum-likelihood estimate](../maximum-likelihood-estimation.md#maximum-likelihood-estimator) substituted for $\sigma^2$ does not by itself account for its posterior uncertainty.

For example, retain the independent coefficient [prior distributions](../bayesian-statistics.md#prior-probability) from part (b) and independently assign $s=\sigma^2$ an [inverse-gamma distribution](../continuous-probability-distribution.md#inverse-gamma-distribution) with shape $a_0>0$ and scale $b_0>0$, whose [probability density function](../continuous-probability-distribution.md#probability-density-function) is proportional to $s^{-a_0-1}e^{-b_0/s}$. Then the joint [posterior density](../bayesian-statistics.md#posterior-density) is proportional to

$$
s^{-a_0-1-n/2}\exp\left\{-\frac{b_0+\frac12\sum_i(y_i-\alpha-\beta x_i)^2}{s}
-\frac{(\alpha-\alpha_0)^2}{2\tau_0^2}
-\frac{(\beta-\beta_0)^2}{2\tau_1^2}\right\}.
$$

Conditional on $s$, the two coefficient [posterior distributions](../bayesian-statistics.md#bayesian-posterior) are still the independent [normal distributions](../probability-distribution.md#normal-distribution) in part (b), with $s$ substituted for $\sigma^2$. The other [full conditional distribution](../probability-theory.md#full-conditional-distribution) is

$$
s\mid\alpha,\beta,\boldsymbol y\sim
\operatorname{InvGamma}\left(a_0+\frac n2,
b_0+\frac12\sum_i(y_i-\alpha-\beta x_i)^2\right).
$$

The marginal coefficient [posterior distribution](../bayesian-statistics.md#bayesian-posterior) requires [marginalization](../probability-distribution.md#marginalization) over $s$; it is generally nonnormal and the two coefficients need not remain independent. In particular, the posterior [cumulative distribution function](../probability-distribution.md#cumulative-distribution-function) of the requested [mean](../expected-value.md) becomes

$$
\mathbb P(M_0\leq t\mid\boldsymbol y)
=\int_0^\infty
\Phi\left(\frac{t-m_\alpha(s)-x_0m_\beta(s)}
{\sqrt{v_\alpha(s)+x_0^2v_\beta(s)}}\right)
\pi(s\mid\boldsymbol y)\,ds.
$$

Its shortest 95% [credible interval](../bayesian-statistics.md#credible-interval) must be found from this marginal [posterior distribution](../bayesian-statistics.md#bayesian-posterior), for example by numerical [integration](../integral.md) or posterior simulation. This is [normal regression with independent coefficient and variance priors](../normal-linear-model.md#normal-regression-with-independent-coefficient-and-variance-priors).

A different, convenient choice is a [normal-inverse-gamma prior](../exponential-family.md#normal-inverse-gamma-prior), in which the conditional coefficient prior [covariance matrix](../variance.md#covariance-matrix) is proportional to $\sigma^2$. That choice gives a multivariate [Student's t-distribution](../continuous-probability-distribution.md#student-s-t-distribution) after [marginalization](../probability-distribution.md#marginalization), so the interval uses its univariate [Student's t-distribution](../continuous-probability-distribution.md#student-s-t-distribution) [quantiles](../probability-distribution.md#quantile-function). It changes the coefficient prior specification; a Student interval does not follow automatically from retaining the fixed-$\tau_0,\tau_1$ priors in part (b).
