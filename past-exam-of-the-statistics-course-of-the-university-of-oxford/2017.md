<h4 id="1/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1/a)

<h5 id="1/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#1/a/i)

For the [independent](../random-variable.md#independent-random-variables) [statistical sample](../statistical-inference.md#statistical-sample), write the [likelihood function](../maximum-likelihood-estimation.md#likelihood-function) and [log-likelihood function](../maximum-likelihood-estimation.md#log-likelihood) as

$$
L_n(\theta)=\prod_{i=1}^nf(X_i;\theta),\qquad
\ell_n(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^n\log f(X_i;\theta).
$$

The expected [Fisher information](../statistical-modelling.md#fisher-information-matrix) in the whole [statistical sample](../statistical-inference.md#statistical-sample) is

$$
\boxed{I_n(\theta)=\mathbb E_\theta\!\left[\left\{\frac{\partial\ell_n(\theta)}{\partial\theta}\right\}^2\right]
=nI_1(\theta),\qquad
I_1(\theta)=\int\left\{\partial_\theta\log f(x;\theta)\right\}^2 f(x;\theta)\,dx.}
$$

Under the regularity conditions that permit [differentiation under the integral sign](../analysis.md#differentiation-under-the-integral-sign), the [score function](../statistical-modelling.md#informant-function) has [expected value](../expected-value.md) zero and

$$
I_n(\theta)=-\mathbb E_\theta\ell_n''(\theta).
$$

For an interior [statistical parameter](../statistical-model.md#statistical-parameter), assuming [identifiability](../statistical-model.md#identifiability) and $0<I_1(\theta)<\infty$, the regular [asymptotic normality of a maximum likelihood estimator](../maximum-likelihood-estimation.md#asymptotic-normality-of-a-maximum-likelihood-estimator) states

$$
\boxed{\sqrt n(\widehat\theta_n-\theta)\xrightarrow dN\!\left(0,I_1(\theta)^{-1}\right).}
$$

Thus the large-sample [normal approximation](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#normal-approximation) is $\widehat\theta_n\mathrel{\dot\sim}N(\theta,I_n(\theta)^{-1})$. The inverse information is the leading sampling [variance](../variance.md) approximation; the displayed convergence specifies its precise asymptotic meaning.

<h4 id="1/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1/a)

<h5 id="1/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#1/a/ii)

For $0<\sigma^2<\infty$, the [central limit theorem](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#central-limit-theorem) gives

$$
\sqrt n(\overline X-\mu)\xrightarrow dN(0,\sigma^2).
$$

If $g$ is differentiable at $\mu$, the [delta method](../statistical-inference.md#delta-method) applies to the first-order expansion

$$
g(\overline X)-g(\mu)=g'(\mu)(\overline X-\mu)+o_p(n^{-1/2}),
$$

where the remainder notation means that its product with $\sqrt n$ tends to zero in [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability). Hence

$$
\boxed{\sqrt n\{g(\overline X)-g(\mu)\}\xrightarrow dN\!\left(0,\sigma^2[g'(\mu)]^2\right),\qquad
g(\overline X)\mathrel{\dot\sim}N\!\left(g(\mu),\frac{\sigma^2[g'(\mu)]^2}{n}\right).}
$$

The useful nondegenerate [normal approximation](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#normal-approximation) requires $g'(\mu)\ne0$. When $g'(\mu)=0$, the first-order limit is zero and a higher-order expansion may be needed.

<h4 id="1/a/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1/a)

<h5 id="1/a/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#1/a/iii)

For a [Bernoulli distribution](../discrete-probability-distribution.md#bernoulli-distribution) with fixed $0<p<1$, the [mean](../expected-value.md) and [variance](../variance.md) are $p$ and $p(1-p)$. The [odds](../probability-theory.md#odds) transformation $g(x)=x/(1-x)$ has [derivative](../calculus.md#derivative) $g'(x)=(1-x)^{-2}$. Applying the [delta method](../statistical-inference.md#delta-method) from part (ii),

$$
\boxed{\frac{\overline X}{1-\overline X}\mathrel{\dot\sim}
N\!\left(\frac p{1-p},\frac{p}{n(1-p)^3}\right).}
$$

More precisely, $\sqrt n\{\overline X/(1-\overline X)-p/(1-p)\}$ converges to a [normal distribution](../probability-distribution.md#normal-distribution) of [mean](../expected-value.md) zero and [variance](../variance.md) $p/(1-p)^3$, after defining the statistic arbitrarily on $\overline X=1$. That exceptional [event](../probability-space.md#event) has [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability) $p^n\to0$, so this extension does not change the limit. The unextended ratio is undefined there and has no finite exact [variance](../variance.md) if interpreted as infinite. At $p=0$ it is zero almost surely; at $p=1$ the denominator is always zero. This is the [delta method for Bernoulli odds](../statistical-inference.md#delta-method-for-bernoulli-odds).

<h3 id="1/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

<h5 id="1/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#1/b/i)

Put $A=\sum_i x_i$, $B=\sum_i x_i^2$ and $M_2=B/n$. The [normal distribution](../probability-distribution.md#normal-distribution) model gives

$$
L(\mu)=(2\pi)^{-n/2}\mu^{-n}\exp\!\left\{-\frac{B-2\mu A+n\mu^2}{2\mu^2}\right\},\qquad\mu>0,
$$

and, up to a constant independent of $\mu$, its [log-likelihood function](../maximum-likelihood-estimation.md#log-likelihood) is

$$
\ell(\mu)=-n\log\mu-\frac{B}{2\mu^2}+\frac A\mu.
$$

The [score function](../statistical-modelling.md#informant-function) and its zero equation are

$$
\ell'(\mu)=\frac{B-A\mu-n\mu^2}{\mu^3},\qquad
n\mu^2+A\mu-B=0.
$$

For $B>0$, this [quadratic equation](../polynomial.md#quadratic-equation) has exactly one positive root, giving the [maximum-likelihood estimator](../maximum-likelihood-estimation.md#maximum-likelihood-estimator)

$$
\boxed{\widehat\mu=\frac{-A+\sqrt{A^2+4nB}}{2n}
=\frac{-\overline X+\sqrt{\overline X^{\,2}+4M_2}}2.}
$$

The numerator $B-A\mu-n\mu^2$ of the [score function](../statistical-modelling.md#informant-function) is positive between its negative and positive roots and negative above its positive root. Thus the [likelihood function](../maximum-likelihood-estimation.md#likelihood-function) increases up to $\widehat\mu$ and decreases afterwards: this is its unique global maximum on $(0,\infty)$. Also, $\ell(\mu)\to-\infty$ at both ends. The all-zero observation vector has [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability) zero; on that exceptional vector $L(\mu)\propto\mu^{-n}$ and there is no attained maximum.

The second [derivative](../calculus.md#derivative) and the relevant [moments](../expected-value.md#moment) are

$$
\ell''(\mu)=\frac n{\mu^2}-\frac{3B}{\mu^4}+\frac{2A}{\mu^3},\qquad
\mathbb E_\mu A=n\mu,\qquad
\mathbb E_\mu B=n(\mu^2+\mu^2)=2n\mu^2.
$$

Therefore the expected [Fisher information](../statistical-modelling.md#fisher-information-matrix) is

$$
I_n(\mu)=-\mathbb E_\mu\ell''(\mu)=\frac{3n}{\mu^2}.
$$

The [support of a probability distribution](../probability-distribution.md#support-of-a-probability-distribution) is the whole [real line](../real-analysis.md#real-line), independent of the positive [statistical parameter](../statistical-model.md#statistical-parameter). The [law of large numbers](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#law-of-large-numbers) gives $\overline X\to\mu$ and $M_2\to2\mu^2$ in [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability), so the displayed [maximum-likelihood estimator](../maximum-likelihood-estimation.md#maximum-likelihood-estimator) is a [consistent estimator](../statistical-inference.md#consistency-statistics). The regular [asymptotic normality of a maximum likelihood estimator](../maximum-likelihood-estimation.md#asymptotic-normality-of-a-maximum-likelihood-estimator) now yields

$$
\boxed{\sqrt n(\widehat\mu-\mu)\xrightarrow dN(0,\mu^2/3),\qquad
\widehat\mu\mathrel{\dot\sim}N\!\left(\mu,\frac{\mu^2}{3n}\right).}
$$

Thus the requested leading [asymptotic variance](../statistical-modelling.md#asymptotic-variance) on the unscaled estimator's scale is $\mu^2/(3n)$. The [normal mean proportional to standard deviation model](../exponential-family.md#normal-mean-proportional-to-standard-deviation-model) provides a reusable version of this calculation.

<h4 id="1/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

<h5 id="1/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#1/b/ii)

Let $z=z_{1-\alpha/2}$ be the $(1-\alpha/2)$ [standard normal quantile](../probability-distribution.md#standard-normal-quantile). Since $\widehat\mu$ is a [consistent estimator](../statistical-inference.md#consistency-statistics), [Slutsky's theorem](../statistical-inference.md#slutsky-theorem) gives

$$
\frac{\sqrt{3n}(\widehat\mu-\mu)}{\widehat\mu}\xrightarrow dN(0,1).
$$

Its estimated [standard error](../statistical-inference.md#standard-error) is $\widehat\mu/\sqrt{3n}$, giving the approximate $(1-\alpha)$ [Wald confidence interval](../statistical-inference.md#wald-confidence-interval)

$$
\boxed{\left[\widehat\mu\left(1-\frac z{\sqrt{3n}}\right),\quad
\widehat\mu\left(1+\frac z{\sqrt{3n}}\right)\right]\cap(0,\infty).}
$$

For each fixed $\mu>0$, this procedure's [coverage probability](../statistical-inference.md#coverage-probability) tends to $1-\alpha$. Intersecting with the positive parameter space preserves coverage of the true positive value.

<h4 id="1/b/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

<h5 id="1/b/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#1/b/iii)

Write $S^2=Q/(n-1)$ and $S=\sqrt{S^2}$, with $n\geq2$. Two exact facts about a [normal sample](../statistical-inference.md#normal-sample) are

$$
\overline X\sim N(\mu,\mu^2/n),\qquad
\frac{(n-1)S^2}{\mu^2}\sim\chi^2_{n-1},
$$

and [independence of the normal sample mean and sample variance](../statistical-inference.md#independence-of-the-normal-sample-mean-and-sample-variance) holds. A [chi-squared distribution](../probability-distribution.md#chi-squared-distribution) with $n-1$ [statistical degrees of freedom](../statistical-inference.md#statistical-degrees-of-freedom) is a sum of $n-1$ [independent](../random-variable.md#independent-random-variables) $\chi^2_1$ variables, each with [mean](../expected-value.md) one and [variance](../variance.md) two. The [central limit theorem](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#central-limit-theorem) therefore gives

$$
\sqrt{n-1}(S^2-\mu^2)\xrightarrow dN(0,2\mu^4).
$$

For $g(x)=\sqrt x$, the [derivative](../calculus.md#derivative) at $\mu^2$ is $1/(2\mu)$ because $\mu>0$. The [delta method](../statistical-inference.md#delta-method) gives the [asymptotic normality of a normal sample standard deviation](../statistical-inference.md#asymptotic-normality-of-a-normal-sample-standard-deviation):

$$
\sqrt n(S-\mu)\xrightarrow dN(0,\mu^2/2).
$$

The exact independence of $\overline X$ and $S$ makes their limiting [normal distributions](../probability-distribution.md#normal-distribution) independent. Consequently,

$$
\sqrt n(\widetilde\mu-\mu)
=\frac13\{\sqrt n(\overline X-\mu)+2\sqrt n(S-\mu)\}
\xrightarrow dN\!\left(0,\frac19\left\{\mu^2+4\frac{\mu^2}2\right\}\right).
$$

Thus

$$
\boxed{\sqrt n(\widetilde\mu-\mu)\xrightarrow dN(0,\mu^2/3),\qquad
\widetilde\mu\mathrel{\dot\sim}N\!\left(\mu,\frac{\mu^2}{3n}\right).}
$$

This has the same leading [asymptotic variance](../statistical-modelling.md#asymptotic-variance) as $\widehat\mu$. The weights $1/3$ and $2/3$ are those in the [optimal asymptotic combination of a normal sample mean and standard deviation](../statistical-inference.md#optimal-asymptotic-combination-of-a-normal-sample-mean-and-standard-deviation). This does not require $S$ to be an exactly [unbiased estimator](../statistical-modelling.md#unbiased-estimator) of $\mu$.

<h4 id="2/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#2/a)

The [Neyman-Pearson lemma](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#neyman-pearson-lemma) concerns a [simple hypothesis](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#simple-hypothesis) $H_0:\theta=\theta_0$ against another [simple hypothesis](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#simple-hypothesis) $H_1:\theta=\theta_1$. For a continuous [statistical sample](../statistical-inference.md#statistical-sample), put $f_j(x)=\prod_i f(x_i;\theta_j)$ for the joint [probability density functions](../continuous-probability-distribution.md#probability-density-function), $j=0,1$. A [randomized statistical test](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#randomized-statistical-test) is a measurable $\phi(x)\in[0,1]$, the conditional [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability) of rejection after observing $x$. Its [test size](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#size-of-a-statistical-test) is $\mathbb E_0\phi$ and its [statistical power](../probability-and-statistics.md#statistical-power) is $\mathbb E_1\phi$.

Choose $k\geq0$ and boundary randomization so that

$$
\phi_*(x)=\begin{cases}
1,&f_1(x)>kf_0(x),\\
0,&f_1(x)<kf_0(x),\\
\gamma(x)\in[0,1],&f_1(x)=kf_0(x),
\end{cases}
\qquad\mathbb E_0\phi_* =\alpha.
$$

Then **$\phi_*$ is most powerful among all tests of size at most $\alpha$**. Thus one rejects for sufficiently large alternative-to-null [likelihood ratio](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#likelihood-ratio). If the equality set has zero [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability) under $H_0$, the test needs no randomization. Continuous observations alone do not guarantee that their [likelihood ratio](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#likelihood-ratio) has a continuous [probability distribution](../probability-distribution.md).

For the proof, let $\phi$ be any competing test with $\mathbb E_0\phi\leq\alpha$. At every $x$,

$$
(\phi_*(x)-\phi(x))(f_1(x)-kf_0(x))\geq0.
$$

Indeed, on $f_1>kf_0$ the first factor is $1-\phi\geq0$; on $f_1<kf_0$ it is $-\phi\leq0$; and on equality the product is zero. [Integration](../integral.md) gives

$$
\mathbb E_1\phi_* -\mathbb E_1\phi
\geq k(\mathbb E_0\phi_* -\mathbb E_0\phi)\geq0,
$$

which proves the claimed optimality. Moreover, when $k>0$, equality of [statistical power](../probability-and-statistics.md#statistical-power) forces equality of [test size](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#size-of-a-statistical-test) and forces $\phi=\phi_*$ almost everywhere where $f_1\ne kf_0$: otherwise the nonnegative integrand would have positive [integral](../integral.md). This proves the usual necessity assertion as well, up to equality sets and sets of zero [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability) under both hypotheses.

<h3 id="2/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

Let $S=\sum_iX_i$. For the [exponential distribution](../continuous-probability-distribution.md#exponential-distribution) with [mean](../expected-value.md) $\theta$, the [likelihood function](../maximum-likelihood-estimation.md#likelihood-function) is $L(\theta)=\theta^{-n}e^{-S/\theta}$ on positive observations. Fix any $0<\theta_1<\theta_0$. The alternative-to-null [likelihood ratio](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#likelihood-ratio) is

$$
\frac{L(\theta_1)}{L(\theta_0)}
=\left(\frac{\theta_0}{\theta_1}\right)^n
\exp\!\left\{-\left(\frac1{\theta_1}-\frac1{\theta_0}\right)S\right\}.
$$

Since $1/\theta_1-1/\theta_0>0$, this is strictly decreasing in $S$. The [Neyman-Pearson lemma](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#neyman-pearson-lemma) says to reject for small $S$. Under the [null hypothesis](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#null-hypothesis), the given sum rule yields $S\sim\operatorname{Gamma}(n,1/\theta_0)$, where the second argument is a rate, and hence

$$
\frac{2S}{\theta_0}\sim\chi^2_{2n}.
$$

Let $q_\alpha$ be the lower $\alpha$ [quantile](../probability-distribution.md#quantile-function) of this [chi-squared distribution](../probability-distribution.md#chi-squared-distribution). The required [critical region](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#rejection-region) is

$$
\boxed{\frac{2\sum_iX_i}{\theta_0}<q_\alpha,\qquad
q_\alpha=F^{-1}_{\chi^2_{2n}}(\alpha).}
$$

Its [test size](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#size-of-a-statistical-test) is exactly $\alpha$ because the [null distribution](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#null-distribution) is continuous. Its cutoff is the same for every $\theta_1<\theta_0$, so it is simultaneously most powerful against every such value: it is a [uniformly most powerful test](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#uniformly-most-powerful-test). Equivalently, reject if $S$ is below the lower $\alpha$ [quantile](../probability-distribution.md#quantile-function) of the specified [gamma distribution](../continuous-probability-distribution.md#gamma-distribution).

<h3 id="2/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/c/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#2/c)

<h5 id="2/c/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#2/c/i)

Put $A=\sum_iX_i$, $B=\sum_jY_j$ and $N=n+m$. Independence gives the [likelihood function](../maximum-likelihood-estimation.md#likelihood-function)

$$
L(\theta,\mu)=\theta^{-n}\mu^{-m}\exp(-A/\theta-B/\mu).
$$

The unrestricted [maximum-likelihood estimators](../maximum-likelihood-estimation.md#maximum-likelihood-estimator) are $\widehat\theta=A/n$ and $\widehat\mu=B/m$. Under the [null hypothesis](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#null-hypothesis), the common [mean](../expected-value.md) $\eta=\theta=\mu$ maximizes $\eta^{-N}e^{-(A+B)/\eta}$ at $\widehat\eta=(A+B)/N$. These are maxima because each one-parameter [score function](../statistical-modelling.md#informant-function) has the sign of its observed sum minus its sample size times its proposed [mean](../expected-value.md).

Define the generalized [likelihood ratio](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#likelihood-ratio) to be the constrained maximum divided by the unrestricted maximum. The exponential factors cancel, giving

$$
\Lambda=\frac{[N/(A+B)]^Ne^{-N}}{(n/A)^n(m/B)^me^{-N}}
=\frac{N^N}{n^nm^m}\frac{A^nB^m}{(A+B)^N}.
$$

Since $T=A/(A+B)$ and $1-T=B/(A+B)$,

$$
\boxed{\Lambda=\frac{(n+m)^{n+m}}{n^nm^m}T^n(1-T)^m.}
$$

It has maximum one at $T=n/N$, when the two fitted [means](../expected-value.md) agree, and is small toward either endpoint. A [likelihood-ratio test](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#likelihood-ratio-test) rejects for small $\Lambda$, or equivalently large $W=-2\log\Lambda$. This is the [likelihood-ratio test for equality of exponential means](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#likelihood-ratio-test-for-equality-of-exponential-means).

<h4 id="2/c/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#2/c)

<h5 id="2/c/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#2/c/ii)

Under the [null hypothesis](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#null-hypothesis), the full model has two free positive [statistical parameters](../statistical-model.md#statistical-parameter) and the constrained model has one. The [Wilks theorem](../statistical-inference.md#wilks-theorem) therefore gives

$$
W=-2\log\Lambda\xrightarrow d\chi^2_1
$$

as both sample sizes tend to infinity, with $n/(n+m)$ tending to a number strictly between zero and one. For the observed $t$, calculate

$$
w_{\rm obs}=2\left[n\log\frac{n}{(n+m)t}+m\log\frac{m}{(n+m)(1-t)}\right].
$$

The large-sample [p-value](../statistical-hypothesis-test.md#p-value) is the upper-tail [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability)

$$
\boxed{p_{\rm approx}=1-F_{\chi^2_1}(w_{\rm obs})
=2\{1-\Phi(\sqrt{w_{\rm obs}})\},}
$$

where $\Phi$ is the [cumulative distribution function](../probability-distribution.md#cumulative-distribution-function) of the [standard normal distribution](../probability-distribution.md#standard-normal-distribution). The last equality uses the fact that the square of a [standard normal random variable](../probability-distribution.md#standard-normal-random-variable) has a [chi-squared distribution](../probability-distribution.md#chi-squared-distribution) with one [statistical degree of freedom](../statistical-inference.md#statistical-degrees-of-freedom). Values of $T$ on either side of $n/(n+m)$ are already included by the transformation to $W$.

<h3 id="3/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

<h5 id="3/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#3/a/i)

Let $s=\sum_i x_i$. The [Bernoulli distribution](../discrete-probability-distribution.md#bernoulli-distribution) [likelihood function](../maximum-likelihood-estimation.md#likelihood-function) is $\theta^s(1-\theta)^{n-s}$. Multiplication by the [beta distribution](../probability-distribution.md#beta-distribution) [prior density](../bayesian-statistics.md#prior-density) and [Bayes' theorem](../probability-theory.md#bayes-theorem) give

$$
\pi(\theta\mid x)\propto\theta^{a+s-1}(1-\theta)^{b+n-s-1},\qquad0<\theta<1.
$$

Recognizing the normalized [beta distribution](../probability-distribution.md#beta-distribution),

$$
\boxed{\theta\mid x\sim\operatorname{Beta}(a+s,b+n-s),\qquad
\pi(\theta\mid x)=\frac{\theta^{a+s-1}(1-\theta)^{b+n-s-1}}{B(a+s,b+n-s)}.}
$$

This is [Beta-binomial conjugacy](../bayesian-statistics.md#beta-binomial-conjugacy); the same update applies whether the data are recorded as the individual [Bernoulli trials](../discrete-probability-distribution.md#bernoulli-trial) or their success count.

<h4 id="3/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

<h5 id="3/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#3/a/ii)

Conditional on $\theta$, the future [Bernoulli random variable](../discrete-probability-distribution.md#bernoulli-distribution) is independent of the observed [statistical sample](../statistical-inference.md#statistical-sample) and has success [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability) $\theta$. Its [posterior predictive probability](../bayesian-statistics.md#posterior-predictive-probability) is therefore the [posterior mean](../bayesian-statistics.md#posterior-mean) of $\theta$. Put $A=a+s$ and $B=b+n-s$. Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb P(X_{n+1}=1\mid x)
&=\int_0^1\theta\,\pi(\theta\mid x)\,d\theta\\
&=\frac{B(A+1,B)}{B(A,B)}
=\frac{\Gamma(A+1)\Gamma(A+B)}{\Gamma(A)\Gamma(A+B+1)}
=\frac A{A+B},
\end{aligned}
$$

using the [beta-gamma identity](../complex-analysis.md#beta-gamma-identity) and the [Gamma function recurrence](../complex-analysis.md#gamma-function-recurrence). Hence

$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(X_{n+1}=1\mid X_1,\ldots,X_n)=\frac{a+\sum_iX_i}{a+b+n}.}
$$

The whole [posterior predictive distribution](../bayesian-statistics.md#posterior-predictive-distribution) of $X_{n+1}$ is the [Bernoulli distribution](../discrete-probability-distribution.md#bernoulli-distribution) with this success [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability).

<h3 id="3/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

<h5 id="3/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#3/b/i)

For a specified [posterior probability](../bayesian-statistics.md#posterior-probability) $1-\alpha$, an interval $I=(\theta_1,\theta_2)$ is a [highest posterior density interval](../bayesian-statistics.md#highest-posterior-density-interval) if

$$
\int_{\theta_1}^{\theta_2}\pi(\theta\mid x)\,d\theta=1-\alpha
$$

and every included point has [posterior density](../bayesian-statistics.md#posterior-density) at least as large as every excluded point, apart from boundary and null-set choices. Equivalently, $I$ is a [level set](../topology.md#level-set) $\{\theta:\pi(\theta\mid x)>k\}$, with possible choices on its boundary, whose [posterior probability](../bayesian-statistics.md#posterior-probability) is $1-\alpha$.

This selects the most densely supported values of the [statistical parameter](../statistical-model.md#statistical-parameter). When the relevant [level set](../topology.md#level-set) is one interval and has no plateau of positive [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability), it is the shortest [credible interval](../bayesian-statistics.md#credible-interval) of that [posterior probability](../bayesian-statistics.md#posterior-probability). With several separated peaks, the corresponding [highest density region](../statistical-inference.md#highest-density-region) can instead be disconnected, so an interval need not exist. The definition is in the stated parameter coordinate: [posterior density](../bayesian-statistics.md#posterior-density) ordering can change under a nonlinear change of parameter.

<h4 id="3/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

<h5 id="3/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#3/b/ii)

Put $A=a+s>1$ and $B=b+n-s>1$ as in part (a), and let $F_{A,B}$ be the [cumulative distribution function](../probability-distribution.md#cumulative-distribution-function) of the [beta distribution](../probability-distribution.md#beta-distribution) $\operatorname{Beta}(A,B)$. The two endpoint equations are

$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
F_{A,B}(\theta_2)-F_{A,B}(\theta_1)&=0.95,\\
\theta_1^{A-1}(1-\theta_1)^{B-1}
&=\theta_2^{A-1}(1-\theta_2)^{B-1}.
\end{aligned}}
$$

The first equation fixes the [posterior probability](../bayesian-statistics.md#posterior-probability), and the second makes the two endpoint [posterior densities](../bayesian-statistics.md#posterior-density) equal. Their admissible ordering is

$$
0<\theta_1<\frac{A-1}{A+B-2}<\theta_2<1.
$$

To justify sufficiency and uniqueness, the [derivative](../calculus.md#derivative) of the logarithm of the [posterior density](../bayesian-statistics.md#posterior-density) is

$$
\frac{A-1}{\theta}-\frac{B-1}{1-\theta},
$$

which is positive below $m=(A-1)/(A+B-2)$ and negative above it. Thus the [posterior density](../bayesian-statistics.md#posterior-density) increases strictly to its unique [mode](../probability-and-statistics.md#mode-statistics) and decreases strictly thereafter, vanishing at both ends. Each density threshold $k$ between zero and the maximum determines exactly one endpoint on each side of $m$. The [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability) between these endpoints decreases continuously and strictly from one to zero as $k$ increases, so exactly one threshold gives 0.95. This is the [beta highest posterior density endpoint characterization](../bayesian-statistics.md#beta-highest-posterior-density-endpoint-characterization).

<h3 id="3/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/c/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#3/c)

<h5 id="3/c/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#3/c/i)

For a scalar [statistical parameter](../statistical-model.md#statistical-parameter) in a regular model with [probability mass function](../probability-distribution.md#probability-mass-function) $f(x\mid\theta)$, define the one-observation expected [Fisher information](../statistical-modelling.md#fisher-information-matrix) by

$$
I(\theta)=\mathbb E_\theta\!\left[\{\partial_\theta\log f(X\mid\theta)\}^2\right]
=\sum_x\frac{\{\partial_\theta f(x\mid\theta)\}^2}{f(x\mid\theta)}.
$$

The [Jeffreys prior](../statistical-modelling.md#jeffreys-prior) is the [prior density](../bayesian-statistics.md#prior-density) specified, up to a factor independent of $\theta$, by

$$
\boxed{\pi_J(\theta)\propto\sqrt{I(\theta)}.}
$$

For $n$ independent observations the [Fisher information](../statistical-modelling.md#fisher-information-matrix) is $nI(\theta)$, so the factor $\sqrt n$ does not change this [prior distribution](../bayesian-statistics.md#prior-probability). If $\int\sqrt{I(\theta)}\,d\theta$ is finite, divide by this integral to normalize; otherwise the rule defines an [improper prior](../bayesian-statistics.md#improper-prior).

<h4 id="3/c/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#3/c)

<h5 id="3/c/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#3/c/ii)

The [Jeffreys prior](../statistical-modelling.md#jeffreys-prior) is **invariant under smooth one-to-one reparameterization**. This means that applying the rule directly in a new parameter $\eta=h(\theta)$ gives the same [prior distribution](../bayesian-statistics.md#prior-probability) as first constructing the rule for $\theta$ and then transforming its [prior density](../bayesian-statistics.md#prior-density) by the change-of-variable formula.

More explicitly, for a differentiable invertible change of parameter with nonzero [derivative](../calculus.md#derivative),

$$
I_\eta(\eta)=I_\theta(\theta)\left(\frac{d\theta}{d\eta}\right)^2,
\qquad
\pi_{J,\eta}(\eta)\propto\sqrt{I_\eta(\eta)}
=\sqrt{I_\theta(\theta)}\left|\frac{d\theta}{d\eta}\right|.
$$

Thus $\pi_{J,\eta}(\eta)\,d\eta$ and $\pi_{J,\theta}(\theta)\,d\theta$ represent the same measure, up to the normalization constant. Invariance concerns the measure: the numerical values of the [prior densities](../bayesian-statistics.md#prior-density) need not be equal in the two coordinates. The same change of variables then makes the [posterior distributions](../bayesian-statistics.md#bayesian-posterior) agree. This is the [reparameterization invariance of Jeffreys prior](../statistical-modelling.md#reparameterization-invariance-of-jeffreys-prior).

<h4 id="3/c/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#3/c)

<h5 id="3/c/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#3/c/iii)

For one [Bernoulli random variable](../discrete-probability-distribution.md#bernoulli-distribution),

$$
\log f(x\mid\theta)=x\log\theta+(1-x)\log(1-\theta),\qquad
\partial_\theta\log f(x\mid\theta)=\frac{x-\theta}{\theta(1-\theta)}.
$$

Its [variance](../variance.md) is $\theta(1-\theta)$, so the expected [Fisher information](../statistical-modelling.md#fisher-information-matrix) and [Jeffreys prior](../statistical-modelling.md#jeffreys-prior) are

$$
I(\theta)=\frac1{\theta(1-\theta)},\qquad
\pi_J(\theta)\propto\theta^{-1/2}(1-\theta)^{-1/2}.
$$

The normalizer is $B(1/2,1/2)=\pi$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\pi_J(\theta)=\frac1{\pi\sqrt{\theta(1-\theta)}},\quad0<\theta<1;\qquad
\theta\sim\operatorname{Beta}(1/2,1/2).}
$$

This [Jeffreys prior for a Bernoulli parameter](../statistical-modelling.md#jeffreys-prior-for-a-bernoulli-parameter) is proper despite its integrable endpoint singularities. It is the [arcsine distribution](../continuous-probability-distribution.md#arcsine-distribution). It replaces the specified $a,b>1$ [prior distribution](../bayesian-statistics.md#prior-probability) in part (a) when the [Jeffreys prior](../statistical-modelling.md#jeffreys-prior) rule is chosen; it is not one of those restricted priors.

<h3 id="3/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#3/d)

Let $y_{(n)}=\max_i y_i$ and $c=\max(\beta,y_{(n)})$. For the observed positive data, the [uniform distribution](../continuous-probability-distribution.md#continuous-uniform-distribution) [likelihood function](../maximum-likelihood-estimation.md#likelihood-function) is

$$
L(\theta;y)=\theta^{-n}\mathbf1_{\{\theta>y_{(n)}\}}.
$$

Multiplying by the [Pareto distribution](../continuous-probability-distribution.md#pareto-distribution) [prior density](../bayesian-statistics.md#prior-density) gives the [posterior density](../bayesian-statistics.md#posterior-density) kernel $\theta^{-(\alpha+n+1)}\mathbf1_{\{\theta>c\}}$. Since

$$
\int_c^\infty\theta^{-(\alpha+n+1)}\,d\theta
=\frac{c^{-(\alpha+n)}}{\alpha+n},
$$

the normalized [posterior distribution](../bayesian-statistics.md#bayesian-posterior) is

$$
\boxed{\theta\mid y\sim\operatorname{Pareto}(\alpha+n,c),\qquad
\pi(\theta\mid y)=\frac{(\alpha+n)c^{\alpha+n}}{\theta^{\alpha+n+1}}\mathbf1_{\{\theta>c\}}.}
$$

This is [uniform-Pareto conjugacy](../exponential-family.md#uniform-pareto-conjugacy). The [posterior density](../bayesian-statistics.md#posterior-density) is strictly decreasing over its support, so the [highest posterior density interval](../bayesian-statistics.md#highest-posterior-density-interval) starts at $c$, where the admissible density is largest in the limiting sense. Its upper endpoint $u$ satisfies

$$
\mathbb P(\theta>u\mid y)=\left(\frac cu\right)^{\alpha+n}=0.05.
$$

Hence a 95% [highest posterior density interval](../bayesian-statistics.md#highest-posterior-density-interval) is

$$
\boxed{\left(c,\;c\,20^{1/(\alpha+n)}\right),\qquad c=\max\!\left(\beta,\max_iY_i\right).}
$$

Inclusion of endpoints does not change its [posterior probability](../bayesian-statistics.md#posterior-probability). Every point inside has higher [posterior density](../bayesian-statistics.md#posterior-density) than every admissible point to its right, and points below $c$ have density zero. This is the [Pareto highest density interval](../continuous-probability-distribution.md#pareto-highest-density-interval); equal endpoint densities are unnecessary because one endpoint is the support boundary.
