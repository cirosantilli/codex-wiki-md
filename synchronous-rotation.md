# Synchronous rotation

↑ **Parent:** [Tidal locking](tidal-locking.md)

[Synchronous rotation](synchronous-rotation.md) means one prograde turn about the body's own axis per [orbital period](orbital-period.md), measured against fixed directions in an [inertial frame](inertial-frame.md). This $1:1$ [spin-orbit resonance](spin-orbit-resonance.md) approximately describes the [Moon](moon.md)'s [synchronous rotation](synchronous-rotation.md) toward [Earth](earth.md). [Tracy Vogel, NASA: synchronous rotation](https://science.nasa.gov/moon/tidal-locking/).

<a id="image-keeping-the-same-face-toward-a-companion-requires-one-turn-per-orbit"></a>
![](synchronous-rotation-facing-companion.png)

**[Figure 3](#image-keeping-the-same-face-toward-a-companion-requires-one-turn-per-orbit). Keeping the same face toward a companion requires one turn per orbit**. Two circular-orbit cartoons viewed along the spin axis. The orange arrow and dot are fixed to the body. With synchronous rotation they turn once in space and always face the companion; without spin they keep a fixed direction in space and different faces point inward. Colors mark material orientation, not sunlight. Sizes are arbitrary; these are not observed lunar states or photographs.

The striking geometric idea is that one turn in space can keep one face toward the companion: a fixed orientation in the frame turning with the orbit is a changing orientation in an [inertial frame](inertial-frame.md). This distinction helps us predict illumination: a synchronously rotating [exoplanet](exoplanet-split.md) orbiting its illuminating [star](star.md) can have persistent day and night hemispheres, whereas the [Moon](moon.md), synchronized to [Earth](earth.md), receives changing illumination from a different body, the [Sun](sun.md).
