<h1 id="newton-s-second-law">Newton's second law</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [Newton's laws of motion](newton-s-laws-of-motion.md)

In an [inertial frame](inertial-frame.md), a [point mass](point-mass.md) of constant [mass](mass.md) $m>0$ obeys

$$
\mathbf F_{\mathrm{net}}=\sum_i\mathbf F_i
=\frac{d\mathbf p}{dt}=m\mathbf a,
\qquad \mathbf p=m\mathbf v,
\qquad \mathbf a=\frac{d\mathbf v}{dt}.
$$

The net [force](force.md) is the sum of all [forces](force.md) acting on that body; [momentum](momentum.md), [velocity](velocity.md) and [acceleration](acceleration.md) refer to the same [reference frame](reference-frame.md). A [force](force.md) changes [velocity](velocity.md), rather than sustaining it: zero net [force](force.md) implies constant [velocity](velocity.md), including a nonzero one. Doubling the net [force](force.md) at fixed [mass](mass.md) doubles [acceleration](acceleration.md); doubling [mass](mass.md) at fixed net [force](force.md) halves it. [MIT mechanics, Chapter 7, section 7.3](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter7.pdf)

For example, an otherwise balanced block of [mass](mass.md) $0.5\,\mathrm{kg}$ receives a constant horizontal net [force](force.md) $2\,\mathrm N$ for $0.5\,\mathrm s$. Its [acceleration](acceleration.md) is $4\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, so its [velocity](velocity.md) changes by $2\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Equivalently, the [impulse](impulse.md) is $F\Delta t=1\,\mathrm{N\,s}$, and $\Delta v=\Delta p/m$. Starting from rest, its [displacement](displacement.md) during the pulse is $a(\Delta t)^2/2=0.5\,\mathrm m$. After the pulse, zero net [force](force.md) leaves it moving at constant [velocity](velocity.md).

Applying the law therefore requires both a specification of the net [force](force.md) and [initial conditions](initial-condition.md). In the [harmonic oscillator](simple-harmonic-motion.md) example, the [force](force.md) is $-kx$, so [Newton's second law](newton-s-second-law.md) becomes a [differential equation](differential-equation-split.md) for [position](position.md). In an accelerating [reference frame](reference-frame.md), the measured relative [acceleration](acceleration.md) includes a contribution from the frame's own motion; the [equation of motion in a rotating frame](equation-of-motion-in-a-rotating-frame.md) illustrates the extra terms required.

Constant [mass](mass.md) is essential to the displayed $m\mathbf a$ form. A [variable-mass system](variable-mass-system.md) exchanging matter also exchanges [momentum](momentum.md); its balance includes the entering or leaving matter's [velocity](velocity.md). Simply differentiating $m\mathbf v$ and equating it to external [force](force.md) on that open body can omit this flux. The existing [variable-mass system](variable-mass-system.md) equation specifies the needed correction.

In the 1687 _Principia_, [Newton's second law](newton-s-second-law.md) was stated in terms of change of motion proportional to impressed [force](force.md), directed along that [force](force.md). Its original statement is verbal; the [derivative](derivative.md) equation above is modern notation. [Newton, Axiomata sive leges motus (1687), Lex II](https://www.newtonproject.ox.ac.uk/view/texts/normalized/NATP00076)
