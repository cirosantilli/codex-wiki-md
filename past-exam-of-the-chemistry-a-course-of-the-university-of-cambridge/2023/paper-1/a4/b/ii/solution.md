<h1 id="a4/b/ii/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](../ii.md)

There are four internal C–N stretching coordinates. A stretch length is unchanged in sign when a [molecular symmetry operation](../../../../../../molecular-symmetry-operation.md) carries a bond to an equivalent bond, so these coordinates have the same permutation [group representation](../../../../../../group-representation.md) as the four ligand sigma functions.

For the square,

$$
\Gamma_{\rm CN}=A_{1g}+B_{1g}+E_u.
$$

Only $E_u\sim(x,y)$ has [vibrational infrared activity](../../../../../../vibrational-infrared-activity.md). The $A_{1g}$ and $B_{1g}$ stretches have [Raman vibrational activity](../../../../../../raman-vibrational-activity.md). Thus the ideal square gives **one IR feature and two Raman features, with no common fundamental**. The two components of $E_u$ have one frequency, not two different lines.

For the tetrahedron,

$$
\Gamma_{\rm CN}=A_1+T_2.
$$

The $T_2$ stretch is IR active, while both $A_1$ and $T_2$ are Raman active. Thus the tetrahedron also gives **one IR feature and two Raman features**, but **one feature, $T_2$, is shared between the spectra**. Its three components likewise describe three independent displacements at one frequency.

**The separate line counts alone do not distinguish the geometries. Comparing their frequencies does:** the tetrahedral IR C–N feature coincides with one Raman C–N feature, whereas the square has no symmetry-enforced coincidence. This assumes resolved fundamentals and excludes accidental equality of unrelated frequencies.
