<h1 id="a10/e/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [E](../e.md)

**[GaN](../../../../../gallium-nitride.md) has a substantial band-gap advantage for high-temperature operation.** Its gap is larger than those of [GaAs](../../../../../gallium-arsenide.md) and [GaP](../../../../../gallium-phosphide.md), suppressing thermally generated intrinsic carriers through $n_i\propto e^{-E_g/(2kT)}$. A doped junction therefore retains its intended carrier imbalance and can have lower thermally generated leakage over a wider [temperature](../../../../../temperature-split.md) range, other factors being equal. The wide gap also supports emission at higher [photon](../../../../../photon.md) energies.

This is a materials-based expectation, not a guarantee for every finished device. Dopant activation and compensation, defects, contact stability, heat extraction and nonradiative recombination can set the practical limit. Increasing [temperature](../../../../../temperature-split.md) also reduces carrier mobility and can broaden or quench light emission. The relevant advantage is reduced intrinsic excitation; a comparison of operating temperatures needs those other device properties too.
