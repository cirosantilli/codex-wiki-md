↑ **Parent:** [Ii](../ii.md)

At low [temperature](../../../../../../temperature-split.md), retain $J=0$ in the even sum and $J=1$ in the odd sum. Their weights are respectively $1$ and $3e^{-2\theta_{\rm rot}/T}$ before the nuclear-spin multiplicity, so

$$
\boxed{K\simeq9e^{-2\theta_{\rm rot}/T}\longrightarrow0\qquad(T\ll\theta_{\rm rot}).}
$$

Equilibrium [hydrogen](../../../../../../hydrogen.md) then becomes almost entirely [parahydrogen](../../../../../../parahydrogen.md). At high [temperature](../../../../../../temperature-split.md), many even and odd rotational levels contribute, and their spatial sums have the same leading value. Hence

$$
\boxed{K\longrightarrow3,\qquad x_o\longrightarrow\frac34,\quad x_p\longrightarrow\frac14
\qquad(T\gg\theta_{\rm rot}).}
$$

This is [normal hydrogen](../../../../../../normal-hydrogen.md). Its familiar high-temperature ratio is a nuclear-spin multiplicity ratio, not the $9:1$ ratio of the two lowest levels. Maintaining conversion is essential: a sample cooled without an effective conversion mechanism can retain its earlier composition instead of following $K(T)$. NBS thermodynamic data distinguish equilibrium composition from [normal hydrogen](../../../../../../normal-hydrogen.md) and compare the state-sum prediction with spectroscopic and thermal evidence.[Section III.2, printed pp. 394–395 and table 12: ortho–para equilibrium and its experimental checks](../../../../../../compilation-of-thermal-properties-of-hydrogen-in-its-various-isotopic-and-ortho-para-modifications-by-harold-w-woolley-and-others.md)
