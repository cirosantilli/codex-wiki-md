# Game theory

↑ **Parent:** [Mathematical optimization](mathematical-optimization-split.md)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_theory)

Game theory studies decisions whose consequences depend on what other decision makers choose. A bidder's gain depends on competing bids in an [auction](auction.md); a driver's travel time depends on other drivers in a [congestion game](congestion-game.md). This strategic interdependence makes choosing well a different problem from [optimization](mathematical-optimization-split.md) against a fixed environment: each participant's [best response](best-response.md) depends on the others' choices.

A [normal-form game](normal-form-game.md) specifies the players, their available [pure strategies](pure-strategy.md), and their [payoffs](payoff.md) for each [strategy profile](strategy-profile.md). Larger [payoffs](payoff.md) mean more preferred outcomes for that player. Unless stated otherwise, a two-player table below lists $(u_1,u_2)$: the row player's [payoff](payoff.md) first, the column player's second. Each player maximizes their own [expected utility](expected-utility.md); the numbers need not represent money, and comparing different players' numerical scales is unnecessary.

The central question is whether everyone's choices fit together. A [Nash equilibrium](nash-equilibrium.md) consists of mutual [best responses](best-response.md): no [unilateral deviation](unilateral-deviation.md) improves a player's [expected payoff](expected-payoff.md). It can involve definite choices, or [mixed strategies](mixed-strategy.md) that deliberately randomize. [Matching pennies](matching-pennies.md) shows why randomization can be essential; the [coordination game](coordination-game.md) shows how players can share an interest in coordinating while disagreeing over which outcome to choose. Such shared gains are absent from a two-player [zero-sum game](zero-sum-game.md), where one player's gain is exactly the other's loss.

These ideas are useful both for analyzing incentives and for designing them. [Mechanism design](mechanism-design.md) asks how changing the rules changes participants' [best responses](best-response.md); [evolutionary game theory](evolutionary-game-theory.md) studies how successful strategies spread. The mathematical appeal is that local comparisons of [payoffs](payoff.md) can reveal a globally consistent pattern of choices. Consistency alone does not imply [Pareto efficiency](pareto-efficiency.md), uniqueness, or that actual play will converge to a [Nash equilibrium](nash-equilibrium.md).

John Nash's [1950 paper, Equilibrium Points in N-Person Games](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1063129/) established existence of a [mixed strategy](mixed-strategy.md) [Nash equilibrium](nash-equilibrium.md) for every [finite game](finite-game.md). His [1951 paper, Non-Cooperative Games, Annals of Mathematics 54, 286–295](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~klarson/teaching/F04-886/papers/nash51.pdf) developed the theory beyond two-player [zero-sum games](zero-sum-game.md). The distinction between conflicting and shared interests is fundamental to the subject.
