# Reduced mass

↑ **Parent:** [Center of mass](center-of-mass.md)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reduced_mass)

The [reduced mass](reduced-mass.md) is the effective [mass](mass.md) for the [two-particle relative coordinate](two-particle-relative-coordinate.md) of two bodies with constant positive [masses](mass.md) $m_1,m_2$:

$$
\mu=\frac{m_1m_2}{m_1+m_2},\qquad \frac1\mu=\frac1{m_1}+\frac1{m_2}.
$$

Both bodies can respond to their mutual [force](force.md), so their relative [acceleration](acceleration.md) is greater than if one were fixed. Consequently $0<\mu<\min(m_1,m_2)$. This explains why using either individual [mass](mass.md), or their sum, generally gives the wrong internal [oscillation](oscillation.md) rate. The same [reduced mass](reduced-mass.md) describes a [two-body problem](two-body-problem.md), a [free two-mass spring](free-two-mass-spring.md), and the approximate [molecular vibration](molecular-vibration.md) of a [diatomic molecule](diatomic-molecule.md).[Reduced Mass, equations 322–328: equal-opposite forces, relative acceleration and reduced mass](newtonian-dynamics-by-richard-fitzpatrick.md)

For equal [masses](mass.md) $m_1=m_2=m$, $\mu=m/2$. Two $1\,\mathrm{kg}$ bodies therefore have $\mu=0.5\,\mathrm{kg}$: in their stationary [centre of mass](center-of-mass.md) frame a relative [speed](speed.md) $2\,\mathrm{m/s}$ means individual [speeds](speed.md) $1\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and total [kinetic energy](kinetic-energy.md) $1\,\mathrm J$. With $m_1=1\,\mathrm{kg}$ and $m_2=100\,\mathrm{kg}$, instead $\mu=100/101\,\mathrm{kg}\approx0.990099\,\mathrm{kg}$. At the same relative [speed](speed.md), the individual [speeds](speed.md) are $200/101$ and $2/101\,\mathrm{m/s}$, and the total [kinetic energy](kinetic-energy.md) is $200/101\,\mathrm J$. These are calculations for stipulated [masses](mass.md) and [velocities](velocity.md).

For fixed $m_1$ and $q=m_2/m_1>0$, $\mu/m_1=q/(1+q)$. The heavy-partner limit $q\to\infty$ gives $\mu\to m_1$ from below; the relative error incurred by replacing $\mu$ with $m_1$ is $(m_1-\mu)/\mu=1/q$. At $q=100$ this is $1\%$. Treating the heavy body as fixed is thus a controlled approximation to internal motion, rather than an exact consequence of its being heavier.
