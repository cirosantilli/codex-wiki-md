# Radioactive decay

↑ **Parent:** [Nuclear physics](nuclear-physics.md)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radioactive_decay)

[Radioactive decay](radioactive-decay.md) is the spontaneous transformation of an unstable [atomic nucleus](atomic-nucleus-split.md) or nuclear state. Its timing can be unpredictable for an individual [atomic nucleus](atomic-nucleus-split.md) while a large population has a reproducible average behavior. A constant [nuclear decay constant](nuclear-decay-constant.md) $\lambda>0$ gives the familiar [exponential decay](exponential-decay.md) law. The [half-life](half-life.md) describes how quickly the parent population falls, while [radioactive activity](radioactive-activity.md) describes its rate of transformations.[University Physics Volume 3 by Samuel J. Ling and others; Radioactive Decay Law, equations 10.7–10.20: decay constant, half-life and activity](university-physics-volume-3-by-samuel-j-ling-and-others/10-3-radioactive-decay.md)

This connection between randomness and a regular population law makes [radioactive decay](radioactive-decay.md) useful for measuring elapsed time and for understanding sources of [radiogenic heating](radiogenic-heating.md). It also distinguishes two questions in an experiment: what the source emits, and what an instrument records. [Becquerel's uranium-radiation experiment](becquerel-s-uranium-radiation-experiment.md) used a [photographic plate](photographic-plate.md); the [Curie ionization-current measurement](curie-ionization-current-measurement.md) measured an electrical consequence of radiation. Their readouts reveal different aspects of the same investigation.
