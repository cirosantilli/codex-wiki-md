# Signal transduction

↑ **Parent:** [Biology](biology-split.md)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signal_transduction)

[Signal transduction](signal-transduction-split.md) converts a detected signal into a change inside a [biological cell](biological-cell-split.md). For chemical signals, distinguish reception by a [receptor](receptor-biochemistry.md), intracellular [signal transduction](signal-transduction-split.md), and the response: altered [gene expression](gene-expression.md), [enzyme](enzyme-split.md) activity or [ion channel](ion-channel.md) opening, for example. A [cell-surface receptor](cell-surface-receptor.md) can relay a [ligand](ligand-biochemistry.md)'s presence across a [cell membrane](cell-membrane-split.md) without the [ligand](ligand-biochemistry.md) entering the [cytoplasm](cytoplasm.md). Thus a [hydrophilic](hydrophilicity.md) signal need not cross the membrane to influence the interior. See [Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e, 9.2: intracellular signal propagation](https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/9-2-propagation-of-the-signal).

**[Figure 1](#image-reception-intracellular-signal-transduction-and-a-cellular-response). Reception, intracellular signal transduction and a cellular response**. Original cartoon of a [ligand](ligand-biochemistry.md) outside a [biological cell](biological-cell-split.md), a [cell-surface receptor](cell-surface-receptor.md) spanning its [cell membrane](cell-membrane-split.md), and intracellular relays branching toward [gene expression](gene-expression.md) or another response. Arrows denote causal steps, not transport of the [ligand](ligand-biochemistry.md) through the membrane. Shapes, distances and relay counts are illustrative.

For a computed example of possible [signal amplification](signal-amplification.md), suppose one activated [receptor](receptor-biochemistry.md) activates five relay [proteins](protein-split.md), and each relay activates twenty target [proteins](protein-split.md) during a chosen interval. If the targets are distinct and available, $1\times5\times20=100$ targets are activated. Those assumed counts illustrate multiplication, not a measured pathway or a universal gain. Actual [signal transduction](signal-transduction-split.md) can branch, saturate or inhibit activity. Changes in [gene expression](gene-expression.md) and changes in existing [enzyme](enzyme-split.md) activity are different responses. See [Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e, 9.3: cellular responses](https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/9-3-response-to-the-signal).

[Signal amplification](signal-amplification.md), [signaling feedback](signaling-feedback.md) and [signaling adaptation](signaling-adaptation.md) are possible properties of [signal transduction](signal-transduction-split.md), rather than requirements of every cascade. Intracellular relays can use [proteins](protein-split.md) or [second messengers](second-messenger.md); different [biological cells](biological-cell-split.md) can interpret the same [ligand](ligand-biochemistry.md) differently because they contain different [receptors](receptor-biochemistry.md) and relay machinery. The useful and striking idea is that recognition and response are separable: an extracellular chemical message can control many internal tasks without itself performing those tasks. See [General Principles of Cell Communication](molecular-biology-of-the-cell-4th-edition-by-bruce-alberts-and-others/general-principles-of-cell-communication.md).

An original experimental route to this separation appears in T. W. Rall and Earl W. Sutherland's 1958 work on [cAMP](cyclic-adenosine-monophosphate.md). They described a heat-stable factor formed from [ATP](adenosine-triphosphate.md) in separated [biological cell](biological-cell-split.md) preparations; it affected [enzyme](enzyme-split.md) activity in other preparations where the initiating chemical signals had no direct effect. This helped distinguish an initial signal from a molecule carrying its effect to another target. [Rall and Sutherland, Formation of a cyclic adenine ribonucleotide by tissue particles, Journal of Biological Chemistry 232 (1958), pp. 1065–1076, introduction](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0021-9258(19)77422-5)
