<h3 id="1/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1/a)

Here [parameter-free definability](../mathematical-logic.md#parameter-free-definability) is intended: parameters are not silently added to the [first-order language](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-language). A subset $D\subseteq A^n$ is a [definable set](../mathematical-logic.md#definable-set) when some $L$-[first-order formula](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-formula) $\varphi(x_1,\ldots,x_n)$ satisfies

$$
\boxed{D=\{\mathbf a\in A^n:\mathcal A\models\varphi(\mathbf a)\}.}
$$

A [definable function](../mathematical-logic.md#definable-function) is one whose graph is a definable set. Thus $f:A^n\to A$ is definable when an $L$-formula $\theta(\mathbf x,y)$ satisfies

$$
\boxed{\mathcal A\models\theta(\mathbf a,b)\iff b=f(\mathbf a).}
$$

In particular $\mathcal A\models\forall\mathbf x\exists!y\,\theta(\mathbf x,y)$. Definability over a parameter set $B$ permits parameters from $B$ in these [first-order formulas](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-formula), equivalently new constants naming those parameters. That is a different requirement: allowing a parameter naming an element would immediately define its singleton.

**Yes.** Zero is the unique absorbing element of the multiplicative [first-order structure](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-structure). It is defined by

$$
\boxed{\forall y\,(x\cdot y=x).}
$$

Zero satisfies this [universal formula](../mathematical-logic.md#universal-formula). Conversely, substituting the actual integer zero for $y$ in the semantic verification forces $x=0$. This use of zero in the verification does not insert a constant symbol into the [first-order formula](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-formula).

**Yes.** One is the unique identity element of multiplication, defined by

$$
\boxed{\forall y\,(x\cdot y=y).}
$$

<h5 id="1/b/ii/gamma">γ</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#1/b/ii)

<h6 id="1/b/ii/gamma/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Γ](#1/b/ii/gamma)

**No.** Use the [prime-sign automorphism of the multiplicative integers](../mathematical-logic.md#prime-sign-automorphism-of-the-multiplicative-integers)

$$
s(0)=0,\qquad s(n)=(-1)^{v_2(n)}n\quad(n\ne0),
$$

where $v_2(n)$ is the exponent of two in $|n|$, a [P-adic valuation](../number-theory.md#p-adic-valuation). For nonzero integers, $v_2(mn)=v_2(m)+v_2(n)$, so $s(mn)=s(m)s(n)$; the identity also holds when a factor is zero. The exponent of two is unchanged by changing the sign, hence $s(s(n))=n$. Thus $s$ is a multiplicative [structure automorphism](../mathematical-logic.md#automorphism-of-a-first-order-structure).

It fixes one and sends two to minus two. The addition-graph point $(1,1,2)$ is sent to $(1,1,-2)$, outside the graph of addition. By [automorphism invariance of parameter-free definability](../mathematical-logic.md#automorphism-invariance-of-parameter-free-definability), **addition is not definable in the multiplicative reduct**.

<h4 id="1/b/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

<h5 id="1/b/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#1/b/iii)

For the additive zero singleton, $x+x=x$ is already a [quantifier-free formula](../mathematical-logic.md#quantifier-free-formula), so it is also an [existential formula](../mathematical-logic.md#existential-formula) and a [universal formula](../mathematical-logic.md#universal-formula) with an empty quantifier block. If a nonempty block is required, add a dummy $\exists y$ or $\forall y$; first-order domains are nonempty.

For the two definable multiplicative singletons, [existential formulas](../mathematical-logic.md#existential-formula) are

$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
0:\quad&\exists y\,(x\cdot x=x\ \land\ x\cdot y\ne y),\\
1:\quad&\exists y\,(x\cdot x=x\ \land\ x\cdot y\ne x).
\end{aligned}}
$$

In the integers, the equation $x^2=x$ restricts $x$ to zero or one. In the first line, one never satisfies the inequality, whereas zero does with $y=1$. In the second, zero never satisfies it, whereas one does with $y=0$. The respective [universal formulas](../mathematical-logic.md#universal-formula) are $\forall y(xy=x)$ and $\forall y(xy=y)$, as already verified.

Neither multiplicative singleton has a [quantifier-free formula](../mathematical-logic.md#quantifier-free-formula). In this constant-free, multiplication-only language, every term in the sole free variable $x$ is $x^k$ for a positive integer $k$. Every [atomic formula](../mathematical-logic.md#atomic-formula) $x^r=x^s$ is true at both zero and one. Every Boolean combination consequently has the same truth value at those two points, and cannot separate their singletons. This is the fact that [zero and one have the same quantifier-free type in the multiplicative integers](../mathematical-logic.md#zero-and-one-have-the-same-quantifier-free-type-in-the-multiplicative-integers).

Thus **additive zero admits all three forms; multiplicative zero and one admit existential and universal forms, but no quantifier-free form**. All remaining cases were excluded from definability itself by [structure automorphisms](../mathematical-logic.md#automorphism-of-a-first-order-structure).

<h3 id="1/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/c/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#1/c)

<h5 id="1/c/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#1/c/i)

An [aleph-zero-categorical theory](../model-theory.md#omega-categorical-theory) has, up to [isomorphism](../algebra.md#isomorphism), exactly one [first-order model](../mathematical-logic.md#model-of-a-first-order-theory) of cardinality $\aleph_0$.

The [Ryll-Nardzewski theorem](../model-theory.md#ryll-nardzewski-theorem) says that, for a [complete theory](../model-theory.md#complete-theory) $T$ in a countable [first-order language](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-language), with infinite [first-order models](../mathematical-logic.md#model-of-a-first-order-theory), the following conditions are equivalent: $T$ is aleph-zero-categorical; for every positive integer $n$, its [type space](../model-theory.md#type-space) $S_n(T)$ of complete types over the empty set is finite; and, for every $n$, there are only finitely many $L$-formulas in $n$ free variables modulo equivalence over $T$. Here $\varphi$ and $\psi$ are equivalent over $T$ when $T\models\forall\mathbf x(\varphi\leftrightarrow\psi)$.

<h4 id="1/c/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#1/c)

<h5 id="1/c/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#1/c/ii)

A [partial type](../mathematical-logic.md#partial-type) $p(\mathbf x)$ is a set of $L$-[first-order formulas](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-formula) in a fixed tuple $\mathbf x=(x_1,\ldots,x_n)$ jointly consistent with $T$. A [complete type](../model-theory.md#complete-type), the usual meaning of an $n$-type here, is maximal among such consistent sets: for each [first-order formula](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-formula) $\varphi(\mathbf x)$ it contains exactly one of $\varphi,\neg\varphi$.

Add new constants $\mathbf c$ and consider

$$
T\cup\{\varphi(\mathbf c):\varphi\in p\}.
$$

Every finite subset is satisfiable by consistency of the type. The [compactness theorem](../mathematical-logic.md#compactness-theorem) gives a [first-order model](../mathematical-logic.md#model-of-a-first-order-theory) of the full set. Its $L$-reduct is a [first-order model](../mathematical-logic.md#model-of-a-first-order-theory) of $T$, and the interpretations of $\mathbf c$ realize $p$. This proof applies to partial as well as complete types.

For a [complete theory](../model-theory.md#complete-theory) in a countable language, **the types realized in every [first-order model](../mathematical-logic.md#model-of-a-first-order-theory) are exactly the isolated types**. An [isolated type](../model-theory.md#isolated-type) has a consistent [first-order formula](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-formula) $\theta\in p$ such that $T\models\forall\mathbf x(\theta\to\varphi)$ for every $\varphi\in p$. Completeness forces $T\models\exists\mathbf x\theta$, so every [first-order model](../mathematical-logic.md#model-of-a-first-order-theory) realizes $p$; the converse is the [omitting types theorem](../model-theory.md#omitting-types-theorem). Countability is a hypothesis of that converse, not of the preceding compactness realization argument. For a [partial type](../mathematical-logic.md#partial-type) the corresponding criterion is an isolating consistent [first-order formula](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-formula), together with its existence in every [first-order model](../mathematical-logic.md#model-of-a-first-order-theory); it need not itself belong to the [partial type](../mathematical-logic.md#partial-type).

If $T$ is not complete, no one complete $n$-type can be realized in every [first-order model](../mathematical-logic.md#model-of-a-first-order-theory) of $T$: a [complete type](../model-theory.md#complete-type) decides sentences, and two completions of $T$ disagree on some sentence. Thus the completeness and countability hypotheses must be retained when using the isolated-type characterization.

<h4 id="1/c/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#1/c)

<h5 id="1/c/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#1/c/iii)

For the additive [first-order structure](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-structure), let

$$
\varphi_n(x):=\exists y\,(x=\underbrace{y+\cdots+y}_{n\text{ terms}}),\qquad n\geq1.
$$

It defines $n\mathbb Z$. These sets are pairwise distinct: if $m>n>0$, the integer $n$ belongs to $n\mathbb Z$ but not to $m\mathbb Z$. Thus there are infinitely many inequivalent one-variable [first-order formulas](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-formula) modulo the [complete theory](../model-theory.md#complete-theory) of the additive integers. The [Ryll-Nardzewski theorem](../model-theory.md#ryll-nardzewski-theorem) implies **that [first-order theory](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-theory) is not aleph-zero-categorical**.

For the multiplicative [first-order structure](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-structure) define divisibility by $d(y,x):=\exists z(yz=x)$. For $k\geq1$, the [first-order formula](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-formula)

$$
\psi_k(x):=\exists y_1\cdots y_{2k}\left(\bigwedge_{i<j}y_i\ne y_j\ \land\ \bigwedge_{i=1}^{2k}d(y_i,x)\right)
$$

asserts the existence of at least $2k$ distinct integer divisors. The integer $2^{k-1}$ has exactly $2k$ such divisors, namely $\pm2^j$ for $0\leq j\leq k-1$, so it satisfies $\psi_k$ but not any $\psi_\ell$ with $\ell>k$. Again there are infinitely many inequivalent one-variable [first-order formulas](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-formula). The [Ryll-Nardzewski theorem](../model-theory.md#ryll-nardzewski-theorem) therefore implies **the [first-order theory](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-theory) of the multiplicative integers is not aleph-zero-categorical**.

<h4 id="2/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#2/a)

<h5 id="2/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#2/a/i)

A [complete theory](../model-theory.md#complete-theory) is a consistent [first-order theory](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-theory) deciding every sentence in its language:

$$
\boxed{T\models\sigma\quad\text{or}\quad T\models\neg\sigma\quad\text{for every }L\text{-sentence }\sigma.}
$$

Equivalently, every two [first-order models](../mathematical-logic.md#model-of-a-first-order-theory) of $T$ are [elementarily equivalent](../model-theory.md#elementary-equivalence). Consistency excludes the vacuous situation in which a [first-order theory](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-theory) has no [first-order models](../mathematical-logic.md#model-of-a-first-order-theory) and entails both alternatives.

<h4 id="2/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#2/a)

<h5 id="2/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#2/a/ii)

A [first-order theory](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-theory) has [quantifier elimination](../model-theory.md#quantifier-elimination) if every [first-order formula](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-formula) is equivalent over the [first-order theory](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-theory) to a [quantifier-free formula](../mathematical-logic.md#quantifier-free-formula) with the same free variables:

$$
\boxed{\text{for each }\varphi(\mathbf x)\text{ there is quantifier-free }\theta(\mathbf x)\text{ with }T\models\forall\mathbf x(\varphi\leftrightarrow\theta).}
$$

Logical truth and falsity may be treated as the empty Boolean combinations when considering sentences in a language without closed terms. The assertion concerns [first-order formulas](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-formula) with free variables, not merely sentences.

<h4 id="2/a/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#2/a)

<h5 id="2/a/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#2/a/iii)

A [model-complete theory](../model-theory.md#model-complete-theory) is a [first-order theory](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-theory) for which every [structure embedding](../mathematical-logic.md#structure-embedding) between two of its [first-order models](../mathematical-logic.md#model-of-a-first-order-theory) is an [elementary embedding](../set-theory.md#elementary-embedding). Thus, if $f:\mathcal A\hookrightarrow\mathcal B$ is such an embedding, then for every [first-order formula](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-formula) $\varphi(\mathbf x)$ and tuple $\mathbf a$ from $A$,

$$
\boxed{\mathcal A\models\varphi(\mathbf a)\iff\mathcal B\models\varphi(f\mathbf a).}
$$

[first-order model](../mathematical-logic.md#model-of-a-first-order-theory) completeness does not assert that every two [first-order models](../mathematical-logic.md#model-of-a-first-order-theory) are elementarily equivalent: it constrains embeddings that exist between [first-order models](../mathematical-logic.md#model-of-a-first-order-theory), whereas completeness constrains all [first-order models](../mathematical-logic.md#model-of-a-first-order-theory).

<h3 id="2/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

<h5 id="2/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#2/b/i)

If $T$ is [model-complete](../model-theory.md#model-complete-theory), the inclusion of a [substructure of a first-order structure](../mathematical-logic.md#substructure-of-a-first-order-structure) $\mathcal A\subseteq\mathcal B$, both [first-order models](../mathematical-logic.md#model-of-a-first-order-theory) of $T$, is a [structure embedding](../mathematical-logic.md#structure-embedding) and hence elementary. Therefore $\mathcal A\preceq\mathcal B$.

Conversely, assume every such inclusion is elementary, and take any [structure embedding](../mathematical-logic.md#structure-embedding) $f:\mathcal A\to\mathcal B$ between [first-order models](../mathematical-logic.md#model-of-a-first-order-theory) of $T$. Its image $f[A]$, with the induced [first-order structure](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-structure), is a substructure of $\mathcal B$ isomorphic to $\mathcal A$, so it is itself a [first-order model](../mathematical-logic.md#model-of-a-first-order-theory) of $T$. By hypothesis $f[A]\preceq\mathcal B$. The [isomorphism](../algebra.md#isomorphism) $\mathcal A\to f[A]$ preserves all [first-order formulas](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-formula), and composing it with this elementary inclusion shows that $f$ is an [elementary embedding](../set-theory.md#elementary-embedding). Thus $\boxed{T\text{ is model-complete}\iff(\mathcal A\subseteq\mathcal B\models T\Rightarrow\mathcal A\preceq\mathcal B)}$.

<h4 id="2/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

<h5 id="2/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#2/b/ii)

A [structure embedding](../mathematical-logic.md#structure-embedding) preserves and reflects all [atomic formulas](../mathematical-logic.md#atomic-formula), hence all [quantifier-free formulas](../mathematical-logic.md#quantifier-free-formula) by induction on Boolean combinations. If $T$ has [quantifier elimination](../model-theory.md#quantifier-elimination), replace any $\varphi(\mathbf x)$ by a quantifier-free $\theta(\mathbf x)$ equivalent over $T$. For $\mathcal A\subseteq\mathcal B\models T$ and a tuple $\mathbf a$ from $A$,

$$
\mathcal A\models\varphi(\mathbf a)\iff\mathcal A\models\theta(\mathbf a)\iff\mathcal B\models\theta(\mathbf a)\iff\mathcal B\models\varphi(\mathbf a).
$$

Thus $\mathcal A\preceq\mathcal B$, and the preceding criterion proves $\boxed{\text{quantifier elimination implies model completeness}.}$

<h3 id="2/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/c/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#2/c)

<h5 id="2/c/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#2/c/i)

Write

$$
S(x):=\forall y(E(x,y)\to y=x),\qquad R(x):=\exists y(E(x,y)\land y\ne x).
$$

These are [first-order formulas](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-formula) in the original language, not new predicate symbols. An axiomatization consists of the [equivalence relation](../set-theory.md#equivalence-relation) axioms for $E$, the bound

$$
\forall x\forall y\forall z\bigl((E(x,y)\land E(x,z))\to(y=z\lor y=x\lor z=x)\bigr),
$$

and, for every $n\geq1$, the two sentences

$$
\exists x_1\cdots x_n\left(\bigwedge_iS(x_i)\land\bigwedge_{i<j}x_i\ne x_j\right),
$$



$$
\exists x_1\cdots x_n\left(\bigwedge_iR(x_i)\land\bigwedge_{i<j}\neg E(x_i,x_j)\right).
$$

The first scheme supplies infinitely many singleton classes. The second supplies infinitely many distinct non-singleton classes; the size bound makes these doubletons.

Every countable [first-order model](../mathematical-logic.md#model-of-a-first-order-theory) has countably infinitely many classes of each size. A bijection between singleton classes, and another between doubleton classes followed by a bijection inside each matched pair, gives an [isomorphism](../algebra.md#isomorphism) between any two countable [first-order models](../mathematical-logic.md#model-of-a-first-order-theory). To infer completeness, suppose a sentence had different truth values in two arbitrary [first-order models](../mathematical-logic.md#model-of-a-first-order-theory). The [Downward Lowenheim-Skolem theorem](../mathematical-logic.md#downward-lowenheim-skolem-theorem) supplies countable [elementary substructures](../mathematical-logic.md#elementary-substructure) of those [first-order models](../mathematical-logic.md#model-of-a-first-order-theory), which would still disagree on the sentence, contradicting the [isomorphism](../algebra.md#isomorphism) just constructed. Thus all [first-order models](../mathematical-logic.md#model-of-a-first-order-theory) satisfy the same sentences. Since the original [first-order structure](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-structure) satisfies the axioms, **these axioms form a complete axiomatization of its [first-order theory](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-theory)**. This is the [theory of infinitely many singleton and doubleton equivalence classes](../mathematical-logic.md#theory-of-infinitely-many-singleton-and-doubleton-equivalence-classes).

<h4 id="2/c/ii">ii</h4>

<h5 id="2/c/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#2/c/ii)

**No.** Let $S$ be the displayed infinite axiom set. Any finite subset $S_0\subseteq S$ mentions only finitely many of the two infinitude requirements. Choose $N\geq1$ at least as large as every index mentioned. A finite [equivalence relation](../set-theory.md#equivalence-relation) with exactly $N$ singleton classes and $N$ doubleton classes satisfies $S_0$, but is not a [first-order model](../mathematical-logic.md#model-of-a-first-order-theory) of the [complete theory](../model-theory.md#complete-theory).

If a finite axiomatization existed, let $\sigma$ be the conjunction of its axioms. Then $S\models\sigma$, so the [compactness theorem](../mathematical-logic.md#compactness-theorem) gives a finite $S_0\subseteq S$ with $S_0\models\sigma$. Every [first-order model](../mathematical-logic.md#model-of-a-first-order-theory) of $S_0$ would then satisfy the full [first-order theory](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-theory), contradicting the finite [first-order model](../mathematical-logic.md#model-of-a-first-order-theory) above. This is the [finite subaxiomatization from an equivalent finite theory](../mathematical-logic.md#finite-subaxiomatization-from-an-equivalent-finite-theory) argument: **finite axiomatizability would force one finite fragment of the explicit axioms to suffice**.

<h4 id="2/c/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#2/c)

<h5 id="2/c/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#2/c/iii)

Take a countable [elementary substructure](../mathematical-logic.md#elementary-substructure) $\mathcal B'$ of $\mathcal A'$ containing the named constant, using the [Downward Lowenheim-Skolem theorem](../mathematical-logic.md#downward-lowenheim-skolem-theorem). Its reduct $\mathcal B$ has infinitely many singleton and doubleton classes. In particular it contains the partner of each of its non-singleton elements, and its interpretation of $P$ is exactly the singleton-class predicate.

Choose new distinct elements $u,v\notin B$. Define

$$
C=B\cup\{u\},\qquad D=B\cup\{u,v\}.
$$

In $\mathcal C$, make $u$ a new singleton class. In $\mathcal D$, make $\{u,v\}$ a new doubleton class; keep every old class of $\mathcal B$ unchanged. All three reducts are [first-order models](../mathematical-logic.md#model-of-a-first-order-theory) of the [complete theory](../model-theory.md#complete-theory), and $\mathcal B\subseteq\mathcal C\subseteq\mathcal D$.

Expand $\mathcal C,\mathcal D$ by interpreting $P$ as singleton-class membership and retaining the original named constant from $B$. The resulting countable [first-order structures](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-structure) $\mathcal C',\mathcal D'$ are each isomorphic to $\mathcal B'$: all have infinitely many classes of each size, and the bijections of classes can be chosen to preserve the constant and its class. Hence they are [first-order models](../mathematical-logic.md#model-of-a-first-order-theory) of $\operatorname{Th}(\mathcal A')$. Both inclusions $\mathcal B'\subseteq\mathcal C'$ and $\mathcal B'\subseteq\mathcal D'$ are genuine [structure embeddings](../mathematical-logic.md#structure-embedding), since no class meeting $B$ was changed. The given [quantifier elimination](../model-theory.md#quantifier-elimination), together with the preceding implication to [first-order model](../mathematical-logic.md#model-of-a-first-order-theory) completeness, yields

$$
\mathcal B'\preceq\mathcal C',\qquad\mathcal B'\preceq\mathcal D'.
$$

Taking reducts gives $\boxed{\mathcal B\preceq\mathcal C,\quad\mathcal B\preceq\mathcal D}$. But

$$
\mathcal C\models\neg\exists y(E(u,y)\land y\ne u),\qquad\mathcal D\models\exists y(E(u,y)\land y\ne u),
$$

so $\boxed{\mathcal C\npreceq\mathcal D}$. There is no conflict with [quantifier elimination](../model-theory.md#quantifier-elimination) in the expanded language: $P(u)$ changes truth value, so $\mathcal C'$ is not a substructure of $\mathcal D'$.

<h4 id="2/c/iv">iv</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#2/c)

<h5 id="2/c/iv/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#2/c/iv)

The inclusion $\mathcal C\subseteq\mathcal D$ is a [structure embedding](../mathematical-logic.md#structure-embedding) between [first-order models](../mathematical-logic.md#model-of-a-first-order-theory) of the same [complete theory](../model-theory.md#complete-theory), but it is not an [elementary embedding](../set-theory.md#elementary-embedding), as the existential partner [first-order formula](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-formula) at $u$ shows. Therefore **the [first-order theory](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-theory) is not model-complete**. The facts $\mathcal B\preceq\mathcal C$ and $\mathcal B\preceq\mathcal D$ do not make the intervening inclusion elementary.

## 3

↑ **Parent:** [2011](2011.md)

<h3 id="3/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

<h5 id="3/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#3/a/i)

An [omega-saturated model](../model-theory.md#omega-saturated-model) realizes every complete one-type over every finite subset of its domain that is consistent with the [first-order structure](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-structure)'s [first-order theory](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-theory) with those named parameters. Equivalently, every [partial type](../mathematical-logic.md#partial-type) in a finite tuple over finitely many parameters that is finitely satisfiable in the [first-order structure](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-structure) has a realization there. The equivalence follows by extending a [partial type](../mathematical-logic.md#partial-type) to a [complete type](../model-theory.md#complete-type); finite tuples can be handled one coordinate at a time.

For an example, take a countably infinite set in the language of equality alone. Over finitely many named elements, a complete one-type either requires equality with one of them or inequality with all of them. Both possibilities are realized. [quantifier elimination](../model-theory.md#quantifier-elimination) for an infinite pure set follows by choosing a quantified variable either equal to one of the finitely many named values or distinct from all of them. Indeed every permutation fixing the parameters is a [structure automorphism](../mathematical-logic.md#automorphism-of-a-first-order-structure), and all other elements have the same type, so there are no further one-types. This [first-order structure](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-structure) is **omega-saturated**.

For a counterexample, take $(\mathbb N;0,S)$, where $S(n)=n+1$. The parameter-free [partial type](../mathematical-logic.md#partial-type)

$$
\boxed{p(x)=\{x\ne S^n(0):n=0,1,2,\ldots\}}
$$

is finitely satisfiable: choose an integer beyond the finitely many excluded numerals. It is not realized in the [first-order structure](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-structure), because every natural number is one of those numerals. Any complete extension of this [partial type](../mathematical-logic.md#partial-type) is therefore omitted as well. Thus the standard successor [first-order structure](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-structure) is **not omega-saturated**.

<h4 id="3/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

<h5 id="3/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#3/a/ii)

Here fixed means **fixed setwise**: $\sigma[D]=D$ for every [structure automorphism](../mathematical-logic.md#automorphism-of-a-first-order-structure) $\sigma$, not that each point must be fixed individually. A parameter-free [definable set](../mathematical-logic.md#definable-set) has this invariance by [automorphism invariance of parameter-free definability](../mathematical-logic.md#automorphism-invariance-of-parameter-free-definability).

For the converse, first establish the needed [saturation implies homogeneity](../model-theory.md#saturation-implies-homogeneity) step. In a countable [omega-saturated model](../model-theory.md#omega-saturated-model), a finite [partial elementary map](../mathematical-logic.md#partial-elementary-map) can be extended to include any prescribed additional element in its domain. Transfer the element's [complete type](../model-theory.md#complete-type) over the old finite domain along the map. Each finite part of this transported type has a witness because the old map is elementary; the [omega-saturated model](../model-theory.md#omega-saturated-model) realizes the full transported type. The inequalities in the type maintain injectivity. Apply the same construction to the inverse map to enlarge the range. Alternating these steps through an enumeration of the countable domain is the [back-and-forth method](../model-theory.md#back-and-forth-method), giving a [structure automorphism](../mathematical-logic.md#automorphism-of-a-first-order-structure). In particular, any two elements with the same [complete type](../model-theory.md#complete-type) over the empty set are related by a [structure automorphism](../mathematical-logic.md#automorphism-of-a-first-order-structure).

Assume now that $D$ is finite and invariant, and fix $a\in D$. All realizations in $A$ of $p_a=\operatorname{tp}(a/\varnothing)$ belong to $D$, by the preceding homogeneity and the invariance assumption. Suppose that no parameter-free [first-order formula](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-formula) $\theta_a\in p_a$ has all its realizations in $D$. Then every finite subset of

$$
p_a(x)\cup\{x\ne d:d\in D\}
$$

is satisfiable in $A$: conjoin the finitely many [first-order formulas](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-formula) from $p_a$, and use the assumed absence of a defining neighbourhood inside $D$ to find a witness outside $D$. This is a [partial type](../mathematical-logic.md#partial-type) over the finite parameter set $D$. The [omega-saturated model](../model-theory.md#omega-saturated-model) would realize it by an element outside $D$ with the same empty-set type as $a$, a contradiction. Hence such a $\theta_a$ does exist.

There are only finitely many $a\in D$, so

$$
\boxed{D=\{x\in A:\mathcal A\models\bigvee_{a\in D}\theta_a(x)\}.}
$$

For $D=\varnothing$, use $x\ne x$. Thus [finite invariant sets in a countable saturated structure are definable](../model-theory.md#finite-invariant-sets-in-a-countable-saturated-structure-are-definable). Finiteness is used twice: the extra parameters form a finite set, and the final disjunction is a [first-order formula](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-formula) rather than an infinite disjunction.

<h3 id="3/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

<h5 id="3/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#3/b/i)

A [filter on a set](../set-theory.md#filter-set-theory) $I$ means a proper family $\mathcal F\subseteq\mathcal P(I)$ with $I\in\mathcal F$, $\varnothing\notin\mathcal F$, closure under finite intersections, and upward closure under inclusion. An [ultrafilter](../set-theory.md#ultrafilter) is a maximal proper filter; equivalently, for every $X\subseteq I$, exactly one of $X$ and $I\setminus X$ belongs to it. A [principal ultrafilter](../set-theory.md#principal-ultrafilter) is $\{X\subseteq I:i\in X\}$ for some $i\in I$. A [nonprincipal ultrafilter](../set-theory.md#nonprincipal-ultrafilter) is one not of that form, equivalently one containing no finite set.

To prove the [ultrafilter lemma](../set-theory.md#ultrafilter-lemma) using [Zorn's lemma](../set-theory.md#zorn-s-lemma), order all proper filters containing $\mathcal F$ by inclusion. The union of any chain is a proper filter: finitely many members lie together in one filter of the chain, and no chain member contains the empty set. Thus every chain has an upper bound, and Zorn's lemma gives a maximal member $\mathcal U$.

For completeness, its decision property follows from maximality. If $X\notin\mathcal U$, adjoining $X$ cannot generate a proper filter. Hence some $F\in\mathcal U$ has $F\cap X=\varnothing$. Upward closure gives $I\setminus X\in\mathcal U$. Both complements cannot be members, since their intersection is empty. Therefore $\boxed{\mathcal F\subseteq\mathcal U\text{ for an ultrafilter }\mathcal U}$. Properness is essential; a family containing the empty set cannot extend to an [ultrafilter](../set-theory.md#ultrafilter).

<h4 id="3/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

<h5 id="3/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#3/b/ii)

Write the [ultrafilter](../set-theory.md#ultrafilter) as $\mathcal U$ to distinguish it from [first-order structure](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-structure) domains. On the direct product $\prod_{i\in I}A_i$, put

$$
\mathbf a\sim\mathbf b\iff\{i\in I:a_i=b_i\}\in\mathcal U.
$$

The domain of the [ultraproduct](../model-theory.md#ultraproduct) is the quotient $(\prod_iA_i)/{\sim}$. Its elements are classes $[\mathbf a]$. A constant $c$ is interpreted by $[(c^{\mathcal A_i})_i]$, and a $k$-ary [function](../function.md) symbol by

$$
f^{\mathcal A}([\mathbf a^1],\ldots,[\mathbf a^k])=\left[(f^{\mathcal A_i}(a_i^1,\ldots,a_i^k))_{i\in I}\right].
$$

For a $k$-ary relation symbol,

$$
\boxed{\mathcal A\models R([\mathbf a^1],\ldots,[\mathbf a^k])\iff\{i:\mathcal A_i\models R(a_i^1,\ldots,a_i^k)\}\in\mathcal U.}
$$

These definitions are independent of representatives: if finitely many input representatives are changed, the coordinates where all old and new inputs agree form a set in the filter. At those coordinates the interpreted outputs and relation truth values agree. Filter closure and upward closure therefore preserve the resulting quotient elements and truth conditions.

<h4 id="3/b/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

<h5 id="3/b/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#3/b/iii)

The [Łoś theorem](../model-theory.md#los-theorem) states that, for every [first-order formula](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-formula) $\varphi(\mathbf x)$ and product representatives $\mathbf a^1,\ldots,\mathbf a^r$,

$$
\boxed{\prod_i\mathcal A_i/\mathcal U\models\varphi([\mathbf a^1],\ldots,[\mathbf a^r])\iff\{i:\mathcal A_i\models\varphi(a_i^1,\ldots,a_i^r)\}\in\mathcal U.}
$$

It extends the defining relation-symbol rule to arbitrary [first-order formulas](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-formula), including quantified ones.

Identify the countable index set with $\mathbb N$. A [nonprincipal ultrafilter](../set-theory.md#nonprincipal-ultrafilter) on it contains every cofinite set. Let $p(x)$ be a type over a finite parameter set in the ultraproduct $\mathcal M$, finitely satisfiable in $\mathcal M$. Because the language and parameter set are countable, enumerate it as $\varphi_0(x),\varphi_1(x),\ldots$, with chosen product representatives for the parameters. By finite satisfiability and Łoś,

$$
X_n=\left\{i:\mathcal A_i\models\exists x\bigwedge_{j\leq n}\varphi_j(x)\right\}\in\mathcal U.
$$

Make these large sets decrease while forcing their intersection to be empty:

$$
Y_n=\{i:i\geq n\}\cap\bigcap_{k\leq n}X_k\in\mathcal U.
$$

For each index $i$, let $r(i)$ be the largest $n\leq i$ with $i\in Y_n$, if one exists. Choose $b_i\in A_i$ satisfying the first $r(i)+1$ [first-order formulas](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-formula); this is possible because $i\in X_{r(i)}$. If no such $n$ exists, choose any $b_i$.

For each fixed $n$, every $i\in Y_n$ has $r(i)\geq n$, so $b_i$ satisfies $\varphi_n$. Its coordinate truth set contains $Y_n$ and belongs to $\mathcal U$. Another use of Łoś gives $\mathcal M\models\varphi_n([\mathbf b])$ for every $n$. Thus $[\mathbf b]$ realizes the type. This proves the [countable saturation of an ultraproduct over omega](../model-theory.md#countable-saturation-of-an-ultraproduct-over-omega), and in particular **the ultraproduct is omega-saturated**. The construction chooses a finite number of requirements at each coordinate; it does not presume the whole infinite type has a witness in any factor.

<h4 id="3/b/iv">iv</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

<h5 id="3/b/iv/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#3/b/iv)

Let $P_1$ and $P_3$ be the sets of primes congruent to one and three modulo four. Both are infinite. For $P_1$, if a finite list contained them all, put $N=(2p_1\cdots p_r)^2+1$. Any prime divisor $q$ of this odd number is new and has minus one as a square modulo $q$, hence belongs to $P_1$ by the given criterion. For $P_3$, use $N=4p_1\cdots p_r-1$. It is three modulo four, so at least one of its odd prime divisors is three modulo four; none of the listed primes divides $N$. These are elementary [Euclidean proofs of infinitely many primes in each odd residue class modulo four](../prime-number.md#euclidean-proofs-of-infinitely-many-primes-in-each-odd-residue-class-modulo-four).

On the set of all primes, the cofinite sets together with $P_1$ have the [finite intersection property](../topology.md#finite-intersection-property); so do the cofinite sets together with $P_3$. Extend the generated filters to [ultrafilters](../set-theory.md#ultrafilter) $\mathcal U_1$ and $\mathcal U_3$ using the [ultrafilter lemma](../set-theory.md#ultrafilter-lemma). They are nonprincipal because they contain every cofinite set, including the complement of any singleton. Form

$$
\boxed{F_1=\prod_p\mathbb F_p/\mathcal U_1,\qquad F_2=\prod_p\mathbb F_p/\mathcal U_3.}
$$

Every sentence of $\Sigma$ has a cofinite truth set, hence belongs to both [ultrafilters](../set-theory.md#ultrafilter). The [Łoś theorem](../model-theory.md#los-theorem) shows $F_1,F_2\models\Sigma$. In particular they are fields, since each field axiom holds in every factor.

For each fixed positive integer $n$, the sentence $n\cdot1\ne0$ holds in $\mathbb F_p$ unless $p$ divides $n$. Its truth set is cofinite, so both fields satisfy it. Therefore $\boxed{\operatorname{char}F_1=\operatorname{char}F_2=0}$.

Finally the sentence $\exists x(x^2+1=0)$ holds at exactly the primes in $P_1\cup\{2\}$. This set belongs to $\mathcal U_1$, whereas its complement contains $P_3$ and belongs to $\mathcal U_3$. Łoś therefore gives

$$
\boxed{F_1\models\exists x(x^2+1=0),\qquad F_2\models\neg\exists x(x^2+1=0).}
$$

Thus minus one is a square in the first characteristic-zero [first-order model](../mathematical-logic.md#model-of-a-first-order-theory) of $\Sigma$ and is not a square in the second. The construction also shows why eventual truth over all primes need not decide a sentence whose truth splits along two infinite prime subsequences.
