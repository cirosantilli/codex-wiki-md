# Excited state

↑ **Parent:** [Energy eigenvalue](energy-eigenvalue-split.md)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excited_state)

In the stationary-state terminology used here, an excited state is a normalized [energy eigenstate](energy-eigenstate.md) with [energy eigenvalue](energy-eigenvalue-split.md) $E>E_0$, where $E_0$ is an attained [ground-state energy](ground-state-energy.md). Its excitation cost above the [ground state](ground-state.md) is $\Delta E=E-E_0>0$. Several [energy eigenstates](energy-eigenstate.md) can share one excited [energy eigenvalue](energy-eigenvalue-split.md) through [degenerate energy levels](degenerate-energy-levels.md). An arbitrary [quantum superposition](quantum-superposition.md) with [energy](energy-split.md) [expectation value](expectation-value.md) above $E_0$ need not be a single stationary [excited state](excited-state.md); its energy content is described by its [Born rule](born-rule.md) probabilities or, for a continuum, its [scalar spectral measure](scalar-spectral-measure.md).

An [excited state](excited-state.md) can be a [stationary state](stationary-state.md): the second [infinite square well](infinite-square-well.md) mode is an immediate example. Spontaneous [photon emission](photon-emission.md) is a transition caused by coupling matter to the [electromagnetic field](electromagnetic-field.md); it is absent from an isolated matter-only [Hamiltonian operator](hamiltonian-quantum-mechanics.md). The relevant [energy eigenstate](energy-eigenstate.md) of that isolated model is therefore not automatically an [energy eigenstate](energy-eigenstate.md) of the full interacting system. This distinction explains how an ideal stationary [excited state](excited-state.md) can approximate a state with a finite experimental lifetime. See [electric-dipole interaction](electric-dipole-interaction.md) for the coupling used in the simplest optical model.

For a one-photon transition between stationary internal states, neglecting recoil, [conservation of energy](conservation-of-energy.md) requires

$$
h\nu=E_f-E_i\quad\text{for absorption},\qquad h\nu=E_i-E_f\quad\text{for emission}.
$$

Here $h$ is the [Planck constant](planck-constant.md) and $\nu$ is the [photon](photon.md) [frequency](frequency.md). Energy matching alone does not guarantee a transition: in the [electric-dipole interaction](electric-dipole-interaction.md) approximation its strength also involves the [matrix element](matrix-element.md) $\langle f|\mathbf d\cdot\boldsymbol\epsilon|i\rangle$, where $\mathbf d$ is the [electric dipole moment](electric-dipole-moment.md) operator and $\boldsymbol\epsilon$ specifies [photon polarization](photon-polarization.md). A zero [matrix element](matrix-element.md) gives an [electric-dipole selection rule](electric-dipole-selection-rule.md). Thus an absent optical line does not prove the corresponding [energy eigenvalue](energy-eigenvalue-split.md) is absent; a different interaction can couple different states. [Richard Fitzpatrick, Quantum Mechanics, Selection Rules](https://farside.ph.utexas.edu/teaching/qmech/lectures/node121.html). The [Franck-Hertz experiment](franck-hertz-experiment.md) gives a particularly instructive comparison between collision excitation and [photon emission](photon-emission.md).

The [first excited state](first-excited-state.md) terminology assumes a least [energy eigenvalue](energy-eigenvalue-split.md) above $E_0$. Such a next level is not guaranteed by [quantum mechanics](quantum-mechanics-split.md): a [continuous spectrum](continuous-spectrum.md) can start immediately above the spectral bottom, or [energy eigenvalues](energy-eigenvalue-split.md) can accumulate there. For example, a [Hamiltonian operator](hamiltonian-quantum-mechanics.md) with [energy eigenvalues](energy-eigenvalue-split.md) $0,\varepsilon,\varepsilon/2,\varepsilon/3,\ldots$, where $\varepsilon>0$, has a [ground-state energy](ground-state-energy.md) zero but no least positive [energy eigenvalue](energy-eigenvalue-split.md): every candidate $\varepsilon/n$ is exceeded downward by $\varepsilon/(n+1)$. A [free particle](free-particle.md) on the whole line has neither a [ground state](ground-state.md) nor a first stationary [excited state](excited-state.md). Each use of a discrete excitation ladder therefore needs a specified [Hamiltonian operator](hamiltonian-quantum-mechanics.md) and its [boundary conditions](boundary-condition.md).
