<h1 id="2/b/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [B](../b.md)

The protein production rate is proportional to the current [messenger RNA](../../../../messenger-rna.md) [concentration](../../../../concentration.md), not an independently constant $\beta_P$: $\beta_P(t)=rX_R(t)$. Setting the two coupled rate equations to zero gives

$$
X_{R,\mathrm{st}}=\frac{\beta_R}{\alpha_R},\qquad
\boxed{X_{P,\mathrm{st}}=\frac{r\beta_R}{\alpha_R\alpha_P}
=\frac{b\beta_R}{\alpha_P}}.
$$

With first-order removal, a [messenger RNA](../../../../messenger-rna.md) molecule has mean lifetime $1/\alpha_R$. At [biological translation](../../../../translation-biology.md) rate $r$ per molecule, it produces an expected $r/\alpha_R=b$ [proteins](../../../../protein-split.md) before its removal. Thus the [translational burst size](../../../../translational-burst-size.md) is a dimensionless mean number of offspring [proteins](../../../../protein-split.md) per [messenger RNA](../../../../messenger-rna.md), not a concentration and not a fixed number produced in every event. When production and removal are independent Poisson events, mixing a Poisson number of [biological translation](../../../../translation-biology.md) events over an exponentially distributed lifetime makes the burst count geometrically distributed on the nonnegative integers. Its [mean](../../../../expected-value-split.md) is $b$ and its [variance](../../../../variance-split.md) is $b(1+b)$.
