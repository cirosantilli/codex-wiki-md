# Measure theory

↑ **Parent:** [Real analysis](real-analysis-split.md)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_theory)

Measure theory studies [measures](measure-split.md), which assign sizes to [measurable sets](measurable-set.md), and the [Lebesgue integral](lebesgue-integral.md), which accumulates values weighted by those sizes. The same framework describes [Lebesgue measure](lebesgue-measure.md) on the [real numbers](real-number.md), [counting measure](counting-measure.md) on discrete collections, and [probability measures](probability-measure.md) on possible outcomes. The choice of [measure](measure-split.md) determines what counts as large; the choice of [sigma-algebra](sigma-algebra.md) determines what can be measured.

A striking example separates size from proximity: the [rational numbers](rational-number.md) in $[0,1]$ form a [dense set](dense-set.md) with [Lebesgue measure](lebesgue-measure.md) zero, although the whole interval has [Lebesgue measure](lebesgue-measure.md) one. This distinction makes [almost everywhere](almost-everywhere.md) statements useful: exceptional [null sets](null-set.md) can be ignored in a [Lebesgue integral](lebesgue-integral.md) even when they meet every open subinterval. The [monotone convergence theorem](monotone-convergence-theorem.md) and [dominated convergence theorem](dominated-convergence-theorem.md) then give precise conditions for passing a [limit](limit-of-a-function.md) through an [integral](integral-split.md).

A historical starting point is Lebesgue's 1902 thesis, [Intégrale, Longueur, Aire (original title page reproduced in USP lecture notes)](https://www.ime.usp.br/~glaucio/mat5798/textos/aula1.pdf#page=2). Its title connects [integration](integral-split.md) with the geometric task of assigning sizes.
