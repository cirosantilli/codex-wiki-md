# Expected value

↑ **Parent:** [Probability theory](probability-theory-split.md)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expected_value)

An [expected value](expected-value.md) is the [probability](probability.md)-weighted [arithmetic mean](arithmetic-mean.md) of a [real-valued random variable](random-variable-split.md). On a [probability space](probability-space.md) $(\Omega,\mathcal F,\mathbb P)$, its general definition is the [Lebesgue integral](lebesgue-integral.md)

$$
\mathbb E[X]=\int_\Omega X\,d\mathbb P=\int_{\mathbb R}x\,d\mu_X(x),
$$

where $\mu_X$ is the [probability distribution](probability-distribution.md) of $X$. For an [integrable random variable](integrable-random-variable.md), this is a finite [real number](real-number.md). With a [probability mass function](probability-mass-function.md) $p_X$, use $\sum_x xp_X(x)$; with a [probability density function](probability-density-function.md) $f_X$, use $\int_{\mathbb R}xf_X(x)\,dx$. These are two ways of computing the same weighted average, rather than different definitions for different experiments.

For example, suppose $X$ takes the values $-1,2,8$ with [probabilities](probability.md) $1/2,3/10,1/5$. Then

$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[X]=(-1)\frac12+2\frac3{10}+8\frac15=\frac{17}{10}=1.7.}
$$

The most likely outcome is $-1$, and $1.7$ is not a possible outcome at all. The [expected value](expected-value.md) summarizes all outcomes and their weights; it need not predict the result of one trial. Geometrically, placing [masses](mass.md) equal to the [probability masses](probability-mass.md) at the outcome positions gives a [center of mass](center-of-mass.md) at $1.7$, since $\sum_xp_X(x)(x-1.7)=0$. This balance-point interpretation also explains the connection to a [weighted arithmetic mean](weighted-arithmetic-mean.md). [Orloff and Bloom, expected value: probability weights and center of mass](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-05-introduction-to-probability-and-statistics-spring-2022/mit18_05_s22_class04-prep-b.pdf).

For the dimensionless [random variable](random-variable-split.md) $Y$ with [probability density function](probability-density-function.md) $f_Y(y)=2y$ on $[0,1]$, the [expected value](expected-value.md) is $\mathbb E[Y]=\int_0^1y(2y)\,dy=2/3$. Integrating the [probability density function](probability-density-function.md) itself gives one; to compute the [expected value](expected-value.md), multiply by the outcome first. [Orloff and Bloom, expectation for continuous random variables](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-05-introduction-to-probability-and-statistics-spring-2022/mit18_05_s22_class06-prep-a.pdf).

**[Figure 31](#image-expected-value-balances-probability-masses-or-integrates-outcomes-weighted-by-a-probability-density-function). Expected value balances probability masses, or integrates outcomes weighted by a probability density function**. The left panel's dashed line marks $1.7$, even though the only possible outcomes are $-1,2,8$. In the right panel, the shaded [area](surface-area.md) under $y f_Y(y)=2y^2$ is $2/3$; the [area](surface-area.md) under the dashed [probability density function](probability-density-function.md) is one. Both examples use dimensionless outcomes.

Why is this useful? [Linearity of expectation](linearity-of-expectation.md) lets us compute an average total by adding average contributions, even for [dependent random variables](statistical-dependence.md). And for [independent and identically distributed](independent-and-identically-distributed-random-variables.md) [integrable random variables](integrable-random-variable.md), the [strong law of large numbers](strong-law-of-large-numbers.md) says their [sample mean](sample-mean.md) approaches their common [expected value](expected-value.md) [almost surely](almost-sure-convergence.md). The theorem supplies the link to repeated observations; the weighted-average definition itself does not promise that any particular finite run will be close to the [expected value](expected-value.md).

There is a useful historical connection to valuing uncertain rewards. In his 1657 _De ratiociniis in ludo aleae_, [Christiaan Huygens](christiaan-huygens.md) assigns the value $(a+b)/2$ to equally likely rewards $a,b$, and $(pa+qb)/(p+q)$ when they have $p,q$ equally likely chances. These are the finite weighted-average formulas for [expected value](expected-value.md). His opening discussion asks how to divide the stakes when a game stops before completion, connecting [probability](probability.md) to a concrete question about an unfinished contest. [Huygens, De ratiociniis in ludo aleae, 1657; reprint of the 1714 English translation, introduction and Propositions I–III](https://math.dartmouth.edu/~doyle/docs/huygens/huygens.pdf).
