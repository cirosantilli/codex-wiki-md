<h1 id="1/a/i/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [I](../i.md)

Extend the given functions continuously to zero by $w_1(0)=w_2(0)=0$. This is forced by their stated differentiability. Their derivatives at zero are both zero, since the corresponding difference quotients tend to zero.

For $x<0$, $w_2=w_2'=0$, and for $x>0$, $w_1=w_1'=0$. At zero all four quantities vanish. Thus the [Wronskian](../../../../../wronskian.md) is

$$
\boxed{W(w_1,w_2)(x)=w_1w_2'-w_1'w_2=0\quad\text{for all }x.}
$$

Nevertheless, if the constant [linear combination](../../../../../linear-combination.md) $Aw_1+Bw_2$ vanishes identically, evaluation at $x=-1$ gives $-A=0$, and evaluation at $x=1$ gives $B=0$. Therefore **the two functions are [linearly independent](../../../../../linear-independence.md)**.

This illustrates why a [vanishing Wronskian does not imply linear dependence](../../../../../vanishing-wronskian-does-not-imply-linear-dependence.md) for arbitrary [differentiable functions](../../../../../differentiable-function.md). Here both functions are continuously differentiable, but neither has a second derivative at zero. The common regular differential equation hypothesis in the next part is absent.
