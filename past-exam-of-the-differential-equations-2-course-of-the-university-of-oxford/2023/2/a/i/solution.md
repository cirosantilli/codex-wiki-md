<h1 id="2/a/i/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [I](../i.md)

Put $N=n(n+1)$. After division by $1-x^2$, the [Legendre differential equation](../../../../../legendre-differential-equation.md) has coefficients

$$
p(x)=\frac{-2x}{1-x^2},\qquad q(x)=\frac{N}{1-x^2}.
$$

Both are [analytic functions](../../../../../holomorphic-function-split.md) at zero, so $\boxed{x=0\text{ is an ordinary point}}$. At either $x_0=\pm1$, $p$ has a genuine [simple pole](../../../../../simple-pole.md); hence these are singular points even when $n=0$ makes $q$ vanish. However,

$$
\lim_{x\to x_0}(x-x_0)p(x)=1,\qquad
\lim_{x\to x_0}(x-x_0)^2q(x)=0,
$$

and both products extend analytically across $x_0$. By the [regular singular point criterion for a second-order equation](../../../../../regular-singular-point-criterion-for-a-second-order-equation.md),

$$
\boxed{x=-1\text{ and }x=1\text{ are regular singular points}}.
$$

None of the three specified points is an [irregular singular point](../../../../../irregular-singular-point.md).
