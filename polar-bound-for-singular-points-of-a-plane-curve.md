# Polar bound for singular points of a plane curve

↑ **Parent:** [Intersection multiplicity](intersection-multiplicity.md)

For a complex [reduced plane curve](reduced-plane-curve.md) of degree $d$, the number $s$ of distinct [singular points](singular-point-of-an-algebraic-variety.md) is at most $d(d-1)/2$. For an [irreducible polynomial](irreducible-polynomial.md) $F$ of degree $d>1$, $F_x$ is nonzero, since a binary [homogeneous polynomial](homogeneous-polynomial.md) over $\mathbb C$ factors into linear factors. It has no common factor with $F$. Each [singular point](singular-point-of-an-algebraic-variety.md) contributes at least two to the intersection with the [polar curve](polar-curve.md) $F_x=0$, so [Bézout's theorem](bezout-s-theorem.md) gives $2s\leq d(d-1)$.

For an irreducible curve with $d>1$, one can improve this to $s\leq d(d-1)/2-1$. Place a nonsingular point at $p=[1:0:0]$. The [Euler theorem for homogeneous functions](euler-theorem-for-homogeneous-functions.md) gives $F_x(p)=0$ and $\nabla F_x(p)=(d-1)\nabla F(p)$, so the [polar curve](polar-curve.md) is nonsingular and tangent to the curve at $p$. This consumes at least two further units of [intersection multiplicity](intersection-multiplicity.md). For a reducible curve with component degrees $d_i$, add the irreducible bounds and at most $d_id_j$ intersections per component pair. The sum is $d(d-1)/2$ because $\sum d_i=d$.
