# Helium slit-flow experiment

↑ **Parent:** [Superfluidity](superfluidity.md)

[Pyotr Leonidovich Kapitza](pyotr-leonidovich-kapitza.md)'s 1938 test compared [helium](helium.md) flow through optically flat glass disks. Suspension set the tube-to-bath level difference; a cathetometer read levels. The gap was estimated as $0.5\,\mathrm{\mu m}$. Levels barely moved over minutes above the transition, but equalized in seconds below. A [laminar flow](laminar-flow.md) [viscosity](dynamic-viscosity-split.md) calculation gave only an upper bound.[Kapitza, 1938 viscosity paper, reprint chapter 34, pp.506–507](viscosity-of-liquid-helium-below-the-lambda-point-by-pyotr-leonidovich-kapitza.md)

The essential control is to compare the same restriction and level readout at different [temperatures](temperature-split.md). A changed [pressure](pressure-split.md) head is not itself evidence for a changed material response: the driving head must be included when interpreting the flow. For an idealized comparison at equal vapor [pressure](pressure-split.md), negligible [surface tension](surface-tension-split.md) corrections and nearly constant [mass density](density.md) $\rho$, [hydrostatic equilibrium](hydrostatic-equilibrium.md) gives

$$
\Delta P\simeq\rho g\Delta h.
$$

Here $\Delta h$ is the difference in free-surface heights and $g$ is [gravitational acceleration](gravitational-acceleration.md). This explains why following a liquid level provides both a driving [pressure](pressure-split.md) and a transported [volume](volume-thermodynamics.md); it does not turn a finite-resolution observation into proof of zero [viscosity](dynamic-viscosity-split.md).
