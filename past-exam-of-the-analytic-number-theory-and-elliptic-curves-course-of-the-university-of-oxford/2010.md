<h3 id="a/1">1</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a)

<h4 id="a/1/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#a/1)

For $N=\lfloor x\rfloor$, interchange a finite sum and an integral:

$$
\int_1^x S(t)F'(t)\,dt
=\sum_{n\le x}f(n)\int_n^x F'(t)\,dt
=F(x)S(x)-\sum_{n\le x}f(n)F(n).
$$

This proves the [Abel's summation formula](../analytic-number-theory.md#abel-s-summation-formula), including the term at $n=1$.

Apply it with $f(n)=1$, $F(t)=1/t$. Writing $\{t\}=t-\lfloor t\rfloor$ gives

$$
H_{\lfloor x\rfloor}=\log x+1-\frac{\{x\}}x-\int_1^x\frac{\{t\}}{t^2}\,dt
=\log x+\gamma-\frac{\{x\}}x+\int_x^\infty\frac{\{t\}}{t^2}\,dt.
$$

The last two terms are $O(x^{-1})$, proving the first expansion. For the refinement let $B_1(t)=\{t\}-1/2$. It has mean [zero](../polynomial.md#zero-of-a-function) on each unit interval, so its [primitive](../group-action.md#primitive-group-action) $P(t)=\int_0^tB_1(u)\,du$ is bounded and periodic. [Integration by parts](../calculus.md#integration-by-parts) gives

$$
\int_x^\infty\frac{B_1(t)}{t^2}\,dt
=-\frac{P(x)}{x^2}+2\int_x^\infty\frac{P(t)}{t^3}\,dt=O(x^{-2}).
$$

Thus for [integer](../integer.md) $x$, where $\{x\}=0$,

$$
\boxed{H_x=\log x+\gamma+\frac1{2x}+O(x^{-2}).}
$$

For the zeta representation, integrating each defining integral by parts yields

$$
sf_n(s)=-(n+1)^{-s}+\frac{(n+1)^{1-s}-n^{1-s}}{1-s}.
$$

Summing from $1$ to $N-1$ and substituting into the definition of $\zeta$ gives

$$
\boxed{\zeta(s)=\frac{N^{1-s}}{s-1}+\sum_{n\le N}n^{-s}-s\sum_{n=N}^\infty f_n(s).}
$$

Since $0\le x-n\le1$,

$$
\left|\sum_{n=N}^\infty f_n(s)\right|
\le\sum_{n=N}^\infty\int_n^{n+1}x^{-\sigma-1}\,dx
=\frac{N^{-\sigma}}\sigma.
$$

This is uniform on compact subsets of $\operatorname{Re}s>0$, so the series is [holomorphic](../holomorphic-function.md) there and limits at $s=1$ are legitimate after subtracting the [pole](../isolated-singularity.md#pole). Expanding $N^{1-s}=1-(s-1)\log N+O((s-1)^2)$ gives

$$
a_0=H_N-\log N-\sum_{n=N}^\infty f_n(1).
$$

The final tail is $O(N^{-1})$, and the harmonic expansion sends the remaining difference to $\gamma$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{a_0=\gamma.}
$$

<h3 id="a/2">2</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a)

<h4 id="a/2/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#a/2)

Use the convention $f(1)=1$ in the definition of a [multiplicative arithmetic function](../arithmetic-function.md#multiplicative-function). For a finite set of primes, multiplying the [absolutely convergent](../sequence-and-series.md#absolute-convergence) local series gives the Dirichlet sum over integers supported on those primes, by [unique prime factorization](../prime-number.md#fundamental-theorem-of-arithmetic). If all primes up to $Y$ are included, any missing [integer](../integer.md) is larger than $Y$, so the difference from the full series is bounded by $\sum_{n>Y}|f(n)|n^{-\sigma}$, which tends to [zero](../polynomial.md#zero-of-a-function). This proves the [Euler product](../analytic-number-theory.md#euler-product) in its absolute-convergence half-plane.

For coprime $m,n$, every divisor of $mn$ is uniquely a product of a divisor of $m$ and a divisor of $n$. Hence $\sigma_\alpha(mn)=\sigma_\alpha(m)\sigma_\alpha(n)$. The squared modulus is also multiplicative when $\alpha=i\theta$.

At a prime put $z=p^{-s}$ and $a=p^{i\theta}$, so $|a|=1$. Summing by the two divisor exponents gives

$$
\sum_{k\ge0}\left|\sum_{j=0}^ka^j\right|^2z^k
=\frac{1-z^2}{(1-z)^2(1-az)(1-a^{-1}z)}.
$$

For example, expand the square as $\sum_{j,l\le k}a^{j-l}$ and sum $z^k$ from $k=\max(j,l)$ to obtain this rational expression. It remains valid for $a=1$. [Absolute convergence](../sequence-and-series.md#absolute-convergence) for $\sigma>1$ follows from $|\sigma_{i\theta}(p^k)|\le k+1$ and convergence of $\sum_p p^{-\sigma}$. Multiplying the factors therefore proves

$$
\boxed{\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{|\sigma_{i\theta}(n)|^2}{n^s}
=\frac{\zeta(s)^2\zeta(s+i\theta)\zeta(s-i\theta)}{\zeta(2s)}.}
$$

Suppose now that $\zeta(1+i\theta)=0$. Necessarily $\theta\ne0$, since $s=1$ is a [pole](../isolated-singularity.md#pole). [Complex conjugation](../complex-analysis.md#complex-conjugation) gives a [zero](../polynomial.md#zero-of-a-function) also at $1-i\theta$. In the numerator, the [double pole](../isolated-singularity.md#double-pole) at $s=1$ is canceled by those two zeros in the shifted factors. At $s=1\pm i\theta$, the possible [simple pole](../isolated-singularity.md#simple-pole) of a shifted factor is canceled by the double [zero](../polynomial.md#zero-of-a-function) of $\zeta(s)^2$. Thus on $\operatorname{Re}s>0$ the only possible remaining poles come from zeros of $\zeta(2s)$. Since the [Euler product](../analytic-number-theory.md#euler-product) excludes zeros for $\operatorname{Re}(2s)>1$, these possible poles lie in $0<\operatorname{Re}s\le1/2$. In particular $f$ is regular for $\operatorname{Re}s>1/2$.

The assumed nonnegative-series principle now makes its [Dirichlet series](../analytic-number-theory.md#dirichlet-series) converge and equal $f$ there. Its first coefficient is one and all coefficients are nonnegative, so $f(\sigma)\ge1$ for real $\sigma>1/2$. But as $\sigma\downarrow1/2$, the numerator remains finite while $\zeta(2\sigma)\to+\infty$, forcing $f(\sigma)\to0$. This contradiction proves

$$
\boxed{\zeta(s)\text{ has no zero on }\operatorname{Re}s=1.}
$$

At the real point $s=1$ the statement is understood as a [pole](../isolated-singularity.md#pole) rather than a finite nonzero value. The [pole](../isolated-singularity.md#pole)-location argument did not assume the [zero](../polynomial.md#zero-of-a-function)-free boundary before proving it.

<h3 id="a/3">3</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a)

<h4 id="a/3/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#a/3)

The vertical integral is [absolutely convergent](../sequence-and-series.md#absolute-convergence) because the denominator is quadratic in the imaginary part. If $x<1$, move its line to the right: no poles are crossed, and the integral on $\operatorname{Re}s=R$ is $O(x^R/R)$, tending to [zero](../polynomial.md#zero-of-a-function). If $x>1$, move left across the simple poles at $0,-1$. Their residues are $1$ and $-x^{-1}$, and the far-left integral tends to [zero](../polynomial.md#zero-of-a-function). Horizontal segments vanish by their $O(|\operatorname{Im}s|^{-2})$ decay for a fixed pair of vertical lines. At $x=1$, dominated convergence on the original line gives the limiting value [zero](../polynomial.md#zero-of-a-function). Hence the [kernel](../kernel-of-a-linear-map.md) is $0$ below one and $1-x^{-1}$ at or above one.

On a line $c>2$, [absolute convergence](../sequence-and-series.md#absolute-convergence) permits interchanging the totient [Dirichlet series](../analytic-number-theory.md#dirichlet-series) and this integral. The $n$th term is $x\phi(n)$ times the [kernel](../kernel-of-a-linear-map.md) at $x/n$, giving exactly $\phi(n)(x-n)$ for $n\le x$. Thus

$$
S(x):=\sum_{n\le x}\phi(n)(x-n)
=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}
\frac{\zeta(s-1)}{\zeta(s)}\frac{x^{s+1}}{s(s+1)}\,ds.
$$

We may take $c=5/2$ and shift to $\operatorname{Re}s=3/2$. The denominator has no zeros in this strip; the only [pole](../isolated-singularity.md#pole) crossed is $s=2$, where $\zeta(s-1)$ has [residue](../analysis.md#residue) one. Its [residue](../analysis.md#residue) in the integrand is

$$
\frac{x^3}{6\zeta(2)}=\frac{x^3}{\pi^2}.
$$

On the new line, the supplied estimates bound the integrand by $O(x^{5/2}|t|^{-3/2}\log^8|t|)$ for $|t|\ge2$, an integrable function. The compact portion is bounded as well. The same estimates, uniformly across the strip, make the horizontal integrals tend to [zero](../polynomial.md#zero-of-a-function). Consequently

$$
\boxed{S(x)=\frac{x^3}{\pi^2}+O(x^{5/2})\sim\frac{x^3}{\pi^2}.}
$$

For the Tauberian step let $A(x)=\sum_{n\le x}\phi(n)$. It is nondecreasing because the summands are nonnegative, and $S(x)=\int_0^xA(t)\,dt$. For fixed $0<\epsilon<1$,

$$
\frac{S(x)-S((1-\epsilon)x)}{\epsilon x}
\le A(x)\le
\frac{S((1+\epsilon)x)-S(x)}{\epsilon x}.
$$

After division by $x^2$ and passage to lower and upper limits, the asymptotic for $S$ gives bounds $(3-3\epsilon+\epsilon^2)/\pi^2$ and $(3+3\epsilon+\epsilon^2)/\pi^2$. Letting $\epsilon\downarrow0$ squeezes them together. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\sum_{n\le x}\phi(n)\sim\frac{3x^2}{\pi^2}.}
$$

This uses monotonicity of the summatory function; differentiating an asymptotic formula without such control would not justify the conclusion.

<h3 id="b/4">4</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b)

The [integer](../integer.md) $n=-3$ gives $n^3-5=-32$, whose 2-adic norm is $2^{-5}<2^{-3}$. For the 5-adic condition, if $5\nmid n$ then $n^3-5$ is a unit, while if $5\mid n$ its valuation is exactly one. Neither case has norm below $5^{-3}$. The Newton construction for the remaining requests follows.

<h4 id="b/4/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#b/4)

<h5 id="b/4/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#b/4/i)

Let $d=|f'(a_0)|>0$ and $q_0=|f(a_0)|/d^2<1$. At any step before reaching a root, set

$$
a_{i+1}=a_i+h_i,\qquad h_i=-\frac{f(a_i)}{f'(a_i)}.
$$

Assuming inductively that $|f'(a_i)|=d$ and $q_i=|f(a_i)|/d^2<1$, this gives

$$
\boxed{|a_{i+1}-a_i|=\frac{|f(a_i)|}{|f'(a_i)|}=dq_i.}
$$

All derivatives of a [polynomial](../polynomial.md) over $R$ are integral on $R$, so $d\le1$. Hence $|h_i|<1$, and the [ultrametric inequality](../non-archimedean-analysis.md#ultrametric-inequality) keeps $a_{i+1}$ in $R$. If a root is reached, retain that value for subsequent terms, and the asserted weak step inequality still holds.

<h4 id="b/4/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#b/4)

<h5 id="b/4/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#b/4/ii)

[Taylor expansion](../calculus.md#taylor-expansion) of $f'$ about $a_i$ has integral coefficients, so

$$
|f'(a_i+h_i)-f'(a_i)|\le|h_i|=dq_i<d.
$$

In a [Non-Archimedean absolute value](../non-archimedean-analysis.md#non-archimedean-absolute-value), adding an error of norm strictly smaller than $d$ cannot change a quantity of norm $d$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{|f'(a_i)|=d\quad\text{for every }i.}
$$

This simultaneously verifies the [derivative](../calculus.md#derivative) assumption used at the next step of part (i).

<h4 id="b/4/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#b/4)

<h5 id="b/4/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#b/4/iii)

The constant and linear Taylor terms cancel in the Newton step, leaving

$$
|f(a_{i+1})|\le|h_i|^2=d^2q_i^2,\qquad
q_{i+1}\le q_i^2\le q_0^{2^{i+1}}.
$$

Thus residuals strictly decrease whenever the current residual is nonzero, and the steps tend to [zero](../polynomial.md#zero-of-a-function) doubly exponentially. By the [ultrametric inequality](../non-archimedean-analysis.md#ultrametric-inequality), the distance between any two sufficiently late iterates is bounded by the largest intervening step. The sequence is Cauchy and converges in the complete [valuation ring](../commutative-algebra.md#valuation-ring) to $a$. Continuity gives $f(a)=0$, and

$$
\boxed{|a-a_0|\le dq_0=\frac{|f(a_0)|}{|f'(a_0)|}.}
$$

The literal infinite chain of strict inequalities needs the root-hit exception: if $f(a_0)=0$, or a later iterate is already a root, it cannot keep strictly decreasing below [zero](../polynomial.md#zero-of-a-function). The construction is eventually constant in that case; all existence and distance conclusions remain valid.

For the equality case, suppose $|f(a_0)|=d^2$ with $0<d<1$. The first Newton step has norm $|h_0|=d$. Write the [Taylor coefficient](../calculus.md#taylor-coefficient) of $h^2$ as $c_2(a_0)=f''(a_0)/2$. Its norm is strictly below one by hypothesis; all higher coefficients have norm at most one. Hence

$$
|f(a_0+h_0)|\le\max(|c_2(a_0)|d^2,d^3)<d^2.
$$

The [derivative](../calculus.md#derivative) changes by $2c_2(a_0)h_0+O(h_0^2)$, of norm strictly below $d$, so it still has norm $d$. The strict Hensel argument applies from this first iterate onward, and the root remains within distance $d=|f(a_0)|/d$ of $a_0$. If characteristic two is admitted, interpret the displayed half-second-[derivative](../calculus.md#derivative) as the second [Taylor coefficient](../calculus.md#taylor-coefficient) $\sum_j\binom j2c_jb^{j-2}$; this is the division-free form of the required hypothesis.

For the final local equation put $F(t)=t^3+3t$. Its values modulo nine are $0,4,5$, so the sum of two such values is confined to $0,1,4,5,8$. Conversely choose initial pairs for these five classes as

$$
(0,0),\quad(-1,-1),\quad(1,0),\quad(-1,0),\quad(1,1),
$$

respectively. Fix the second coordinate $y_0$ and apply the equality-case Hensel result to $g(X)=F(X)+F(y_0)-n$ at the first coordinate $x_0$. Its residual is divisible by nine, $g'(x_0)=3(x_0^2+1)$ has 3-adic norm $1/3$, and $g''(b)/2=3b$ has norm below one for every $b\in\mathbb Z_3$. The strict or equality case therefore supplies a root in $\mathbb Z_3$. Thus

$$
\boxed{n\equiv0,1,4,5,8\pmod9}
$$

is the necessary and sufficient condition.

<h3 id="b/5">5</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b)

We first prove all three general torsion assertions before treating the lettered cases. If a finite point has $|x|_p>1$, the cubic term dominates the integral lower terms, so $v_p(x)=-2m$, $v_p(y)=-3m$ for an [integer](../integer.md) $m\ge1$. Its parameter $z=-x/y$ then lies in $p\mathbb Z_p$, with norm at most $p^{-1}$. The integral Weierstrass model defines its [formal group](../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#formal-group-law) at infinity over $\mathbb Z_p$: using $w=-1/y$ gives $w=z^3+Azw^2+Bw^3$, an integral power series starting in degree three.

The supplied torsion estimate excludes a nonzero torsion parameter for $p\ge3$, since $p^{-1}<p^{-1/(p-1)}$, and the lower bound increases for higher $p$-powers. For $p=2$ it excludes orders $2^n$ with $n\ge2$, because their bound is strictly above $1/2$. The remaining possibility is order two, which would imply the finite point equals its negative and hence $y=0$. A nonintegral $x$ cannot solve the monic cubic with integral coefficients, so this is impossible too. Thus every finite torsion point has integral $x$, and the equation then forces integral $y$.

For [good reduction](../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#good-reduction-of-an-elliptic-curve) the reduction map is a [group homomorphism](../group-theory.md#group-homomorphism) whose [kernel](../kernel-of-a-linear-map.md) is this [formal group](../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#formal-group-law) at infinity. Its torsion-free [kernel](../kernel-of-a-linear-map.md) proves injectivity on torsion, giving a [subgroup](../group.md#subgroup) of the finite reduced group. Applying local integrality to a rational torsion point at every prime gives $x,y\in\mathbb Z$.

If $y\ne0$, the double of a rational torsion point is finite and integral. The doubling formula

$$
x(2P)=\frac{(3x^2+a)^2}{4y^2}-2x
$$

therefore implies $y^2\mid(3x^2+a)^2$. Also $y^2=x^3+ax+b$. Evaluating the supplied [polynomial](../polynomial.md) identity at the integral $x$ now gives

$$
\boxed{y=0\quad\text{or}\quad y^2\mid4a^3+27b^2.}
$$

This establishes the needed [Nagell–Lutz theorem](../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#nagell-lutz-theorem) conclusions without assuming them in the lettered applications.

<h4 id="b/5/a">a</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#b/5)

<h5 id="b/5/a/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#b/5/a)

Nonsingularity with $b=a^2$ forces $a\ne0$. Take

$$
\boxed{P=(0,a).}
$$

It lies on the curve. Its [tangent](../geometry-and-topology.md#tangent) slope is $a/(2a)=1/2$, so the doubling formula gives $x(2P)=1/4$. If $P$ were torsion, $2P$ would be torsion as well, contradicting the integrality just proved. Therefore $P$ has infinite order.

<h4 id="b/5/b">b</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#b/5)

<h5 id="b/5/b/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b/5/b)

For $y^2=x^3+x+2$, the point $P=(1,2)$ doubles to $(-1,0)$: its [tangent](../geometry-and-topology.md#tangent) slope is $(3+1)/(2\cdot2)=1$, giving $x(2P)=-1$ and $y(2P)=0$. Thus $P$ has order four.

The curve has [good reduction](../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#good-reduction-of-an-elliptic-curve) at $3$, since $4+27\cdot4=112$ is nonzero modulo three. Its reduced points are the point at infinity, two points above $x=1$, and one above $x=2$, so there are four in total. Injectivity of torsion reduction bounds the rational torsion order by four. The known four points exhaust it:

$$
\boxed{E(\mathbb Q)_{\rm tors}=\{O,(-1,0),(1,2),(1,-2)\}\cong C_4.}
$$

<h4 id="b/5/c">c</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#b/5)

<h5 id="b/5/c/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#b/5/c)

Modulo five the curve is $y^2=x^3+x$, with [good reduction](../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#good-reduction-of-an-elliptic-curve) because $4a^3+27b^2\equiv4\pmod5$. It has one point above each of $x=0,2,3$ and no others, plus infinity, so its group has order four. Rational torsion therefore has order dividing four.

A nonzero [rational point](../algebraic-variety.md#rational-point) of order two would have $y=0$ and integral $x$, so would give an [integer](../integer.md) root of $x^3+ax+b$. But modulo two this [polynomial](../polynomial.md) is $x^3+x+1$, which is one at both residues. Thus there is no rational [2-torsion](../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#2-torsion). Every nontrivial finite group of order dividing four contains an element of order two, so

$$
\boxed{E(\mathbb Q)_{\rm tors}=\{O\}.}
$$

<h3 id="b/6">6</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b)

<h4 id="b/6/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#b/6)

Complete the square-class map at the identity by $q(O)=1$; at the finite point $T=(0,0)$ its value is the class of $b$. For another [rational point](../algebraic-variety.md#rational-point) write

$$
x=r/s^2,\qquad y=t/s^3,\qquad\gcd(r,s)=1.
$$

These denominator powers follow because a negative valuation of $x$ forces the leading cubic term to dominate, so that valuation is even and the valuation of $y$ is three halves of it. The equation gives

$$
t^2=r(r^2+ars^2+bs^4).
$$

If a prime $\ell\nmid b$ divides $r$, it does not divide the second factor, since $\ell\nmid s$. Thus $v_\ell(r)$ is even. All denominator valuations are even too. Every image class therefore has a representative which is a signed squarefree divisor of $b$, proving that $\operatorname{im}q$ is finite.

Use the standard 2-isogeny with [kernel](../kernel-of-a-linear-map.md) $\{O,T\}$ and [dual isogeny](../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#dual-isogeny) satisfying $\widehat\phi\phi=[2]$, $\phi\widehat\phi=[2]$. Away from the exceptional points one may take

$$
\phi(x,y)=\left(x+a+\frac bx,\ y\left(1-\frac b{x^2}\right)\right).
$$

Applying the analogous isogeny to $E'$ gives a curve with coefficients $4a,16b$; scaling its coordinates by $1/4,1/8$ gives the dual map back to $E$. The analogous descent map $q'$ on $D$ has [kernel](../kernel-of-a-linear-map.md) $\phi(C)$ and finite image. The sequence

$$
D/\phi(C)\ \xrightarrow{\widehat\phi}\ C/2C\longrightarrow C/\widehat\phi(D)\longrightarrow0
$$

is exact at the last two terms. Its outside groups are finite, identified with the two finite descent images, so **$C/2C$ is finite**.

For $E_p:y^2=x(x^2-p^2)$, the rational [2-torsion](../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#2-torsion) is $O,(0,0),(p,0),(-p,0)$, of order four. The first descent image is exactly $\{1,-1,p,-p\}$: these classes occur on the [2-torsion](../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#2-torsion), and the divisor-support bound permits no others. The isogenous curve is $E'_p:y^2=x(x^2+4p^2)$. Its real points have $x\ge0$, so the second image lies in the four positive classes $\{1,2,p,2p\}$.

Here the [kernel](../kernel-of-a-linear-map.md) $\{O,(0,0)\}$ of the dual is contained in $\phi(C)$, since $\phi(\pm p,0)=(0,0)$. Thus the displayed map $D/\phi(C)\to C/2C$ is injective, and exactness gives

$$
|C/2C|=|\operatorname{im}q|\,|\operatorname{im}q'|=4|\operatorname{im}q'|.
$$

The allowed rank formula makes the left side $2^{R+2}$, so

$$
\boxed{2^R=|\operatorname{im}q'|\le4,\qquad R\le2.}
$$

Now consider the quartic relation. Normalize a nontrivial [integer](../integer.md) solution so that $p$ does not divide both $x,y$, by dividing them by $p$ and $z$ by $p^2$ whenever necessary. If $p\nmid x$, reduction modulo $p$ gives $x^4=2z^2$, with $z\ne0$ modulo $p$, making $2$ a [quadratic residue](../quadratic-residue.md). If $p\mid x$, then $p\nmid y$ and $p\mid z$; write $x=pX$, $z=pZ$, divide by $p^2$, and reduce to get $y^4=2Z^2$ modulo $p$. Again $2$ is a square. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\left(\frac2p\right)=1,\qquad p\equiv\pm1\pmod8.}
$$

If the class $2$ occurred in $\operatorname{im}q'$, write the corresponding coordinate as $x'=2u^2/v^2$. The curve equation then gives

$$
u^4+p^2v^4=2z^2,\qquad z=\frac{y'v^3}{4u}.
$$

Here $u,v$ are nonzero coprime integers and $z$ is rational. Since $2z^2$ is an [integer](../integer.md), its reduced denominator must be one, so $z$ is an [integer](../integer.md) too. For $p\equiv3,5\pmod8$ this contradicts the quartic obstruction. Hence class $2$ is absent. A [subgroup](../group.md#subgroup) of $\{1,2,p,2p\}$ omitting $2$ has order at most two, so $2^R\le2$ and $R\le1$.

For $p=5$, direct substitution verifies the nonintegral point

$$
\boxed{(x,y)=\left(\frac{25}{4},\frac{75}{8}\right).}
$$

A rational torsion point would have integral coordinates by the preceding theorem, so this point has infinite order. Thus $R\ge1$, and together with the upper bound,

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{rank}E_5(\mathbb Q)=1.}
$$
