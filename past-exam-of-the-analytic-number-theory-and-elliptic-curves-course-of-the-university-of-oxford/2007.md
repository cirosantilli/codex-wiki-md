<h4 id="a/1/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#a/1)

<h5 id="a/1/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#a/1/i)

The [Dirichlet convolution](../arithmetic-function.md#dirichlet-convolution) is $(f_1*f_2)(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}f_1(d)f_2(n/d)$. Fix $s$ with $\sigma=\operatorname{Re}s>1$. [Absolute convergence](../sequence-and-series.md#absolute-convergence) of the two [Dirichlet series](../analytic-number-theory.md#dirichlet-series) gives

$$
\sum_{a,b\ge1}|f_1(a)f_2(b)(ab)^{-s}|
=\left(\sum_a|f_1(a)|a^{-\sigma}\right)
\left(\sum_b|f_2(b)|b^{-\sigma}\right)<\infty.
$$

Thus the double [series](../sequence-and-series.md#series-mathematics) may be regrouped by $n=ab$, proving

$$
\boxed{F_1(s)F_2(s)=\sum_{n\ge1}(f_1*f_2)(n)n^{-s}.}
$$

The same estimate proves [absolute convergence](../sequence-and-series.md#absolute-convergence) of the resulting [Dirichlet series](../analytic-number-theory.md#dirichlet-series).

<h4 id="a/1/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#a/1)

<h5 id="a/1/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#a/1/ii)

For a finite set of primes $p\le y$, multiply the absolutely convergent local [series](../sequence-and-series.md#series-mathematics). Unique [prime factorization](../prime-number.md#fundamental-theorem-of-arithmetic) and multiplicativity, with $g(1)=1$, give

$$
\prod_{p\le y}\sum_{e\ge0}g(p^e)p^{-es}
=\sum_{\substack{n\ge1\\\text{every prime divisor of }n\le y}}g(n)n^{-s}.
$$

The omitted [integers](../integer.md) all exceed $y$, so the absolute difference from $G(s)$ is at most $\sum_{n>y}|g(n)|n^{-\sigma}$, which tends to zero. Hence the finite products converge to $G(s)$:

$$
\boxed{G(s)=\prod_pG_p(s)\qquad(\operatorname{Re}s>1).}
$$

This proves the [Euler product](../analytic-number-theory.md#euler-product) directly from [absolute convergence](../sequence-and-series.md#absolute-convergence), rather than treating an infinite formal multiplication as automatically valid.

<h4 id="a/1/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#a/1)

<h5 id="a/1/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#a/1/iii)

The [Möbius function](../arithmetic-function.md#mobius-function) satisfies $\mu(p^e)^2=1$ for $e=0,1$ and is zero for $e\ge2$. Therefore

$$
H_1(s)=\prod_p(1+p^{-s})=\frac{\zeta(s)}{\zeta(2s)}.
$$

The inverse local factor is $(1+p^{-s})^{-1}=\sum_{e\ge0}(-1)^ep^{-es}$. Consequently the inverse under [Dirichlet convolution](../arithmetic-function.md#dirichlet-convolution) is the [Liouville function](../arithmetic-function.md#liouville-function)

$$
\boxed{h_2(n)=(-1)^{\Omega(n)},}
$$

where $\Omega(n)$ counts prime factors with multiplicity. Both functions are [multiplicative functions](../arithmetic-function.md#multiplicative-function). At $p^e$ with $e\ge1$, their convolution is $(-1)^e+(-1)^{e-1}=0$, while its value at one is one. Multiplicativity then proves $h_1*h_2=I$ on every positive [integer](../integer.md). Its [Dirichlet series](../analytic-number-theory.md#dirichlet-series) is $H_2(s)=\zeta(2s)/\zeta(s)$ for $\operatorname{Re}s>1$.

<h3 id="a/2">2</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a)

<h4 id="a/2/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#a/2)

For $n\le x$, write $F(n)=F(x)-\int_n^xF'(t)dt$, multiply by $f(n)$ and sum. Interchanging a finite sum with the [integral](../integral.md) gives the [Abel summation formula](../analytic-number-theory.md#abel-s-summation-formula)

$$
\sum_{n\le x}f(n)F(n)=S(x)F(x)-\int_1^xS(t)F'(t)\,dt.
$$

Apply it with $F(t)=t^{-s}$ and $S(t)=\sum_{n\le t}a_n$. If $\sigma>\alpha$, the boundary term is $O(x^{\alpha-\sigma})\to0$ and $\int_1^\infty|S(t)t^{-s-1}|dt$ converges. Therefore the [Dirichlet series](../analytic-number-theory.md#dirichlet-series) converges and

$$
A(s)=s\int_1^\infty S(t)t^{-s-1}dt=s\sum_{m\ge1}I_m(s).
$$

Choose $C$ with $|S(t)|\le Ct^\alpha$ for $t\ge1$. For $\sigma>\alpha+\delta$,

$$
|I_m(s)|\le C\int_m^{m+1}t^{\alpha-\sigma-1}dt\le Cm^{-1-\delta}.
$$

The [Weierstrass M-test](../probability-and-statistics.md#weierstrass-m-test) proves [uniform convergence](../sequence-and-series.md#uniform-convergence) of $\sum I_m$ on this entire open half-plane. For fixed $s$, the same estimate with its actual $\sigma$ gives $I_m(s)=O(m^{\alpha-1-\sigma})$. Each $I_m$ is an [entire function](../holomorphic-function.md#entire-function), since its [integral](../integral.md) has bounded positive $t$ range and can be differentiated in $s$ under an integrable bound on each [compact set](../topology.md#compact-space). Thus the locally uniform sum is [holomorphic](../holomorphic-function.md), and so is its product with $s$. This gives [holomorphy from polynomial growth of arithmetic partial sums](../analytic-number-theory.md#holomorphy-from-polynomial-growth-of-arithmetic-partial-sums) and proves holomorphy of $A$ for $\operatorname{Re}s>\alpha$. Multiplication by the unbounded factor $s$ does not assert [uniform convergence](../sequence-and-series.md#uniform-convergence) of $A$ on a whole unbounded half-plane; local [uniform convergence](../sequence-and-series.md#uniform-convergence) is what the holomorphy argument needs.

With $a_n=\mu(n)$ and the hypothetical bound, $A_\mu(s)$ is [holomorphic](../holomorphic-function.md) for $\operatorname{Re}s>3/4$. On $\operatorname{Re}s>1$, the [Euler product](../analytic-number-theory.md#euler-product) gives $\zeta(s)A_\mu(s)=1$. The [identity theorem](../complex-analysis.md#identity-theorem) extends this as a meromorphic identity throughout the connected half-plane $\operatorname{Re}s>3/4$. Hence $\zeta$ has **no zeros in that half-plane**: at any zero, the product with the [holomorphic](../holomorphic-function.md) $A_\mu$ would be zero. The [pole](../isolated-singularity.md#pole) at one is not a zero; $A_\mu$ has a compensating zero there.

<h3 id="a/3">3</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a)

<h4 id="a/3/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#a/3)

<h5 id="a/3/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#a/3/i)

The [double-angle formula](../geometry-and-topology.md#double-angle-formula) gives

$$
3\pm4\cos\theta+\cos2\theta=2(1\pm\cos\theta)^2\ge0.
$$

For $\sigma>1$, the [logarithm](../calculus.md#logarithm) of the absolutely convergent [Euler product](../analytic-number-theory.md#euler-product) gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
&3\log\zeta(\sigma)+4\log|\zeta(\sigma+it)|+\log|\zeta(\sigma+2it)|\\
&=\sum_p\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{3+4\cos(mt\log p)+\cos(2mt\log p)}{mp^{m\sigma}}\ge0.
\end{aligned}
$$

Exponentiation proves

$$
\boxed{\zeta(\sigma)^3|\zeta(\sigma+it)|^4|\zeta(\sigma+2it)|\ge1.}
$$

Suppose $\zeta(1+it)=0$ for $t\ne0$, of order $r\ge1$. As $\sigma\downarrow1$, its middle factor is $O((\sigma-1)^{4r})$, the first is $O((\sigma-1)^{-3})$ because the [pole](../isolated-singularity.md#pole) at one is simple, and the final factor is bounded because $1+2it\ne1$. The product is $O((\sigma-1)^{4r-3})\to0$, contradicting the lower bound one. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\zeta(1+it)\ne0\qquad(t\ne0).}
$$

This is the [three-four-one zero-free-region argument](../analytic-number-theory.md#three-four-one-zero-free-region-argument) specialized to the line with real part one.

<h4 id="a/3/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#a/3)

<h5 id="a/3/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#a/3/ii)

Complete multiplicativity and $f(p)=\pm1$ give, for $\operatorname{Re}s>1$,

$$
\boxed{F(s)=\prod_p(1-f(p)p^{-s})^{-1}.}
$$

The product is nonzero there; its absolutely convergent [logarithm](../calculus.md#logarithm) has terms $f(p)^mp^{-ms}/m$. Thus

$$
3\log\zeta(\sigma)+4\log|F(\sigma+it)|+\log|\zeta(\sigma+2it)|
=\sum_{p,m\ge1}\frac{3+4f(p)^m\cos(mt\log p)+\cos(2mt\log p)}{mp^{m\sigma}}.
$$

Each numerator is one of $3\pm4\cos\theta+\cos2\theta$, so it is nonnegative. Exponentiation gives the required lower bound one.

If $F(1+it)=0$ with $t\ne0$, its assumed holomorphy makes $|F(\sigma+it)|^4=O((\sigma-1)^4)$, or smaller for a higher-order zero. The zeta factor at real $\sigma$ grows at most as $(\sigma-1)^{-3}$, while the zeta factor near $1+2it$ stays bounded. The product tends to zero, a contradiction. Hence

$$
\boxed{F(1+it)\ne0\qquad(t\ne0).}
$$

<h3 id="a/4">4</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a)

<h4 id="a/4/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#a/4)

For $x>0$, $(e^x-1)^{-1}=\sum_{n\ge1}e^{-nx}$. If $\sigma=\operatorname{Re}s>1$, the [integral](../integral.md) of the sum of absolute values is $\Gamma(\sigma)\sum n^{-\sigma}<\infty$. Therefore interchange of sum and [integral](../integral.md) is justified, and the substitution $u=nx$ gives

$$
J(s)=\sum_{n\ge1}\int_0^\infty x^{s-1}e^{-nx}dx
=\boxed{\Gamma(s)\zeta(s).}
$$

On the two unbounded rays of the specified [Hankel contour](../complex-analysis.md#hankel-contour), $x\ge\pi$ and $1/(e^x-1)$ decays exponentially, dominating every fixed power $x^{\sigma-1}$. The branch multiplier on the lower ray is a fixed factor $e^{2\pi i(s-1)}$. On the circular part, the chosen branch is continuous along the parametrized arc, and $e^z-1$ never vanishes because its nonzero zeros have modulus at least $2\pi$. Thus $I(s)$ exists for every $s$. The estimates are uniform when $s$ ranges over a [compact set](../topology.md#compact-space), including after differentiation in $s$, so $I$ is an [entire function](../holomorphic-function.md#entire-function).

Let $C(s)=(e^{2\pi is}-1)\Gamma(s)$. The given identity shows that $I(s)/C(s)$ agrees with $\zeta(s)$ for $\operatorname{Re}s>1$ wherever the quotient is directly defined. It supplies the [meromorphic continuation](../holomorphic-function.md#meromorphic-continuation) elsewhere wherever $C$ is finite and nonzero, with removable points filled by limits. At a nonpositive [integer](../integer.md) $-k$, the simple zero of the exponential factor cancels the supplied gamma [pole](../isolated-singularity.md#pole), giving

$$
C(-k)=\frac{2\pi i(-1)^k}{k!}.
$$

At $s=-k$, the [integrals](../integral.md) on the two rays cancel because the integrand is single-valued. The remaining positively oriented circle has only the [pole](../isolated-singularity.md#pole) at zero inside it, so

$$
I(-k)=2\pi i\operatorname{Res}_{z=0}\frac{z^{-k-1}}{e^z-1}
=2\pi i\,[z^{k+1}]\frac z{e^z-1}.
$$

The coefficient at $k=0$ is $-1/2$, hence $\boxed{\zeta(0)=-1/2}$. For positive even $k$, the relevant odd coefficient has degree at least three and is zero, giving $\boxed{\zeta(-2m)=0}$ for $m\ge1$. For $k=2m-1$, the given generating function gives the coefficient $(-1)^{m-1}B_m/(2m)!$. Division by $C(-(2m-1))=-2\pi i/(2m-1)!$ yields

$$
\boxed{\zeta(1-2m)=\frac{(-1)^mB_m}{2m}\qquad(m\ge1).}
$$

The symbols $B_m$ here follow the printed generating function: they are the positive magnitudes of the usual even-index [Bernoulli numbers](../bernoulli-number.md), so $B_1=1/6$, $B_2=1/30$. This convention accounts for the alternating sign in the final expression.

<h5 id="b/5/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#b/5/i)

The strict inequality means $v_3(x^2+2)>2$. Taking $x=5$ gives $x^2+2=27$, so

$$
\boxed{|5^2+2|_3=3^{-3}<3^{-2}.}
$$

For the other expression, if $3\nmid x$ then $x^2+3\not\equiv0\pmod3$ and its [P-adic valuation](../number-theory.md#p-adic-valuation) is zero. If $3\mid x$, then $x^2+3=3(3(x/3)^2+1)$ has [P-adic valuation](../number-theory.md#p-adic-valuation) exactly one. It can never have valuation greater than two. Thus **no such [integer](../integer.md) exists** for the second strict inequality.

<h4 id="b/5/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#b/5)

<h5 id="b/5/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#b/5/ii)

In the [P-adic numbers](../non-archimedean-analysis.md#p-adic-number), $\sum_{j\ge0}p^{2j}$ converges to $1/(1-p^2)$. Consequently

$$
\frac{1+2p}{p-p^3}=(p^{-1}+2)\sum_{j\ge0}p^{2j}
=\boxed{p^{-1}+2+p+2p^2+p^3+2p^4+\cdots.}
$$

Since $p\ne2$, the digits one and two are both between zero and $p-1$, so no carrying is required. These are its [p-adic digits](../number-theory.md#p-adic-digit), with the unique negative-power digit at exponent minus one.

<h4 id="b/5/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#b/5)

<h5 id="b/5/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#b/5/iii)

**No prime works.** The [P-adic valuation](../number-theory.md#p-adic-valuation) induces a well-defined [homomorphism](../algebra.md#homomorphism)

$$
\mathbb Q_p^*/(\mathbb Q_p^*)^p\longrightarrow\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z,
\qquad[a]\longmapsto v_p(a)\bmod p.
$$

The class of $p$ maps to one, whereas the class of $p^p$ maps to zero. They therefore cannot be equal. Equivalently, $p$ cannot be a $p$th power because its valuation is not divisible by $p$.

<h4 id="b/5/iv">iv</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#b/5)

<h5 id="b/5/iv/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#b/5/iv)

Choose a nonzero [integer](../integer.md) $D$ with $D\alpha,D\beta\in\mathbb Z$. By the [Chinese remainder theorem](../mathematics.md#chinese-remainder-theorem), choose $z_n\in\mathbb Z$ satisfying $z_n\equiv D\alpha\pmod{q^n}$ and $z_n\equiv D\beta\pmod{r^n}$. Put $x_n=z_n/D$. Then

$$
|x_n-\alpha|_q\le|D|_q^{-1}q^{-n}\to0,\qquad
|x_n-\beta|_r\le|D|_r^{-1}r^{-n}\to0.
$$

This proves the requested simultaneous approximation, even when the rational targets have denominators divisible by either prime.

A [rational sequence converging to zero at every standard absolute value](../non-archimedean-analysis.md#rational-sequence-converging-to-zero-at-every-standard-absolute-value) is:

$$
\boxed{y_n=\frac{n!}{1+(n!)^2}.}
$$

Its ordinary absolute value is at most $1/n!$, hence tends to zero. For a fixed prime $p$ and all sufficiently large $n$, the denominator is congruent to one modulo $p$, so $v_p(y_n)=v_p(n!)\to\infty$. Therefore $|y_n|_p\to0$ for each prime as well. The assertion concerns each fixed prime separately, rather than [uniform convergence](../sequence-and-series.md#uniform-convergence) over all primes.

<h3 id="b/6">6</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b)

<h4 id="b/6/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#b/6)

<h5 id="b/6/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#b/6/i)

If $p=0$ as an element of $K$, the stated lower bound is zero and is immediate. Otherwise set $\rho_1=|p|^{1/(p-1)}$. The supplied [formal group](../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#formal-group-law) expansion has $g(0)=0$, because $[p](0)=0$. For $z\in\mathcal M$, the non-Archimedean estimates give

$$
|f(z)|=|z|,\qquad |g(z^p)|\le|z|^p,\qquad
|[p](z)|\le\max\{|p||z|,|z|^p\}.
$$

If $0<|z|<\rho_1$, then $|z|^p<|p||z|$, so the two terms cannot cancel and $|[p](z)|=|p||z|>0$. The same strict inequality persists under iteration, so no such $z$ can have finite $p$-power order. Every nonzero $p$-power torsion point therefore satisfies $|z|\ge\rho_1$.

For a point of order $p$, $[p](z)=0$ itself forces $|p||z|\le|z|^p$, proving the bound for $n=1$. For $n\ge2$, put $w=[p](z)$, of order $p^{n-1}$. Since $|z|\ge\rho_1$, the preceding maximum is $|z|^p$. By induction,

$$
|z|^p\ge|w|\ge|p|^{1/(p^{n-1}-p^{n-2})}.
$$

This proves the [formal torsion radius for prime power order](../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#formal-torsion-radius-for-prime-power-order). Taking the positive $p$th root gives

$$
\boxed{|z|\ge|p|^{1/(p^n-p^{n-1})}.}
$$

<h4 id="b/6/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#b/6)

<h5 id="b/6/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#b/6/ii)

For a short [Weierstrass equation of an elliptic curve](../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#weierstrass-equation-of-an-elliptic-curve) with coefficients in the [p-adic integers](../number-theory.md#p-adic-integer), a point with nonintegral $x$ has valuations $v_p(x)=-2m$ and $v_p(y)=-3m$ for some [integer](../integer.md) $m\ge1$: the term $x^3$ strictly dominates $Ax+B$ in the equation, so $2v_p(y)=3v_p(x)$. Its formal parameter $T=-x/y$ therefore has valuation $m\ge1$. These points, together with the identity, form the formal neighborhood $E_1(\mathbb Q_p)$ of the identity, with parameter in $p\mathbb Z_p$ and the elliptic [formal group](../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#formal-group-law) law over the [p-adic integers](../number-theory.md#p-adic-integer). For a point with $x\in\mathbb Z_p$, the equation gives $y\in\mathbb Z_p$ too.

The formal neighborhood has no nontrivial torsion. Multiplication by an [integer](../integer.md) coprime to $p$ has [series](../sequence-and-series.md#series-mathematics) $[m](T)=mT+O(T^2)$ and preserves $|T|_p$, so any torsion there must have $p$-power order. If $p\ge3$, part (i) gives $|T|_p\ge p^{-1/(p-1)}>p^{-1}$, contradicting $T\in p\mathbb Z_p$.

The printed hypotheses also allow $p=2$. In that case, a nontrivial torsion point in the formal neighborhood would have order a power of two and hence yield a point of order two in that neighborhood. But every nonidentity point of order two on a short [Weierstrass equation of an elliptic curve](../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#weierstrass-equation-of-an-elliptic-curve) has $y=0$ and $x$ a root of the monic cubic over the [p-adic integers](../number-theory.md#p-adic-integer) $X^3+AX+B$. Such a root lies in the [p-adic integers](../number-theory.md#p-adic-integer), so it cannot belong to the formal neighborhood, whose nonidentity points have nonintegral coordinates. This excludes the remaining case. This is the [torsion-free formal subgroup for a short Weierstrass equation](../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#torsion-free-formal-subgroup-for-a-short-weierstrass-equation). Thus every affine torsion point satisfies

$$
\boxed{|x|_p\le1,\qquad|y|_p\le1.}
$$

With [good reduction](../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#good-reduction-of-an-elliptic-curve), the reduction [homomorphism](../algebra.md#homomorphism) $E(\mathbb Q_p)\to\widetilde E(\mathbb F_p)$ has kernel exactly this torsion-free formal neighborhood. Its restriction to the [torsion subgroup](../group-theory.md#torsion-subgroup) is therefore injective, proving the required subgroup isomorphism. The short model over the [p-adic integers](../number-theory.md#p-adic-integer) is essential to the prime-two argument; no blanket torsion-injectivity assertion for arbitrary models is needed.

<h4 id="b/6/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#b/6)

<h5 id="b/6/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#b/6/iii)

The discriminant is $-64D^3$, which is nonzero modulo three because $D\equiv2\pmod3$. Thus there is [good reduction](../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#good-reduction-of-an-elliptic-curve) at three. For every $x\in\mathbb F_3$, the reduced right side is $x^3-x=0$, so its only affine points are $(0,0),(1,0),(2,0)$. Together with the identity they form $C_2\times C_2$.

By part (ii), the rational [torsion subgroup](../group-theory.md#torsion-subgroup) injects into this four-element [group](../group.md) and hence has exponent two. Over $\mathbb Q$, the two-torsion points have $Y=0$, requiring $X(X^2+D)=0$. Since $D>0$, the only rational root is zero. Therefore

$$
\boxed{E(\mathbb Q)_{\mathrm{tors}}=\{O,(0,0)\}\cong C_2.}
$$

<h3 id="b/7">7</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b)

<h4 id="b/7/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [7](#b/7)

<h5 id="b/7/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#b/7/i)

In the typical case, the line through $P,Q$, or their tangent when they coincide, is $v=mu+c$. Its intersections with $D$ have first coordinates given by

$$
u^3+(-2a-m^2)u^2+(b_1-2mc)u-c^2=0.
$$

Their product is $c^2$. The third intersection is $-(P+Q)$, which has the same first coordinate as $P+Q$. Consequently

$$
u(P)u(Q)u(P+Q)=c^2.
$$

Passing to the quotient by rational squares gives $q(P)q(Q)q(P+Q)=1$. Every [square class](../galois-theory.md#square-class) is its own inverse, so

$$
\boxed{q(P+Q)=q(P)q(Q).}
$$

The excluded cases have their specified special values; the source asks only for this chord-and-tangent argument away from them.

<h4 id="b/7/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [7](#b/7)

<h5 id="b/7/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#b/7/ii)

For an affine point of $C$ with $x\ne0$, the first coordinate of its image is $(y/x)^2$. Thus its image has trivial [square class](../galois-theory.md#square-class) whenever that coordinate is nonzero. The identity and $T=(0,0)$ map to the identity. If a point maps to $T_1=(0,0)$, it has $y=0$ and $x^2+ax+b=0$, forcing $b_1=a^2-4b$ to be a rational square; in that case $q(T_1)=1$ too. Hence $\phi(C(\mathbb Q))\subseteq\ker q$.

Conversely, let $(u,v)\in D(\mathbb Q)$ have $q(u,v)=1$ and $u\ne0$. Write $u=t^2$ with $t\in\mathbb Q^*$. The equation of $D$ gives

$$
(v/t)^2=(u-a)^2-4b.
$$

Set $x=(u-a+v/t)/2$ and $y=tx$. Then $x$ solves $x^2+(a-u)x+b=0$ and is nonzero because $b\ne0$. Hence $y^2=ux^2=x(x^2+ax+b)$, so $(x,y)\in C(\mathbb Q)$. Also $x-b/x=v/t$, giving $\phi(x,y)=(u,v)$.

The identity is an image of the identity. Finally, $T_1\in\ker q$ exactly when $b_1$ is a rational square; then either nonzero root $x=(-a\pm\sqrt{b_1})/2$ gives $(x,0)\in C(\mathbb Q)$ mapping to $T_1$. This covers all exceptional points and proves

$$
\boxed{\ker q=\phi(C(\mathbb Q)).}
$$

<h4 id="b/7/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [7](#b/7)

<h5 id="b/7/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#b/7/iii)

Here $a=1$, $b=-2$ and the two-isogenous curve is $D:v^2=u(u^2-2u+9)$. Let $\delta_C$ and $\delta_D$ be the first-coordinate square-class maps, using the coefficient $b$ or $b_1$ at the point $(0,0)$. The same chord argument shows that both maps are homomorphisms.

For an equation $y^2=x(x^2+ax+b)$ with [integer](../integer.md) coefficients, its first-coordinate [square class](../galois-theory.md#square-class) has a square-free representative dividing $b$, including a possible sign. Indeed, at a prime not dividing $b$, a negative valuation of $x$ must be even because $x^3$ dominates; a positive valuation must be even because $x^2+ax+b$ is a unit. Thus only primes dividing $b$ can occur to odd order. For $C$, the possibilities are $1,-1,2,-2$. The classes $1,-2$ occur at the identity and $(0,0)$. If class two occurred, write $x=2M^2/e^2$ with coprime [integers](../integer.md) $M,e$. Substituting in the equation would give a rational square, necessarily an [integer](../integer.md) square,

$$
N^2=2M^4+M^2e^2-e^4.
$$

Modulo three, the right side is two whenever $M,e$ are not both divisible by three: this holds whether just one or both are units. That is impossible. Since the image is a subgroup containing $-2$, exclusion of two also excludes minus one. Therefore $|\delta_C(C(\mathbb Q))|=2$.

For $D$, the quadratic $u^2-2u+9=(u-1)^2+8$ is positive, so every nonzero first coordinate is positive. Its [square class](../galois-theory.md#square-class) can only be one or three. Both occur, with three supplied by $(3,6)$, so $|\delta_D(D(\mathbb Q))|=2$.

Let $\psi:D\to C$ be the dual two-isogeny, with $\psi\phi=[2]$. The kernel identities of part (ii), applied to both curves and the usual coordinate scaling, give $[C:\psi D]=2$ and $[D:\phi C]=2$. Moreover $\ker\psi=\{O,T_1\}$ lies in $\phi C$, since $b_1=9$ is a square. Thus $[\psi D:2C]=[D:\phi C]=2$, and

$$
[C(\mathbb Q):2C(\mathbb Q)]=4.
$$

This is a [rank-zero two-isogeny descent for roots zero one and minus two](../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#rank-zero-two-isogeny-descent-for-roots-zero-one-and-minus-two). The rational two-torsion has four points, since the cubic factors as $x(x-1)(x+2)$. By the [Mordell-Weil theorem](../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#mordell-weil-group), the last index is $2^r\,|C(\mathbb Q)[2]|=2^{r+2}$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{rank}C(\mathbb Q)=0.}
$$

<h3 id="b/8">8</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b)

<h4 id="b/8/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [8](#b/8)

<h5 id="b/8/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#b/8/i)

The doubling slope at $P=(2,1)$ is $(3\cdot2^2-1)/(2\cdot1)=11/2$, giving

$$
2P=\left(\frac{105}{4},-\frac{1075}{8}\right).
$$

These denominators are invertible modulo $N$. To add $P$ again, the difference of first coordinates is $105/4-2=97/4$, so the new slope would be $-1083/194$. This requires inversion of a number divisible by 97. The [elliptic curve method](../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#elliptic-curve-method) instead computes

$$
\boxed{\gcd(97,10573)=97,\qquad10573=97\cdot109.}
$$

It is a proper factor. The failure of the group-law division modulo the composite [integer](../integer.md) reveals different behavior modulo its prime factors.

<h4 id="b/8/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [8](#b/8)

<h5 id="b/8/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#b/8/ii)

Write $x=c/d$ in lowest terms and put $H=\max\{|c|,|d|\}\ge1$. For $P\ne O,(0,0)$,

$$
x(\phi(P))=\frac{c^2+acd+bd^2}{cd}.
$$

Before reduction to lowest terms, both numerator and denominator are bounded in magnitude by $(1+|a|+|b|)H^2$. Cancellation can only decrease the height. Hence one may choose

$$
\boxed{k=\log(1+|a|+|b|).}
$$

The duplication formula for $C$ is

$$
x(2P)=\frac{(x^2-b)^2}{4x(x^2+ax+b)}
=\frac{(c^2-bd^2)^2}{4cd(c^2+acd+bd^2)}.
$$

The numerator has magnitude at most $(1+|b|)^2H^4$, and the denominator at most $4(1+|a|+|b|)H^4$. Thus

$$
\boxed{\ell=\log\max\{(1+|b|)^2,\ 4(1+|a|+|b|)\}}
$$

satisfies the second bound. At the identity or any point whose relevant image is the identity, the image height is zero and the same nonnegative constants work directly. Thus the formulas cover every rational point, including the exceptional points of the displayed coordinate expressions.

<h4 id="b/8/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [8](#b/8)

<h5 id="b/8/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#b/8/iii)

If $p\equiv1\pmod4$, choose $i\in\mathbb F_p$ with $i^2=-1$. The cubic has the three distinct roots $0,im,-im$, so all four two-torsion points are rational over $\mathbb F_p$. They form a subgroup of order four, hence $4\mid\#E(\mathbb F_p)$ by [Lagrange's theorem](../group-theory.md#lagrange-s-theorem).

If $p\equiv3\pmod4$, let $\chi$ be the [Legendre symbol](../quadratic-residue.md#legendre-symbol) with $\chi(0)=0$. Since $f(x)=x(x^2+m^2)$ is odd and $\chi(-1)=-1$, the terms for $x$ and $-x$ cancel in $\sum_x\chi(f(x))$. Counting the possible second coordinates gives

$$
\#E(\mathbb F_p)=1+\sum_x(1+\chi(f(x)))=p+1\equiv0\pmod4.
$$

Thus in both cases

$$
\boxed{4\mid\#E(\mathbb F_p).}
$$

The assumptions $p\ne2$ and $m\ne0$ ensure nonsingularity and keep the displayed two-torsion roots distinct when they exist.
