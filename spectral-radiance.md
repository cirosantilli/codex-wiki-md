# Spectral radiance

↑ **Parent:** [Radiance](radiance.md)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_radiance)

[Spectral radiance](spectral-radiance.md) is [radiance](radiance.md) per unit [frequency](frequency.md) or [wavelength](wavelength.md) interval. Its astronomical synonym [specific intensity](spectral-radiance.md) is usually written $I_\nu=L_\nu$ or $I_\lambda=L_\lambda$; this $I_\nu$ is a spectral [radiance](radiance.md), distinct from the [radiant intensity](radiant-intensity.md) $I$ in [radiometry](radiometry.md). For positive small intervals,

$$
d^3\Phi=L_\nu\cos\theta\,dA\,d\Omega\,d\nu
=L_\lambda\cos\theta\,dA\,d\Omega\,d\lambda.
$$

The two expressions refer to the same spectral slice, with the positive widths related through [frequency](frequency.md) $\nu$ and [wavelength](wavelength.md) $\lambda$. For [frequency](frequency.md) measured in hertz, $\mathrm{Hz}=\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, the units are $[L_\nu]=\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,sr^{-1}\,Hz^{-1}}$. For [wavelength](wavelength.md) measured in metres, $[L_\lambda]=\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,sr^{-1}\,m^{-1}}$. Integrating over a spectral band gives its [radiance](radiance.md), and then multiplying by the collecting [projected area](projected-area.md) and accepted [solid angle](solid-angle.md) gives a small uniform beam's [radiant flux](radiant-flux.md). A spectral measurement therefore retains information that the band-integrated [power](power.md) alone loses.

For an ideal [blackbody](blackbody.md), [Planck's law](planck-s-law.md) gives $L_\nu=B_\nu(T)$ and $L_\lambda=B_\lambda(T)$ at [temperature](temperature.md) $T$, independent of outgoing direction. Its existing [frequency and wavelength peaks of Planck's law](frequency-and-wavelength-peaks-of-planck-s-law.md) illustrate why the coordinate matters. The simpler flat-band calculation below isolates that same [change of variables](change-of-variables-formula.md) without the thermal physics.
