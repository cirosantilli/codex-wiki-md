# Orthogonal vectors

↑ **Parent:** [Inner product](inner-product-split.md)

Two [vectors](vector.md) are orthogonal when their [inner product](inner-product-split.md) is zero.

For real [Euclidean vectors](euclidean-vector.md), the [inner product](inner-product-split.md) is the [dot product](dot-product.md), so $(3,4)$ and $(4,-3)$ are [orthogonal](orthogonal-vectors.md): $3(4)+4(-3)=0$. For nonzero [Euclidean vectors](euclidean-vector.md), this means their [angle](angle.md) is $90^\circ$. The [zero vector](zero-vector.md) is [orthogonal](orthogonal-vectors.md) to every [vector](vector.md), although it has no direction or defined [angle](angle.md).[Strang, MIT 18.06, Lecture 14: orthogonal vectors and subspaces](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-06-linear-algebra-spring-2010/resources/lecture-14-orthogonal-vectors-and-subspaces/)

In an abstract [inner product space](inner-product-space.md), [orthogonality](orthogonal-vectors.md) always refers to its chosen [inner product](inner-product-split.md). For example, the [weighted inner product](weighted-inner-product.md) $\langle u,v\rangle_w=4u_1v_1+u_2v_2$ makes $(1,1)$ and $(1,-4)$ [orthogonal](orthogonal-vectors.md), since $4-4=0$. Their ordinary [dot product](dot-product.md) is $-3$, so their arrows are not perpendicular in the usual [Cartesian coordinate system](cartesian-coordinate-system.md). The algebraic definition also applies to [functions](function-split.md), where arrows need not be available.
