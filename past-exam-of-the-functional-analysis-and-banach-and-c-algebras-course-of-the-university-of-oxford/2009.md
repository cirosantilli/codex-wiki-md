<h4 id="a/1/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#a/1)

<h5 id="a/1/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#a/1/i)

The [quotient norm](../banach-space.md#quotient-norm) is

$$
\boxed{\|x+Y\|_{X/Y}=\inf_{y\in Y}\|x-y\|.}
$$

Changing the representative does not change the infimum. The [triangle inequality](../topological-analysis.md#triangle-inequality) and absolute homogeneity follow from those of the original [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm). Vanishing means $x$ belongs to the closure of $Y$, hence to $Y$ because the subspace is closed. Thus this is a [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm).

Use the absolutely convergent series criterion for completeness. If $\sum\|\xi_n\|_{X/Y}<\infty$, choose representatives $z_n$ of $\xi_n$ satisfying $\|z_n\|\leq\|\xi_n\|_{X/Y}+2^{-n}$. The series $\sum z_n$ converges in the [Banach space](../banach-space.md) $X$ to some $z$. Since the [quotient map](../topology.md#quotient-map) is contractive, $\sum\xi_n$ converges to $z+Y$. Consequently **the quotient is a [Banach space](../banach-space.md)**.

<h4 id="a/1/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#a/1)

<h5 id="a/1/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#a/1/ii)

A set is [totally bounded](../topological-analysis.md#totally-bounded-space) if for every $\varepsilon>0$ it can be covered by finitely many open balls of radius $\varepsilon$.

Here is the needed separated-sequence argument, including the relevant [Riesz lemma](../banach-space.md#riesz-s-lemma). For a proper [closed subspace](../topology.md#closed-subspace) $Y$, choose $z\notin Y$, put $d=\operatorname{dist}(z,Y)>0$, and choose $y\in Y$ with $\|z-y\|<2d$. Then $w=(z-y)/\|z-y\|$ is a [unit vector](../vector-space.md#unit-vector) at distance greater than $1/2$ from $Y$. Starting with one [unit vector](../vector-space.md#unit-vector), apply this to the span of the vectors already chosen. These spans are closed and proper because they are finite-dimensional while $X$ is infinite-dimensional. The resulting unit vectors satisfy $\|w_m-w_n\|>1/2$ for $m\ne n$. A ball of radius $1/4$ contains at most one of them. Hence **the [closed unit ball](../normed-vector-space.md#closed-unit-ball) is not [totally bounded](../topological-analysis.md#totally-bounded-space)**.

<h4 id="a/1/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#a/1)

<h5 id="a/1/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#a/1/iii)

Take $S=T(B_X)$. A [bounded linear operator](../topological-vector-space.md#continuous-linear-operator) is compact exactly when the closure of this set is compact. In a [complete metric space](../topological-analysis.md#complete-metric-space) this is equivalent to its being [totally bounded](../topological-analysis.md#totally-bounded-space).

If $T$ is compact, choose a finite $\varepsilon$-net $z_1,\ldots,z_m$ for $T(B_X)$ and let $Y$ be their span. Then $\operatorname{dist}(Tx,Y)<\varepsilon$ for $\|x\|\leq1$, giving $\|\pi_YT\|\leq\varepsilon$.

Conversely, fix $\delta>0$ and take $Y$ with $\|\pi_YT\|\leq\delta/4$. For every $x\in B_X$ choose $y_x\in Y$ with $\|Tx-y_x\|<\delta/2$. These approximants satisfy $\|y_x\|\leq\|T\|+\delta/2$, so form a bounded subset of a finite-dimensional space and have a finite $\delta/2$-net. The same centers give a finite $\delta$-net for $T(B_X)$. Thus it is [totally bounded](../topological-analysis.md#totally-bounded-space) and $T$ is compact. In particular

$$
\boxed{T\text{ compact}\iff\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists\dim Y<\infty:\ \|\pi_YT\|\leq\varepsilon.}
$$

<h4 id="a/2/a">a</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#a/2)

<h5 id="a/2/a/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a/2/a)

The natural [canonical embedding into the bidual](../functional-analysis.md#canonical-embedding-into-the-bidual) is $J_Xx(f)=f(x)$. It is linear and $\|J_Xx\|\leq\|x\|$. A norming functional from the [Hahn-Banach theorem](../functional-analysis.md#hahn-banach-theorem) gives the reverse inequality, so it is an [isometry](../riemannian-geometry.md#isometry). A [reflexive Banach space](../functional-analysis.md#reflexive-banach-space) is one for which this canonical embedding is onto.

**A nonreflexive example is $c_0$.** Its dual is $\ell^1$ and its bidual is $\ell^\infty$; the canonical map is the natural inclusion, which misses the constant sequence one. This also shows why merely having an isometric embedding in the bidual is insufficient.

<h4 id="a/2/b">b</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#a/2)

<h5 id="a/2/b/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#a/2/b)

**An infinite-dimensional reflexive example is $\ell^2$**, by the [Riesz representation theorem](../hilbert-space.md#riesz-representation-theorem).

The [weak topology](../weak-topology.md) on $X$ is the coarsest topology making every $f\in X^*$ continuous. The [weak-star topology](../weak-topology.md#weak-star-topology) on $X^*$ is the coarsest making every evaluation $f\mapsto f(x)$, $x\in X$, continuous. In each case basic neighborhoods impose finitely many evaluation inequalities. The [Banach-Alaoglu theorem](../functional-analysis.md#banach-alaoglu-theorem) states that the [closed unit ball](../normed-vector-space.md#closed-unit-ball) of a dual space is compact for its [weak-star topology](../weak-topology.md#weak-star-topology). The separation theorem needed here says: if $K$ is a nonempty weak-star compact [convex](../real-analysis.md#convex-function) subset of a dual space $E^*$ and $z\notin K$, then some $e\in E$ strictly separates them,

$$
\operatorname{Re}z(e)>\sup_{k\in K}\operatorname{Re}k(e).
$$

The real-part convention covers complex spaces; for real spaces omit it.

Let $K$ be the weak-star closure of $J_X(B_X)$ inside $B_{X^{**}}$. This is compact by Banach-Alaoglu, [convex](../real-analysis.md#convex-function), and nonempty. If $z\in B_{X^{**}}\setminus K$, separation in the dual pair $(X^{**},X^*)$ gives $f\in X^*$ with

$$
\operatorname{Re}z(f)>\sup_{x\in B_X}\operatorname{Re}f(x)=\|f\|.
$$

But $|z(f)|\leq\|z\|\|f\|\leq\|f\|$, a contradiction. Therefore $K=B_{X^{**}}$, proving the required [Goldstine theorem](../functional-analysis.md#goldstine-theorem) density.

If $X$ is reflexive, $J_X$ identifies its [weak topology](../weak-topology.md) with the [weak-star topology](../weak-topology.md#weak-star-topology) of $X^{**}$ and carries $B_X$ onto $B_{X^{**}}$, so Banach-Alaoglu gives weak compactness. Conversely, if $B_X$ is weakly compact, its image is weak-star compact, hence closed in the [Hausdorff](../topology.md#hausdorff-space) bidual topology. Density then forces $J_X(B_X)=B_{X^{**}}$. Scaling gives surjectivity on the entire bidual. Thus

$$
\boxed{B_X\text{ weakly compact}\iff X\text{ reflexive}.}
$$

<h4 id="a/3/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#a/3)

<h5 id="a/3/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#a/3/i)

The [Baire category theorem](../topological-analysis.md#baire-category-theorem) says that a nonempty [complete metric space](../topological-analysis.md#complete-metric-space) is not a countable union of closed sets with empty interior; equivalently, every countable intersection of open dense sets is dense. To prove the latter, start with any nonempty open set $V$ and open dense sets $G_n$. Choose closed balls of positive radii with

$$
\overline B_1\subset V\cap G_1,\qquad\overline B_{n+1}\subset B_n\cap G_{n+1},\qquad r_n<2^{-n}.
$$

Each choice is possible by density and openness. Their centers form a [Cauchy sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#cauchy-sequence); its limit exists by completeness and belongs to every closed ball, hence to $V\cap\bigcap G_n$. This proves density and the theorem.

The [Uniform boundedness principle](../banach-space.md#uniform-boundedness-principle) says that a pointwise bounded family $\mathcal T$ of bounded linear maps from a [Banach space](../banach-space.md) $X$ to a [normed space](../normed-vector-space.md) $Z$ has uniformly bounded operator norms. Put $E_m=\{x:\sup_{T\in\mathcal T}\|Tx\|\leq m\}$. These sets are closed and cover $X$. Baire gives an open ball $B(x_0,r)\subset E_m$. For $\|h\|<r$, the [triangle inequality](../topological-analysis.md#triangle-inequality) applied to $T(x_0+h)-Tx_0$ gives $\|Th\|\leq2m$. Taking $h=(r/2)u$ for unit $u$ proves $\sup_T\|T\|\leq4m/r$.

For the particular expansion, uniqueness makes all coefficient maps and partial-sum maps linear. Since $P_kx\to x$, the norms $\|P_kx\|$ form a bounded sequence with limit $\|x\|$. Also, with $P_0=0$,

$$
|\varphi_n(x)|=\|\varphi_n(x)x_n\|=\|(P_n-P_{n-1})x\|\leq2\sup_k\|P_kx\|.
$$

This proves both preliminary bounds. The supremum therefore defines a finite [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm) $\|\cdot\|'$, with $\|x\|\leq\|x\|'$.

To prove **(i) implies (ii)**, the identity map from $(X,\|\cdot\|')$ to $(X,\|\cdot\|)$ is a bounded linear bijection. If (i) holds both spaces are Banach, so the [bounded inverse theorem](../functional-analysis.md#bounded-inverse-theorem) makes its inverse bounded: $\|x\|'\leq C\|x\|$. This is precisely (ii).

<h4 id="a/3/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#a/3)

<h5 id="a/3/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#a/3/ii)

**(ii) implies (i):** together with the preliminary estimate, (ii) gives [equivalent norms](../normed-vector-space.md#equivalent-norms),

$$
\|x\|\leq\|x\|'\leq C\|x\|.
$$

An equivalent [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm) on a [Banach space](../banach-space.md) is complete: a primed-[norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm) [Cauchy sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#cauchy-sequence) has an original-[norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm) limit, and the upper bound makes convergence hold also in the primed [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm). **(ii) implies (iii)** immediately because $\|P_k\|\leq C$ for every $k$.

<h4 id="a/3/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#a/3)

<h5 id="a/3/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#a/3/iii)

**(iii) implies (ii):** the preliminary bounds show $\sup_k\|P_kx\|<\infty$ for every $x$. Applying the [Uniform boundedness principle](../banach-space.md#uniform-boundedness-principle) to the bounded maps $P_k$ gives $\sup_k\|P_k\|<\infty$.

**(iii) implies (iv):** the normalization $\|x_n\|=1$ gives

$$
|\varphi_n(x)|=\|(P_n-P_{n-1})x\|\leq(\|P_n\|+\|P_{n-1}\|)\|x\|.
$$

Hence every coordinate functional is bounded.

<h4 id="a/3/iv">iv</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#a/3)

<h5 id="a/3/iv/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#a/3/iv)

**(iv) implies (iii):** $P_k=\sum_{n=1}^kx_n\varphi_n$ is a finite sum of bounded [rank](../quadratic-form.md#rank-one-quadratic-form)-one operators.

**(iv) implies (v):** continuity gives $\varphi_n(y_r)\to0$ whenever $y_r\to0$, so any scalar limit $\beta$ must vanish.

**(iv) implies (vi):** fix $n$. Continuity gives $\varphi_n(y_r)\to\varphi_n(x)$, while the assumed uniform coordinate convergence gives $\varphi_n(y_r)\to\beta_n$. Uniqueness of the scalar limit yields $\beta_n=\varphi_n(x)$ for every $n$.

<h4 id="a/3/v">v</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#a/3)

<h5 id="a/3/v/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [V](#a/3/v)

**(v) implies (iv).** Fix $n$ and consider any sequence $y_r\to y$ with $\varphi_n(y_r)\to\gamma$. Apply (v) to $y_r-y$ and $\beta=\gamma-\varphi_n(y)$. It gives $\gamma=\varphi_n(y)$. Thus the graph of $\varphi_n:X\to\mathbb K$ is closed. Both spaces are Banach, so the [closed graph theorem](../functional-analysis.md#closed-graph-theorem) makes $\varphi_n$ bounded.

<h4 id="a/3/vi">vi</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#a/3)

<h5 id="a/3/vi/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Vi](#a/3/vi)

**(vi) implies (iv)** without assuming beforehand that any coordinate functional is continuous. Define the everywhere-defined [linear map](../vector-space.md#linear-map)

$$
\Phi:X\to\ell^\infty,\qquad\Phi(x)=(\varphi_n(x))_{n\geq1}.
$$

The preliminary coordinate bound ensures it really takes values in $\ell^\infty$. Condition (vi) states exactly that its graph is sequentially closed: $y_r\to x$ and $\Phi(y_r)\to\beta$ in the supremum [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm) imply $\Phi(x)=\beta$. The product is a metric space, so sequentially closed means closed. The [closed graph theorem](../functional-analysis.md#closed-graph-theorem) therefore makes $\Phi$ bounded. Every coordinate projection on $\ell^\infty$ is bounded, hence so is each $\varphi_n$.

Together with the preceding implications this proves **all six conditions are equivalent**. No continuity of the coordinate functionals was assumed during the preliminary bounds.

<h4 id="b/4/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#b/4)

A [character of an algebra](../banach-algebra.md#character-of-an-algebra) is a nonzero [multiplicative](../arithmetic-function.md#multiplicative-function) complex-[linear map](../vector-space.md#linear-map) $\chi:A\to\mathbb C$. A maximal proper ideal is a proper ideal contained in no larger proper ideal. For a character, $\chi(1)=1$ and $\chi(a)\in\sigma(a)$: otherwise $a-\chi(a)1$ would be invertible although its character value is zero. The spectral bound gives $|\chi(a)|\leq\|a\|$, so the character is continuous. It is onto, and its kernel is a closed [maximal ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#maximal-ideal) because $A/\ker\chi\cong\mathbb C$.

Conversely let $M$ be maximal. Its closure is an ideal and remains proper: otherwise an element of $M$ would lie within distance one of the unit, hence would be invertible by the [Neumann series](../banach-algebra.md#neumann-series), forcing $1\in M$. Maximality then gives $\overline M=M$. The [Banach algebra](../banach-algebra.md) $A/M$ is a field. For any $b$ in this quotient choose $\lambda\in\sigma(b)$, using nonemptiness of the complex spectrum. In a field only zero is noninvertible, so $b-\lambda1=0$. Hence every quotient element is a scalar multiple of the unit. The [quotient map](../topology.md#quotient-map) therefore determines a character $\chi_M$, uniquely normalized by $\chi_M(1)=1$. These constructions are inverse:

$$
\boxed{\Delta(A)\longleftrightarrow M(A),\qquad\chi\longmapsto\ker\chi.}
$$

For the convolution algebra, write $e_j$ for the sequence supported at $j$ with value one. Then $e_j e_k=e_{j+k}$, $e_0=1$, and $e_1e_{-1}=1$. If $z=\chi(e_1)$, continuity and $\|e_{\pm1}\|_1=1$ imply $|z|\leq1$ and $|z^{-1}|\leq1$. Thus $|z|=1$, so uniquely $z=e^{it}$ for $0\leq t<2\pi$, and $\chi(e_j)=e^{ijt}$ for every integer $j$. [Absolute convergence](../sequence-and-series.md#absolute-convergence) in $\ell^1$ now gives

$$
\boxed{\chi_t(\alpha)=\sum_{j\in\mathbb Z}\alpha_je^{ijt}.}
$$

Conversely each displayed map is bounded, nonzero, and [multiplicative](../arithmetic-function.md#multiplicative-function), since the absolutely convergent double sum can be rearranged along $k+l=j$. Thus these are all characters, and all maximal proper ideals are

$$
\boxed{M_t=\left\{\alpha\in\ell^1(\mathbb Z):\sum_j\alpha_je^{ijt}=0\right\},\qquad0\leq t<2\pi.}
$$

They are distinct because the character is uniquely recovered from its kernel and its value on the unit.

<h4 id="b/5/b">b</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#b/5)

<h5 id="b/5/b/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b/5/b)

An element is a [Positive element of a C-star algebra](../banach-algebra.md#positive-element-of-a-c-star-algebra) if it is [self-adjoint](../linear-operator-theory.md#self-adjoint-operator) and its spectrum lies in $[0,\infty)$, equivalently if $a=b^*b$ for some $b$.

For a normal $a$, the [continuous functional calculus](../banach-algebra.md#continuous-functional-calculus) is the unique unital isometric \*-isomorphism $C(\sigma(a))\to C^*(1,a)$ sending the coordinate function $z$ to $a$. It respects sums, products, conjugation and the unit, satisfies $\|f(a)\|=\max_{\sigma(a)}|f|$, and has $\sigma(f(a))=f(\sigma(a))$. For [self-adjoint](../linear-operator-theory.md#self-adjoint-operator) $a$ it preserves the order of real-valued functions.

The piecewise definition of $g_n$ is consistent for $n\geq2$. **At $n=1$ the printed branches conflict**, even assigning both zero and one at $t=1/2$. All the requested limit and face arguments use only $n\geq2$; discarding this first index resolves that harmless initial-index defect without altering any limit.

For $n\geq2$, interpolate linearly from $(1/(2n),0)$ to $(1/n,g(1/n))$, and from $(1-1/n,g(1-1/n))$ to $(1-1/(2n),1)$. On the middle interval the error is zero. On the left both $g$ and $g_n$ lie between zero and $g(1/n)$; on the right both lie between $g(1-1/n)$ and one. Thus

$$
\|g-g_n\|_\infty\leq\max\{g(1/n),1-g(1-1/n)\}\longrightarrow0
$$

by continuity at the endpoints.

Put $\delta=1/(2n)$. To prove the bounds on $h_n$, it suffices to show $0\leq t-\delta g_n(t)\leq1-\delta$. For $t\leq\delta$, $g_n=0$; for $t\geq1-\delta$, $g_n=1$. On the remaining interval, $\delta\leq t\leq1-\delta$ and $0\leq g_n\leq1$, so both inequalities follow. Consequently **$g_n$ and $h_n$ are positive contractions**.

For the face assertion, write $r=\|a\|$. Since $a\in[0,1_A]$ and $0\notin F$, $0<r\leq1$. If $r<1$, then $a=r(a/r)+(1-r)0$, with both endpoints in $[0,1_A]$. The face property would force $0\in F$, a contradiction. Thus $\|a\|=1$. Its spectrum contains one by positivity and the [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm) formula, and contains zero because it is noninvertible.

Applying functional calculus to the scalar identity gives

$$
a=\delta g_n(a)+(1-\delta)h_n(a),\qquad g_n(a),h_n(a)\in[0,1_A].
$$

Since $0<\delta<1$ and $a\in F$, the face property gives $g_n(a)\in F$. [Isometry](../riemannian-geometry.md#isometry) of functional calculus gives $g_n(a)\to g(a)$ in [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm); closedness of $F$ therefore proves

$$
\boxed{\|a\|=1,\qquad g(a)\in F.}
$$

<h3 id="b/6">6</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b)

<h4 id="b/6/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#b/6)

For a [bounded linear functional](../topological-vector-space.md#continuous-linear-functional) on a unital C\*-algebra, the state criterion is

$$
\boxed{x(1_A)=\|x\|=1.}
$$

Equivalently it is positive and takes the unit to one.

In the [Gelfand–Naimark–Segal construction](../banach-algebra.md#gelfand-naimark-segal-construction), set $N_x=\{b:x(b^*b)=0\}$. This is a [left ideal](../associative-algebra.md#left-ideal). Give $A/N_x$ the [inner product](../inner-product.md) $\langle[b],[c]\rangle=x(c^*b)$, linear in the first variable, and complete it to a [Hilbert space](../hilbert-space.md) $H_x$. Left multiplication gives the bounded \*-representation $\pi_x(a)[b]=[ab]$. The vector $\xi=[1_A]$ is cyclic and satisfies $x(a)=\langle\pi_x(a)\xi,\xi\rangle$.

The Hermitian matrix $\rho$ has eigenvalues $\lambda_\pm=1/2\pm|\alpha|$ and [trace](../linear-algebra.md#matrix-trace) one. In an [orthonormal eigenbasis](../linear-operator-theory.md#orthonormal-eigenbasis) $v_+,v_-$,

$$
x_\rho(a)=\lambda_+\langle av_+,v_+\rangle+\lambda_-\langle av_-,v_-\rangle.
$$

If $|\alpha|\leq1/2$, both weights are nonnegative and sum to one, so this functional is positive and normalized, hence a state. If $|\alpha|>1/2$, evaluating on the positive [rank](../quadratic-form.md#rank-one-quadratic-form)-one projection onto the negative-[eigenvalue](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) eigenspace gives a negative value, so it is not a state. Therefore

$$
\boxed{x_\rho\text{ is a state}\iff|\alpha|\leq1/2.}
$$

To obtain the representation dimension, cyclicity of [trace](../linear-algebra.md#matrix-trace) gives

$$
x_\rho(b^*b)=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho b^*b)=\|b\rho^{1/2}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2.
$$

Thus $A/N_{x_\rho}$ identifies isometrically with the matrix space $\{b\rho^{1/2}:b\in M_2(\mathbb C)\}$, with left multiplication as the representation. If $r=\operatorname{rank}\rho$, this space consists, after a [unitary](../vector-bundle.md#unitary-connection) change of basis, of arbitrary first $r$ columns and zero remaining columns. Its complex dimension is $2r$. It is already complete because it is finite-dimensional. [Rank](../quadratic-form.md#rank-one-quadratic-form) is one at $|\alpha|=1/2$ and two at $|\alpha|<1/2$, proving

$$
\boxed{\dim H_{x_\rho}=2\text{ at }|\alpha|=1/2,\qquad\dim H_{x_\rho}=4\text{ at }|\alpha|<1/2.}
$$
