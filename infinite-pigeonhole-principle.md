# Infinite pigeonhole principle

↑ **Parent:** [Pigeonhole principle](pigeonhole-principle.md)

The [infinite pigeonhole principle](infinite-pigeonhole-principle.md) says that if an [infinite set](infinite-set.md) has a [partition of a set](partition-of-a-set.md) into finitely many parts, at least one part is an [infinite set](infinite-set.md). Equivalently, assigning infinitely many objects to a finite number of boxes forces some box to contain infinitely many objects. For example, separating the [natural numbers](natural-number.md) into their two [residue classes](residue-class.md) modulo $2$ forces at least one class to be an [infinite set](infinite-set.md); here both are, as the lists $0,2,4,\ldots$ and $1,3,5,\ldots$ show.

**Proof outline.** A [union](set-union.md) of finitely many [finite sets](finite-set.md) is a [finite set](finite-set.md). If all the parts were [finite sets](finite-set.md), their [union](set-union.md) could not be the original [infinite set](infinite-set.md).

The assumption of finitely many parts is essential. With one box labelled by each [natural number](natural-number.md), assigning $n$ to box $n$ puts exactly one object in every box. Infinitely many objects and infinitely many boxes therefore need not produce even a shared box. The [infinite pigeonhole principle](infinite-pigeonhole-principle.md) is useful whenever finitely many labels organize an endless collection: one label must keep occurring infinitely often, providing an [infinite set](infinite-set.md) on which that label is constant.
