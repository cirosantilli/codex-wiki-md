<h1 id="section-d/d13/b/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [B](../b.md)

In an [electrical insulator](../../../../../../electrical-insulator.md), [lattice](../../../../../../lattice.md) vibrations usually carry most heat. A small [temperature gradient](../../../../../../temperature-gradient.md) produces an imbalance between [phonons](../../../../../../phonon.md) moving in opposite directions. In an isotropic kinetic approximation,

$$
\boxed{\kappa_{\rm ph}\simeq\frac13c_Vv_s\ell_{\rm tr}
=\frac13c_Vv_s^2\tau_{\rm tr},}
$$

where $c_V$ is [heat capacity](../../../../../../heat-capacity.md) per unit [volume](../../../../../../volume.md), $v_s$ an appropriate acoustic speed and $\ell_{\rm tr}$ the transport [mean free path](../../../../../../mean-free-path.md). The factor one third is the angular average of the squared direction cosine. With several branches and frequency-dependent scattering, replace this estimate by the corresponding weighted mode integral. A short lifetime matters for conductivity only insofar as it relaxes heat current.

For a clean three-dimensional [crystal](../../../../../../crystal.md) at $T\ll\Theta_D$, the [Debye heat capacity](../../../../../../debye-heat-capacity.md) varies as $T^3$. If diffuse boundary scattering fixes $\ell_{\rm tr}$ near a specimen dimension, then **$\kappa_{\rm ph}\propto T^3$**. The size dependence is a test of that boundary-limited regime. Specular surfaces, defects and small specimens can change the simple law; at ballistic scales a local conductivity description itself needs care.

As [temperature](../../../../../../temperature-split.md) rises, more [phonons](../../../../../../phonon.md) are excited, but phonon-phonon scattering shortens their transport path. Normal processes conserve total [crystal momentum](../../../../../../crystal-momentum.md), whereas an [Umklapp process](../../../../../../umklapp-scattering.md) satisfies $\mathbf q_1+\mathbf q_2-\mathbf q_3=\mathbf G\ne0$ with reciprocal vector $\mathbf G$ and can relax directed heat flow. Low-temperature [Umklapp scattering](../../../../../../umklapp-scattering.md) is strongly suppressed; its increasing importance often produces a conductivity maximum followed by a decline. Isotope and impurity scattering lower or broaden that maximum.

At sufficiently high [temperature](../../../../../../temperature-split.md), $c_V$ approaches its classical constant while a typical anharmonic Umklapp rate grows approximately as $T$. Under that further approximation,

$$
\boxed{\ell_{\rm tr}\propto T^{-1},\qquad
\kappa_{\rm ph}\propto T^{-1}.}
$$

Thus a clean crystalline insulator commonly shows a low-temperature rise, a peak, then a high-temperature fall. This is not a universal curve for every insulator: glasses, strong disorder, multiple scattering mechanisms, radiative heat transfer and structural transitions can give different behavior.

A steady-state bar experiment supplies a concrete test. Inject known heating power, measure the small [temperature](../../../../../../temperature-split.md) difference between two separated sensors and infer $\kappa=PL/(A\Delta T)$ after correcting for heat leaks. Keep the gradient small enough that a single representative [temperature](../../../../../../temperature-split.md) is meaningful, calibrate sensor contact effects and subtract radiative/background losses. Repeat with different widths or isotope/defect content to distinguish boundary limitation from intrinsic scattering. A power-dependent result may reflect a changing [temperature](../../../../../../temperature-split.md) profile rather than a new microscopic law.
