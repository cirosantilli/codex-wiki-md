<h1 id="section-a/4/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [4](../4.md)

For [radiative temperature uncertainty after background subtraction](../../../../../radiative-temperature-uncertainty-after-background-subtraction.md), write $P_{\rm back}=B$, so $P_{\rm total}\simeq10B$ and $P\simeq9B$. The two absolute uncertainties are both $0.1B$. If they are independent [standard uncertainties](../../../../../standard-uncertainty.md),

$$
\frac{\sigma_T}{T}=\frac14\frac{\sigma_P}{P}
=\frac{\sqrt{(0.1B)^2+(0.1B)^2}}{36B}
=\boxed{3.93\times10^{-3}\simeq0.39\%}.
$$

If the percentages instead denote absolute error bounds, linear worst-case propagation gives **0.56%**. With [correlation coefficient](../../../../../pearson-correlation-coefficient.md) $\rho$, the standard-uncertainty result is $\sqrt{2(1-\rho)}/360$; subtraction makes positive common errors partly cancel. The independence convention is therefore an assumption, not information supplied by the percentages.
