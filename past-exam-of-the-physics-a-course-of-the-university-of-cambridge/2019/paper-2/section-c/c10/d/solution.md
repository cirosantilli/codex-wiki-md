<h1 id="section-c/c10/d/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [D](../d.md)

The direct [body-centered cubic lattice](../../../../../../body-centered-cubic-lattice.md) has a face-centred cubic [reciprocal lattice](../../../../../../reciprocal-lattice.md), whereas the direct [face-centered cubic lattice](../../../../../../face-centered-cubic-lattice.md) has a body-centred cubic [reciprocal lattice](../../../../../../reciprocal-lattice.md). Thus the [reciprocal lattice change in a structural phase transition](../../../../../../reciprocal-lattice-change-in-a-structural-phase-transition.md) reverses the two cubic types: the reciprocal lattice changes from FCC to BCC when the real material changes from BCC to FCC. If the direct conventional side is $a$, the reciprocal conventional side is $4\pi/a$ in either case, with the appropriate phase-specific $a$.

Within one phase, ordinary [thermal expansion](../../../../../../thermal-expansion.md) increases direct spacings and decreases reciprocal spacings. At the structural [phase transition](../../../../../../phase-transition.md), reciprocal points acquire the new symmetry and allowed positions. A first-order transformation may show both phases simultaneously and may create differently oriented domains; it need not continuously preserve one perfect reciprocal array throughout.

Using the fixed-radius estimates from part (b), $\Omega_F/\Omega_B\simeq0.919$, so the reciprocal primitive cell volume would increase by about $8.9\%$. That comparison belongs to the ideal contact model. The question supplies neither the actual [thermal expansion](../../../../../../thermal-expansion.md) nor a crystallographic orientation relationship, so it does not determine a unique trajectory of individual reciprocal points during the transition.
