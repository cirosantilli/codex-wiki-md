<h1 id="section-b/b7/transit-and-pulse-evolution">Transit and pulse evolution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [Solution](solution.md)

Taking $L=1.00\,\mathrm{cm}$ as the distance travelled in the medium, the narrowband [group delay](../../../../../group-delay.md) is

$$
\boxed{t_g=L/v_g=8.76\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm s=87.6\,\mathrm{ps}}.
$$

If $1\,\mathrm{cm}$ denotes a slab thickness measured normal to the interface instead, the central ray travels $L/\cos17.80^\circ$, giving about $92.1\,\mathrm{ps}$. The coordinate-mixed velocity above would give $89.3\,\mathrm{ps}$ for a $1\,\mathrm{cm}$ path; it should not replace the consistent calculation silently.

The pulse envelope travels at the local [group velocity](../../../../../group-velocity.md), while individual carrier crests move at the [phase velocity](../../../../../phase-velocity.md). In general, variation of [group velocity](../../../../../group-velocity.md) across the pulse spectrum produces different delays, temporal broadening and chirp. Absorption can reshape the spectrum and envelope as well. The refraction data also show wavelength-dependent angles, so a finite-width incident beam acquires spatial separation of its spectral components. The supplied first derivative determines the central delay but does not specify a pulse duration, absorption spectrum or reliable second derivative of the [dispersion relation](../../../../../dispersion-relation-split.md); it therefore does not determine a numerical broadening, or its sign. If propagation [phase](../../../../../phase-waves.md) is effectively linear across a sufficiently narrow spectrum, the leading approximation is a delayed pulse with unchanged shape.
