<h1 id="section-b/b8/why-the-given-slit-width-removes-the-chosen-order">Why the given slit width removes the chosen order</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [Solution](solution.md)

For a slit of width $a$, the [single-slit diffraction](../../../../../single-slit-diffraction.md) amplitude is proportional to $\operatorname{sinc}(\pi a\sin\theta/\lambda)$. At a grating maximum, $D\sin\theta=m\lambda$, so the envelope factor is

$$
\operatorname{sinc}\!\left(\frac{\pi ma}{D}\right).
$$

The specified $a=1\,\mathrm{\mu m}=D/2$ gives $ma/D=1$ at $m=2$. Thus

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{sinc}(\pi)=0:\quad\text{the second order is a missing diffraction order}.}
$$

The grating maximum coincides exactly with the first single-slit minimum for each wavelength. The preceding width and detector calculations describe the ideal array factor, but this particular rectangular-aperture grating cannot deliver the assumed second-order signal. Pixel refinement cannot repair the missing optical peak. One must change the [aperture](../../../../../aperture.md) duty cycle or grating profile, or use an available order and recalculate its resolving [power](../../../../../power.md).
