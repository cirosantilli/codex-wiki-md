<h1 id="section-c/c11/carrier-density-and-the-conductivity-ratio-assumption">Carrier density and the conductivity-ratio assumption</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [Solution](solution.md)

The atomic [number density](../../../../../number-density.md) is

$$
n_{\rm at}=\frac{5300\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}}{0.073\,\mathrm{kg\,mol^{-1}}}N_A=4.372\times10^{28}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}.
$$

One fully ionised single-electron donor per million atoms gives

$$
\boxed{N_D=4.372\times10^{22}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}},\qquad
\boxed{N_D/n_i=1.46\times10^3},\quad n_i=3\times10^{19}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}.
$$

The usual simplified answer treats conductivity as proportional to [electron](../../../../../electron.md) concentration and assumes unchanged [electron mobility](../../../../../electron-mobility.md), giving **about $1.46\times10^3$ times the undoped conductivity**. There is a qualification: the [conductivity ratio of doped and intrinsic semiconductors](../../../../../conductivity-ratio-of-doped-and-intrinsic-semiconductors.md) is not fixed by [electron](../../../../../electron.md) counts alone. Intrinsic material also has $p=n_i$, whereas the heavily n-type sample has $n\simeq N_D$ and $p\simeq n_i^2/N_D$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\frac{\sigma_d}{\sigma_i}\simeq
\frac{N_D\mu_{e,d}}{n_i(\mu_{e,i}+\mu_{h,i})}}.
$$

With unchanged [electron](../../../../../electron.md) mobility and negligible intrinsic hole conductivity this reduces to $1.46\times10^3$; with equal, unchanged [electron](../../../../../electron.md) and hole mobilities it is about $7.29\times10^2$. The problem supplies no mobilities, so the density-ratio estimate requires its stated approximation. Ionised impurities may additionally reduce mobility.
