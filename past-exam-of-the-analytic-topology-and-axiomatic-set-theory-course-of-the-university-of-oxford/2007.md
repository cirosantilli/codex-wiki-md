<h3 id="a/1">1</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a)

<h4 id="a/1/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#a/1)

Use these standard [ultrafilter](../set-theory.md#ultrafilter) facts: a space is [compact](../topology.md#compact-space) if and only if every [ultrafilter](../set-theory.md#ultrafilter) on it converges; the image [ultrafilter](../set-theory.md#ultrafilter) under a map is an [ultrafilter](../set-theory.md#ultrafilter); and a convergent [ultrafilter](../set-theory.md#ultrafilter) contains every [neighborhood](../topology.md#neighbourhood-mathematics) of its limit.

Let $X=\prod_{\lambda\in\Lambda}X_\lambda$ with all factors [compact](../topology.md#compact-space). An empty factor makes $X$ empty and hence [compact](../topology.md#compact-space). Otherwise, given an [ultrafilter](../set-theory.md#ultrafilter) $\mathcal U$ on $X$, push it forward by each [product projection](../geometry-and-topology.md#product-projection) to obtain an [ultrafilter](../set-theory.md#ultrafilter) $\mathcal U_\lambda$ on $X_\lambda$. Choose a limit $x_\lambda$ of each, and put $x=(x_\lambda)_\lambda$. A basic product [neighborhood](../topology.md#neighbourhood-mathematics) of $x$ is an intersection of finitely many inverse images of coordinate [neighborhoods](../topology.md#neighbourhood-mathematics). Each inverse image belongs to $\mathcal U$, so their [finite intersection](../set.md#finite-intersection) does too. Thus $\mathcal U$ converges to $x$, proving **[compactness](../topology.md#compact-space) of the product**. Uniqueness of coordinate limits is not needed, so the factors need not be [Hausdorff](../topology.md#hausdorff-space).

For [local compactness](../topology.md#locally-compact-space), suppose only the factors indexed by a finite set $F$ may be noncompact. At $x\in X$ choose, for each $\lambda\in F$, a [compact](../topology.md#compact-space) [neighborhood](../topology.md#neighbourhood-mathematics) $K_\lambda$ of $x_\lambda$, with an [open neighborhood](../topology.md#open-neighbourhood) $U_\lambda\subseteq K_\lambda$. For other indices put $K_\lambda=X_\lambda$. The [compact](../topology.md#compact-space) product $\prod_\lambda K_\lambda$ contains the open product [neighborhood](../topology.md#neighbourhood-mathematics) restricting just the finitely many coordinates in $F$ to $U_\lambda$. Hence every [point](../geometry-and-topology.md#point-geometry) has a [compact](../topology.md#compact-space) [neighborhood](../topology.md#neighbourhood-mathematics) and **the product is locally [compact](../topology.md#compact-space)**.

The unrestricted assertion is **false**. Each factor of $\mathbb R^{\mathbb N}$ is locally [compact](../topology.md#compact-space), but its product is not. Any basic [neighborhood](../topology.md#neighbourhood-mathematics) restricts only finitely many coordinates. Choose a free coordinate $j$; its projection of that [neighborhood](../topology.md#neighbourhood-mathematics) is all of $\mathbb R$. A [compact](../topology.md#compact-space) [neighborhood](../topology.md#neighbourhood-mathematics) containing it would consequently have [compact](../topology.md#compact-space) image equal to $\mathbb R$, impossible. This shows directly that no [point](../geometry-and-topology.md#point-geometry) has a [compact](../topology.md#compact-space) [neighborhood](../topology.md#neighbourhood-mathematics).

<h4 id="a/2/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#a/2)

For $x$ in a [compact Hausdorff space](../topology.md#compact-hausdorff-space) $K$, its [quasi-component](../geometry-and-topology.md#quasi-component) $Q$ is the intersection of all [clopen sets](../topology.md#clopen-set) containing $x$. It is closed and therefore [compact](../topology.md#compact-space). Suppose $Q=A\sqcup B$ is a [separation of a topological space](../geometry-and-topology.md#separation-of-a-topological-space) into two nonempty relatively [closed sets](../topology.md#closed-set), with $x\in A$. The sets $A,B$ are [compact](../topology.md#compact-space) and hence closed in $K$. [Normality of a compact Hausdorff space](../topology.md#normality-of-a-compact-hausdorff-space) gives disjoint [open neighborhoods](../topology.md#open-neighbourhood) $U,V$ of $A,B$.

The [compact set](../topology.md#compact-space) $K\setminus(U\cup V)$ is disjoint from $Q$. For each [point](../geometry-and-topology.md#point-geometry) $y$ of that set choose a [clopen](../topology.md#clopen-set) $C_y$ containing $x$ but not $y$. Finitely many complements cover $K\setminus(U\cup V)$, so their corresponding intersection $C$ is [clopen](../topology.md#clopen-set), contains $Q$, and is contained in $U\cup V$. Now $C\cap U$ is [clopen](../topology.md#clopen-set) in $C$, hence [clopen](../topology.md#clopen-set) in $K$, and contains $x$ while missing $B$. This contradicts the definition of $Q$. Thus **$Q$ is [connected](../geometry-and-topology.md#connected-space)**. Any [connected set](../geometry-and-topology.md#connected-set) containing $x$ lies in every [clopen](../topology.md#clopen-set) [neighborhood](../topology.md#neighbourhood-mathematics) of $x$, so the [connected component](../geometry-and-topology.md#connected-component) of $x$ equals $Q$.

For the second assertion, $D$ is closed in the ambient [Hausdorff space](../topology.md#hausdorff-space) $X$, and is itself [compact](../topology.md#compact-space) [Hausdorff](../topology.md#hausdorff-space). Let $C$ be a [connected component](../geometry-and-topology.md#connected-component) of $D$ disjoint from $\partial_XD$. Choose $p\in C$. By the first assertion $C$ is the [quasi-component](../geometry-and-topology.md#quasi-component) of $p$ in $D$. For each $y\in\partial_XD$ choose a [clopen](../topology.md#clopen-set) subset of $D$ containing $p$ but not $y$. [Compactness](../topology.md#compact-space) of $\partial_XD\subseteq D$ gives a [finite intersection](../set.md#finite-intersection) $H$ of these sets which contains $C$ and avoids the whole [boundary](../topology.md#boundary-of-a-set). Thus $H\subseteq\operatorname{int}_XD$. It is closed in $X$, because it is closed in the [closed set](../topology.md#closed-set) $D$, and is open in $X$, because it is relatively open in $D$ and contained in its [interior](../topology.md#interior-topology). It is nonempty and proper, contradicting [connectedness](../geometry-and-topology.md#connected-space) of $X$. Hence **every [connected component](../geometry-and-topology.md#connected-component) of $D$ meets its [boundary](../topology.md#boundary-of-a-set)**.

Finally suppose a closed subset $A$ of the square has the [point](../geometry-and-topology.md#point-geometry)-separating [clopen](../topology.md#clopen-set) property specified in the paper. Every nonempty relatively open subset of the square contains a nontrivial [line segment](../convex-set.md#line-segment): it contains an [interior](../topology.md#interior-topology) [point](../geometry-and-topology.md#point-geometry) of the square and a small ball about that [point](../geometry-and-topology.md#point-geometry). If such a segment lay in $A$, a [clopen](../topology.md#clopen-set) subset of $A$ separating its endpoints would induce a [separation of a topological space](../geometry-and-topology.md#separation-of-a-topological-space) of the [connected](../geometry-and-topology.md#connected-space) segment. This is impossible. Therefore $A$ contains no nonempty relatively open subset of the square. Being closed, it is **[nowhere dense](../topological-analysis.md#nowhere-dense-set) in the square**, and also [nowhere dense](../topological-analysis.md#nowhere-dense-set) in $\mathbb R^2$.

<h4 id="a/3/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#a/3)

Write $\alpha X=X\cup\{\infty_X\}$. Its [open sets](../topology.md#open-set) are the open subsets of $X$ together with sets $\alpha X\setminus K$, where $K$ is a [compact subset](../topology.md#compact-space) of $X$. [Compact subsets](../topology.md#compact-space) of the [Hausdorff space](../topology.md#hausdorff-space) $X$ are closed. This fact, and finite unions of [compact sets](../topology.md#compact-space), verify the topology axioms, including intersections of an old [open set](../topology.md#open-set) with a [neighborhood](../topology.md#neighbourhood-mathematics) of infinity. The inclusion $h_1:X\to\alpha X$ is a [topological embedding](../topology.md#topological-embedding) with open image.

An [open cover](../topology.md#open-cover) of $\alpha X$ has a member containing infinity; its complement is [compact](../topology.md#compact-space) in $X$ and therefore is covered by finitely many other members. This proves [compactness](../topology.md#compact-space). Two [points](../geometry-and-topology.md#point-geometry) of $X$ can be separated in $X$. A [point](../geometry-and-topology.md#point-geometry) $x$ and infinity can be separated by an [open neighborhood](../topology.md#open-neighbourhood) $U$ of $x$ contained in a [compact](../topology.md#compact-space) [neighborhood](../topology.md#neighbourhood-mathematics) $K$, and $\alpha X\setminus K$. Thus $\alpha X$ is [Hausdorff](../topology.md#hausdorff-space). Since $X$ is noncompact, every [neighborhood](../topology.md#neighbourhood-mathematics) of infinity meets $X$, so the embedded copy of $X$ is [dense](../topology.md#dense-set). This proves it is a **[Hausdorff](../topology.md#hausdorff-space) compactification**.

For a [proper map](../cohomology.md#proper-map), use the standard locally [compact](../topology.md#compact-space) [Hausdorff](../topology.md#hausdorff-space) convention: it is [continuous](../calculus.md#continuous-function) and inverse images of [compact sets](../topology.md#compact-space) are [compact](../topology.md#compact-space). Define

$$
\boxed{\alpha f(h_1(x))=h_2(f(x)),\qquad\alpha f(\infty_X)=\infty_Y.}
$$

An open subset of $Y$ has open inverse image in $X$, hence in $\alpha X$. A basic [neighborhood](../topology.md#neighbourhood-mathematics) $\alpha Y\setminus K$ of infinity has inverse image $\alpha X\setminus f^{-1}(K)$, open because $f^{-1}(K)$ is [compact](../topology.md#compact-space). Hence $\alpha f$ is [continuous](../calculus.md#continuous-function) and the diagram commutes. Any [continuous](../calculus.md#continuous-function) map from a [compact space](../topology.md#compact-space) to a [Hausdorff space](../topology.md#hausdorff-space) is proper: a [compact subset](../topology.md#compact-space) of the target is closed, its inverse image is closed in the [compact](../topology.md#compact-space) domain and therefore [compact](../topology.md#compact-space). This proves the required properness.

The last requested extension **does not exist**, even as a [continuous](../calculus.md#continuous-function) map. Both sequences $h_1(0,n)$ and $h_1(1,n)$ tend to $\infty_{\mathbb R^2}$, since they eventually leave every [compact subset](../topology.md#compact-space) of the plane. Their prescribed images would be the constant sequences $h_2(0)$ and $h_2(1)$. [Continuity](../calculus.md#continuous-function) would force both distinct [points](../geometry-and-topology.md#point-geometry) to equal $g(\infty_{\mathbb R^2})$, contradicting uniqueness of limits in the [Hausdorff](../topology.md#hausdorff-space) compactification of the line. The original [product projection](../geometry-and-topology.md#product-projection) is not proper, as its inverse image of a singleton is an unbounded line.

<h4 id="a/4/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#a/4)

If $\mathcal B$ is a [countable](../cardinal-number.md#countable-set) [topological basis](../topology.md#basis-of-a-topology) of $X$, then $\{B\cap Y:B\in\mathcal B\}$ is a [countable](../cardinal-number.md#countable-set) [topological basis](../topology.md#basis-of-a-topology) of the subspace $Y$. Indeed every subspace-[open neighborhood](../topology.md#open-neighbourhood) is $Y\cap U$ for an open $U$ of $X$, and a suitable basic set lies between its [point](../geometry-and-topology.md#point-geometry) and $U$. This proves the first assertion.

**[Urysohn's metrisation theorem](../mathematics.md#urysohn-s-metrization-theorem):** every [second-countable space](../topology.md#second-countable-space) that is also a [T3 space](../topology.md#t3-space) is a [metrizable space](../mathematics.md#metrizable-space). Here [regularity of a topological space](../topology.md#regular-topological-space) allows a [neighborhood](../topology.md#neighbourhood-mathematics) of a [point](../geometry-and-topology.md#point-geometry) to be shrunk to an [open neighborhood](../topology.md#open-neighbourhood) whose [closure](../topology.md#closure-topology) is still inside the original one.

We first prove the separation facts needed in a complete proof. [Second countability](../topology.md#second-countable-space) implies the [Lindelöf](../topology.md#lindelof-space) property: for each basic set contained in some member of an [open cover](../topology.md#open-cover) choose one such member; these countably many members cover. In particular closed subspaces are [Lindelöf](../topology.md#lindelof-space). For disjoint [closed sets](../topology.md#closed-set) $F,G$, [regularity of a topological space](../topology.md#regular-topological-space) and this property give open covers $F\subseteq\bigcup_nU_n$, $G\subseteq\bigcup_nV_n$ with $\overline U_n\cap G=\varnothing$ and $\overline V_n\cap F=\varnothing$. Define

$$
U=\bigcup_n\left(U_n\setminus\bigcup_{j\leq n}\overline V_j\right),\qquad V=\bigcup_n\left(V_n\setminus\bigcup_{j\leq n}\overline U_j\right).
$$

These sets are open and contain $F,G$ respectively. They are disjoint: if a [point](../geometry-and-topology.md#point-geometry) belonged to a term indexed by $n$ in the first union and by $m$ in the second, the term with the larger index would have removed the [closure](../topology.md#closure-topology) of the other covering set. Thus the space is a [normal space](../topology.md#normal-space).

For completeness, [Urysohn's lemma](../topology.md#urysohn-s-lemma) supplies [continuous](../calculus.md#continuous-function) separating functions in a [normal topological space](../topology.md#normal-space). Its construction is as follows. Given disjoint closed $F,G$, choose [open sets](../topology.md#open-set) $O_r$ for dyadic $r\in[0,1]$, with $F\subseteq O_0$, $O_1=X\setminus G$, and $\overline O_r\subseteq O_s$ whenever $r<s$. Start by shrinking a [neighborhood](../topology.md#neighbourhood-mathematics) of $F$ inside $X\setminus G$, and insert the successive dyadic sets by the [closed-neighborhood shrinking in a normal space](../topology.md#closed-neighborhood-shrinking-in-a-normal-space). Define

$$
f(x)=\inf\bigl(\{r:x\in O_r\}\cup\{1\}\bigr).
$$

Then $f=0$ on $F$, $f=1$ on $G$, and $0\leq f\leq1$. The identities $\{f<a\}=\bigcup_{r<a}O_r$ and $\{f>a\}=\bigcup_{a<r<1}(X\setminus\overline O_r)$ prove [continuity](../calculus.md#continuous-function) for $0<a<1$, with the endpoint cases immediate.

Let $\{B_n\}$ be a [countable](../cardinal-number.md#countable-set) [topological basis](../topology.md#basis-of-a-topology). For each pair $(n,m)$ with $\overline B_n\subseteq B_m$, choose a [continuous function](../calculus.md#continuous-function) $f_{nm}:X\to[0,1]$ equal to one on $\overline B_n$ and zero outside $B_m$. These form a [countable](../cardinal-number.md#countable-set) family. Given $x\in O$ open, choose $B_m$ with $x\in B_m\subseteq O$, shrink a [neighborhood](../topology.md#neighbourhood-mathematics) of $x$ inside $B_m$, and then choose $B_n$ inside that smaller [neighborhood](../topology.md#neighbourhood-mathematics). Thus

$$
x\in\{f_{nm}>1/2\}\subseteq B_m\subseteq O.
$$

Consequently these functions generate the original topology. They also separate distinct [points](../geometry-and-topology.md#point-geometry): use the $T_1$ [open set](../topology.md#open-set) $X\setminus\{y\}$ when $x\ne y$. Enumerate them as $f_j$, padding by zero functions if necessary. The map $x\mapsto(f_j(x))_j$ is a [topological embedding](../topology.md#topological-embedding) into the [countable](../cardinal-number.md#countable-set) cube. Pulling back its [metric](../topological-analysis.md#metric), or directly using

$$
\boxed{d(x,y)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}2^{-j}|f_j(x)-f_j(y)|,}
$$

gives a compatible [metric](../topological-analysis.md#metric). The fact that the functions distinguish distinct points proves positivity for distinct [points](../geometry-and-topology.md#point-geometry), the [triangle inequality](../topological-analysis.md#triangle-inequality) is termwise, and the uniformly bounded tails show that this [metric](../topological-analysis.md#metric) induces exactly the topology generated by the coordinates. Empty and singleton spaces are trivially [metrizable spaces](../mathematics.md#metrizable-space). This proves the theorem.

For the application, a [separable](../topology.md#separable-topological-space) [metric space](../topological-analysis.md#metric-space) has a [countable](../cardinal-number.md#countable-set) [topological basis](../topology.md#basis-of-a-topology) consisting of balls with rational positive radii about a [countable](../cardinal-number.md#countable-set) [dense](../topology.md#dense-set) set, and it is a [T3 space](../topology.md#t3-space). The given product theorems make a [countable](../cardinal-number.md#countable-set) product of these spaces [second countable](../topology.md#second-countable-space) and a [T3 space](../topology.md#t3-space). Urysohn's theorem makes it a [metrizable space](../mathematics.md#metrizable-space). Finally every [second countable](../topology.md#second-countable-space) space is [separable](../topology.md#separable-topological-space): choose a [point](../geometry-and-topology.md#point-geometry) from each nonempty basic [open set](../topology.md#open-set); the resulting [countable](../cardinal-number.md#countable-set) set meets every nonempty [open set](../topology.md#open-set). Hence the product is **[separable](../topology.md#separable-topological-space) and a [metrizable space](../mathematics.md#metrizable-space)**.

Here $ZF^*$ denotes [ZF without foundation](../set-theory.md#zf-without-foundation). [Transfinite recursion](../ordinal.md#transfinite-recursion) on [ordinals](../ordinal.md) defines the [cumulative hierarchy](../set-theory.md#cumulative-hierarchy) by

$$
\boxed{V_0=\varnothing,\qquad V_{\alpha+1}=\mathcal P(V_\alpha),\qquad V_\lambda=\bigcup_{\beta<\lambda}V_\beta\quad(\lambda\text{ a nonzero limit}).}
$$

The associated class is $V=\{x:\exists\alpha\in\operatorname{Ord}\ (x\in V_\alpha)\}$. Each level is a set, while $V$ is a definable class. Without [Foundation](../set-theory.md#axiom-of-regularity) it need not contain every ambient set; it consists of the sets built by this [well-founded](../set-theory.md#well-founded-relation) hierarchy.

<h4 id="b/5/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#b/5)

<h5 id="b/5/ii/a">a</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#b/5/ii)

<h6 id="b/5/ii/a/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#b/5/ii/a)

Use [transfinite induction](../ordinal.md#transfinite-induction). The inclusion at zero is immediate. If $V_\beta\subseteq V_{\beta+1}$, monotonicity of the [power set](../set.md#power-set) gives

$$
V_{\beta+1}=\mathcal P(V_\beta)\subseteq\mathcal P(V_{\beta+1})=V_{\beta+2}.
$$

If $\alpha$ is a limit and $x\in V_\alpha$, choose $\beta<\alpha$ with $x\in V_\beta$. The inductive inclusion $V_\beta\subseteq\mathcal P(V_\beta)$ means that $x\subseteq V_\beta\subseteq V_\alpha$. Thus $x\in\mathcal P(V_\alpha)=V_{\alpha+1}$. This proves **$V_\alpha\subseteq V_{\alpha+1}$ for every [ordinal](../ordinal.md)**. These successive inclusions and the union definition also give $V_\beta\subseteq V_\alpha$ whenever $\beta\leq\alpha$.

<h5 id="b/5/ii/b">b</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#b/5/ii)

<h6 id="b/5/ii/b/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b/5/ii/b)

The preceding inclusion is exactly $V_\alpha\subseteq\mathcal P(V_\alpha)$. Therefore every $x\in V_\alpha$ is a subset of $V_\alpha$. Equivalently,

$$
\boxed{y\in x\in V_\alpha\Longrightarrow y\in V_\alpha,}
$$

which says that $V_\alpha$ is a [transitive set](../set-theory.md#transitive-set). This uses the [power set](../set.md#power-set) definition and the proved inclusion, without any ambient use of [Foundation](../set-theory.md#axiom-of-regularity).

<h5 id="b/5/ii/c">c</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#b/5/ii)

<h6 id="b/5/ii/c/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#b/5/ii/c)

We prove the stronger [ordinal](../ordinal.md) identity

$$
V_\alpha\cap\operatorname{Ord}=\alpha
$$

by [transfinite induction](../ordinal.md#transfinite-induction). It holds at zero. At a successor stage an [ordinal](../ordinal.md) $\gamma$ belongs to $\mathcal P(V_\beta)$ exactly when every $\delta<\gamma$ belongs to $V_\beta$. By induction this means every $\delta<\gamma$ is less than $\beta$, equivalently $\gamma\leq\beta$. Thus the [ordinals](../ordinal.md) in $V_{\beta+1}$ are precisely those less than $\beta+1$. At a limit the identity follows by taking unions.

Every $\beta<\alpha$ consequently belongs to $V_\alpha$, so $\alpha\subseteq V_\alpha$ and $\alpha\in\mathcal P(V_\alpha)=V_{\alpha+1}$. But $\alpha\notin V_\alpha$, since an [ordinal](../ordinal.md) is not less than itself. Hence

$$
\boxed{\alpha\in V_{\alpha+1}\setminus V_\alpha.}
$$

The only well-foundedness used is that of the [ordinal](../ordinal.md) order, available independently of [Foundation](../set-theory.md#axiom-of-regularity) for arbitrary ambient sets.

<h5 id="b/5/ii/d">d</h5>

<h6 id="b/5/ii/d/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#b/5/ii/d)

Every set is a subset of itself, so $V_\alpha\subseteq V_\alpha$. By the successor definition,

$$
\boxed{V_\alpha\in\mathcal P(V_\alpha)=V_{\alpha+1}.}
$$

Membership and inclusion are different statements here; this argument establishes membership of the whole level in its successor.

<h4 id="b/5/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#b/5)

<h5 id="b/5/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#b/5/iii)

The class $V$ is transitive because all its levels are transitive. Let $a\in V$ be nonempty. There is a least [ordinal](../ordinal.md) $\alpha$ for which $a\cap V_\alpha\ne\varnothing$: existence follows by choosing an element of $a$, which lies in $V$, and then minimizing within some witnessing [ordinal](../ordinal.md) bound. The least [ordinal](../ordinal.md) is neither zero nor a limit, so write $\alpha=\beta+1$. Choose $x\in a\cap V_{\beta+1}$. Then $x\subseteq V_\beta$, while minimality gives $a\cap V_\beta=\varnothing$. Thus $a\cap x=\varnothing$. Also $x\in V$, proving **[Foundation](../set-theory.md#axiom-of-regularity) inside $\langle V,\in\rangle$**.

By the given hypothesis this same class satisfies every axiom of $ZF^*$, and hence it satisfies every axiom of $ZF$. Translate any finite formal $ZF$ proof by restricting each [quantifier](../mathematical-logic.md#quantifier) to the definable class $V$. Within $ZF^*$ the translated axioms have proofs, logical steps remain valid, and the translated contradiction is still a contradiction. Substituting those axiom proofs therefore converts the original derivation effectively into a finite derivation of contradiction from $ZF^*$. Equivalently,

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Con}(ZF^*)\Longrightarrow\operatorname{Con}(ZF).}
$$

This is the syntactic relative-consistency construction; it does not assume that $V$ is a set or that it equals the whole ambient universe.

<h3 id="b/6">6</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b)

<h4 id="b/6/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#b/6)

<h5 id="b/6/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#b/6/i)

The [transitive closure](../set-theory.md#transitive-closure) $\operatorname{TC}(a)$ is the least [transitive set](../set-theory.md#transitive-set) containing $a$ as a subset. Put $a_0=a$, $a_{n+1}=\bigcup a_n$ and define

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{TC}(a)=\bigcup_{n<\omega}a_n.}
$$

Recursion and [Replacement](../set-theory.md#axiom-schema-of-replacement) give the set of iterates, and Union gives its union. It contains $a=a_0$ as a subset. If $u\in v\in\operatorname{TC}(a)$, then $v\in a_n$ for some $n$, so $u\in a_{n+1}\subseteq\operatorname{TC}(a)$; hence it is transitive.

If $T$ is transitive with $a\subseteq T$, induction gives $a_n\subseteq T$ for every $n$, because $\bigcup T\subseteq T$. Therefore $\operatorname{TC}(a)\subseteq T$. This proves minimality and uniqueness. If the alternative convention asks for the least [transitive set](../set-theory.md#transitive-set) containing $a$ as an element, that set is $\operatorname{TC}(\{a\})$.

<h4 id="b/6/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#b/6)

<h5 id="b/6/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#b/6/ii)

The membership induction principle is the schema

$$
\boxed{\bigl[\forall x\bigl((\forall y\in x\ \varphi(y))\Rightarrow\varphi(x)\bigr)\bigr]\Longrightarrow\forall x\ \varphi(x),}
$$

allowing fixed parameters in $\varphi$.

Suppose the progressiveness hypothesis holds but $\neg\varphi(a)$. The [transitive set](../set-theory.md#transitive-set) $T=\operatorname{TC}(\{a\})$ contains $a$. [Separation](../set-theory.md#axiom-schema-of-specification) gives the nonempty set $B=\{x\in T:\neg\varphi(x)\}$. By [Foundation](../set-theory.md#axiom-of-regularity) choose $b\in B$ with $b\cap B=\varnothing$. Every $y\in b$ belongs to $T$ because it is a [transitive set](../set-theory.md#transitive-set) and cannot belong to $B$, so every such $y$ satisfies $\varphi$. Progressiveness now gives $\varphi(b)$, a contradiction. Thus the principle holds. The [transitive closure](../set-theory.md#transitive-closure) is needed to turn a possibly proper class of counterexamples into a set to which [Foundation](../set-theory.md#axiom-of-regularity) applies.

<h4 id="b/6/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#b/6)

<h5 id="b/6/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#b/6/iii)

An $\in$-[isomorphism](../algebra.md#isomorphism) is a [bijection](../function.md#bijection) $f:X\to Y$ such that $x\in x'$ if and only if $f(x)\in f(x')$ for all $x,x'\in X$. When $Y$ is transitive, [surjectivity](../algebra.md#surjective-function) gives the stronger recursive description

$$
f(x)=\{f(u):u\in x\cap X\}\qquad(x\in X).
$$

Indeed every member of $f(x)$ belongs to $Y$ because it is a [transitive set](../set-theory.md#transitive-set) and so has a preimage in $X$; the [isomorphism](../algebra.md#isomorphism) property identifies exactly which preimages occur.

If $f,g$ are two such maps, possibly to different [transitive sets](../set-theory.md#transitive-set), form $B=\{x\in X:f(x)\ne g(x)\}$. If $B$ were nonempty, [Foundation](../set-theory.md#axiom-of-regularity) would give $x\in B$ with $x\cap B=\varnothing$. All predecessors $u\in x\cap X$ then satisfy $f(u)=g(u)$, and the recursive descriptions force $f(x)=g(x)$, a contradiction. Therefore **the map and its transitive range are unique**.

The necessary and sufficient condition is that $\langle X,\in\rangle$ be **[well-founded](../set-theory.md#well-founded-relation) and extensional as a relation on $X$**. Well-foundedness says every nonempty $A\subseteq X$ has a member with no $\in$-predecessor in $A$. The [extensional relation](../set-theory.md#extensional-relation) condition says distinct elements $x,x'\in X$ have distinct predecessor sets $x\cap X$, $x'\cap X$. Sufficiency is the [Mostowski collapse theorem](../ordinal.md#mostowski-collapse-theorem).

For necessity, an [isomorphism](../algebra.md#isomorphism) transfers well-foundedness from its transitive range, where [Foundation](../set-theory.md#axiom-of-regularity) applies. For extensionality, if $x\cap X=x'\cap X$, the recursive descriptions give $f(x)=f(x')$, and [injectivity](../algebra.md#injective-function) gives $x=x'$. Notice that ambient [axiom of extensionality](../set-theory.md#axiom-of-extensionality) alone does not establish this internal predecessor condition; for example $X=\{\{0\},\{2\}\}$ can have distinct elements with no predecessors in $X$.

<h3 id="b/7">7</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b)

<h4 id="b/7/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [7](#b/7)

<h5 id="b/7/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#b/7/i)

A [cardinal number](../cardinal-number.md) is an [initial ordinal](../cardinal-number.md#initial-ordinal): an [ordinal](../ordinal.md) not in [bijection](../function.md#bijection) with any smaller [ordinal](../ordinal.md). In $ZFC$ every set has the [cardinality](../cardinal-number.md#cardinality) of a unique such [ordinal](../ordinal.md).

For an infinite [cardinal](../cardinal-number.md) $\kappa$, its [cofinality](../cardinal-number.md#cofinality) is the least [cardinality](../cardinal-number.md#cardinality) of an unbounded subset of the [ordinal](../ordinal.md) $\kappa$. Equivalently,

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{cf}(\kappa)=\min\{\theta:\exists\text{ a cofinal map }f:\theta\to\kappa\}.}
$$

A subset is unbounded when no [ordinal](../ordinal.md) below $\kappa$ bounds all its elements. A [cofinal](../cardinal-number.md#cofinal-subset) sequence can be taken increasing, and $\operatorname{cf}(\kappa)$ is an infinite [regular cardinal](../cardinal-number.md#regular-cardinal).

<h4 id="b/7/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [7](#b/7)

<h5 id="b/7/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#b/7/ii)

[König theorem for cardinal numbers](../cardinal-number.md#konig-s-theorem-set-theory) says that for families of [cardinals](../cardinal-number.md) $\kappa_i<\lambda_i$ indexed by a set $I$,

$$
\boxed{\sum_{i\in I}\kappa_i<\prod_{i\in I}\lambda_i.}
$$

The sum is the [cardinality](../cardinal-number.md#cardinality) of the disjoint union of sets $A_i$ of sizes $\kappa_i$, and the product is the [cardinality](../cardinal-number.md#cardinality) of $\prod_iB_i$ for sets of sizes $\lambda_i$.

Consider any function $F:\bigsqcup_iA_i\to\prod_iB_i$. For each $i$, the set of $i$th coordinates of images of [points](../geometry-and-topology.md#point-geometry) from the $i$th block has [cardinality](../cardinal-number.md#cardinality) at most $\kappa_i<\lambda_i$. Choose $b_i\in B_i$ outside it. Then $b=(b_i)_i$ is not the image of any [point](../geometry-and-topology.md#point-geometry): a [point](../geometry-and-topology.md#point-geometry) from block $i$ has the wrong $i$th coordinate. Thus there is no [surjection](../algebra.md#surjective-function) from the sum set onto the product set. [Cardinal](../cardinal-number.md) comparability in $ZFC$ makes the inequality strict in the indicated direction. For the empty index set the statement is $0<1$, also true. This is the diagonal argument underlying the inequality.

<h4 id="b/7/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [7](#b/7)

<h5 id="b/7/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#b/7/iii)

First derive the standard consequence of [König theorem for cardinal numbers](../cardinal-number.md#konig-s-theorem-set-theory) that every infinite [cardinal](../cardinal-number.md) $\rho$ satisfies

$$
\rho^{\operatorname{cf}(\rho)}>\rho.
$$

Let $\theta=\operatorname{cf}(\rho)$ and take [cofinal](../cardinal-number.md#cofinal-subset) [ordinals](../ordinal.md) $\alpha_i<\rho$, $i<\theta$. Put $\rho_i=\max(1,|\alpha_i|)<\rho$. Their sum is $\rho$: it is at least the size of the union of the [cofinal](../cardinal-number.md#cofinal-subset) [ordinals](../ordinal.md) and at most $\rho\cdot\theta=\rho$. Choose the next larger [cardinal](../cardinal-number.md) $\rho_i^+\leq\rho$. [König theorem for cardinal numbers](../cardinal-number.md#konig-s-theorem-set-theory) gives

$$
\rho=\sum_{i<\theta}\rho_i<\prod_{i<\theta}\rho_i^+\leq\rho^\theta.
$$

This also covers $\rho=\aleph_0$, using finite [cardinals](../cardinal-number.md) and their next larger finite [cardinals](../cardinal-number.md).

Suppose $\lambda=\kappa^\mu$ with both $\kappa,\mu$ infinite. Infinite [cardinal](../cardinal-number.md) multiplication gives

$$
\lambda^\mu=(\kappa^\mu)^\mu=\kappa^{\mu\cdot\mu}=\lambda.
$$

If $\operatorname{cf}(\lambda)\leq\mu$, monotonicity of exponentiation would contradict the preceding strict inequality for $\rho=\lambda$. Thus $\operatorname{cf}(\lambda)>\mu\geq\aleph_0$. This direction does not require [GCH](../cardinal-number.md#generalized-continuum-hypothesis).

Conversely assume [GCH](../cardinal-number.md#generalized-continuum-hypothesis) and $\operatorname{cf}(\lambda)>\aleph_0$. Every function $\omega\to\lambda$ has bounded range. For each infinite [cardinal](../cardinal-number.md) $\nu<\lambda$, [GCH](../cardinal-number.md#generalized-continuum-hypothesis) gives

$$
\nu^{\aleph_0}\leq(2^\nu)^{\aleph_0}=2^{\nu\cdot\aleph_0}=2^\nu=\nu^+\leq\lambda.
$$

Finite bounded ranges are also covered by the bound $\aleph_0^{\aleph_0}\leq\aleph_1\leq\lambda$. Counting functions according to an [ordinal](../ordinal.md) bound on their range now gives $\lambda^{\aleph_0}\leq\lambda\cdot\lambda=\lambda$. Constant functions give the reverse inequality. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\lambda=\lambda^{\aleph_0},}
$$

which is the required representation with $\kappa=\lambda$, $\mu=\aleph_0$. This proves both directions.

<h3 id="b/8">8</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b)

<h4 id="b/8/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [8](#b/8)

<h5 id="b/8/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#b/8/i)

For a set $A$, its [definable power set](../definable-power-set.md) is

$$
\operatorname{Def}(A)=\{\{x\in A:\langle A,\in\rangle\models\varphi(x,p_1,\ldots,p_n)\}:\varphi\text{ a formula},\ p_1,\ldots,p_n\in A\}.
$$

Only finitely many parameters occur in each definition. Satisfaction for the set structure is definable, so this specifies a uniformly definable class function assigning a set to every set $A$, with $\operatorname{Def}(A)\subseteq\mathcal P(A)$. Every subset definable over the structure with its parameters occurs. The full set $A$ itself occurs by the formula $x=x$. If $A$ is transitive, then $A\subseteq\operatorname{Def}(A)$, since any $p\in A$ is defined as a subset of $A$ by $x\in p$. In that case $\operatorname{Def}(A)$ is transitive as well: a member of one of its elements lies in $A$, hence in $\operatorname{Def}(A)$.

The [constructible hierarchy](../definable-power-set.md#constructible-hierarchy) and universe are

$$
\boxed{L_0=\varnothing,\quad L_{\alpha+1}=\operatorname{Def}(L_\alpha),\quad L_\lambda=\bigcup_{\beta<\lambda}L_\beta,\quad L=\bigcup_{\alpha\in\operatorname{Ord}}L_\alpha.}
$$

The displayed properties prove by induction that levels are transitive and increasing, so $L$ is transitive. It contains every [ordinal](../ordinal.md): inductively $L_\alpha\cap\operatorname{Ord}=\alpha$, and the subset of $L_\alpha$ consisting of [ordinals](../ordinal.md) is definable there, giving $\alpha\in L_{\alpha+1}$. Ordinalhood is absolute here because the levels are transitive. Both the level function and membership in $L$ are definable in $ZF$. This is Gödel's [constructible universe](../definable-power-set.md#constructible-universe), which can contain fewer subsets than the full universe.

<h4 id="b/8/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [8](#b/8)

<h5 id="b/8/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#b/8/ii)

For each $x\in a$, let $\beta_x$ be the least [ordinal](../ordinal.md) with $x\in L_{\beta_x}$. It exists because $a\subseteq L$, and it is uniquely definable. [Replacement](../set-theory.md#axiom-schema-of-replacement) therefore gives the set $\{\beta_x:x\in a\}$. Choose an [ordinal](../ordinal.md) $\alpha$ at least their [supremum](../real-analysis.md#supremum). Increasingness of the hierarchy gives

$$
\boxed{a\subseteq L_\alpha.}
$$

If $a$ is empty, $\alpha=0$ works. No assumption that $a$ itself belongs to $L$ is used; it is enough that its elements do.

<h4 id="b/8/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [8](#b/8)

<h5 id="b/8/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#b/8/iii)

Fix $a\in L$. In the ambient universe, [Separation](../set-theory.md#axiom-schema-of-specification) forms the set

$$
S=\{x\in\mathcal P(a):x\in L\}.
$$

It is a set of constructible sets, so the previous part gives an [ordinal](../ordinal.md) bound on all its members. Increase the bound to a level $L_\beta$ also containing $a$. [Transitivity](../set-theory.md#transitive-relation) ensures $a\subseteq L_\beta$. Then

$$
S=\{x\in L_\beta:x\subseteq a\}.
$$

The inclusion test is absolute for the transitive level $L_\beta$, since all members of $x$ and $a$ lie there. The right side is consequently a subset definable over $L_\beta$ with parameter $a$, so

$$
\boxed{S\in\operatorname{Def}(L_\beta)=L_{\beta+1}\subseteq L.}
$$

For $x\in L$, transitivity of the [constructible universe](../definable-power-set.md#constructible-universe) makes internal and ambient subsethood of $a$ agree. Hence $L$ satisfies that $S$ consists exactly of all its subsets of $a$. This is the [Power set](../set.md#power-set) Axiom in $\langle L,\in\rangle$. It is not the assertion that every ambient subset of $a$ belongs to $L$.

<h4 id="b/8/iv">iv</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [8](#b/8)

<h5 id="b/8/iv/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#b/8/iv)

Use the [constructible universe theorem](../definable-power-set.md#constructible-universe-theorem) that $L$ satisfies $ZFC$ and [GCH](../cardinal-number.md#generalized-continuum-hypothesis). In particular, internally it has a [bijection](../function.md#bijection)

$$
b:\omega_1^L\longrightarrow\mathcal P(\omega)^L.
$$

This [bijection](../function.md#bijection) belongs to $L$ and is also an actual [bijection](../function.md#bijection) in the ambient universe, because its domain, range and graph are transitive-model sets with the same membership relation.

We must compare the [ordinals](../ordinal.md) rather than assume [cardinality](../cardinal-number.md#cardinality) is absolute. Every [ordinal](../ordinal.md) below $\omega_1^L$ is [countable](../cardinal-number.md#countable-set) in $L$, and its witnessing enumeration belongs to the ambient universe too. Thus every such [ordinal](../ordinal.md) is [countable](../cardinal-number.md#countable-set) externally, proving $\omega_1^L\leq\omega_1$. Consequently the ambient [cardinality](../cardinal-number.md#cardinality) of the constructible reals is at most $\aleph_1$.

If every subset of $\omega$ were constructible, the displayed [bijection](../function.md#bijection) would enumerate the full ambient [power set](../set.md#power-set) using at most $\aleph_1$ [points](../geometry-and-topology.md#point-geometry). This contradicts the assumption $2^{\aleph_0}=\aleph_2$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\exists X\subseteq\omega\quad X\notin L.}
$$

The argument permits $\omega_1^L$ to be strictly smaller than the ambient $\omega_1$, exactly the [cardinal](../cardinal-number.md)-absoluteness issue highlighted in the source.
