<h1 id="9/f/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [F](../f.md)

Neither formula requires a bridging [carbonyl](../../../../../carbonyl-group.md) to repair an [electron](../../../../../electron.md) deficiency: the terminal-CO donor–acceptor structures in (d) and (e) already give an electron-counting rationale for the metal–metal interaction. **A predominantly terminal-carbonyl model is a reasonable starting proposal for A; bridging or semibridging CO is more geometrically plausible for the shorter Fe–Fe contact in B.** Larger metal separation makes a [carbonyl](../../../../../carbonyl-group.md) simultaneously engaging both Mo atoms less favourable, while [carbonyl](../../../../../carbonyl-group.md) redistribution or tilting can be easier around a compact Fe pair.

This is a qualified structural prediction, not a determination from molecular formula. A bridging CO can change the relative hapticity and local [electron](../../../../../electron.md) allocation, and the two metal centres are inequivalent; a large frequency shift by itself need not prove a symmetric bridge. Infrared [carbonyl](../../../../../carbonyl-group.md) frequencies combined with diffraction would test terminal versus bridging or semibridging assignments. The given low-temperature preparation can trap a coordination pattern before complete [carbonyl](../../../../../carbonyl-group.md) redistribution.
