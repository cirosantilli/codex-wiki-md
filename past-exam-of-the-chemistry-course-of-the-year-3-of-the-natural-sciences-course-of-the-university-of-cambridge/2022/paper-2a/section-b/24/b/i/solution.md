<h1 id="section-b/24/b/i/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [I](../i.md)

With equal masses m, the two mass-weighted Hessians are

$$
\boxed{D_{(1)}=\frac1m\begin{pmatrix}k_1+k_2&-k_1\\-k_1&k_1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
D_{(2)}=\frac1m\begin{pmatrix}k_1+k_3&-k_1\\-k_1&k_1+k_3\end{pmatrix}.}
$$

For a uniform displacement vector $t=(1,1)$,

$$
D_{(1)}t=\frac1m(k_2,0),\qquad D_{(2)}t=\frac{k_3}{m}(1,1).
$$

Therefore **uniform translation is not a [normal mode](../../../../../../../normal-mode.md) in orientation (1)** when $k_2\ne0$: anchoring only one atom mixes translation with internal stretching. **It is an exact [normal mode](../../../../../../../normal-mode.md) displacement in orientation (2)** because both atoms have identical restoring terms. Its [frequency](../../../../../../../frequency.md) is nevertheless nonzero, $\omega_T=\sqrt{k_3/m}$; the antisymmetric stretch has $\omega_S=\sqrt{(2k_1+k_3)/m}$. The rigid surface breaks free translational invariance in both orientations. A vector resembling a uniform translation need not be a zero-frequency mode when the molecule is anchored.
