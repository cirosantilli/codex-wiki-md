## 1

↑ **Parent:** [2009](2009.md)

<h3 id="1/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

The [matrix exponential](../linear-operator-theory.md#matrix-exponential) is the absolutely convergent series $e^{tA}=\sum_{n=0}^\infty t^nA^n/n!$, with derivative $Ae^{tA}$ and inverse $e^{-tA}$. If $K^T=-K$, then $(e^{tK})^Te^{tK}=e^{-tK}e^{tK}=I$. Its [determinant](../multilinear-algebra.md#determinant) is [continuous](../calculus.md#continuous-function) in $t$, is never zero, and starts at one, so it is one throughout: $e^{tK}\in SO(3)$. Conversely, differentiating $(e^{tK})^Te^{tK}=I$ at zero gives $K^T+K=0$. Thus

$$
\boxed{e^{tK}\in SO(3)\text{ for all real }t\iff K^T=-K.}
$$

The quantifier matters: membership at one isolated time alone would not imply skewness.

Write $P(F)=DW(F)$ and $J=\det F>0$. For any skew matrix $K$, differentiate frame indifference along $e^{tK}F$:

$$
0=P(F):(KF)=(P(F)F^T):K.
$$

A matrix orthogonal to every skew matrix is symmetric. Therefore $P(F)F^T$ and the [Cauchy stress tensor](../continuum-mechanics.md#cauchy-stress-tensor) $T=J^{-1}P(F)F^T$ are symmetric.

Let $\mathcal C=D^2W(I)$ and assume $P(I)=0$. Differentiating symmetry of $P(F)F^T$ at $I$ in direction $H$ shows $\mathcal C[H]$ is symmetric for every $H$. Hence $c_{ijkl}=c_{jikl}$. As a [Hessian](../multivariable-calculus.md#hessian-matrix), $\mathcal C$ also has pair-interchange symmetry $c_{ijkl}=c_{klij}$. Combining the two gives $c_{ijkl}=c_{ijlk}$. Equivalently $\mathcal C$ annihilates [infinitesimal rotations](../linear-algebra.md#skew-symmetric-matrix). This proves both requested minor symmetries and shows exactly where the stress-free assumption enters. It is [stress-free linearization of an objective energy](../continuum-mechanics.md#stress-free-linearization-of-an-objective-energy).

For the particular energy, $\sum_iv_i^2=\operatorname{tr}(F^TF)$ and $\prod_iv_i=J$, so

$$
P(F)=4\alpha F+h'(J)\operatorname{cof}F.
$$

At $I$ this is $[4\alpha+h'(1)]I$. Therefore the reference is stress-free exactly when $\boxed{h'(1)=-4\alpha}$. To differentiate again, use $\operatorname{cof}F=JF^{-T}$. The [determinant](../multilinear-algebra.md#determinant) and inverse derivatives at $I$ give

$$
\boxed{D(\operatorname{cof})(I)[H]=(\operatorname{tr}H)I-H^T,}
$$

which also proves the supplied hint. It follows that

$$
\mathcal C[H]=4\alpha H+h''(1)(\operatorname{tr}H)I-4\alpha[(\operatorname{tr}H)I-H^T].
$$

For a symmetric $e$ this becomes

$$
\boxed{\mathcal Ce=8\alpha e+[h''(1)-4\alpha](\operatorname{tr}e)I=2\mu e+\lambda(\operatorname{tr}e)I,\quad\mu=4\alpha,\ \lambda=h''(1)-4\alpha.}
$$

No additional positivity of the [bulk modulus](../elasticity-physics.md#bulk-modulus) follows from the stated smoothness of $h$ alone; this calculation identifies the moduli rather than imposing extra material assumptions.

## 2

↑ **Parent:** [2009](2009.md)

<h3 id="2/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

The first [Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor](../continuum-mechanics.md#piola-kirchhoff-stress-tensors) $T_R$ maps a reference normal to force per reference area. The [Cauchy stress tensor](../continuum-mechanics.md#cauchy-stress-tensor) $T$ maps a current normal to force per current area. [Nanson's formula](../continuum-mechanics.md#nanson-s-formula) gives $\mathbf n\,da=JF^{-T}\mathbf N\,dA$. Equating the actual surface force in both descriptions gives $T_R=JTF^{-T}$, and therefore

$$
\boxed{T=J^{-1}T_RF^T.}
$$

For [incompressibility](../fluid-mechanics.md#incompressible-flow) $\boxed{J=1}$. The strain-energy derivative only fixes the constitutive stress up to a [constraint reaction in hyperelastic stress](../elasticity-physics.md#constraint-reaction-in-hyperelastic-stress): $T_R=DW-p\operatorname{cof}F$, hence $T=DW F^T-pI$. The scalar $p$ is a [Lagrange multiplier](../mathematical-optimization.md#lagrange-multiplier) determined by equilibrium and boundary [tractions](../continuum-mechanics.md#traction), rather than by the isochoric energy alone.

In [principal stretch](../continuum-mechanics.md#principal-stretch) axes, the extra [principal stress](../continuum-mechanics.md#principal-stress) is $v_i\partial W/\partial v_i=2\alpha v_i^2-2\beta v_i^{-2}$, up to that arbitrary spherical reaction. With $B=FF^T$, its [eigenvalues](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) are $v_i^2$, so isotropy yields

$$
\boxed{T^*=2\alpha B-2\beta B^{-1},\qquad T=T^*-pI.}
$$

For the prescribed [simple shear](../continuum-mechanics.md#simple-shear),

$$
F=\begin{pmatrix}1&\gamma&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad
B=\begin{pmatrix}1+\gamma^2&\gamma&0\\\gamma&1&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad
B^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&-\gamma&0\\-\gamma&1+\gamma^2&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The $x_3$ faces retain normal $\pm\mathbf e_3$, and zero [traction](../continuum-mechanics.md#traction) there gives $p=2(\alpha-\beta)$. Thus

$$
\boxed{T=2\gamma\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\gamma&\alpha+\beta&0\\\alpha+\beta&-\beta\gamma&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}.}
$$

This constant stress satisfies the interior equilibrium equation, and the remaining face forces maintain the deformation.

To compute the angle, the material face $x_1=1$ becomes the spatial plane $y_1-\gamma y_2=1$. Its outward [unit normal](../differential-geometry.md#unit-normal) and an in-plane unit tangent are

$$
\mathbf n=\frac{(1,-\gamma,0)}{\sqrt{1+\gamma^2}},\qquad\mathbf m=\frac{(\gamma,1,0)}{\sqrt{1+\gamma^2}}.
$$

Resolve the current [traction](../continuum-mechanics.md#traction) $\mathbf t=T\mathbf n$ into these directions:

$$
N=\mathbf n\cdot T\mathbf n=-\frac{2\gamma^2(\alpha+2\beta+\beta\gamma^2)}{1+\gamma^2},\qquad
S=\mathbf m\cdot T\mathbf n=\frac{2\gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{1+\gamma^2}.
$$

Since $N<0$ for nonzero $\gamma$, the angle lies above $\pi/2$, and

$$
\boxed{\theta-\frac\pi2=\arctan\left(\frac{|\gamma|(\alpha+2\beta+\beta\gamma^2)}{\alpha+\beta}\right).}
$$

Using $\arctan z\sim z$ at zero gives

$$
\boxed{\lim_{\gamma\to0}\frac{\theta-\pi/2}{|\gamma|}=\frac{\alpha+2\beta}{\alpha+\beta}.}
$$

Using the undeformed normal $\mathbf e_1$ would give the wrong coefficient. The transformed [unit normal](../differential-geometry.md#unit-normal) is essential to this [simple-shear traction angle for a Mooney-Rivlin solid](../elasticity-physics.md#simple-shear-traction-angle-for-a-mooney-rivlin-solid).

## 3

↑ **Parent:** [2009](2009.md)

<h3 id="3/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

The [material symmetry group](../elasticity-physics.md#material-symmetry-group) consists of invertible admissible reference transformations $H$ for which $W(FH)=W(F)$ for every admissible $F$. Its rotational part lies in $SO(3)$; a [determinant](../multilinear-algebra.md#determinant)-one reference convention restricts the full group to the corresponding special linear group. This right action is distinct from the spatial left action $W(QF)=W(F)$ of [material frame indifference](../continuum-mechanics.md#material-frame-indifference).

The proper cube-rotation group has $\boxed{24}$ elements. Choose the image of one outward face normal in six ways, then the image of an adjacent perpendicular normal in four ways; orientation fixes the third direction. No reflections are included. For an isotropic material every proper rotation belongs to the [material symmetry group](../elasticity-physics.md#material-symmetry-group).

Combine [material isotropy](../elasticity-physics.md#material-isotropy) with the physical frame-indifference condition. The [singular value decomposition](../linear-algebra.md#singular-value-decomposition) may be written $F=R\operatorname{diag}(v_1,v_2,v_3)Q$ with $R,Q\in SO(3)$ because $\det F>0$. Left and right rotational invariance remove $R,Q$. Signed permutation rotations permit any permutation of the three diagonal stretches, so

$$
\boxed{W(F)=\Phi(v_1,v_2,v_3),\qquad\Phi\text{ symmetric in its three arguments}.}
$$

Right invariance alone, without the usual [material frame indifference](../continuum-mechanics.md#material-frame-indifference) assumption, would not suffice to remove spatial orientation.

The strict [Baker-Ericksen inequalities](../elasticity-physics.md#baker-ericksen-inequalities) say that larger distinct principal stretches carry larger principal Cauchy stresses:

$$
\boxed{(\sigma_i-\sigma_j)(v_i-v_j)>0\quad\text{if }v_i\ne v_j,\qquad
\sigma_i=J^{-1}v_i\Phi_i.}
$$

A [natural state of a hyperelastic material](../elasticity-physics.md#natural-state-of-a-hyperelastic-material) has zero stress. If two stretches differed, their equal zero stresses would contradict the strict inequality. Thus all three equal some $v>0$, and [polar decomposition](../linear-operator-theory.md#polar-decomposition) gives $\boxed{F=vQ}$, $Q\in SO(3)$. A weak non-strict inequality alone would not prove this conclusion, so its strict convention is explicit here.

For the radial deformation put $f(R)=r(R)/R$, $R=|\mathbf x|$, $\boldsymbol\theta=\mathbf x/R$. Since $\partial_jR=\theta_j$,

$$
D\mathbf y=fI+Rf'\boldsymbol\theta\otimes\boldsymbol\theta
=\boxed{\frac rR I+\left(r'-\frac rR\right)\boldsymbol\theta\otimes\boldsymbol\theta.}
$$

This symmetric tensor has radial [eigenvalue](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) $r'$ and the repeated tangential [eigenvalue](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) $r/R$. Both are positive in the open annulus under the stated monotonicity/nonnegativity conditions. Therefore these are its principal stretches. The two cases below establish [radial zero-energy rigidity](../elasticity-physics.md#radial-zero-energy-rigidity) by different arguments.

<h3 id="3/i">i</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/i/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#3/i)

At every point the [deformation gradient](../continuum-mechanics.md#deformation-gradient) attains the differentiable energy's minimum, so $DW(D\mathbf y)=0$ and it is a natural state. By the strict Baker-Ericksen argument just proved, all its principal stretches are equal. In the radial formula this gives

$$
r'(R)=r(R)/R,\qquad\frac d{dR}\left(\frac{r(R)}R\right)=0.
$$

Thus $r(R)=vR$ for one constant $v>0$ on the [connected](../geometry-and-topology.md#connected-space) radial interval, and

$$
\boxed{\mathbf y(\mathbf x)=v\mathbf x.}
$$

The constancy is global through the annulus, not a separately chosen dilation at each point.

<h3 id="3/ii">ii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/ii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#3/ii)

The radial gradient $F=D\mathbf y$ is symmetric positive definite. If it belongs to a well $SO(3)U_i$, write $F=QU_i$. Then $F^TF=U_i^2$, while positive symmetry makes $F$ itself the unique [positive square root of an operator](../hilbert-space.md#positive-square-root-of-an-operator) of $F^TF$. Hence $F=U_i$. Likewise a [symmetric positive-definite matrix](../linear-algebra.md#symmetric-positive-definite-matrix) in $SO(3)$ must be $I$. Therefore

$$
D\mathbf y(\mathbf x)\in\{I,U_1,\ldots,U_N\}.
$$

For each radius, the tangential stretch $r(R)/R$ must belong to the finite set of eigenvalues of these matrices. The ratio is [continuous](../calculus.md#continuous-function) on the [connected](../geometry-and-topology.md#connected-space) radial interval, so it is one constant $v>0$. Thus $r(R)=vR$ and differentiation gives $r'=v$ as well. Hence

$$
\boxed{\mathbf y(\mathbf x)=v\mathbf x.}
$$

This proof uses finiteness and positive definiteness of the wells, not isotropy or the [Baker-Ericksen inequalities](../elasticity-physics.md#baker-ericksen-inequalities). It also covers $R_1=0$: the punctured three-dimensional ball remains [connected](../geometry-and-topology.md#connected-space), and no condition at the excluded center was used.
