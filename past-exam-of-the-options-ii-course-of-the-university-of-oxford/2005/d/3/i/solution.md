The [monotone convergence theorem](../../../../../monotone-convergence-theorem.md) says that if $0\le f_n\uparrow f$ [almost everywhere](../../../../../almost-everywhere.md) and the functions are [measurable](../../../../../measurability.md) on a [measure space](../../../../../measure-space.md), then

$$
\int f=\lim_n\int f_n,
$$

with infinite [integrals](../../../../../integral-split.md) permitted. The limit is [measurable](../../../../../measurability.md) after the usual modification on any exceptional [null set](../../../../../null-set.md).

The [dominated convergence theorem](../../../../../dominated-convergence-theorem.md) says that if [measurable functions](../../../../../measurable-function.md) $f_n$ converge [almost everywhere](../../../../../almost-everywhere.md) to $f$ and satisfy $|f_n|\le g$ [almost everywhere](../../../../../almost-everywhere.md) for an integrable [nonnegative function](../../../../../nonnegative-function.md) $g$, then $f$ is integrable and

$$
\boxed{\int|f_n-f|\longrightarrow0,\qquad\int f_n\longrightarrow\int f.}
$$

The theorem applies equally to real or complex functions. In particular domination must be by one fixed [Lebesgue integrable function](../../../../../lebesgue-integrable-function.md), rather than by a different bound for each $n$.
