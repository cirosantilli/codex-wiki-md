<h1 id="c/2/b/iii/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](../iii.md)

If a [prime divisor](../../../../../../prime-factor.md) $p$ of $(n!)^2+1$ satisfied $p\le n$, it would divide $n!$ and therefore divide the difference $(n!)^2+1-(n!)^2=1$, impossible. Hence $p>n$.

Every [integer](../../../../../../integer-split.md) $(n!)^2+1>1$ has a [prime divisor](../../../../../../prime-factor.md), and (ii) makes each of its [prime divisors](../../../../../../prime-factor.md) congruent to 1 modulo 4. Taking arbitrarily large $n$ produces such [primes](../../../../../../prime-number-split.md) beyond every bound. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\text{There are infinitely many primes congruent to1 modulo4}.}
$$

Equivalently, if their list were finite, choosing $n$ at least its largest member would produce a new member larger than that supposed maximum.
