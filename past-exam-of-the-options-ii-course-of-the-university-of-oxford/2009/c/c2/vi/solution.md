<h1 id="c/c2/vi/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [Vi](../vi.md)

Suppose all primes congruent to $3$ modulo eight are $p_1,\ldots,p_r$, and put $N=p_1\cdots p_r$, an odd [integer](../../../../../integer-split.md). The [integer](../../../../../integer-split.md) $N^2+2$ is odd and congruent to three modulo eight. If a prime $q$ divides it, then $N^2\equiv-2\pmod q$ and $q\nmid N$, since a common divisor of $N$ and $N^2+2$ would divide two. Thus $(-2/q)=1$, so part (v) forces $q\equiv1$ or $3\pmod8$.

If all prime factors congruent to three had even multiplicity, their product, including the factors congruent to one, would be one modulo eight. Since $N^2+2$ is three modulo eight, at least one prime factor is congruent to three and occurs to odd multiplicity. But it is none of the $p_i$, since those divide $N$. This contradiction proves **infinitely many primes are congruent to three modulo eight**.
