<h3 id="a/1">1</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a)

<h4 id="a/1/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#a/1)

<h5 id="a/1/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#a/1/i)

A [normed vector space](../normed-vector-space.md) is a real or complex [vector space](../vector-space.md) $X$ with a [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm) satisfying positivity, definiteness, absolute homogeneity and the [triangle inequality](../topological-analysis.md#triangle-inequality). It is [separable](../topology.md#separable-topological-space) if it has a countable [dense subset](../topology.md#dense-set) for the [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm) distance.

First prove the unlabelled general assertions. If $(x_n)$ is dense in $X$, choose $y_{n,m}\in Y\cap B(x_n,1/m)$ whenever that intersection is nonempty. These choices form a countable subset of $Y$. For any $y\in Y$ and $\varepsilon>0$, choose $m$ with $2/m<\varepsilon$ and $n$ with $\|x_n-y\|<1/m$. The corresponding intersection is nonempty and $\|y-y_{n,m}\|<2/m<\varepsilon$. Thus **every subspace of a [separable](../topology.md#separable-topological-space) [normed vector space](../normed-vector-space.md) is [separable](../topology.md#separable-topological-space)**.

If a countable $S$ has dense [linear span](../vector-space.md#linear-span), take all finite linear combinations of its elements with rational coefficients in the real case, or coefficients in $\mathbb Q+i\mathbb Q$ in the complex case. This set is countable and dense in the [linear span](../vector-space.md#linear-span), by approximating the finitely many coefficients of each combination. Its closure is therefore $X$, proving separability. Conversely, a countable dense set already has dense [linear span](../vector-space.md#linear-span).

For $c_0$, use all [finitely supported sequences](../banach-space.md#finitely-supported-sequence) with coordinates in $\mathbb Q+i\mathbb Q$. Truncate a sequence tending to zero to control its supremum tail, then approximate the finite initial coordinates. This produces a countable [dense subset](../topology.md#dense-set), so

$$
\boxed{c_0\text{ is separable}.}
$$

<h4 id="a/1/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#a/1)

<h5 id="a/1/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#a/1/ii)

Map each bounded sequence $a=(a_n)$ to the continuous function that takes value $a_n$ at the integer $n\geq1$ and is affine on every interval $[n,n+1]$. Convexity of absolute value gives $|F_a(t)|\leq\sup_n|a_n|$, and evaluation at integers gives equality of suprema. Thus $a\mapsto F_a$ is an [isometric embedding](../riemannian-geometry.md#isometric-embedding) of the [l-infinity sequence space](../banach-space.md#l-infinity-sequence-space) into $C^b([1,\infty))$.

The former is not [separable](../topology.md#separable-topological-space) by the allowed fact. If the latter were [separable](../topology.md#separable-topological-space), the subspace result just proved would make its isometric copy of $\ell_\infty$ [separable](../topology.md#separable-topological-space), a contradiction. Hence

$$
\boxed{C^b([1,\infty))\text{ is not separable}.}
$$

<h4 id="a/1/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#a/1)

<h5 id="a/1/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#a/1/iii)

For $1<p<\infty$, truncation makes the $\ell^p$ tail arbitrarily small, since $\sum_j|a_j|^p$ converges. Approximate the finite remaining coordinates by numbers in $\mathbb Q+i\mathbb Q$. The resulting countable set of [finitely supported sequences](../banach-space.md#finitely-supported-sequence) is dense. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\ell^p\text{ is separable for }1<p<\infty.}
$$

The same construction also works for $p=1$, though that endpoint is not needed here.

<h3 id="a/2">2</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a)

<h4 id="a/2/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#a/2)

The [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm)-preserving form of the [Hahn-Banach theorem](../functional-analysis.md#hahn-banach-theorem) states: if $M$ is a [linear subspace](../vector-space.md#vector-subspace) of a real or complex [normed vector space](../normed-vector-space.md) $X$ and $f:M\to\mathbb K$ is a bounded [linear functional](../dual-space.md#linear-functional), there exists $F\in X^*$ with $F|_M=f$ and $\|F\|=\|f\|$.

For a real [separable](../topology.md#separable-topological-space) $X$, put $C=\|f\|$. The case $C=0$ is immediate. To extend over one vector $x_0\notin M$, choose a real number $c$ between

$$
\sup_{m\in M}\bigl(f(m)-C\|m-x_0\|\bigr)
\quad\text{and}\quad
\inf_{n\in M}\bigl(C\|n+x_0\|-f(n)\bigr).
$$

The lower endpoint is no greater than the upper: for any $m,n\in M$,

$$
f(m)+f(n)=f(m+n)\leq C\|m+n\|\leq C\|m-x_0\|+C\|n+x_0\|.
$$

The endpoints are finite because testing zero bounds them between finite numbers. Define $F(m+tx_0)=f(m)+tc$. The upper inequality ensures $F(z)\leq C\|z\|$ for vectors with positive $x_0$ coefficient, and the lower inequality gives it for negative coefficient; it already holds on $M$. Applying the same bound to $-z$ gives $|F(z)|\leq C\|z\|$. Thus the extension preserves the [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm).

Now add a countable dense sequence of vectors one at a time. The consistent extensions define a bounded functional of [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm) $C$ on the union of the increasing domains. That union is dense in $X$, so extension by [continuity](../calculus.md#continuous-function) gives the desired functional on all of $X$. This proves the real [separable](../topology.md#separable-topological-space) case without a maximality argument.

For the density application, suppose the [closed linear span](../vector-space.md#closed-linear-span) $M$ were proper. The [Hahn-Banach theorem](../functional-analysis.md#hahn-banach-theorem) supplies a nonzero continuous [linear functional](../dual-space.md#linear-functional) annihilating $M$. By [duality of sequence spaces](../continuous-dual-space.md#duality-of-sequence-spaces), it has the form $f(z)=\sum_{j\geq1}a_jz_j$ with $a\in\ell_1$, not identically zero. Let $m$ be the first index with $a_m\ne0$. Writing $t_n=(n+1)^{-1}$, annihilation of $x_n$ gives

$$
0=t_n^{-m}f(x_n)=a_m+\sum_{j>m}a_jt_n^{j-m}.
$$

The tail has absolute value at most $t_n\sum_{j>m}|a_j|\to0$. Letting $n\to\infty$ contradicts $a_m\ne0$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\overline{\operatorname{span}}\{x_n:n\geq1\}=c_0.}
$$

This is [geometric sequences with accumulating ratios span c0](../functional-analysis.md#geometric-sequences-with-accumulating-ratios-span-c0).

<h3 id="a/3">3</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a)

<h4 id="a/3/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#a/3)

<h5 id="a/3/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#a/3/i)

First give the general definitions and radius facts. The [spectrum of a bounded operator](../linear-operator-theory.md#spectrum-of-a-bounded-operator) is $\sigma(A)=\{\lambda:A-\lambda I\text{ has no bounded inverse}\}$, and the [point spectrum](../linear-operator-theory.md#point-spectrum) is $p\sigma(A)=\{\lambda:\ker(A-\lambda I)\ne0\}$. If $|\lambda|>\|A\|$, the [Neumann series](../banach-algebra.md#neumann-series) inverts $I-A/\lambda$, hence also $A-\lambda I=-\lambda(I-A/\lambda)$. Thus $\sigma(A)$ is contained in the closed disc of radius $\|A\|$ and is bounded.

The [spectral radius](../analysis.md#spectral-radius) is $\nu(A)=\sup_{\lambda\in\sigma(A)}|\lambda|$. The [spectral radius formula](../analysis.md#spectral-radius-formula) is

$$
\boxed{\nu(A)=\lim_{m\to\infty}\|A^m\|^{1/m}=\inf_{m\geq1}\|A^m\|^{1/m}.}
$$

For the left shift $R$, an eigenvector satisfies $a_{j+1}=\lambda a_j$, so $a_j=\lambda^{j-1}a_1$. A nonzero such sequence is in $\ell_1$ exactly when $|\lambda|<1$. Since $\|R\|=1$, its spectrum is contained in the closed unit disc. It contains the open unit disc as [point spectrum](../linear-operator-theory.md#point-spectrum) and therefore its closure by closedness of the spectrum. Hence

$$
\boxed{p\sigma(R)=\{\lambda:|\lambda|<1\},\qquad\sigma(R)=\{\lambda:|\lambda|\leq1\}.}
$$

<h4 id="a/3/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#a/3)

<h5 id="a/3/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#a/3/ii)

For the diagonal operator $S$, the equation $Sa=\lambda a$ gives $(j^{-1}-\lambda)a_j=0$ for every $j$. Thus a nonzero eigenvector exists exactly for $\lambda=1/j$, with eigenvector $e_j$; zero is not an eigenvalue because all diagonal entries are nonzero.

If $\lambda$ lies outside $\{0\}\cup\{1/j:j\geq1\}$, its distance from this closed set is positive. Dividing each coordinate by $j^{-1}-\lambda$ gives a [bounded inverse](../topological-vector-space.md#bounded-inverse) of $S-\lambda I$. Zero lies in the spectrum: $\|Se_j\|_1=1/j\to0$, excluding a [bounded inverse](../topological-vector-space.md#bounded-inverse). Therefore

$$
\boxed{p\sigma(S)=\{1/j:j\geq1\},\qquad\sigma(S)=\{0\}\cup\{1/j:j\geq1\}.}
$$

<h4 id="a/3/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#a/3)

<h5 id="a/3/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#a/3/iii)

The weighted right shift $T$ is injective, so zero is not an eigenvalue. If $Ta=\lambda a$ with $\lambda\ne0$, the first coordinate forces $a_1=0$ and subsequent coordinates force every $a_j=0$. Thus its [point spectrum](../linear-operator-theory.md#point-spectrum) is empty.

For $m\geq1$,

$$
T^me_j=\frac{e_{j+m}}{j(j+1)\cdots(j+m-1)},\qquad\|T^m\|=\frac1{m!},
$$

since the largest column sum is attained at $j=1$. Therefore the [spectral radius formula](../analysis.md#spectral-radius-formula) gives $\nu(T)=\lim_m(m!)^{-1/m}=0$. The limit follows, for example, from $m!\geq(m/2)^{m/2}$ for large $m$. Zero is in the spectrum because the output first coordinate is always zero, so $T$ is not onto. Hence

$$
\boxed{p\sigma(T)=\varnothing,\qquad\sigma(T)=\{0\}.}
$$

This is a nonzero [quasinilpotent element](../banach-algebra.md#quasinilpotent-element), specifically the [factorial weighted right shift](../linear-operator-theory.md#factorial-weighted-right-shift).

<h4 id="b/4/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#b/4)

<h5 id="b/4/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#b/4/i)

A complete [orthonormal sequence](../hilbert-space.md#orthonormal-sequence) $(e_n)$ has $\langle e_n,e_m\rangle=\delta_{nm}$ and dense [linear span](../vector-space.md#linear-span); equivalently, the only vector orthogonal to every $e_n$ is zero. The [Parseval identity](../fourier-analysis.md#parseval-identity) says $\|x\|^2=\sum_n|\langle x,e_n\rangle|^2$, with expansion $x=\sum_n\langle x,e_n\rangle e_n$ in the Hilbert [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm).

Put $G(t,z)=(1-2zt+z^2)^{-1/2}$ and $D=1-2zt+z^2$. Direct differentiation gives

$$
G_t=zD^{-3/2},\quad G_{tt}=3z^2D^{-5/2},\quad G_z=(t-z)D^{-3/2},\quad G_{zz}=-D^{-3/2}+3(t-z)^2D^{-5/2}.
$$

Substitution yields $(1-t^2)G_{tt}-2tG_t+z^2G_{zz}+2zG_z=0$. Extracting the coefficient of $z^n$ in the [generating function](../sequence-and-series.md#generating-function) gives

$$
\boxed{(1-t^2)P_n''-2tP_n'+n(n+1)P_n=0,}
$$

the [Legendre differential equation](../linear-ordinary-differential-equation.md#legendre-differential-equation).

<h4 id="b/4/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#b/4)

<h5 id="b/4/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#b/4/ii)

The [generating function](../sequence-and-series.md#generating-function) gives $P_n(-t)=(-1)^nP_n(t)$, so the odd function $f$ is orthogonal to every even-degree [Legendre polynomial](../linear-ordinary-differential-equation.md#legendre-polynomial). For $n\geq1$, integrate the self-adjoint form of the [Legendre differential equation](../linear-ordinary-differential-equation.md#legendre-differential-equation) from zero to one:

$$
[(1-t^2)P_n'(t)]_0^1+n(n+1)\int_0^1P_n(t)dt=0.
$$

Thus $\int_0^1P_n=P_n'(0)/(n(n+1))$. Differentiating the [generating function](../sequence-and-series.md#generating-function) in $t$ and then setting $t=0$ gives

$$
\sum_nP_n'(0)z^n=z(1+z^2)^{-3/2},\qquad P_{2m+1}'(0)=(-1)^m\frac{(2m+1)!}{2^{2m}(m!)^2}.
$$

Combining these identities with the normalization of $p_n$ yields

$$
\boxed{\langle f,p_{2m}\rangle=0,\qquad\langle f,p_{2m+1}\rangle=(-1)^m\sqrt{\frac{4m+3}{2}}\frac{(2m)!}{2^{2m}m!(m+1)!}.}
$$

For the [Legendre expansion of the sign function](../linear-ordinary-differential-equation.md#legendre-expansion-of-the-sign-function), $\|f\|_2^2=2$, so the [Parseval identity](../fourier-analysis.md#parseval-identity) gives

$$
2=\sum_{m\geq0}\frac{4m+3}{2}\frac{((2m)!)^2}{2^{4m}(m!(m+1)!)^2}.
$$

Therefore the requested sum equals

$$
\boxed{4.}
$$

<h4 id="b/4/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#b/4)

<h5 id="b/4/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#b/4/iii)

Expand both generating functions and use [orthogonality](../inner-product.md#orthogonal-vectors) of the [Legendre polynomials](../linear-ordinary-differential-equation.md#legendre-polynomial), with $\int_{-1}^1P_nP_m=2\delta_{nm}/(2n+1)$. The allowed exchanges give

$$
\int_{-1}^1\frac{dt}{\sqrt{1-2wt+w^2}\sqrt{1-2zt+z^2}}=2\sum_{n\geq0}\frac{(wz)^n}{2n+1}.
$$

Hence it depends only on $r=wz$. Explicitly,

$$
\boxed{F(r)=2\int_0^1\frac{ds}{1-rs^2}=\begin{cases}2\operatorname{artanh}(\sqrt r)/\sqrt r,&r>0,\\2,&r=0,\\2\arctan(\sqrt{-r})/\sqrt{-r},&r<0.\end{cases}}
$$

The [series](../sequence-and-series.md#series-mathematics) form alone already proves the required dependence.

<h3 id="b/5">5</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b)

<h4 id="b/5/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#b/5)

With $\widehat f(k)=(2\pi)^{-1}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(s)e^{-iks}ds$, the classical [Fourier partial sum](../fourier-series.md#fourier-partial-sum) is $s_N^f(t)=\sum_{|k|\leq N}\widehat f(k)e^{ikt}$. Its [Cesàro mean](../sequence-and-series.md#cesaro-mean) is

$$
\sigma_N^f=\frac1{N+1}\sum_{n=0}^Ns_n^f=\sum_{|k|\leq N}\left(1-\frac{|k|}{N+1}\right)\widehat f(k)e^{ikt}.
$$

For the uniform [Fejér theorem](../fourier-series.md#fejer-s-theorem), extend the continuous endpoint-matching $f$ periodically. The given [Fejér kernel](../fourier-series.md#fejer-kernel) is nonnegative, even and has total integral $\pi$. Thus

$$
\sigma_N^f(t)-f(t)=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}[f(t-u)-f(t)]F_N(u)du.
$$

Given $\varepsilon>0$, uniform [continuity](../calculus.md#continuous-function) supplies $\delta>0$ so the bracket has modulus less than $\varepsilon$ for $|u|<\delta$, uniformly in $t$. That part contributes at most $\varepsilon$. On the remainder, $F_N(u)\leq[2(N+1)\sin^2(\delta/2)]^{-1}$, and the bracket is at most $2\|f\|_\infty$. Hence

$$
\|\sigma_N^f-f\|_\infty\leq\varepsilon+\frac{2\|f\|_\infty}{(N+1)\sin^2(\delta/2)}\longrightarrow\varepsilon.
$$

Letting $\varepsilon$ decrease to zero proves **[uniform convergence](../sequence-and-series.md#uniform-convergence) of the [Fejér sums](../fourier-series.md#fejer-sum)**.

The later assertions attached to the printed factorial-frequency example are inconsistent. The following calculations resolve them while keeping the Fourier definition just given. Put $a_j=e^{i\sqrt{j+1}}-e^{i\sqrt j}$ and consider its formal trigonometric [series](../sequence-and-series.md#series-mathematics). For $t=\pi p/q$, there is $J_0$ such that $\cos(j!t)=1$ for every $j\geq J_0$, because $2q$ divides $j!$. At $N_J=(J+1)!-1$, its ordinary partial sum therefore telescopes to

$$
s_{N_J}(t)=C(t)+e^{i\sqrt{J+1}}.
$$

The exponential has subsequences tending to $1$ and $-1$: take $J+1$ to be integers nearest $(2\pi m)^2$ and $((2m+1)\pi)^2$. Thus **the ordinary sums diverge at every $t\in\pi\mathbb Q$**.

But their genuine Fourier [Cesàro means](../sequence-and-series.md#cesaro-mean) also diverge there. Indeed,

$$
\sigma_{N_J}(t)-s_{N_J}(t)=-\frac1{(J+1)!}\sum_{j=0}^Jj!a_j\cos(j!t).
$$

Since $|a_j|\leq2$ and $\sum_{j=0}^Jj!\leq2J!$ for $J\geq3$, its absolute value is at most $4/(J+1)\to0$, uniformly in $t$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\sigma_{N_J}(t)=C(t)+e^{i\sqrt{J+1}}+o(1),}
$$

which has the same incompatible limit points. This is an instance of [Cesàro sums at factorial cutoffs track ordinary sums](../sequence-and-series.md#cesaro-sums-at-factorial-cutoffs-track-ordinary-sums). **The printed Cesàro-convergence claim is false.** Averaging the sparse [series](../sequence-and-series.md#series-mathematics) by its index $j$ would be a different operation from Fourier Cesàro summation by frequency.

There is a further issue: **no $L^1$ function has exactly this [Fourier series](../fourier-series.md)**. Here is a direct proof using [strongly lacunary polynomial L1-L2 bound](../fourier-series.md#strongly-lacunary-polynomial-l1-l2-bound). For a [trigonometric polynomial](../fourier-series.md#trigonometric-polynomial) $p$ supported on $\{\pm j!:j\geq3\}$, consecutive positive frequencies have ratio at least four. Every zero sum of four signed frequencies pairs into cancelling equal frequencies: a largest unpaired frequency would exceed the sum of the other three. Expanding the fourth moment with normalized measure $dt/(2\pi)$ and bounding the three possible pairings gives

$$
\|p\|_4^4\leq3\|p\|_2^4.
$$

The [Holder inequality](../functional-analysis.md#holder-inequality) gives $\|p\|_2\leq\|p\|_1^{1/3}\|p\|_4^{2/3}$, hence $\|p\|_2\leq\sqrt3\|p\|_1$.

If the asserted $f\in L^1$ existed, positivity and unit mass of the summation kernel would bound $\|\sigma_N^f\|_1\leq\|f\|_1$. Subtract the finitely many modes from $j<3$; their norms stay uniformly bounded, and the remainder is a [polynomial](../polynomial.md) of the type above. Its $L^2$ norms would therefore be uniformly bounded. [Orthogonality](../inner-product.md#orthogonal-vectors) then forces $\sum_{j\geq3}|a_j|^2<\infty$, by fixing any finite coefficient block and letting $N\to\infty$. However,

$$
|a_j|^2=4\sin^2\left(\frac{\sqrt{j+1}-\sqrt j}{2}\right)\sim\frac1{4j},
$$

so that sum diverges. This contradiction disproves the stated existence premise.

Even considered only formally, these coefficients rule out an $L^2$ function by [Bessel's inequality](../hilbert-space.md#bessel-s-inequality), and hence rule out a bounded function on the finite interval. Thus a bounded continuous representative cannot satisfy the asserted [series](../sequence-and-series.md#series-mathematics) either. **The asserted $L^1$ function does not exist.**

<h3 id="b/6">6</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b)

<h4 id="b/6/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#b/6)

<h5 id="b/6/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#b/6/i)

The [Hilbert projection theorem](../hilbert-space.md#hilbert-projection-theorem) states that every vector $x$ has a unique decomposition $x=m+n$ relative to a closed [linear subspace](../vector-space.md#vector-subspace) $M$, with $m\in M$ and $n\in M^\perp$. The vector $m$ is the unique nearest point in $M$.

The [Riesz representation theorem](../hilbert-space.md#riesz-representation-theorem) states that every bounded complex-[linear functional](../dual-space.md#linear-functional) $F$ on a [Hilbert space](../hilbert-space.md) has a unique representation $F(x)=\langle x,y\rangle$, with $\|F\|=\|y\|$, using the convention of linearity in the first argument. If $F=0$, use $y=0$. Otherwise $M=\ker F$ is closed. Project a vector outside $M$ to obtain $z\in M^\perp$ with $F(z)\ne0$, and rescale it so $F(z)=1$. Then $x-F(x)z\in M$, so

$$
\langle x,z\rangle=F(x)\|z\|^2,\qquad y=z/\|z\|^2.
$$

This gives the representation. Uniqueness follows because a vector orthogonal to every $x$ is zero. The [Cauchy-Schwarz inequality](../probability-and-statistics.md#cauchy-schwarz-inequality) gives $\|F\|\leq\|y\|$, and evaluation at $y/\|y\|$ gives equality when $y\ne0$.

For the adjoint, fix $v$. The functional $u\mapsto\langle Tu,v\rangle$ is bounded with [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm) at most $\|T\|\|v\|$. By the [Riesz representation theorem](../hilbert-space.md#riesz-representation-theorem), there is a unique vector $T^*v$ satisfying

$$
\boxed{\langle u,T^*v\rangle=\langle Tu,v\rangle\quad(u\in V).}
$$

For scalars $a,b$, conjugate-linearity in the second argument shows that $T^*(av+bw)=aT^*v+bT^*w$, by uniqueness of the representing vector. The same [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm) estimate gives $\|T^*v\|\leq\|T\|\|v\|$. Thus $T^*$ is a unique [bounded linear operator](../topological-vector-space.md#continuous-linear-operator), the [adjoint operator](../hilbert-space.md#adjoint-operator).

<h4 id="b/6/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#b/6)

<h5 id="b/6/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#b/6/ii)

For the [adjoint of an integration operator anchored at zero](../functional-analysis.md#adjoint-of-an-integration-operator-anchored-at-zero), use the [inner product](../inner-product.md) $\langle f,g\rangle=\int f\bar g$. On the positive half-interval, reversing the triangular integration order gives

$$
\int_0^\pi\left(\int_0^tf(s)ds\right)\overline{g(t)}dt=\int_0^\pi f(s)\overline{\left(\int_s^\pi g(t)dt\right)}ds.
$$

On the negative half-interval the corresponding calculation gives

$$
\int_{-\pi}^0\left(-\int_t^0f(s)ds\right)\overline{g(t)}dt=\int_{-\pi}^0f(s)\overline{\left(-\int_{-\pi}^sg(t)dt\right)}ds.
$$

These exchanges are justified by the [Fubini theorem](../measure-theory.md#fubini-s-theorem), since on a finite interval $L^2$ functions belong to $L^1$ and the absolute double integral is at most $\|f\|_1\|g\|_1$. Therefore

$$
(T^*g)(s)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\int_s^\pi g(t)dt,&s>0,\\\displaystyle-\int_{-\pi}^sg(t)dt,&s<0.\end{cases}
$$

Adding $Tg$ gives $\int_0^\pi g$ on the positive half and $-\int_{-\pi}^0g$ on the negative half, exactly the prescribed $Ug$. Values at zero do not matter in $L^2$. Consequently

$$
\boxed{T+T^*=U.}
$$

<h4 id="b/6/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#b/6)

<h5 id="b/6/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#b/6/iii)

Since $f(0)=0$, the [fundamental theorem of calculus](../calculus.md#fundamental-theorem-of-calculus) gives $f=Tf'$, with the same oriented integral on the negative half-interval. Set $h(t)=t^2$. The adjoint calculation gives the real-valued function

$$
\boxed{g(t)=(T^*h)(t)=\begin{cases}(\pi^3-t^3)/3,&0<t\leq\pi,\\-(\pi^3+t^3)/3,&-\pi<t<0.\end{cases}}
$$

Choose any value at zero. This function is bounded and hence belongs to $L^2(-\pi,\pi]$. Since it is real, the [adjoint operator](../hilbert-space.md#adjoint-operator) identity yields

$$
\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}t^2f(t)dt=\langle Tf',h\rangle=\langle f',T^*h\rangle=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}g(t)f'(t)dt.
$$

One can also check this by [integration by parts](../calculus.md#integration-by-parts) separately on each half-interval: $g'=-t^2$, $g(\pm\pi)=0$, and the two possible jump terms at zero vanish because $f(0)=0$.
