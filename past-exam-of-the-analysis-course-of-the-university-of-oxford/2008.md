## A

↑ **Parent:** [2008](2008.md)

<h3 id="a/1">1</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a)

<h4 id="a/1/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#a/1)

If $(a^{(n)})$ is Cauchy in the [maximum norm](../normed-vector-space.md#supremum-norm) on $\mathbb C^m$, every coordinate is Cauchy in $\mathbb C$ and has a limit $a_j$. Given $\varepsilon>0$, for sufficiently large $n,k$ every coordinate difference is at most $\varepsilon$. Letting $k\to\infty$ gives $\max_j|a_j^{(n)}-a_j|\leq\varepsilon$. Thus **$\mathbb C^m$ with its [maximum norm](../normed-vector-space.md#supremum-norm) is [complete](../topological-analysis.md#completeness)**.

Let $F(a)=\sum a_jz_j$. The [triangle inequality](../topological-analysis.md#triangle-inequality) gives

$$
\|F(a)\|\leq\left(\sum_{j=1}^m\|z_j\|\right)\|a\|_\infty.
$$

Take $\delta=\sum\|z_j\|$. This estimate also makes $a\mapsto\|F(a)\|$ [continuous](../calculus.md#continuous-function) on the maximum-[norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm) unit sphere, which is [compact](../topology.md#compact-space) by coordinatewise Bolzano-Weierstrass. [Linear independence](../vector-space.md#linear-independence) makes this function strictly positive there, so its minimum $\gamma$ is positive. [Homogeneity](../real-analysis.md#homogeneity) then gives

$$
\boxed{\gamma\|a\|_\infty\leq\|F(a)\|\leq\delta\|a\|_\infty.}
$$

A [Cauchy sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#cauchy-sequence) in $X$ consequently has Cauchy coordinate vectors in $\mathbb C^m$. Their coordinate limit maps by the upper estimate to a limit in $X$. Therefore **every finite-dimensional normed complex space is a [Banach space](../banach-space.md)**.

<h3 id="a/2">2</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a)

<h4 id="a/2/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#a/2)

The [dual space](../dual-space.md) $X'$ consists of bounded linear maps $g:X\to\mathbb C$, with [operator norm](../continuous-dual-space.md#operator-norm) $\|g\|=\sup_{\|x\|\leq1}|g(x)|$. It is Banach even if $X$ is not: an operator-[norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm) [Cauchy sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#cauchy-sequence) has pointwise limits, these define a [bounded linear functional](../topological-vector-space.md#continuous-linear-functional), and the uniform Cauchy estimate on the unit ball gives operator-[norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm) convergence.

Restriction $R(g)=g|_Z$ is linear and contractive. If $Z$ is [dense](../topology.md#dense-set), for every $x\in X$ choose $z_n\in Z$ with $z_n\to x$. The bound $|g(z_n)|\leq\|Rg\|\|z_n\|$ passes to the limit, proving $|g(x)|\leq\|Rg\|\|x\|$. Hence $\|g\|=\|Rg\|$ and restriction is [injective](../algebra.md#injective-function). Conversely, for $h\in Z'$ define $g(x)=\lim h(z_n)$. The estimate $|h(z_n)-h(z_k)|\leq\|h\|\|z_n-z_k\|$ gives existence and independence of the approximating sequence. It also gives linearity and $\|g\|\leq\|h\|$. Thus **restriction is an onto linear [isometry](../riemannian-geometry.md#isometry) of dual spaces**.

For the sequence subspace in this part, the [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm) is the unweighted $\ell^1$ [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm); only its membership condition uses the weight $j$. It contains all finitely supported sequences, hence is [dense](../topology.md#dense-set) in $\ell^1$. Applying restriction to $X=\ell^1$ and using its known dual yields

$$
\boxed{Z'\cong\ell^\infty\text{ isometrically},\qquad h_b(\alpha)=\sum_{j=1}^\infty b_j\alpha_j,\quad\|h_b\|=\|b\|_\infty.}
$$

<h3 id="a/3">3</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a)

<h4 id="a/3/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#a/3)

The complex [Hahn-Banach theorem](../functional-analysis.md#hahn-banach-theorem) says that every bounded complex-[linear functional](../dual-space.md#linear-functional) on a [linear subspace](../vector-space.md#vector-subspace) of a normed complex space extends to the whole space with the same [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm).

For the separation assertion choose $x_0\notin M$ and $d=\operatorname{dist}(x_0,M)>0$. On $M+\mathbb Cx_0$ define $g_0(m+\lambda x_0)=\lambda d$. The inequality $\|m+\lambda x_0\|\geq|\lambda|d$ makes its [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm) at most one; approximating the distance $d$ shows the [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm) is exactly one. Hahn-Banach gives an extension $g$ of [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm) one annihilating $M$.

Each [geometric sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#geometric-progression) satisfies $\|x_k\|_1=(1-|\beta_k|)^{-1}\leq(1-\delta)^{-1}$. Let $M$ be their [closed linear span](../vector-space.md#closed-linear-span). If $M\ne\ell^1$, the separation just proved gives a nonzero bounded functional annihilating all $x_k$. Represent it by $b\in\ell^\infty$ and form

$$
F(z)=\sum_{j=1}^\infty b_jz^{j-1},\qquad|z|<1.
$$

The series converges locally uniformly and defines a [holomorphic function](../holomorphic-function.md). It vanishes at every $\beta_k$. These distinct points lie in the [compact](../topology.md#compact-space) disk $|z|\leq\delta<1$, so have an accumulation point inside the domain. The [identity theorem](../complex-analysis.md#identity-theorem) gives $F=0$, hence every [Taylor coefficient](../calculus.md#taylor-coefficient) $b_j=0$, contradicting the functional's nonzero [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm). Therefore

$$
\boxed{\overline{\operatorname{span}\{x_k:k\geq1\}}=\ell^1.}
$$

<h3 id="a/4">4</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a)

<h4 id="a/4/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#a/4)

The [spectrum of a bounded operator](../linear-operator-theory.md#spectrum-of-a-bounded-operator) is $\sigma(T)=\{\lambda:\lambda I-T\text{ has no bounded two-sided inverse}\}$. Its [point spectrum](../linear-operator-theory.md#point-spectrum) is the set of eigenvalues, where $\lambda I-T$ is not [injective](../algebra.md#injective-function).

Here $T$ is linear and takes [continuous](../calculus.md#continuous-function) functions to continuously differentiable functions. Also $\|Tx\|_\infty\leq2\|x\|_\infty$; the constant function one attains this bound at $t=1$, so $\|T\|=2$. Its range is contained in $C^1([0,1])$, a proper subset of $C([0,1])$, so zero is in its spectrum. It is not an [eigenvalue](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue): $Tx=0$ gives $x(0)=0$ at zero and $x=0$ upon differentiation.

For a nonzero [eigenvalue](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue), $\lambda x=Tx$ implies $x'=x/\lambda$ and $(\lambda-1)x(0)=0$. A nonzero solution is therefore possible exactly at $\lambda=1$, with eigenfunctions $x(t)=ce^t$. Hence $p\sigma(T)=\{1\}$.

For the proposed [resolvent](../functional-analysis.md#resolvent-of-an-operator) put

$$
F(t)=e^{t/\lambda}\int_0^t y(s)e^{-s/\lambda}ds,\qquad F'=y+F/\lambda.
$$

The proposed $x$ is $y/\lambda+y(0)e^{t/\lambda}/[\lambda(\lambda-1)]+F/\lambda^2$. Consequently $x(0)=y(0)/(\lambda-1)$ and

$$
\int_0^tx(s)ds=\frac{F(t)}\lambda+\frac{y(0)}{\lambda-1}(e^{t/\lambda}-1).
$$

Adding $x(0)$ and subtracting from $\lambda x(t)$ gives exactly $y(t)$. Thus $\lambda I-T$ is onto for $\lambda\ne0,1$. It is also [injective](../algebra.md#injective-function) by the [eigenvalue](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) calculation. The displayed formula defines a bounded map in the [supremum norm](../normed-vector-space.md#supremum-norm), since its exponential kernels are bounded on the [compact](../topology.md#compact-space) interval. It is therefore the [bounded inverse](../topological-vector-space.md#bounded-inverse), and

$$
\boxed{\sigma(T)=\{0,1\},\qquad p\sigma(T)=\{1\}.}
$$

<h3 id="b/5">5</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b)

<h4 id="b/5/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#b/5)

Use the convention that the [inner product](../inner-product.md) is linear in its first argument. If $v\ne0$, nonnegativity of $\|u-\langle u,v\rangle v/\|v\|^2\|^2$ gives

$$
\boxed{|\langle u,v\rangle|\leq\|u\|\|v\|.}
$$

The case $v=0$ is immediate. This [Cauchy-Schwarz inequality](../probability-and-statistics.md#cauchy-schwarz-inequality) shows that $f_v$ is bounded linear with [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm) at most $\|v\|$. For $v\ne0$, evaluation at $v/\|v\|$ gives equality; for $v=0$ both norms vanish. The Banach dual is the space of all bounded linear functionals with the [operator norm](../continuous-dual-space.md#operator-norm).

The [Riesz representation theorem](../hilbert-space.md#riesz-representation-theorem) asserts that every $f\in V'$ is uniquely $f(u)=\langle u,v\rangle$ for some $v\in V$, with $\|f\|=\|v\|$. If $f=0$, take $v=0$. Otherwise put $W=\ker f$, a [closed subspace](../topology.md#closed-subspace), and use the projection theorem to obtain $w\in W^\perp$ with $f(w)=1$. Since $u-f(u)w\in W$, $\langle u,w\rangle=f(u)\|w\|^2$. Thus $v=w/\|w\|^2$ represents $f$. If two vectors represent it, their difference has [inner product](../inner-product.md) zero with every vector, in particular itself, giving uniqueness. The [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm) statement was already proved.

The space $P_n$ is finite-dimensional and hence [complete](../topological-analysis.md#completeness); evaluation at zero is bounded there by equivalence of finite-dimensional norms. Riesz therefore supplies exactly one $q_n\in P_n$. In any [orthonormal](../inner-product.md#orthonormal-set) [polynomial](../polynomial.md) basis,

$$
\boxed{q_n(t)=\sum_{j=0}^n\overline{p_j(0)}p_j(t),\qquad\|q_n\|^2=\sum_{j=0}^n|p_j(0)|^2.}
$$

For the real Legendre functions the bars may be omitted.

**The printed even-degree normalization has an error.** [Orthogonality](../inner-product.md#orthogonal-vectors) forces a degree-two [polynomial](../polynomial.md) to be a scalar multiple of $3t^2-1$, whose squared integral on $[-1,1]$ is $8/5$. Hence any [orthonormal](../inner-product.md#orthonormal-set) choice has $|p_2(0)|^2=5/8$, whereas the printed formula gives $3/8$. The factor under its square root must be $4m+1$ instead of $2m+1$. Signs of the individual polynomials can be chosen so that the values at zero are positive; these signs do not affect $q_n$ or its [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm).

Put $M=\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ and $c_m=\binom{2m}{m}/4^m$. With the consistent [orthonormal](../inner-product.md#orthonormal-set) normalization the [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm) is

$$
\boxed{\|q_n\|^2=\frac12\sum_{m=0}^M(4m+1)c_m^2=\frac{(2M+1)^2c_M^2}{2},\qquad\|q_n\|=\frac{(2M+1)c_M}{\sqrt2}.}
$$

The finite sum telescopes because $c_m=(2m-1)c_{m-1}/(2m)$, so $(2m+1)^2c_m^2-(2m-1)^2c_{m-1}^2=(4m+1)c_m^2$. If one substitutes the erroneous printed values formally instead, one obtains $\frac12\sum_{m=0}^M(2m+1)c_m^2$; that is not the [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm) of the actual [orthonormal](../inner-product.md#orthonormal-set) Legendre evaluation representer.

For a [polynomial](../polynomial.md) $r$ of degree $d$, the defining identity gives $\langle r,q_n\rangle=r(0)$ for every $n\geq d$, so the requested limit is eventually constant. It does **not** extend to all of $L^2$. First, evaluation at a point is not well-defined on equivalence classes. More strongly, the functional norms $\|q_n\|$ are unbounded. Indeed the largest binomial coefficient in row $2m$ is at least the average, so $c_m\geq1/(2m+1)$, and the displayed sum dominates a divergent constant multiple of the [harmonic series](../sequence-and-series.md#harmonic-series). The [Uniform boundedness principle](../banach-space.md#uniform-boundedness-principle) then provides an $L^2$ function for which $\sup_n|\langle r,q_n\rangle|=\infty$. Thus no choice of point representative can restore the assertion for all $L^2$ functions.

<h3 id="b/6">6</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b)

<h4 id="b/6/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#b/6)

Write $L_n(t)=\sum_{k=0}^na_kt^k$. Matching coefficients in the differential equation gives

$$
(k+1)^2a_{k+1}+(n-k)a_k=0.
$$

Taking $a_0=1$ yields

$$
\boxed{L_n(t)=\sum_{k=0}^n(-1)^k\binom nk\frac{t^k}{k!}.}
$$

The top coefficient is $(-1)^n/n!\ne0$, so this is a real [polynomial](../polynomial.md) of degree exactly $n$.

Put $D_n=L_n'-L_n$. [Integration by parts](../calculus.md#integration-by-parts), with vanishing endpoint terms because of the factor $t$ at zero and exponential decay at infinity, gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^\infty tD_nD_me^{-t}dt
&=-\int_0^\infty[(tD_ne^{-t})'+tD_ne^{-t}]L_mdt\\
&=-\int_0^\infty[D_n+tD_n']L_me^{-t}dt\\
&=(n+1)\int_0^\infty L_nL_me^{-t}dt.
\end{aligned}
$$

The last step uses $D_n+tD_n'=tL_n''+(1-t)L_n'-L_n=-(n+1)L_n$. The left side is symmetric in $m,n$. Interchanging them therefore gives $(n-m)\int L_nL_me^{-t}=0$, proving [orthogonality](../inner-product.md#orthogonal-vectors) for distinct indices. These integrals are exactly $\langle\varphi_n,\varphi_m\rangle$, and each $\varphi_n$ is square-integrable and nonzero. Thus they form an [orthogonal](../inner-product.md#orthogonal-vectors) sequence in $L^2(0,\infty)$.

Multiplying the equations by $z^n$ and summing gives

$$
\boxed{tG_{tt}+(1-t)G_t+zG_z=0.}
$$

Conversely equality of power-series coefficients recovers every [polynomial](../polynomial.md) equation. For the proposed expression,

$$
\frac{G_t}G=-\frac z{1-z},\qquad\frac{G_{tt}}G=\frac{z^2}{(1-z)^2},\qquad\frac{G_z}G=\frac1{1-z}-\frac t{(1-z)^2}.
$$

Substitution makes the PDE left side zero. Its coefficient of $z^n$ has value one at $t=0$, so it corresponds to the normalization $L_n(0)=1$ chosen above.

<h3 id="b/7">7</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b)

<h4 id="b/7/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [7](#b/7)

With the classical real coefficients

$$
a_j=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^\pi f(x)\cos(jx)dx,\qquad b_j=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^\pi f(x)\sin(jx)dx,
$$

the partial sum is $s_n^f=a_0/2+\sum_{j=1}^n(a_j\cos jx+b_j\sin jx)$. It is the [orthogonal projection](../hilbert-space.md#orthogonal-projection) onto the first trigonometric modes. [Completeness](../topological-analysis.md#completeness) of the trigonometric [orthonormal](../inner-product.md#orthonormal-set) system therefore gives $\|s_n^f-f\|_2\to0$.

For $f\in C^1$ periodic, [integration by parts](../calculus.md#integration-by-parts) has zero endpoint terms and gives $a_j(f')=jb_j(f)$, $b_j(f')=-ja_j(f)$, and $a_0(f')=0$. Thus

$$
\boxed{s_n^{f'}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^n[jb_j(f)\cos jx-ja_j(f)\sin jx]=(s_n^f)'(x).}
$$

For periodic $C^2$ functions, another [integration by parts](../calculus.md#integration-by-parts) gives $a_j(f)=-a_j(f'')/j^2$ and $b_j(f)=-b_j(f'')/j^2$. Riemann-Lebesgue makes the numerator sequences tend to zero, in particular bounded. The [Weierstrass M-test](../probability-and-statistics.md#weierstrass-m-test) and $\sum j^{-2}<\infty$ prove absolute and [uniform convergence](../sequence-and-series.md#uniform-convergence). The resulting [continuous](../calculus.md#continuous-function) sum agrees with $f$ in $L^2$ by the first result. Two [continuous](../calculus.md#continuous-function) functions equal almost everywhere are equal everywhere, so the pointwise sum is $f$ for every real $x$.

For the [polynomial](../polynomial.md) example the periodic extension is $C^2$: the function, its first [derivative](../calculus.md#derivative) and its second [derivative](../calculus.md#derivative) match at the endpoints. It is even, so all sine coefficients vanish. Direct integration gives

$$
\frac{a_0}{2}=\frac{7\pi^4}{15},\qquad a_j=\frac{48(-1)^j}{j^4}\quad(j\geq1).
$$

For example, [integration by parts](../calculus.md#integration-by-parts) gives $\int_0^\pi x^2\cos(jx)dx=2\pi(-1)^j/j^2$ and $\int_0^\pi x^4\cos(jx)dx=4\pi^3(-1)^j/j^2-24\pi(-1)^j/j^4$, from which the cancellation of the $j^{-2}$ terms follows. Therefore

$$
\boxed{f(x)=\frac{7\pi^4}{15}+48\sum_{j=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^j\cos(jx)}{j^4}.}
$$

At $x=0$, the left side vanishes, yielding

$$
\boxed{\sum_{j=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^{j+1}}{j^4}=\frac{7\pi^4}{720}.}
$$

<h4 id="b/8/a">a</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [8](#b/8)

<h5 id="b/8/a/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#b/8/a)

Write $\lambda=a+ib$ with $b\ne0$. Since $aI-T$ is self-adjoint, $\langle(aI-T)v,v\rangle$ is real. The cross term in the squared [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm) therefore vanishes:

$$
\|(\lambda I-T)v\|^2=\|(aI-T)v\|^2+b^2\|v\|^2=\|(\overline\lambda I-T)v\|^2.
$$

This proves the equality and lower bound, and makes both $S=\lambda I-T$ and $S^*=\overline\lambda I-T$ [injective](../algebra.md#injective-function).

If $Sv_n$ converges, the lower bound shows $v_n$ is Cauchy. [Completeness](../topological-analysis.md#completeness) gives $v_n\to v$ and boundedness gives $Sv_n\to Sv$, so the range is closed. Also $(\operatorname{ran}S)^\perp=\ker S^*=0$, so the range is [dense](../topology.md#dense-set). Being both closed and [dense](../topology.md#dense-set), it equals $V$. Hence $S$ is bijective, and its lower bound gives

$$
\boxed{\|(\lambda I-T)^{-1}\|\leq|\operatorname{Im}\lambda|^{-1},\qquad\sigma(T)\subseteq\mathbb R.}
$$

<h4 id="b/8/b">b</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [8](#b/8)

<h5 id="b/8/b/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b/8/b)

Define $f^\sharp(s)=\overline{f(-s)}$. Interchanging the integrals and substituting variables gives

$$
\langle T_fg_1,g_2\rangle=\langle g_1,T_{f^\sharp}g_2\rangle.
$$

Moreover Cauchy-Schwarz in the inner integral gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
|\langle T_fg_1,g_2\rangle|
&\leq\int_{\mathbb R}|f(s)|\left|\int_{\mathbb R}g_1(t-s)\overline{g_2(t)}dt\right|ds\\
&\leq\|f\|_1\|g_1\|_2\|g_2\|_2.
\end{aligned}
$$

Taking the supremum over unit $g_2$ proves $\|T_fg_1\|_2\leq\|f\|_1\|g_1\|_2$, so $T_f$ is bounded with [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm) at most $\|f\|_1$, and the adjoint identity is justified.

It remains to ensure necessity is a condition on the [kernel](../kernel-of-a-linear-map.md) itself. If $T_h=0$ for some $h\in L^1$, convolve $h$ with a smooth compactly supported [approximate identity](../fourier-analysis.md#approximate-identity) $\eta_\varepsilon\in L^1\cap L^2$. Since $T_h\eta_\varepsilon=0$, all these convolutions vanish. But $h*\eta_\varepsilon\to h$ in $L^1$, by continuity of translations in $L^1$, so $h=0$ almost everywhere. Thus the map from kernels to [convolution](../fourier-analysis.md#convolution) operators is [injective](../algebra.md#injective-function). Applying this to $h=f-f^\sharp$ yields the exact criterion

$$
\boxed{T_f=T_f^*\iff f(s)=\overline{f(-s)}\text{ for almost every }s.}
$$

For real-valued kernels this reduces to evenness; real-valuedness by itself is insufficient.
