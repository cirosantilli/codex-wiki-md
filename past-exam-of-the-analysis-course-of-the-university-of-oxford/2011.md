## 1

↑ **Parent:** [2011](2011.md)

<h3 id="1/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

A [normed vector space](../normed-vector-space.md) over $\mathbb R$ is a real [vector space](../vector-space.md) $X$ with a function $\|\cdot\|:X\to[0,\infty)$ satisfying

$$
\|x\|=0\iff x=0,\qquad\|ax\|=|a|\|x\|,
\qquad\|x+y\|\leq\|x\|+\|y\|.
$$

Its [distance](../geometry-and-topology.md#distance) is $d(x,y)=\|x-y\|$. It is a [Banach space](../banach-space.md) if this [metric](../topological-analysis.md#metric) is complete: **every [Cauchy sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#cauchy-sequence) converges to an element of $X$**.

<h3 id="1/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1/a)

The [series](../sequence-and-series.md#series-mathematics) $\sum_{n\geq1}x_n$ converges in $X$ when its [partial sums](../sequence-and-series.md#partial-sum) $s_N=\sum_{n=1}^Nx_n$ converge in [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm) to an element of $X$. It is absolutely convergent when $\sum_n\|x_n\|<\infty$.

If $X$ is complete, then

$$
\|s_M-s_N\|\leq\sum_{n=N+1}^M\|x_n\|\longrightarrow0
$$

for an absolutely convergent [series](../sequence-and-series.md#series-mathematics). Its [partial sums](../sequence-and-series.md#partial-sum) are Cauchy and therefore converge.

Conversely suppose every absolutely convergent [series](../sequence-and-series.md#series-mathematics) converges. Given a [Cauchy sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#cauchy-sequence) $(z_n)$, choose a subsequence $(z_{n_k})$ with $\|z_{n_{k+1}}-z_{n_k}\|\leq2^{-k}$. The [series](../sequence-and-series.md#series-mathematics)

$$
z_{n_1}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(z_{n_{k+1}}-z_{n_k})
$$

is absolutely convergent. Its [partial sums](../sequence-and-series.md#partial-sum) are precisely the subsequence, which therefore converges to some $z\in X$. A [Cauchy sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#cauchy-sequence) with a convergent subsequence converges to that same limit: estimate $\|z_n-z\|$ by $\|z_n-z_{n_k}\|+\|z_{n_k}-z\|$. Hence

$$
\boxed{X\text{ is Banach}\iff\text{every absolutely convergent series in }X\text{ converges}.}
$$

<h3 id="1/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

<h5 id="1/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#1/b/i)

Show that $c$ is a closed [linear subspace](../vector-space.md#vector-subspace) of $\ell^\infty$. Suppose $a^{(j)}\in c$ and $\|a^{(j)}-a\|_\infty\to0$. Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose $j$ with uniform error below $\varepsilon/3$. Since $a^{(j)}$ converges, choose $N$ such that $|a_m^{(j)}-a_n^{(j)}|<\varepsilon/3$ for $m,n\geq N$. Then

$$
|a_m-a_n|\leq|a_m-a_m^{(j)}|+|a_m^{(j)}-a_n^{(j)}|+|a_n^{(j)}-a_n|<\varepsilon.
$$

Thus $(a_n)$ is a real [Cauchy sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#cauchy-sequence) and converges, so $a\in c$. A closed [linear subspace](../vector-space.md#vector-subspace) of a [Banach space](../banach-space.md) is complete in the inherited [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm). Therefore $\boxed{(c,\|\cdot\|_\infty)\text{ is Banach}}$.

<h4 id="1/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

<h5 id="1/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#1/b/ii)

Let $e_j$ be the [coordinate vectors](../vector-space.md#coordinate-vector) and define finitely supported vectors

$$
v_k=2^{-k}\sum_{j=2^k}^{2^{k+1}-1}e_j,\qquad k=0,1,\ldots.
$$

Each $v_k$ belongs to $\ell^1$, and $\|v_k\|_\infty=2^{-k}$. Hence $\sum_kv_k$ is absolutely convergent for the [supremum norm](../normed-vector-space.md#supremum-norm). Its [partial sums](../sequence-and-series.md#partial-sum) converge uniformly in $\ell^\infty$ to the sequence $v$ whose entries on the $k$th block all equal $2^{-k}$. But every block contributes one to its $\ell^1$ sum:

$$
\sum_j|v_j|=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}2^k2^{-k}=\infty.
$$

Thus $v\notin\ell^1$. Any supremum-[norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm) limit in $\ell^1$ would have the same coordinates as $v$, an impossibility. The [partial sums](../sequence-and-series.md#partial-sum) are Cauchy and have no limit in the space. Therefore **$(\ell^1,\|\cdot\|_\infty)$ is not Banach**, and the displayed [series](../sequence-and-series.md#series-mathematics) is the required absolutely convergent but nonconvergent [series](../sequence-and-series.md#series-mathematics) in that space.

<h4 id="1/b/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

<h5 id="1/b/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#1/b/iii)

Write $q(x)=\sup_k|\sum_{n=1}^kx_n|$. This is indeed a [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm): the [triangle inequality](../topological-analysis.md#triangle-inequality) and homogeneity follow from the scalar absolute value, and vanishing of every [partial sum](../sequence-and-series.md#partial-sum) forces every coordinate to vanish. It is finite on $\ell^1$ because $q(x)\leq\|x\|_1$.

Let $a_n=(-1)^{n+1}/n$ and let $a^{(N)}$ be its first $N$ entries followed by zeros. The alternating-[series](../sequence-and-series.md#series-mathematics) bound gives, for $M>N$,

$$
q(a^{(M)}-a^{(N)})\leq\frac1{N+1}.
$$

Thus these truncations are Cauchy for $q$. But $|x_n|\leq2q(x)$ for every coordinate, since $x_n$ is the difference of two consecutive [partial sums](../sequence-and-series.md#partial-sum). Any $q$-limit would consequently have coordinates $a_n$. This sequence is not in $\ell^1$. Hence

$$
\boxed{(\ell^1,q)\text{ is not a Banach space}.}
$$

↑ **Parent:** [2011](2011.md)

<h3 id="2/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

The [continuous dual space](../continuous-dual-space.md) $X'$ is the [vector space](../vector-space.md) of bounded scalar-valued [linear functionals](../dual-space.md#linear-functional), with [operator norm](../continuous-dual-space.md#operator-norm)

$$
\boxed{\|f\|=\sup_{\|x\|\leq1}|f(x)|.}
$$

This makes it a normed space, and in fact a [Banach space](../banach-space.md) whether or not $X$ is complete.

The [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm)-preserving [Hahn-Banach theorem](../functional-analysis.md#hahn-banach-theorem) states that a [bounded linear functional](../topological-vector-space.md#continuous-linear-functional) $f_0$ on any [linear subspace](../vector-space.md#vector-subspace) $M\subseteq X$ extends to a [bounded linear functional](../topological-vector-space.md#continuous-linear-functional) $f$ on all of $X$ with $f|_M=f_0$ and $\|f\|=\|f_0\|$. The subspace need not be closed. This holds over both the real and complex scalar fields, using the complex version when necessary.

<h3 id="2/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#2/a)

Put $d=\operatorname{dist}(x,Z)$. If $d=0$, the zero functional works. If $d>0$, then $x\notin\overline Z$, and on $M=Z+\operatorname{span}\{x\}$ define

$$
f_0(z+ax)=ad.
$$

The representation is unique, so this is linear. For $a\ne0$,

$$
|f_0(z+ax)|=|a|d=\operatorname{dist}(ax,Z)\leq\|z+ax\|,
$$

and the bound also holds for $a=0$. Thus $\|f_0\|\leq1$. The [Hahn-Banach theorem](../functional-analysis.md#hahn-banach-theorem) extends it to $f\in X'$ with the same bound, proving

$$
\boxed{f|_Z=0,\qquad f(x)=d,\qquad\|f\|\leq1.}
$$

No closedness assumption on $Z$ was required; [distance](../geometry-and-topology.md#distance) is unchanged on taking its [closure](../topology.md#closure-topology).

<h3 id="2/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

For the centered ball, the [Hahn-Banach theorem](../functional-analysis.md#hahn-banach-theorem) supplies a [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm)-one real functional with $f(y)=\|y\|>r$. Hence $f(z)\leq r$ on the ball, and any $t\in(r,\|y\|)$ separates it strictly from $y$.

For the general center, apply the same construction to $y-x$: choose $\|f\|=1$ and $f(y-x)=\|y-x\|$. Then

$$
f(z)\leq f(x)+r\quad(z\in B),\qquad
f(y)=f(x)+\|y-x\|>f(x)+r.
$$

For example,

$$
\boxed{t=f(x)+\frac{r+\|y-x\|}{2}}
$$

gives $f(z)<t<f(y)$ for every $z\in B$. The strict gap uses exactly the assumption that $y$ lies outside the closed ball.

<h3 id="2/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#2/c)

Let $Z=\overline{\operatorname{span}}\{x_n:n\geq1\}$. If $Z\ne X$, part (a) provides a nonzero bounded functional annihilating $Z$; normalize it to have [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm) one. Density of the sequence in the dual unit ball gives an index $n$ with $\|f_n-f\|<1/4$. Since $f(x_n)=0$ and $\|x_n\|\leq1$,

$$
|f_n(x_n)|\leq\|f_n-f\|<\frac14.
$$

But the hypothesis and reverse [triangle inequality](../topological-analysis.md#triangle-inequality) give

$$
|f_n(x_n)|\geq\frac12\|f_n\|>\frac12\left(1-\frac14\right)=\frac38,
$$

a contradiction. Therefore **the span is dense**.

If $X'$ is [separable](../topology.md#separable-topological-space), its unit ball has a countable [dense set](../topology.md#dense-set). For each nonzero $f_n$, the definition of its [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm) permits choosing $x_n$ in the unit ball with $|f_n(x_n)|\geq\|f_n\|/2$; choose zero for a zero functional. The preceding result gives a dense countable span over $\mathbb Q$, or over $\mathbb Q+i\mathbb Q$ in the complex case. Thus **$X$ is [separable](../topology.md#separable-topological-space)**.

The converse fails: $\ell^1$ is [separable](../topology.md#separable-topological-space), since finite rational-coordinate sequences are dense, but $(\ell^1)'\cong\ell^\infty$ is not [separable](../topology.md#separable-topological-space). The dual identification sends a bounded sequence $a$ to $x\mapsto\sum_na_nx_n$, isometrically; conversely a bounded functional is determined by its values on the [coordinate vectors](../vector-space.md#coordinate-vector) and hence has this form by truncation. The uncountable family of zero-one sequences in $\ell^\infty$ has pairwise [distance](../geometry-and-topology.md#distance) one, which no countable [dense set](../topology.md#dense-set) can approximate within a radius smaller than $1/2$.

↑ **Parent:** [2011](2011.md)

<h3 id="3/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

<h5 id="3/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#3/a/i)

For a [bounded linear operator](../topological-vector-space.md#continuous-linear-operator) on a complex [Banach space](../banach-space.md), its [spectrum](../linear-operator-theory.md#spectrum-functional-analysis) is

$$
\boxed{\sigma(T)=\{\lambda\in\mathbb C:\lambda I-T
\text{ has no bounded two-sided inverse}\}.}
$$

The complement is the [resolvent set](../functional-analysis.md#resolvent-set-of-an-operator). For a real space, one may use its complexification when speaking of the complex [spectrum](../linear-operator-theory.md#spectrum-functional-analysis); the algebraic arguments below apply over the underlying scalar field as well.

<h4 id="3/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

<h5 id="3/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#3/a/ii)

The bounded operators on a [Banach space](../banach-space.md) form a [Banach algebra](../banach-algebra.md) in the [operator norm](../continuous-dual-space.md#operator-norm). If $\|T\|<1$, the [Neumann series](../banach-algebra.md#neumann-series) $A=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}T^k$ converges absolutely in that [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm). Its [partial sums](../sequence-and-series.md#partial-sum) satisfy

$$
(I-T)\sum_{k=0}^NT^k=I-T^{N+1}
=\left(\sum_{k=0}^NT^k\right)(I-T).
$$

Taking limits proves $\boxed{(I-T)^{-1}=\sum_{k\geq0}T^k}$.

If $|\lambda|>\|T\|$, factor $\lambda I-T=\lambda(I-T/\lambda)$ and apply the same argument. Thus such $\lambda$ are in the [resolvent set](../functional-analysis.md#resolvent-set-of-an-operator) and

$$
\boxed{\sigma(T)\subseteq\{\lambda:|\lambda|\leq\|T\|\}.}
$$

<h4 id="3/a/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

<h5 id="3/a/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#3/a/iii)

If $T$ has a bounded inverse, it is onto and $\|x\|\leq\|T^{-1}\|\,\|Tx\|$, giving the claimed lower bound. Conversely a positive lower bound makes $T$ injective. Together with surjectivity it gives an everywhere-defined linear inverse with [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm) at most $1/\varepsilon$. Hence it is bounded, proving the equivalence.

If $R,S$ are individually invertible, their product has inverse $S^{-1}R^{-1}$. Conversely assume $RS=SR$ and put $A=RS$, with $A$ invertible. Both factors commute with $A$ and therefore with $A^{-1}$. Direct multiplication gives

$$
R(SA^{-1})=I=(SA^{-1})R,qquad
S(RA^{-1})=I=(RA^{-1})S.
$$

These are bounded two-sided inverses, so **an invertible commuting product has both factors invertible**. The commutation assumption is used when verifying the second side of each inverse.

<h4 id="3/a/iv">iv</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

<h5 id="3/a/iv/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#3/a/iv)

The factorization

$$
T^n-\lambda^nI=(T-\lambda I)
\left(T^{n-1}+\lambda T^{n-2}+\cdots+\lambda^{n-1}I\right)
$$

has commuting factors because both are polynomials in $T$. If $\lambda^n\notin\sigma(T^n)$, their product is invertible. The preceding part would then make $T-\lambda I$ invertible, contradicting $\lambda\in\sigma(T)$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\lambda\in\sigma(T)\Longrightarrow\lambda^n\in\sigma(T^n).}
$$

This proves the required inclusion without assuming the full [spectral mapping theorem](../mathematics.md#spectral-mapping-theorem).

<h3 id="3/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

Linearity and $\|Tf\|_\infty\leq2\pi\|f\|_\infty$ show that the [integral](../integral.md) defines a [bounded linear operator](../topological-vector-space.md#continuous-linear-operator). Inductively,

$$
\boxed{|T^kf(t)|\leq\frac{t^k}{k!}\|f\|_\infty\qquad(0\leq t\leq2\pi).}
$$

For $k=0$ this is immediate. If it holds for $k$, then $|\sin s|\leq1$ gives $|T^{k+1}f(t)|\leq\int_0^t s^k\|f\|_\infty/k!\,ds$, which proves the next case. Thus $\|T^k\|\leq(2\pi)^k/k!$.

For every $\lambda\ne0$, the [series](../sequence-and-series.md#series-mathematics) $A_\lambda=\sum_{k\geq0}\lambda^{-k-1}T^k$ converges in [operator norm](../continuous-dual-space.md#operator-norm), dominated by $|\lambda|^{-1}e^{2\pi/|\lambda|}$. Multiplying its [partial sums](../sequence-and-series.md#partial-sum) by $\lambda I-T$ on either side gives the identity minus a remainder $\lambda^{-N-1}T^{N+1}$, which tends to zero by the factorial estimate. Therefore $A_\lambda$ is the bounded inverse of $\lambda I-T$.

Finally every function in the [image](../set-theory.md#image-of-a-function) of $T$ vanishes at zero, so $T$ is not onto the [continuous functions](../calculus.md#continuous-function). Hence $0$ belongs to its [spectrum](../linear-operator-theory.md#spectrum-functional-analysis) and

$$
\boxed{\sigma(T)=\{0\}.}
$$

↑ **Parent:** [2011](2011.md)

<h3 id="4/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#4)

<h4 id="4/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#4/a)

The graph $G(T)=\{(x,Tx):x\in X\}$ is closed if it is a closed subset of $X\times Y$. Equivalently, $x_n\to x$ and $Tx_n\to y$ imply $Tx=y$.

The [closed graph theorem](../functional-analysis.md#closed-graph-theorem) states that **an everywhere-defined [linear map](../vector-space.md#linear-map) between [Banach spaces](../banach-space.md) is bounded if and only if its graph is closed**. Completeness of both spaces is part of the theorem. A bounded map has a closed graph by continuity.

Conversely, if the graph is closed, it is a [Banach space](../banach-space.md) in the product [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm) $\|(x,y)\|=\|x\|+\|y\|$. The coordinate projection $P:G(T)\to X$, $P(x,Tx)=x$, is a bounded linear bijection. The [open mapping theorem](../functional-analysis.md#open-mapping-theorem-functional-analysis) makes $P^{-1}$ bounded. The other coordinate projection $Q:G(T)\to Y$ is bounded, and $T=QP^{-1}$ is therefore bounded. This proves the theorem from the permitted result.

<h3 id="4/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#4)

<h4 id="4/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#4/b)

<h5 id="4/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#4/b/i)

For scalars $a,b$ and $f,h\in X$, linearity of the [Fourier coefficients](../fourier-series.md#fourier-coefficient) gives

$$
\widehat{aTf+bTh}(n)=\alpha_n\bigl(a\widehat f(n)+b\widehat h(n)\bigr)
=\alpha_n\widehat{af+bh}(n).
$$

The assumed uniqueness of the function with these coefficients implies $T(af+bh)=aTf+bTh$. Thus **$T$ is linear**.

<h4 id="4/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#4/b)

<h5 id="4/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#4/b/ii)

For each fixed $n$, the coefficient functional is bounded since

$$
|\widehat f(n)|\leq\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}|f(t)|\,dt
\leq\sqrt{2\pi}\|f\|_\infty.
$$

Suppose $f_j\to f$ and $Tf_j\to g$ uniformly. Taking coefficients and passing to the limit gives

$$
\widehat g(n)=\lim_j\widehat{Tf_j}(n)
=\alpha_n\lim_j\widehat f_j(n)=\alpha_n\widehat f(n)
$$

for every fixed $n$. No uniform bound on $\alpha_n$ is needed for this passage. Uniqueness gives $g=Tf$, so $T$ has a closed graph. The space $X$ is Banach, being the closed subspace defined by $f(-\pi)=f(\pi)$ inside the [continuous functions](../calculus.md#continuous-function) with the uniform [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm). The [closed graph theorem](../functional-analysis.md#closed-graph-theorem) therefore proves **$T$ is bounded**.

<h4 id="4/b/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#4/b)

<h5 id="4/b/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#4/b/iii)

The normalization gives

$$
\widehat{e_n}(m)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{i(n-m)t}\,dt=\delta_{nm}.
$$

Thus $\alpha_ne_n$ has exactly the coefficients prescribed for $Te_n$. Uniqueness yields

$$
\boxed{Te_n=\alpha_ne_n\quad(n\in\mathbb Z).}
$$

These are [eigenvectors](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvector), including the zero-[eigenvalue](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) cases.

<h4 id="4/b/iv">iv</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#4/b)

<h5 id="4/b/iv/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#4/b/iv)

Since every $e_n$ is nonzero, boundedness gives

$$
|\alpha_n|\,\|e_n\|_\infty=\|Te_n\|_\infty
\leq\|T\|\,\|e_n\|_\infty.
$$

Cancel its [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm) to obtain $\boxed{\sup_{n\in\mathbb Z}|\alpha_n|\leq\|T\|}$. Thus **the multiplier sequence is bounded**.

<h4 id="4/b/v">v</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#4/b)

<h5 id="4/b/v/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [V](#4/b/v)

Evaluation at zero is a [bounded linear functional](../topological-vector-space.md#continuous-linear-functional) for the uniform [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm). Define

$$
\boxed{\phi(f)=\sqrt{2\pi}\,(Tf)(0).}
$$

It is linear and satisfies $|\phi(f)|\leq\sqrt{2\pi}\|T\|\|f\|_\infty$. Since $e_n(0)=(2\pi)^{-1/2}$,

$$
\phi(e_n)=\sqrt{2\pi}\,\alpha_ne_n(0)=\alpha_n.
$$

The factor $\sqrt{2\pi}$ is required by the source's basis normalization.

<h3 id="5/a">a</h3>

<h4 id="5/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#5/a)

The [orthogonal complement](../hilbert-space.md#orthogonal-complement) is

$$
\boxed{Y^\perp=\{w\in X:\langle w,y\rangle=0\text{ for every }y\in Y\}.}
$$

It is a [linear subspace](../vector-space.md#vector-subspace) and is closed, since it is an intersection of [kernels](../kernel-of-a-linear-map.md) of the continuous [linear functionals](../dual-space.md#linear-functional) $w\mapsto\langle w,y\rangle$. Continuity follows from the [Cauchy-Schwarz inequality](../probability-and-statistics.md#cauchy-schwarz-inequality).

The minimizing point and orthogonality proved below write every $x$ as $x=y_0+(x-y_0)\in Y+Y^\perp$. If $w\in Y\cap Y^\perp$, then $\|w\|^2=\langle w,w\rangle=0$, so the intersection is zero. Consequently

$$
\boxed{X=Y\oplus Y^\perp.}
$$

In particular the minimizing point is unique: an alternative orthogonal decomposition would have a difference in this zero intersection.

<h4 id="5/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#5/a)

<h5 id="5/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#5/a/i)

Let $d=\inf_{y\in Y}\|x-y\|$ and choose $y_n\in Y$ with $\|x-y_n\|\to d$. The [parallelogram law](../quadratic-form.md#parallelogram-law) gives

$$
\|y_n-y_m\|^2
=2\|x-y_n\|^2+2\|x-y_m\|^2
-4\left\|x-\frac{y_n+y_m}{2}\right\|^2
\leq2\|x-y_n\|^2+2\|x-y_m\|^2-4d^2.
$$

The right side tends to zero. Thus $(y_n)$ is Cauchy. The closed subspace $Y$ of a [Hilbert space](../hilbert-space.md) is complete, so $y_n\to y_0\in Y$. Continuity of the [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm) gives $\|x-y_0\|=d$, proving **the [distance](../geometry-and-topology.md#distance) is attained**.

<h4 id="5/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#5/a)

<h5 id="5/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#5/a/ii)

Put $w=x-y_0$. For any $y\in Y$ and any real $t$, minimality gives

$$
\|w-ty\|^2-\|w\|^2=-2t\operatorname{Re}\langle w,y\rangle+t^2\|y\|^2\geq0.
$$

The derivative at zero must vanish, so $\operatorname{Re}\langle w,y\rangle=0$. In a complex [Hilbert space](../hilbert-space.md), apply the same argument to $iy$ to get the imaginary part zero as well. In the real case the first conclusion is already enough. Hence

$$
\boxed{x-y_0\in Y^\perp.}
$$

<h3 id="5/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#5)

<h4 id="5/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#5/b)

For $h,g\in Y$, their product $hg$ is holomorphic and integrable on the disk by the [Cauchy-Schwarz inequality](../probability-and-statistics.md#cauchy-schwarz-inequality). Its angular mean on every centered circle is $h(0)g(0)$ by the [mean value property](../partial-differential-equation.md#mean-value-property-for-harmonic-functions). Polar integration therefore gives

$$
\langle h,\overline g\rangle=\int_Dh(z)g(z)\,dA(z)
=\pi h(0)g(0).
$$

If $g(0)=0$, this vanishes for every $h\in Y$, proving $\boxed{Z\subseteq Y^\perp}$. Integrability of the product justifies integration over the full disk, even when the individual [holomorphic functions](../holomorphic-function.md) grow near its boundary.

The inclusion is **strict**. Set $w(z)=|z|^2-1/2$, a nonzero bounded function in $L^2(D)$. For every $h\in Y$,

$$
\langle w,h\rangle
=2\pi\overline{h(0)}\int_0^1(r^2-1/2)r\,dr=0.
$$

Thus $w\in Y^\perp$. It cannot equal $\overline g$ for holomorphic $g$: equality almost everywhere between these [continuous functions](../calculus.md#continuous-function) would give equality everywhere, making $g=w$ a real-valued nonconstant [holomorphic function](../holomorphic-function.md), which is impossible. Therefore

$$
\boxed{Z\ne Y^\perp.}
$$

## 6

↑ **Parent:** [2011](2011.md)

<h3 id="6/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#6)

<h4 id="6/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#6/a)

Use the convention that the [inner product](../inner-product.md) is linear in its first argument. The precise [polarization identity](../quadratic-form.md#polarization-identity) is

$$
\boxed{\langle x,y\rangle=\frac14\left(
\|x+y\|^2-\|x-y\|^2+i\|x+iy\|^2-i\|x-iy\|^2\right).}
$$

If (1) holds, apply this formula before and after $U$ to get preservation of all [inner products](../inner-product.md), proving (2). If (2) holds, the identity $\langle Ux,Uy\rangle=\langle x,U^*Uy\rangle$ gives $U^*U=I$. For $z=Ux$, surjectivity then gives $UU^*z=UU^*Ux=Ux=z$, proving (3).

From (3), $\|Ux\|^2=\langle x,U^*Ux\rangle=\|x\|^2$ and likewise $\|U^*x\|=\|x\|$, proving (4). Finally, if (4) holds, apply the same [polarization identity](../quadratic-form.md#polarization-identity) to $U^*$ to obtain $UU^*=I$. This makes $U$ onto; the other [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm) identity supplies the remaining requirement of (1). Thus

$$
\boxed{(1)\iff(2)\iff(3)\iff(4).}
$$

These four statements are conditions in a single equivalence proof, rather than four independent source questions.

<h3 id="6/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#6)

<h4 id="6/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#6/b)

The four claims below show that $I+T$ has a bounded inverse and that the [Cayley transform of a bounded accretive operator](../vector-space.md#cayley-transform-of-a-bounded-accretive-operator) is contractive. For an arbitrary $z\in X$, write $z=(I+T)x$. Then

$$
\|Sz\|=\|(I-T)x\|\leq\|(I+T)x\|=\|z\|.
$$

Taking the supremum over the unit ball gives

$$
\boxed{S=(I-T)(I+T)^{-1},\qquad\|S\|\leq1.}
$$

<h4 id="6/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#6/b)

<h5 id="6/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#6/b/i)

Expanding both squared [norms](../normed-vector-space.md#norm) gives

$$
\|(I+T)x\|^2-\|(I-T)x\|^2
=4\operatorname{Re}\langle Tx,x\rangle\geq0.
$$

Both [norms](../normed-vector-space.md#norm) are nonnegative, so taking square roots proves $\boxed{\|(I+T)x\|\geq\|(I-T)x\|}$.

<h4 id="6/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#6/b)

<h5 id="6/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#6/b/ii)

A separate expansion gives

$$
\|(I+T)x\|^2=\|x\|^2+\|Tx\|^2+2\operatorname{Re}\langle Tx,x\rangle
\geq\|x\|^2.
$$

Hence $\boxed{\|(I+T)x\|\geq\|x\|}$. In particular $I+T$ is injective and bounded below by one.

<h4 id="6/b/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#6/b)

<h5 id="6/b/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#6/b/iii)

By the definition of the [adjoint operator](../hilbert-space.md#adjoint-operator) and conjugate symmetry,

$$
\langle T^*x,x\rangle=\langle x,Tx\rangle
=\overline{\langle Tx,x\rangle}.
$$

Taking real parts proves

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Re}\langle T^*x,x\rangle
=\operatorname{Re}\langle Tx,x\rangle\geq0.}
$$

<h4 id="6/b/iv">iv</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#6/b)

<h5 id="6/b/iv/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#6/b/iv)

The lower bound in (ii) makes the [image](../set-theory.md#image-of-a-function) of $I+T$ closed: if $(I+T)x_n$ converges, the bound makes $(x_n)$ Cauchy; completeness gives its limit and hence a [image](../set-theory.md#image-of-a-function) preimage. The same bound applies to $I+T^*$ by (iii), so its [kernel](../kernel-of-a-linear-map.md) is zero. The [orthogonal complement](../hilbert-space.md#orthogonal-complement) identity

$$
\operatorname{ran}(I+T)^\perp=\ker(I+T^*)=\{0\}
$$

shows that the [image](../set-theory.md#image-of-a-function) is dense, using the [orthogonal decomposition by a closed subspace](../hilbert-space.md#orthogonal-decomposition-by-a-closed-subspace) for its [closure](../topology.md#closure-topology). Being both closed and dense, it equals $X$. Thus $I+T$ is onto and bounded below, and by part 3(a)(iii) it has a bounded inverse. Moreover

$$
\boxed{\|(I+T)^{-1}\|\leq1.}
$$

<h3 id="6/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#6)

<h4 id="6/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#6/c)

If the real part vanishes identically, the [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm)-difference identity becomes equality, so

$$
\|(I-T)x\|=\|(I+T)x\|\quad\text{for all }x.
$$

Thus the same substitution as above gives $\|Sz\|=\|z\|$ for every $z$. To prove surjectivity as well, observe that both $T$ and $-T$ satisfy the nonnegative-real-part hypothesis. The preceding part makes both $I+T$ and $I-T$ invertible. Their product $S=(I-T)(I+T)^{-1}$ is therefore invertible and onto. It is a surjective [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm)-preserving [linear operator](../vector-space.md#linear-operator), so the equivalences in (a) prove

$$
\boxed{S\text{ is unitary}.}
$$
