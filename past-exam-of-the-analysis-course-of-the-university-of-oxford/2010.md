<h4 id="a/1/a">a</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#a/1)

<h5 id="a/1/a/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a/1/a)

For $\alpha,\beta\in\ell_{1,2}$ and real scalars $c,d$, the scalar [triangle inequality](../topological-analysis.md#triangle-inequality) gives

$$
\sum_{j\geq1}j^2|c\alpha_j+d\beta_j|\leq|c|\sum_jj^2|\alpha_j|+|d|\sum_jj^2|\beta_j|<\infty.
$$

The zero sequence belongs to the space, so it is a real [vector space](../vector-space.md). The weighted sum is nonnegative and homogeneous, and the same inequality with $c=d=1$ proves its [triangle inequality](../topological-analysis.md#triangle-inequality). Since every weight $j^2$ is strictly positive, zero weighted sum forces every coordinate to vanish. Therefore **$\|\cdot\|_{1,2}$ is a [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm)**.

<h4 id="a/1/b">b</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#a/1)

<h5 id="a/1/b/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#a/1/b)

Define $U:\ell_{1,2}\to\ell_1$ by $(U\alpha)_j=j^2\alpha_j$. This is a linear bijection, with inverse $(U^{-1}\gamma)_j=j^{-2}\gamma_j$, and

$$
\boxed{\|U\alpha\|_1=\|\alpha\|_{1,2}.}
$$

Thus $U$ is an [isometric isomorphism of normed spaces](../normed-vector-space.md#isometric-isomorphism-of-normed-spaces) onto the [absolutely summable sequence space](../banach-space.md#absolutely-summable-sequence-space), which is a [Banach space](../banach-space.md). Explicitly, a [Cauchy sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#cauchy-sequence) $\alpha^{(n)}$ maps to a [Cauchy sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#cauchy-sequence) $U\alpha^{(n)}\to\gamma\in\ell_1$; setting $\alpha=U^{-1}\gamma$ gives [convergence](../calculus.md#convergence) in the weighted [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm). Hence **$\ell_{1,2}$ is a [Banach space](../banach-space.md)**, an instance of [weighted summable sequence space isometry](../banach-space.md#weighted-summable-sequence-space-isometry).

<h4 id="a/1/c">c</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#a/1)

<h5 id="a/1/c/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#a/1/c)

Let $D$ be the set of [finitely supported sequences](../banach-space.md#finitely-supported-sequence) with rational coordinates. It is countable: it is the union over $N$ of copies of $\mathbb Q^N$. Given $\alpha\in\ell_{1,2}$ and $\varepsilon>0$, choose $N$ with $\sum_{j>N}j^2|\alpha_j|<\varepsilon/2$. Choose rational numbers $q_j$ for $1\leq j\leq N$ so that $\sum_{j=1}^Nj^2|q_j-\alpha_j|<\varepsilon/2$, and put $q_j=0$ thereafter. Then $q\in D$ and $\|q-\alpha\|_{1,2}<\varepsilon$. Thus **$D$ is countable and dense, so the space is [separable](../topology.md#separable-topological-space)**.

<h4 id="a/1/d">d</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#a/1)

<h5 id="a/1/d/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#a/1/d)

For the first [linear map](../vector-space.md#linear-map), take $(\alpha,\beta)=(0,e_n)$. Its input [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm) is one, while $T_1(0,e_n)=(e_n,0)$ has [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm) $n^2$. Thus **$T_1$ is unbounded**.

For the second map, since $j^2\geq1$,

$$
\|T_2(\alpha,\beta)\|_X=\|\alpha+\beta\|_1\leq\|\alpha\|_1+\|\beta\|_1\leq\|\alpha\|_{1,2}+\|\beta\|_1=\|(\alpha,\beta)\|_X.
$$

Equality holds at $(0,e_1)$, so

$$
\boxed{T_2\text{ is bounded and }\|T_2\|=1.}
$$

Both formulas map $c_{00}\times c_{00}$ into itself because adding two [finitely supported sequences](../banach-space.md#finitely-supported-sequence) preserves finite support.

<h3 id="a/2">2</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a)

<h4 id="a/2/a">a</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#a/2)

<h5 id="a/2/a/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a/2/a)

Equip the [continuous dual space](../continuous-dual-space.md) $X'$ with the [operator norm](../continuous-dual-space.md#operator-norm)

$$
\|f\|=\sup_{\|x\|\leq1}|f(x)|.
$$

[Continuity](../calculus.md#continuous-function) of a [linear functional](../dual-space.md#linear-functional) makes this finite; homogeneity, definiteness and the [triangle inequality](../topological-analysis.md#triangle-inequality) follow directly from the supremum formula.

For [completeness of the dual space](../continuous-dual-space.md#completeness-of-the-dual-space), let $(f_n)$ be Cauchy in this [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm). For each $x\in X$, $|f_n(x)-f_m(x)|\leq\|f_n-f_m\|\|x\|$, so $(f_n(x))$ is Cauchy in $\mathbb R$. Define $f(x)=\lim_nf_n(x)$. Passing to the limit in the linearity identities makes $f$ linear. Since a [Cauchy sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#cauchy-sequence) is bounded, $\sup_n\|f_n\|\leq C$ and $|f(x)|\leq C\|x\|$, so $f\in X'$.

Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose $N$ with $\|f_n-f_m\|\leq\varepsilon$ for $n,m\geq N$. Let $m\to\infty$ [pointwise](../sequence-and-series.md#pointwise-convergence); then $|f_n(x)-f(x)|\leq\varepsilon\|x\|$ and therefore $\|f_n-f\|\leq\varepsilon$. Thus **$X'$ is a [Banach space](../banach-space.md)**. This proof in fact works even if $X$ itself is not complete.

<h4 id="a/2/b">b</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#a/2)

<h5 id="a/2/b/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#a/2/b)

If a [bilinear map](../multilinear-algebra.md#bilinear-map) is continuous, [continuity](../calculus.md#continuous-function) at $(0,0)$ gives $\delta>0$ such that $\|F(x,y)\|\leq1$ whenever $\|x\|,\|y\|<\delta$. For nonzero $x,y$, apply this to $\delta x/(2\|x\|)$ and $\delta y/(2\|y\|)$. Bilinearity gives

$$
\boxed{\|F(x,y)\|\leq\frac4{\delta^2}\|x\|\|y\|.}
$$

If either input is zero, the inequality follows from bilinearity directly.

Conversely, suppose the product bound holds with $C\geq0$. At a fixed point $(x,y)$,

$$
F(x+h,y+k)-F(x,y)=F(h,y)+F(x,k)+F(h,k).
$$

Its [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm) is at most $C(\|h\|\|y\|+\|x\|\|k\|+\|h\|\|k\|)$, which tends to zero as $(h,k)\to(0,0)$. Thus **the product bound is equivalent to joint [continuity](../calculus.md#continuous-function)**. This is [boundedness criterion for a bilinear map](../multilinear-algebra.md#boundedness-criterion-for-a-bilinear-map).

<h4 id="a/2/c">c</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#a/2)

<h5 id="a/2/c/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#a/2/c)

Put $\rho=r-\sqrt{r^2-1}$. Since $r>1$, $0<\rho<1$ and $\rho^2-2r\rho+1=0$. The nonzero square-summable sequence $\beta_j=\rho^{j-1}$ defines a [continuous linear functional](../topological-vector-space.md#continuous-linear-functional) on $\ell_2$ by

$$
f(\alpha)=\sum_{j\geq1}\alpha_j\rho^{j-1}.
$$

The [Cauchy-Schwarz inequality](../probability-and-statistics.md#cauchy-schwarz-inequality) proves [continuity](../calculus.md#continuous-function). For every $k$,

$$
f(x_k)=\rho^{k-1}(1-2r\rho+\rho^2)=0.
$$

Hence $f$ vanishes on the [closed linear span](../vector-space.md#closed-linear-span) $M$, but $f(e_1)=1$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{M\ne\ell_2.}
$$

More precisely, any vector in the [orthogonal complement](../hilbert-space.md#orthogonal-complement) of $M$ satisfies the recurrence $\beta_{k+2}-2r\beta_{k+1}+\beta_k=0$. Its roots are $r\pm\sqrt{r^2-1}$, one less than one and the other greater than one. Square summability eliminates the growing solution, so $M^\perp$ is exactly the span of $(\rho^{j-1})$. Thus $M=\ker f$ is a closed hyperplane of codimension one. This is [annihilator of a second-difference span in l2](../vector-space.md#annihilator-of-a-second-difference-span-in-l2).

<h3 id="a/3">3</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a)

<h4 id="a/3/a">a</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#a/3)

<h5 id="a/3/a/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a/3/a)

The [spectrum of a bounded operator](../linear-operator-theory.md#spectrum-of-a-bounded-operator) is $\sigma(T)=\{\lambda\in\mathbb C:T-\lambda I\text{ has no bounded inverse}\}$. The [point spectrum](../linear-operator-theory.md#point-spectrum) consists of those $\lambda$ for which $\ker(T-\lambda I)\ne0$, namely the [eigenvalues](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue). The [spectral radius](../analysis.md#spectral-radius) is $r(T)=\sup_{\lambda\in\sigma(T)}|\lambda|$.

If $\|T\|<1$, the [Neumann series](../banach-algebra.md#neumann-series) $S=\sum_{n=0}^\infty(-T)^n$ converges in the [Banach space of bounded linear operators](../topological-vector-space.md#banach-space-of-bounded-linear-operators), since its term norms are bounded by a convergent [geometric series](../sequence-and-series.md#geometric-series). Multiplying the finite sums on either side by $I+T$ gives $I-(-T)^{N+1}$, which tends in [operator norm](../continuous-dual-space.md#operator-norm) to $I$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{(I+T)^{-1}=\sum_{n=0}^\infty(-T)^n.}
$$

<h4 id="a/3/b">b</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#a/3)

<h5 id="a/3/b/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#a/3/b)

If $|\lambda|>\|T\|$, write $T-\lambda I=-\lambda(I-T/\lambda)$. The [Neumann series](../banach-algebra.md#neumann-series) applies to $T/\lambda$, so $\lambda$ is outside the [spectrum of a bounded operator](../linear-operator-theory.md#spectrum-of-a-bounded-operator). Thus $\sigma(T)\subseteq\{\lambda:|\lambda|\leq\|T\|\}$ and $r(T)\leq\|T\|$.

The polynomial [spectral mapping theorem](../mathematics.md#spectral-mapping-theorem) gives $\sigma(T^m)=\{\lambda^m:\lambda\in\sigma(T)\}$, and hence

$$
r(T)^m=r(T^m)\leq\|T^m\|.
$$

Taking $m$th roots and then the limit, whose existence is allowed, proves

$$
\boxed{r(T)\leq\lim_{m\to\infty}\|T^m\|^{1/m}.}
$$

Only this inequality is required; the reverse inequality is the additional content of the full [spectral radius formula](../analysis.md#spectral-radius-formula).

<h4 id="a/3/c">c</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#a/3)

<h5 id="a/3/c/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#a/3/c)

For the [multiplication operator](../vector-space.md#multiplication-operator), $\|Tf\|_\infty\leq\|g\|_\infty\|f\|_\infty=\|f\|_\infty$. Taking $f=1$ gives equality, so $\|T\|=1$.

An [eigenfunction](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenfunction) obeys $(g(t)-\lambda)f(t)=0$ at every $t$. If $\lambda\in[0,1)$, its level set is the single point $t=\lambda$, and a [continuous function](../calculus.md#continuous-function) supported at a single point is zero. If $\lambda\notin[0,1]$, that level set is empty. For $\lambda=1$, every [continuous function](../calculus.md#continuous-function) vanishing on $[0,1]$ is an [eigenfunction](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenfunction), and nonzero examples exist, such as $f(t)=\max\{t-1,0\}$. Thus $\sigma_p(T)=\{1\}$.

If $\lambda\notin[0,1]$, multiplication by $(g-\lambda)^{-1}$ is a bounded inverse of $T-\lambda I$. If $\lambda\in[0,1]$, choose $t_0$ with $g(t_0)=\lambda$. Every function in the range of $T-\lambda I$ vanishes at $t_0$, so the range omits the constant function one. Hence it is not onto. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\|T\|=1,\qquad\sigma_p(T)=\{1\},\qquad\sigma(T)=[0,1].}
$$

<h4 id="a/3/d">d</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#a/3)

<h5 id="a/3/d/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#a/3/d)

The [unilateral shift operator](../linear-operator-theory.md#unilateral-shift-operator) $R$ is not onto, since its outputs have first coordinate zero. The [left shift operator](../linear-operator-theory.md#left-shift-operator) $L$ is not injective, since $Le_1=0$. Both have [operator norm](../continuous-dual-space.md#operator-norm) one, and $LR=I$. Consequently

$$
L(R+A)=LR+LA=I+LA.
$$

If $\|A\|<1$, then $\|LA\|<1$, so the [Neumann series](../banach-algebra.md#neumann-series) makes $I+LA$ invertible. If $R+A$ were invertible too, the identity above would give

$$
L=(I+LA)(R+A)^{-1},
$$

a product of invertible operators, contradicting the nontrivial [kernel](../kernel-of-a-linear-map.md) of $L$. Thus

$$
\boxed{\|A\|<1\Longrightarrow R+A\text{ is not invertible}.}
$$

This is [norm stability of noninvertibility for the unilateral right shift](../linear-operator-theory.md#norm-stability-of-noninvertibility-for-the-unilateral-right-shift).

<h2 id="b">B</h2>

↑ **Parent:** [2010](2010.md)

<h3 id="b/4">4</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b)

<h4 id="b/4/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#b/4)

The [Baire category theorem](../topological-analysis.md#baire-category-theorem) states that a nonempty complete [metric space](../topological-analysis.md#metric-space) cannot be a countable union of closed [nowhere dense sets](../topological-analysis.md#nowhere-dense-set). The [Uniform boundedness principle](../banach-space.md#uniform-boundedness-principle) says that if $V$ is a [Banach space](../banach-space.md), $W$ a [normed vector space](../normed-vector-space.md), and $\mathcal T\subseteq B(V,W)$ is [pointwise](../sequence-and-series.md#pointwise-convergence) bounded, meaning $\sup_{T\in\mathcal T}\|Tx\|<\infty$ for every $x\in V$, then $\sup_{T\in\mathcal T}\|T\|<\infty$.

To prove it, let $E_n=\{x:\sup_{T\in\mathcal T}\|Tx\|\leq n\}$. Each set is closed by [continuity](../calculus.md#continuous-function) of the individual operators, and their union is $V$. The [Baire category theorem](../topological-analysis.md#baire-category-theorem) gives a ball $B(x_0,r)$ inside some $E_N$. For $\|h\|<r$, both $x_0+h$ and $x_0$ are in $E_N$, so $\|Th\|\leq\|T(x_0+h)\|+\|Tx_0\|\leq2N$ for every $T$. Scaling gives $\|T\|\leq2N/r$ uniformly. The zero-space and empty-family cases are immediate.

For the first family on a [Hilbert space](../hilbert-space.md), fix $x$. The functionals $y\mapsto\langle y,Tx\rangle$ have [operator norm](../continuous-dual-space.md#operator-norm) $\|Tx\|$ by the [Cauchy-Schwarz inequality](../probability-and-statistics.md#cauchy-schwarz-inequality) and equality on the direction of $Tx$. Their values are [pointwise](../sequence-and-series.md#pointwise-convergence) bounded by the hypothesis, after taking complex conjugates if necessary. Apply the [Uniform boundedness principle](../banach-space.md#uniform-boundedness-principle) to obtain $\sup_{T\in\mathcal E}\|Tx\|<\infty$. Apply it a second time to the operator family itself to conclude **$\sup_{T\in\mathcal E}\|T\|<\infty$**.

For the diagonal hypothesis, the answer depends on the scalar [field](../algebra.md#field). Over $\mathbb C$, let $q_T(x)=\langle Tx,x\rangle$, using the convention that the [inner product](../inner-product.md) is linear in its first variable. The complex [polarization identity](../quadratic-form.md#polarization-identity) gives

$$
4\langle Tx,y\rangle=q_T(x+y)-q_T(x-y)+i q_T(x+iy)-i q_T(x-iy).
$$

The assumed bounds at these four fixed vectors make every matrix coefficient uniformly bounded, so the preceding argument applies. Thus **the complex-space answer is yes**, even without assuming self-adjointness.

Over $\mathbb R$, take $X=\mathbb R^2$ and

$$
J=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathcal F=\{nJ:n\geq1\}.
$$

Every diagonal value $\langle nJx,x\rangle$ is zero, whereas $\|nJ\|=n$. Thus **the real-space answer is no in general**. Real diagonal values do not detect the skew-symmetric part of an operator. This is [diagonal boundedness of operator families over real and complex Hilbert spaces](../banach-space.md#diagonal-boundedness-of-operator-families-over-real-and-complex-hilbert-spaces).

<h3 id="b/5">5</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b)

<h4 id="b/5/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#b/5)

<h5 id="b/5/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#b/5/i)

A [bounded linear operator](../topological-vector-space.md#continuous-linear-operator) on a complex [Hilbert space](../hilbert-space.md) is [self-adjoint](../linear-operator-theory.md#self-adjoint-operator) if $T=T^*$, equivalently $\langle Tx,y\rangle=\langle x,Ty\rangle$ for every $x,y$.

If $Tx=0$, then $\langle x,Ty\rangle=\langle Tx,y\rangle=0$ for all $y$, so $x\in(\operatorname{Ran}T)^\perp$. Conversely, membership in that [orthogonal complement](../hilbert-space.md#orthogonal-complement) gives $\langle Tx,y\rangle=0$ for every $y$, and choosing $y=Tx$ yields $Tx=0$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\ker T=(\operatorname{Ran}T)^\perp.}
$$

<h4 id="b/5/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#b/5)

<h5 id="b/5/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#b/5/ii)

Self-adjointness and conjugate symmetry of the [inner product](../inner-product.md) give

$$
\langle Tx,x\rangle=\langle x,Tx\rangle=\overline{\langle Tx,x\rangle}.
$$

A complex number equal to its conjugate is real, so **$\langle Tx,x\rangle\in\mathbb R$** for every $x$.

<h4 id="b/5/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#b/5)

<h5 id="b/5/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#b/5/iii)

For a [positive operator](../hilbert-space.md#positive-operator), $\langle Tx,x\rangle\geq0$. We show directly that every nonreal or negative real $\lambda$ lies in the [resolvent set](../functional-analysis.md#resolvent-set-of-an-operator).

If $\operatorname{Im}\lambda\ne0$, the [Cauchy-Schwarz inequality](../probability-and-statistics.md#cauchy-schwarz-inequality) and reality of $\langle Tx,x\rangle$ give

$$
\|(T-\lambda I)x\|\|x\|\geq|\langle(T-\lambda I)x,x\rangle|\geq|\operatorname{Im}\lambda|\|x\|^2.
$$

Thus $T-\lambda I$ is bounded below, hence injective with closed range: a convergent sequence of outputs has Cauchy preimages, whose limit maps to that output. Its adjoint is $T-\bar\lambda I$, which has trivial [kernel](../kernel-of-a-linear-map.md) by the same estimate. The identity $\operatorname{Ran}(A)^\perp=\ker A^*$, proved by the same inner-product calculation as in (i), makes its range dense. Being closed and dense, it is all of $X$, and the lower bound makes the inverse bounded.

If $\lambda<0$ is real, positivity gives $\langle(T-\lambda I)x,x\rangle\geq(-\lambda)\|x\|^2$, so the same argument applies with lower bound $-\lambda$. Here the operator is [self-adjoint](../linear-operator-theory.md#self-adjoint-operator), so its range is dense because its own [kernel](../kernel-of-a-linear-map.md) is zero. Hence

$$
\boxed{\sigma(T)\subseteq[0,\infty).}
$$

<h4 id="b/5/iv">iv</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#b/5)

<h5 id="b/5/iv/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#b/5/iv)

Set $B(x,y)=\langle Tx,y\rangle$. It is a positive semidefinite [Hermitian form](../linear-algebra.md#hermitian-form). Write $A=B(x,x)$, $C=B(y,y)$ and $c=B(x,y)$. For every complex $t$,

$$
0\leq B(x+ty,x+ty)=A+\bar t c+t\bar c+|t|^2C.
$$

If $C>0$, choosing $t=-c/C$ gives $A-|c|^2/C\geq0$. If $C=0$ and $c\ne0$, choosing $t=-sc$ for large positive $s$ makes the expression negative, a contradiction. Thus $c=0$ in that case. In all cases,

$$
\boxed{|\langle Tx,y\rangle|^2\leq\langle Tx,x\rangle\langle Ty,y\rangle.}
$$

This is the [Cauchy-Schwarz inequality for a positive operator](../hilbert-space.md#cauchy-schwarz-inequality-for-a-positive-operator).

<h4 id="b/5/v">v</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#b/5)

<h5 id="b/5/v/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [V](#b/5/v)

For $x\in\ker(S+T)$,

$$
0=\langle(S+T)x,x\rangle=\langle Sx,x\rangle+\langle Tx,x\rangle.
$$

Both terms are nonnegative because $S,T$ are [positive operators](../hilbert-space.md#positive-operator). Their sum is zero, so each vanishes. In particular,

$$
\boxed{\langle Tx,x\rangle=0.}
$$

<h4 id="b/5/vi">vi</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#b/5)

<h5 id="b/5/vi/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Vi](#b/5/vi)

By the [Cauchy-Schwarz inequality for a positive operator](../hilbert-space.md#cauchy-schwarz-inequality-for-a-positive-operator), for every $y$,

$$
|\langle Tx,y\rangle|^2\leq\langle Tx,x\rangle\langle Ty,y\rangle=0.
$$

Taking $y=Tx$ gives $Tx=0$, and $(S+T)x=0$ then gives $Sx=0$. Thus

$$
\boxed{\ker(S+T)=\ker S\cap\ker T.}
$$

The reverse inclusion is immediate. This is the [kernel of a sum of positive operators](../hilbert-space.md#kernel-of-a-sum-of-positive-operators) identity.

<h4 id="b/5/vii">vii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#b/5)

<h5 id="b/5/vii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Vii](#b/5/vii)

Commutativity and equality of squares give

$$
(S-T)(S+T)=S^2-T^2=0.
$$

Thus $S-T$ vanishes on $\operatorname{Ran}(S+T)$, and by [continuity](../calculus.md#continuous-function) on its closure. The preceding [kernel of a sum of positive operators](../hilbert-space.md#kernel-of-a-sum-of-positive-operators) identity shows that it also vanishes on $\ker(S+T)$. Since $S+T$ is [self-adjoint](../linear-operator-theory.md#self-adjoint-operator), part (i) and the [Hilbert projection theorem](../hilbert-space.md#hilbert-projection-theorem) give the [orthogonal direct sum](../vector-space.md#orthogonal-direct-sum)

$$
X=\overline{\operatorname{Ran}(S+T)}\oplus\ker(S+T).
$$

Therefore $S-T$ vanishes on all of $X$, proving

$$
\boxed{S=T.}
$$

This proves [commuting positive operators with equal squares are equal](../hilbert-space.md#commuting-positive-operators-with-equal-squares-are-equal) without assuming a square-root uniqueness theorem.

<h3 id="b/6">6</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b)

<h4 id="b/6/a">a</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#b/6)

<h5 id="b/6/a/i">i</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#b/6/a)

<h6 id="b/6/a/i/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#b/6/a/i)

For finite subsets $F\subset\mathbb Z$, orthonormality gives

$$
\left\|\sum_{n\in F}\alpha_ne_n\right\|^2=\sum_{n\in F}|\alpha_n|^2.
$$

For $S_N=\sum_{|n|\leq N}\alpha_ne_n$, the squared [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm) of $S_N-S_M$ is the corresponding finite tail sum. Since $\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}|\alpha_n|^2<\infty$, these tails tend to zero. Thus $(S_N)$ is a [Cauchy sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#cauchy-sequence) in the complete [Hilbert space](../hilbert-space.md) and converges.

The same estimate for arbitrary finite tails proves that the sum is unconditional, so **the bilateral orthogonal series converges in $X$ independently of its ordering**. This is the [Riesz-Fischer theorem for an orthonormal system](../measure-theory.md#riesz-fischer-theorem-for-an-orthonormal-system).

<h5 id="b/6/a/ii">ii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#b/6/a)

<h6 id="b/6/a/ii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#b/6/a/ii)

For $a_n=\langle x,e_n\rangle$, the finite [orthogonal projection](../hilbert-space.md#orthogonal-projection) $P_Nx=\sum_{|n|\leq N}a_ne_n$ satisfies

$$
0\leq\|x-P_Nx\|^2=\|x\|^2-\sum_{|n|\leq N}|a_n|^2.
$$

This is [Bessel's inequality](../hilbert-space.md#bessel-s-inequality), so the coefficients are square summable. The preceding result gives a limit $s=\sum_na_ne_n$. For each fixed $k$, [continuity](../calculus.md#continuous-function) of the [inner product](../inner-product.md) gives $\langle x-s,e_k\rangle=0$. The [completeness](../topological-analysis.md#completeness) assumption on the [orthonormal system](../hilbert-space.md#orthonormal-sequence) forces $x-s=0$. Hence

$$
\boxed{x=\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}\langle x,e_n\rangle e_n.}
$$

Taking squared norms also gives [Parseval's identity](../fourier-analysis.md#parseval-identity), $\|x\|^2=\sum_n|\langle x,e_n\rangle|^2$.

<h5 id="b/6/a/iii">iii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#b/6/a)

<h6 id="b/6/a/iii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#b/6/a/iii)

Apply [continuity](../calculus.md#continuous-function) of the [inner product](../inner-product.md) to the convergent expansion of $x$:

$$
\boxed{\langle x,y\rangle=\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}\langle x,e_n\rangle\langle e_n,y\rangle.}
$$

The series is absolutely convergent because the [Cauchy-Schwarz inequality](../probability-and-statistics.md#cauchy-schwarz-inequality) for scalar sequences and [Bessel's inequality](../hilbert-space.md#bessel-s-inequality) give

$$
\sum_n|\langle x,e_n\rangle\langle e_n,y\rangle|\leq\left(\sum_n|\langle x,e_n\rangle|^2\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_n|\langle y,e_n\rangle|^2\right)^{1/2}\leq\|x\|\|y\|.
$$

This is the polarized [Parseval identity](../fourier-analysis.md#parseval-identity).

<h4 id="b/6/b">b</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#b/6)

<h5 id="b/6/b/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b/6/b)

Use the [inner product](../inner-product.md) convention $\langle f,h\rangle=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f\bar h$. For $h=\mathbf1_{[-\pi/2,\pi/2]}$, integration gives

$$
\langle e_0,h\rangle=\sqrt{\pi/2},\qquad\langle e_k,h\rangle=\sqrt{2/\pi}\,\frac{\sin(k\pi/2)}k\quad(k\ne0).
$$

The nonzero even coefficients vanish, while $\sin((2n+1)\pi/2)=(-1)^n$. The preceding polarized [Parseval identity](../fourier-analysis.md#parseval-identity) therefore yields

$$
\boxed{\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}f(t)dt=\sqrt{\frac\pi2}\langle f,e_0\rangle+\sqrt{\frac2\pi}\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}(-1)^n\frac{\langle f,e_{2n+1}\rangle}{2n+1}.}
$$

Absolute [convergence](../calculus.md#convergence) follows from square summability of the [Fourier coefficients](../fourier-series.md#fourier-coefficient) and of $((2n+1)^{-1})_{n\in\mathbb Z}$.

For the convolution identity, fix any real $t$ and define $h_t(s)=\overline{g(t-s)}$. Periodicity makes $h_t\in L^2(-\pi,\pi)$ with $\|h_t\|_2=\|g\|_2$. A change of variable and integration over one full period give

$$
\langle e_n,h_t\rangle=\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{ins}g(t-s)ds=e^{int}\langle g,e_n\rangle.
$$

Applying polarized [Parseval identity](../fourier-analysis.md#parseval-identity) to $f,h_t$ gives, for every fixed $t$,

$$
\boxed{\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(s)g(t-s)ds=\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}\langle f,e_n\rangle\langle g,e_n\rangle e^{int}.}
$$

The integral exists by the [Cauchy-Schwarz inequality](../probability-and-statistics.md#cauchy-schwarz-inequality). The same inequality gives $\sum_n|\langle f,e_n\rangle\langle g,e_n\rangle|\leq\|f\|_2\|g\|_2$, so the displayed series converges absolutely and uniformly in $t$. In particular the equality holds at every $t$, without requiring [pointwise](../sequence-and-series.md#pointwise-convergence) [convergence](../calculus.md#convergence) of either original [Fourier series](../fourier-series.md). This is [Fourier convolution of two periodic L2 functions](../fourier-analysis.md#fourier-convolution-of-two-periodic-l2-functions).
