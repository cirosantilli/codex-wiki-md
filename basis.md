# Basis

↑ **Parent:** [Vector space](vector-space-split.md)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basis_(linear_algebra))

A basis is a [linearly independent](linear-independence.md) [spanning set](spanning-set.md) of [vectors](vector.md) in a [vector space](vector-space-split.md). Every [vector](vector.md) is a unique finite [linear combination](linear-combination.md) of [basis vectors](basis-vector.md). Independence supplies uniqueness, and spanning supplies existence. An [ordered basis](ordered-basis.md) turns the [coefficients](coefficient.md) into a [coordinate vector](coordinate-vector.md).

For $\mathcal B=(b_1,b_2)=((2,1),(1,2))$, the [matrix](matrix.md) with these [vectors](vector.md) as columns is

$$
B=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix},\qquad
B^{-1}=\frac13\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Hence the [coordinate vector](coordinate-vector.md) of $v=(5,4)$ is $[v]_{\mathcal B}=B^{-1}v=(2,1)$, and $B[v]_{\mathcal B}=v$ recovers the usual coordinates. The [determinant](determinant.md) $3$ is nonzero, so this recovery works for every [vector](vector.md) in $\mathbb R^2$. A [basis](basis.md) need not have perpendicular or unit-length [vectors](vector.md); an [orthonormal basis](orthonormal-basis.md) adds those geometric conveniences when an [inner product](inner-product-split.md) is available.

The point of changing [basis](basis.md) is to simplify the operation being studied. [Diagonalization](diagonalization-of-a-matrix.md) uses an [eigenbasis](eigenbasis.md) to turn a coupled [linear map](linear-map.md) into independent scalings. In a space of [polynomials](polynomial-split.md), the [basis](basis.md) $1,x,x^2$ makes [differentiation](differentiation.md) easy to encode, while [orthogonal polynomials](orthogonal-polynomial-split.md) can make approximation errors easier to measure. Gilbert Strang's MIT lecture on independence, basis and dimension develops this connection between attainable columns, redundant columns and coordinates.[Strang, MIT 18.06SC, Independence, Basis and Dimension](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-06sc-linear-algebra-fall-2011/)
