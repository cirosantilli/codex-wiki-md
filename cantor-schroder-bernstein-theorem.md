<h1 id="cantor-schroder-bernstein-theorem">Cantor-Schröder-Bernstein theorem</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [Cardinal number](cardinal-number-split.md)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cantor-Schröder-Bernstein_theorem)

If there are injections $X\to Y$ and $Y\to X$, then there is a bijection $X\cong Y$.

For arbitrary [sets](set-split.md) $X$ and $Y$, the precise equivalence is

$$
\bigl(\exists\text{ injection }f:X\to Y\bigr)\ \land\ \bigl(\exists\text{ injection }g:Y\to X\bigr)
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\exists\text{ bijection }h:X\to Y.
$$

The reverse direction uses the [bijection](bijection.md) $h$ and its [inverse function](inverse-function.md). Neither [set](set-split.md) is assumed to be a [subset](subset.md) of the other: the hypothesis concerns [functions](function-split.md), rather than containments. The forward direction holds in [ZF](zermelo-fraenkel-set-theory.md) without the [axiom of choice](axiom-of-choice.md).[Paul Garrett, Cantor-Schroeder-Bernstein Theorem, p. 1, theorem and paragraph explicitly excluding the axiom of choice](cantor-schroeder-bernstein-theorem-by-paul-garrett.md)

This makes [cardinality](cardinality.md) comparison an [antisymmetric relation](antisymmetric-relation.md): if each [set](set-split.md) is no larger than the other in the sense of an [injection](injective-function.md), they have equal [cardinality](cardinality.md). It also gives a practical strategy: build two convenient [injections](injective-function.md) and let the theorem supply the [bijection](bijection.md). The [Injection-chain proof of the Cantor-Schröder-Bernstein theorem](injection-chain-proof-of-the-cantor-schroder-bernstein-theorem.md) explains how to combine them without losing an output or pairing two inputs with the same output.
