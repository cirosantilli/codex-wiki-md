<h4 id="1f/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1f/a)

The [characteristic polynomial](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#characteristic-polynomial) of the given [matrix](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) is $(t-1)(t-2)^2$. Solving the two [eigenvalue equations](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue-equation) gives

$$
E_1=\operatorname{span}\{(3,-1,3)^T\},\qquad E_2=\operatorname{span}\{(2,1,0)^T,(2,0,1)^T\}.
$$

For example, multiplying the [matrix](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) by these three [vectors](../../../vector-space.md#vector) gives respectively $1$, $2$ and $2$ times the original [vector](../../../vector-space.md#vector). The [eigenvectors](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvector) form a [basis](../../../vector-space.md#basis): the [matrix](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) with these columns has nonzero [determinant](../../../linear-algebra.md#determinant) $-1$. Thus **an eigenbasis and its diagonal matrix** are

$$
\boxed{\mathcal B=((3,-1,3)^T,(2,1,0)^T,(2,0,1)^T),\qquad [\alpha]_{\mathcal B}=\operatorname{diag}(1,2,2).}
$$

<h3 id="1f/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1F](#1f)

<h4 id="1f/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1f/b)

Write $J_r(\lambda)$ for a size-$r$ [Jordan block](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#jordan-block). The [characteristic polynomial](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#characteristic-polynomial) fixes the total dimensions at $2$ and $-7$, while the [minimal polynomial](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#minimal-polynomial) fixes the largest [Jordan block](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#jordan-block) at each [eigenvalue](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue). Consequently, the blocks for $2$ have total size four, largest size two, and at least one size-two block. The blocks for $-7$ both have size one. **The complete list, up to similarity, is**

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{diag}(J_2(2),J_2(2),-7,-7),\qquad \operatorname{diag}(J_2(2),2,2,-7,-7),\quad J_2(2)=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix}.}
$$

These [Jordan normal forms](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#jordan-normal-form) are inequivalent because their $2$-[eigenspaces](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenspace) have different dimensions, two and three.

## 2A

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="2a/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2A](#2a)

At the origin, the [Taylor series](../../../calculus.md#taylor-series) $\sin z=z+O(z^3)$ and $1-\cos z=z^2/2+O(z^4)$ give

$$
f_1(z)=z^{-3}(1+O(z)),\qquad f_2(z)=2z^{-4}(1+O(z^2)).
$$

Thus **$f_1$ has a pole of order three and $f_2$ a pole of order four at zero**. For the third function, the [Laurent series](../../../analysis.md#laurent-series)

$$
f_3(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^k z^{1-2k}}{(2k+1)!}
$$

has infinitely many nonzero negative-power terms, so **zero is an essential singularity of $f_3$**.

To classify the point at infinity on the [Riemann sphere](../../../complex-analysis.md#riemann-sphere), use the coordinate $w=1/z$. Then

$$
f_3(1/w)=w^{-2}\sin w=w^{-1}-\frac w6+O(w^3),
$$

so **$f_3$ has a simple pole at infinity**.

For the other two functions, an important qualification is necessary. The [poles](../../../isolated-singularity.md#pole) of $f_1$ at nonzero integer multiples of $\pi$, and those of $f_2$ at nonzero integer multiples of $2\pi$, approach infinity. Equivalently, their images in the $w$ coordinate accumulate at zero. Hence **infinity is a non-isolated singularity of $f_1$ and $f_2$**. Neither function is [holomorphic](../../../complex-analysis.md#complex-differentiability-at-a-point) on any punctured neighbourhood of infinity, so the [classification of isolated singularities](../../../isolated-singularity.md#classification-of-isolated-singularities) into removable singularities, poles and essential singularities does not apply there.

## 3F

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="3f/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3F](#3f)

<h4 id="3f/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3f/a)

The [Poincare disc model](../../../geometry-and-topology.md#poincare-disk-model) is the open [unit disc](../../../topology.md#unit-disc) $D=\{z=x+iy:|z|<1\}$ equipped with the [Riemannian metric](../../../differential-geometry.md#riemannian-metric)

$$
\boxed{ds^2=\frac{4(dx^2+dy^2)}{(1-x^2-y^2)^2}=\frac{4|dz|^2}{(1-|z|^2)^2}.}
$$

This normalization gives [Gaussian curvature](../../../second-fundamental-form.md#gaussian-curvature) $-1$. In particular, a Euclidean curve has [hyperbolic length](../../../geometry-and-topology.md#hyperbolic-length-in-the-poincare-disc) $\int 2|dz|/(1-|z|^2)$; the boundary circle is infinitely far away in [hyperbolic distance](../../../geometry-and-topology.md#hyperbolic-distance).

<h3 id="3f/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3F](#3f)

<h4 id="3f/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3f/b)

**A suitable [Poincare disc automorphism](../../../geometry-and-topology.md#poincare-disc-automorphism)** is the [Möbius transformation](../../../group-theory.md#mobius-transformation)

$$
\boxed{f(z)=\frac{z-a}{1-\overline a z}.}
$$

It sends $a$ to zero and has inverse $(w+a)/(1+\overline a w)$. To verify the [isometry](../../../riemannian-geometry.md#isometry) directly, note that

$$
1-|f(z)|^2=\frac{(1-|a|^2)(1-|z|^2)}{|1-\overline a z|^2},\qquad |f'(z)|=\frac{1-|a|^2}{|1-\overline a z|^2}.
$$

These identities show both that $f$ maps the [unit disc](../../../topology.md#unit-disc) onto itself and that $2|df|/(1-|f|^2)=2|dz|/(1-|z|^2)$, preserving the [Riemannian metric](../../../differential-geometry.md#riemannian-metric).

<h3 id="3f/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3F](#3f)

<h4 id="3f/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#3f/c)

The radial segment is a [geodesic](../../../riemannian-geometry.md#geodesic) of the [Poincare disc model](../../../geometry-and-topology.md#poincare-disk-model). Its [hyperbolic length](../../../geometry-and-topology.md#hyperbolic-length-in-the-poincare-disc) is

$$
\boxed{d_D(0,re^{i\theta})=\int_0^r\frac{2\,dt}{1-t^2}=\log\frac{1+r}{1-r}=2\operatorname{artanh}r.}
$$

One can also verify that it minimizes [length of a curve](../../../riemannian-geometry.md#length-of-a-curve): along any piecewise smooth curve from zero to radius $r$, the Euclidean speed is at least the absolute radial speed. Integrating $2|dr|/(1-r^2)$ is at least the absolute change in $\log((1+r)/(1-r))$, with equality on the radial segment. Thus the answer is independent of $\theta$.

<h3 id="3f/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3F](#3f)

<h4 id="3f/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#3f/d)

Use an [isometry](../../../riemannian-geometry.md#isometry) of the [Poincare disc model](../../../geometry-and-topology.md#poincare-disk-model) to move the centre to zero. A [hyperbolic disc](../../../geometry-and-topology.md#hyperbolic-disc) of radius $\rho$ then has Euclidean radius $R=\tanh(\rho/2)$ by the [hyperbolic distance](../../../geometry-and-topology.md#hyperbolic-distance) formula. The [area element of a surface](../../../differential-geometry.md#area-element-of-a-surface) of the [Riemannian metric](../../../differential-geometry.md#riemannian-metric) is $4r\,dr\,d\theta/(1-r^2)^2$, so

$$
\operatorname{Area}=\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^R\frac{4r}{(1-r^2)^2}\,dr\,d\theta=4\pi\left(\frac1{1-R^2}-1\right).
$$

Therefore **the area is independent of the centre**, and

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Area}=4\pi\sinh^2(\rho/2)=2\pi(\cosh\rho-1).}
$$

## 4C

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="4c/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4C](#4c)

<h4 id="4c/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#4c/a)

For a twice differentiable function, [convexity](../../../real-analysis.md#convex-function) on $\mathbb R^2$ is equivalent to its [Hessian matrix](../../../calculus.md#hessian-matrix) being [positive semidefinite](../../../linear-algebra.md#positive-semidefinite-matrix) everywhere. Here the [Hessian matrix](../../../calculus.md#hessian-matrix) is constant:

$$
H=\begin{pmatrix}4&\alpha\\\alpha&2\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Its diagonal entries are positive, so [positive semidefiniteness](../../../linear-algebra.md#positive-semidefinite-matrix) is equivalent to $\det H=8-\alpha^2\geq0$. **The required parameter range is**

$$
\boxed{-2\sqrt2\leq\alpha\leq2\sqrt2.}
$$

The endpoints give [convex functions](../../../real-analysis.md#convex-function) with one flat direction; strict inequalities give a [Hessian matrix](../../../calculus.md#hessian-matrix) defining a [positive-definite quadratic form](../../../linear-algebra.md#positive-definite-quadratic-form) and a [strictly convex function](../../../real-analysis.md#strictly-convex-function).

<h3 id="4c/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4C](#4c)

<h4 id="4c/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#4c/b)

Completing the square in the [optimization constraint](../../../numerical-analysis.md#optimization-constraint) gives

$$
\left(x_2-\frac32x_1\right)^2=\frac14x_1^2-1.
$$

The left side is nonnegative, so every feasible point has $x_1^2\geq4$. Equality is feasible precisely when $x_2=3x_1/2$ and $x_1=\pm2$. Thus **the global minimum is**

$$
\boxed{\min x_1^2=4,\qquad (x_1,x_2)=(2,3)\text{ or }(-2,-3).}
$$

There is **no maximum**: for every $|x_1|\geq2$, the values $x_2=3x_1/2\pm\sqrt{x_1^2/4-1}$ satisfy the [optimization constraint](../../../numerical-analysis.md#optimization-constraint), allowing $x_1^2$ to be arbitrarily large. This direct argument also establishes that the two stationary points are the only [local minima](../../../analysis.md#local-minimum) on the two branches of the constraint curve.

## 5C

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="5c/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [5C](#5c)

<h4 id="5c/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#5c/a)

Put $r^2=x^2+y^2$. On the specified domain, $r>0$, and direct differentiation gives the [divergence](../../../calculus.md#divergence) and [vorticity](../../../fluid-mechanics.md#vorticity)

$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=U'(z),\qquad \nabla\times\mathbf u=\mathbf0.
$$

For example, the third component of the [curl](../../../calculus.md#curl) is $A(r^2-2x^2)/r^4+A(r^2-2y^2)/r^4=0$; the other two components vanish because $U$ depends only on $z$ and the transverse velocity is independent of $z$. Hence **the flow is both incompressible and irrotational exactly when**

$$
\boxed{U(z)=U_0\text{ is constant},\qquad A\text{ is arbitrary}.}
$$

The exclusion of the axis matters: these are ordinary derivatives on the domain, with no assertion of regularity on the removed axis.

<h3 id="5c/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [5C](#5c)

<h4 id="5c/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#5c/b)

In cylindrical coordinates, the transverse velocity is $A\mathbf e_\theta/r$, so its contribution to the [circulation](../../../fluid-mechanics.md#circulation-physics) is $A\,d\theta$. The vertical contribution is $U(z)\,dz$, the differential of an antiderivative of $U$, and therefore has zero integral around any closed curve. If $k$ is the oriented [winding number](../../../complex-analysis.md#winding-number) of the curve's projection about the origin, then

$$
\boxed{\oint\mathbf u\cdot d\mathbf r=2\pi A k.}
$$

In particular, **a single positive winding gives $2\pi A$**; reversing orientation reverses the sign. The nonzero [circulation](../../../fluid-mechanics.md#circulation-physics) is compatible with [irrotational flow](../../../fluid-mechanics.md#irrotational-flow) because the domain is not [simply connected](../../../algebraic-topology.md#simply-connected-space). For $A\ne0$, an enclosing curve cannot bound a surface contained entirely in the domain to which [Stokes theorem](../../../calculus.md#stokes-theorem) would apply.

## 6D

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="6d/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [6D](#6d)

<h4 id="6d/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#6d/a)

A sequence of real [orthogonal polynomials](../../../numerical-analysis.md#orthogonal-polynomial) consists of real polynomials $p_n$ with [degree of a polynomial](../../../polynomial.md#degree-of-a-polynomial) $n$ and

$$
\langle p_m,p_n\rangle=0\quad(m\ne n),\qquad \langle p_n,p_n\rangle>0.
$$

Orthogonality is relative to the specified [inner product](../../../linear-algebra.md#inner-product), rather than to the ordinary coefficients. A [monic polynomial](../../../polynomial.md#monic-polynomial) has leading coefficient one, so a [monic orthogonal polynomial](../../../numerical-analysis.md#monic-orthogonal-polynomial) has the form $p_n(x)=x^n+\text{terms of lower degree}$. In particular, the monic degree-zero polynomial is $p_0=1$.

<h3 id="6d/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [6D](#6d)

<h4 id="6d/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#6d/b)

Since $xp_n-p_{n+1}$ has [degree of a polynomial](../../../polynomial.md#degree-of-a-polynomial) at most $n$, expand it in the [basis](../../../vector-space.md#basis) $p_0,\ldots,p_n$. Multiplication by $x$ is [self-adjoint](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#self-adjoint-operator) for this weighted [inner product](../../../linear-algebra.md#inner-product): $\langle xp,q\rangle=\langle p,xq\rangle$. For $k\leq n-2$, the polynomial $xp_k$ has degree at most $n-1$, so

$$
\langle xp_n-p_{n+1},p_k\rangle=\langle p_n,xp_k\rangle=0.
$$

Only the $p_n$ and $p_{n-1}$ coefficients remain. Taking their [inner products](../../../linear-algebra.md#inner-product) gives **the three-term recurrence**

$$
\boxed{p_{n+1}=(x-\alpha_n)p_n-\beta_n p_{n-1},\qquad \alpha_n=\frac{\langle xp_n,p_n\rangle}{\langle p_n,p_n\rangle},\quad \beta_n=\frac{\langle p_n,p_n\rangle}{\langle p_{n-1},p_{n-1}\rangle}>0\ (n\geq1).}
$$

For the second formula, $xp_{n-1}=p_n+$ a polynomial of lower degree, hence $\langle xp_n,p_{n-1}\rangle=\langle p_n,xp_{n-1}\rangle=\langle p_n,p_n\rangle$. At $n=0$, take $p_{-1}=0$, $\beta_0=0$ and the same formula for $\alpha_0$. Positivity of the weight ensures that all the squared [norms](../../../functional-analysis.md#norm) in the denominators are nonzero.

## 7H

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="7h/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [7H](#7h)

Let $S=\sum_{i=1}^nX_i$. The [likelihood ratio](../../../statistical-modelling.md#likelihood-ratio) of rate two to rate one is $2^n e^{-S}$, which decreases strictly with $S$. By the [Neyman-Pearson lemma](../../../statistical-modelling.md#neyman-pearson-lemma), the **most powerful test rejects for small values of the sum**:

$$
\boxed{\text{reject if }S<c_\alpha,\qquad \mathbb P_{\lambda=1}(S<c_\alpha)=\alpha.}
$$

Under the null, $S$ has the [gamma distribution](../../../continuous-probability-distribution.md#gamma-distribution) with shape $n$ and rate one. Thus $c_\alpha$ is its $\alpha$-quantile, equivalently half the $\alpha$-quantile of the [chi-squared distribution](../../../probability-theory.md#chi-squared-distribution) with $2n$ degrees of freedom. The [distribution](../../../distribution-theory.md#distribution-mathematical-analysis) is continuous, so boundary randomization is unnecessary for $0<\alpha<1$.

For every fixed alternative $\lambda>1$, the [likelihood ratio](../../../statistical-modelling.md#likelihood-ratio) against rate one is $\lambda^n e^{-(\lambda-1)S}$, again strictly decreasing. Therefore the same region is most powerful against every such alternative. Moreover, if $G$ has the shape-$n$, rate-one [gamma distribution](../../../continuous-probability-distribution.md#gamma-distribution), then $S$ at rate $\lambda$ has the same [distribution](../../../distribution-theory.md#distribution-mathematical-analysis) as $G/\lambda$. It follows that $\mathbb P_\lambda(S<c_\alpha)=\mathbb P(G<\lambda c_\alpha)$ increases with $\lambda$, so the maximum rejection probability over $\lambda\leq1$ is $\alpha$ at one. Any competing size-$\alpha$ test for the composite null is also of level at most $\alpha$ at rate one, and is bounded in power by the [Neyman-Pearson lemma](../../../statistical-modelling.md#neyman-pearson-lemma) for each alternative. **The test is uniformly most powerful for the composite hypotheses as well.** This is the [uniformly most powerful test for an exponential rate](../../../statistical-modelling.md#uniformly-most-powerful-test-for-an-exponential-rate) based on the sample sum.

## 8H

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="8h/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [8H](#8h)

Let $p$ be the row player's [mixed strategy](../../../game-theory.md#mixed-strategy) and $q$ the column player's [mixed strategy](../../../game-theory.md#mixed-strategy). The fourth row is strictly dominated by the first. A useful candidate mixes the first two rows so that their first and third column payoffs agree. If the first row has probability $t$, these payoffs are $7t-2$ and $2-7t$, giving $t=2/7$ and common value zero.

To prove that these are [optimal mixed strategies](../../../game-theory.md#optimal-mixed-strategy), use the two explicit [mixed strategies](../../../game-theory.md#mixed-strategy)

$$
\boxed{p=(2/7,5/7,0,0)^T,\qquad q=(1/2,0,1/2)^T,\qquad v=0.}
$$

Their payoff vectors satisfy

$$
p^TA=(0,11/7,0),\qquad Aq=(0,0,-1/2,-1)^T.
$$

Thus the row player guarantees a payoff at least zero against every column [mixed strategy](../../../game-theory.md#mixed-strategy), and the column player guarantees a payoff at most zero against every row [mixed strategy](../../../game-theory.md#mixed-strategy). This matching pair proves that **the value of the zero-sum game is zero**.

These [optimal mixed strategies](../../../game-theory.md#optimal-mixed-strategy) are also unique. Since $Aq$ is strictly negative in rows three and four, any optimal row strategy gives them probability zero. Against columns one and three it must then satisfy both $7t-2\geq0$ and $2-7t\geq0$, so $t=2/7$. Similarly, $p^TA$ is strictly positive in column two, forcing an optimal column strategy to avoid that column; its constraints against rows one and two then force equal weights on columns one and three.

## 9F

<h3 id="9f/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [9F](#9f)

<h4 id="9f/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#9f/a)

For [matrices](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) $A,B$ and scalars $r,s\in\mathbb F$, the definition of the [trace](../../../linear-algebra.md#matrix-trace) gives

$$
\operatorname{Tr}(rA+sB)=\sum_i(ra_{ii}+sb_{ii})=r\operatorname{Tr}(A)+s\operatorname{Tr}(B).
$$

Hence **the trace is a linear functional** on the [vector space](../../../vector-space.md) of $n\times n$ [matrices](../../../vector-space.md#matrix).

<h3 id="9f/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [9F](#9f)

<h4 id="9f/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#9f/b)

The definition of [matrix multiplication](../../../vector-space.md#matrix-multiplication) gives

$$
\operatorname{Tr}(AB)=\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}b_{ji}=\sum_{j,i}b_{ji}a_{ij}=\operatorname{Tr}(BA).
$$

The middle equality uses commutativity of scalar multiplication in $\mathbb R$ or $\mathbb C$, together with interchange of finite sums. Thus **the trace is unchanged by cyclically moving the two factors**.

<h3 id="9f/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [9F](#9f)

<h4 id="9f/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#9f/c)

To prove that [trace is the unique normalized cyclic linear functional](../../../linear-algebra.md#trace-is-the-unique-normalized-cyclic-linear-functional), let $E_{ij}$ be the [matrix units](../../../vector-space.md#matrix-unit). For $i\ne j$, $E_{ii}E_{ij}=E_{ij}$ whereas $E_{ij}E_{ii}=0$, so the assumed identity for the [linear functional](../../../linear-algebra.md#linear-functional) gives $f(E_{ij})=0$. Also, $E_{ij}E_{ji}=E_{ii}$ and $E_{ji}E_{ij}=E_{jj}$, giving $f(E_{ii})=f(E_{jj})$. Write this common value as $c$. Since the [matrix units](../../../vector-space.md#matrix-unit) form a [basis](../../../vector-space.md#basis),

$$
\boxed{f(A)=\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}f(E_{ij})=c\sum_i a_{ii}=c\operatorname{Tr}(A).}
$$

For $n=1$ the same conclusion follows immediately from the one-dimensional [basis](../../../vector-space.md#basis) $E_{11}$. Finally, $f(I)=nc$; if this equals $n$, then $c=1$ over either specified field. **The normalization makes $f$ exactly the trace.**

<h3 id="9f/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [9F](#9f)

<h4 id="9f/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#9f/d)

Every [commutator](../../../lie-algebra.md#commutator) $[A,B]=AB-BA$ has zero [trace](../../../linear-algebra.md#matrix-trace), by the cyclic identity. Hence its linear span $W$ is contained in $\ker\operatorname{Tr}$. For the reverse inclusion, the [matrix units](../../../vector-space.md#matrix-unit) satisfy

$$
E_{ij}=[E_{ii},E_{ij}]\quad(i\ne j),\qquad E_{ii}-E_{jj}=[E_{ij},E_{ji}].
$$

The off-diagonal [matrix units](../../../vector-space.md#matrix-unit) and the differences $E_{ii}-E_{nn}$ for $i<n$ span all [traceless matrices](../../../linear-algebra.md#traceless-matrix): a traceless diagonal part is $\sum_{i<n}a_{ii}(E_{ii}-E_{nn})$. Thus every element of the [kernel](../../../linear-algebra.md#kernel-of-a-linear-map) is a linear combination of [commutators](../../../lie-algebra.md#commutator), proving

$$
\boxed{W=\ker\operatorname{Tr}.}
$$

This establishes that [traceless matrices are spanned by commutators](../../../linear-algebra.md#traceless-matrices-are-spanned-by-commutators). For $n=1$, both [vector subspaces](../../../vector-space.md#vector-subspace) are zero. Notice that the argument needs a span of [commutators](../../../lie-algebra.md#commutator); it does not assume that every such sum has already been written as a single commutator.

We prove [prime avoidance](../../../commutative-algebra.md#prime-avoidance) by contradiction. Remove any [prime ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) contained in another one on the list; this leaves the same union. If the [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) $I$ is contained in none of the remaining [prime ideals](../../../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal), choose $a_i\in I\setminus P_i$. For every $j\ne i$, incomparability lets us choose $b_{ij}\in P_j\setminus P_i$. Set

$$
b_i=\prod_{j\ne i}b_{ij},\qquad c=\sum_i a_i b_i.
$$

For a one-element list, use the empty product $b_1=1$. By the defining property of a [prime ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal), $b_i\notin P_i$, and also $a_i b_i\notin P_i$. But $b_j\in P_i$ for every $j\ne i$. Thus all summands except $a_i b_i$ lie in $P_i$, and consequently $c\notin P_i$ for every $i$. On the other hand, $c\in I$ because each $a_i\in I$. This contradicts the assumed containment in the union. Therefore

$$
\boxed{I\subseteq P_i\text{ for at least one }i.}
$$

<h3 id="10e/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [10E](#10e)

<h4 id="10e/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#10e/b)

The evaluation map $\varphi:\mathbb R[x]\to\mathbb C$, $p\mapsto p(i)$, is a [ring homomorphism](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ring-homomorphism) and is onto because $a+bx$ maps to any $a+bi$. By [polynomial division](../../../polynomial.md#polynomial-division), write

$$
p(x)=(x^2+1)q(x)+a+bx.
$$

Then $p(i)=0$ precisely when $a=b=0$. Thus $\ker\varphi=(x^2+1)$, and the [first isomorphism theorem](../../../group-theory.md#first-isomorphism-theorem) gives

$$
\boxed{\mathbb R[x]/(x^2+1)\cong\mathbb C.}
$$

Since the [quotient ring](../../../commutative-algebra.md#quotient-ring) is a [field](../../../algebra.md#field), **$(x^2+1)$ is a maximal ideal**.

<h3 id="10e/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [10E](#10e)

<h4 id="10e/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#10e/c)

Evaluation at a real point defines a surjective [ring homomorphism](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ring-homomorphism) $p\mapsto p(a,b)$ from $\mathbb R[x,y]$ to $\mathbb R$. Its [kernel](../../../linear-algebra.md#kernel-of-a-linear-map) is $(x-a,y-b)$: subtracting the constant $p(a,b)$ expresses the remainder as a multiple of $x-a$ plus a multiple of $y-b$, by successive [polynomial division](../../../polynomial.md#polynomial-division). The [first isomorphism theorem](../../../group-theory.md#first-isomorphism-theorem) therefore identifies the [quotient ring](../../../commutative-algebra.md#quotient-ring) with the [field](../../../algebra.md#field) $\mathbb R$, proving **each $I_{a,b}$ is maximal**.

For a [maximal ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#maximal-ideal) not on this list, take

$$
\boxed{J=(x^2+1,y).}
$$

Evaluation $p(x,y)\mapsto p(i,0)$ is onto $\mathbb C$, and its [kernel](../../../linear-algebra.md#kernel-of-a-linear-map) is $J$: first subtract a multiple of $y$, then divide the remaining polynomial in $x$ by $x^2+1$. Hence $\mathbb R[x,y]/J\cong\mathbb C$, a [field](../../../algebra.md#field). Finally, if $J=I_{a,b}$ for real $a,b$, the member $x^2+1\in J$ would vanish at $(a,b)$, requiring $a^2+1=0$, which is impossible over $\mathbb R$.

## 11G

↑ **Parent:** [A](#11g/a)

A [Cauchy sequence](../../../real-analysis.md#cauchy-sequence) in a [metric space](../../../topological-analysis.md#metric-space) satisfies: for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $N$ such that $d(x_m,x_n)<\varepsilon$ whenever $m,n\geq N$. Suppose a [subsequence](../../../real-analysis.md#subsequence) $x_{n_k}$ converges to $x\in X$. Choose $N$ so that the Cauchy bound is $\varepsilon/2$, and then choose $k$ with $n_k\geq N$ and $d(x_{n_k},x)<\varepsilon/2$. For every $n\geq N$, the [triangle inequality](../../../topological-analysis.md#triangle-inequality) gives

$$
d(x_n,x)\leq d(x_n,x_{n_k})+d(x_{n_k},x)<\varepsilon.
$$

Thus **the whole Cauchy sequence converges to the subsequential limit**.

<h3 id="11g/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [11G](#11g)

<h4 id="11g/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#11g/b)

<h5 id="11g/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#11g/b/i)

For fixed $m$, the [reverse triangle inequality](../../../topological-analysis.md#reverse-triangle-inequality) gives

$$
|d(x_m,x_n)-d(x_m,x_k)|\leq d(x_n,x_k).
$$

The right side tends uniformly to zero as $n,k\to\infty$ because $(x_n)$ is a [Cauchy sequence](../../../real-analysis.md#cauchy-sequence). Hence $(d(x_m,x_n))_n$ is a real [Cauchy sequence](../../../real-analysis.md#cauchy-sequence). By [completeness](../../../topological-analysis.md#completeness) of $\mathbb R$, it has a finite, nonnegative limit:

$$
\boxed{d_m=\lim_{n\to\infty}d(x_m,x_n)\geq0.}
$$

This conclusion uses completeness only of the real line, without assuming that $X$ is complete.

<h4 id="11g/b/ii">ii</h4>

Given $\varepsilon>0$, the [Cauchy sequence](../../../real-analysis.md#cauchy-sequence) property gives $N$ with $d(x_m,x_n)<\varepsilon/2$ for all $m,n\geq N$. Fixing $m\geq N$ and letting $n\to\infty$ yields $0\leq d_m\leq\varepsilon/2<\varepsilon$. Therefore

\boxed{d(y_\ell,y_n)\longrightarrow d_m.}

The conclusion depends on the point $x_m$ itself, even if that point appears at several indices.

<h4 id="11g/b/v">v</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#11g/b)

<h5 id="11g/b/v/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [V](#11g/b/v)

First handle possible zero values. If $d_m=d_n=0$, the previous upper bound forces $x_m=x_n$. Thus, if infinitely many $d_m$ vanish, choosing those indices gives a constant [subsequence](../../../real-analysis.md#subsequence), which satisfies both inequalities.

Otherwise, discard the finitely many zero values and use $d_m\to0$ to choose increasing indices $k_j$ with

$$
\delta_j:=d_{k_j}>0,\qquad \delta_{j+1}\leq\delta_j/8.
$$

Set $y_j=x_{k_j}$. The preceding bounds give $|\delta_j-\delta_\ell|\leq d(y_j,y_\ell)\leq\delta_j+\delta_\ell$. For $m\geq2$,

$$
\frac{d(y_{m+1},y_m)}{d(y_m,y_{m-1})}\leq\frac{(9/8)\delta_m}{(7/8)\delta_{m-1}}\leq\frac9{56}<\frac13.
$$

For $m<n$, use $\delta_n\leq\delta_m/8$ and $\delta_{n+1}\leq\delta_m/64$ to obtain

$$
\frac{d(y_{m+1},y_{n+1})}{d(y_m,y_n)}\leq\frac{\delta_m/8+\delta_m/64}{(7/8)\delta_m}=\frac9{56}<\frac12.
$$

The same holds when $n<m$ by symmetry; when $m=n$, both distances are zero. This proves **both required inequalities**. The adjacent-distance inequality is understood for $m\geq2$, since this [subsequence](../../../real-analysis.md#subsequence) starts at index one and has no $y_0$.

<h3 id="11g/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [11G](#11g)

<h4 id="11g/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#11g/c)

We argue by contradiction using the previous [subsequence](../../../real-analysis.md#subsequence) construction. If $X$ is not [complete](../../../topological-analysis.md#completeness), choose a [Cauchy sequence](../../../real-analysis.md#cauchy-sequence) with no limit in $X$. None of its $d_m$ can be zero: if $d_m=0$, then $d(x_m,x_n)\to0$, giving a limit in $X$. The construction therefore produces pairwise distinct $y_j$ with

$$
d(y_{m+1},y_{n+1})\leq\tfrac12d(y_m,y_n).
$$

Let $Y=\{y_1,y_2,\ldots\}$. **This subset is closed in $X$.** Indeed, if a point $x\notin Y$ lay in its [closure](../../../topology.md#closure-topology), there would be points of $Y$ tending to $x$. Their indices must be unbounded, because the distance from $x$ to any finite subset of $Y$ is positive. Passing to increasing indices gives a [convergent subsequence](../../../real-analysis.md#convergent-subsequence) of the original [Cauchy sequence](../../../real-analysis.md#cauchy-sequence). Part (a) would then make the whole sequence converge to $x$, a contradiction.

Now define $F:Y\to Y$ by $F(y_j)=y_{j+1}$. Distinctness makes this well-defined, and the displayed inequality makes it a [contraction mapping](../../../analysis.md#contraction-mapping) with constant at most $1/2$. It has no [fixed point](../../../function.md#fixed-point), contradicting the assumed property of closed subsets. Consequently

$$
\boxed{X\text{ is complete}.}
$$

This is the converse direction of [contraction property of closed subsets characterizes completeness](../../../analysis.md#contraction-property-of-closed-subsets-characterizes-completeness).

## 12E

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="12e/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [12E](#12e)

For $q\in\mathbb Q\setminus\{0\}$ write $q=p^k u/v$, where $k\in\mathbb Z$ and neither integer $u$ nor integer $v$ is divisible by $p$. Define the [P-adic valuation](../../../number-theory.md#p-adic-valuation) $v_p(q)=k$ and the [p-adic absolute value](../../../arithmetic.md#p-adic-absolute-value) $|q|_p=p^{-k}$, with $|0|_p=0$. **The p-adic metric** is

$$
\boxed{d_p(a,b)=|a-b|_p.}
$$

Factoring out the smaller power of $p$ from two rational numbers shows $v_p(q+r)\geq\min\{v_p(q),v_p(r)\}$, with the usual convention $v_p(0)=+\infty$. Consequently the [ultrametric inequality](../../../arithmetic.md#ultrametric-inequality) gives

$$
d_p(a,b)=|(a-c)+(c-b)|_p\leq\max\{d_p(a,c),d_p(c,b)\}.
$$

Together with symmetry, positivity and separation of points, this verifies that $d_p$ is a [metric](../../../topological-analysis.md#metric).

For the given geometric partial sums, use the finite [geometric series](../../../real-analysis.md#geometric-series) identity. Since $p$ does not divide $p-1$,

$$
a_n=\frac{p^n-1}{p-1},\qquad \left|a_n+\frac1{p-1}\right|_p=\left|\frac{p^n}{p-1}\right|_p=p^{-n}\longrightarrow0.
$$

Thus **the limit is the rational number** $\boxed{1/(1-p)}$, even though the partial sums diverge in the ordinary real [metric](../../../topological-analysis.md#metric).

If $|a|_p>|b|_p$, the [ultrametric inequality](../../../arithmetic.md#ultrametric-inequality) gives $|a+b|_p\leq|a|_p$. Applying it to $a=(a+b)-b$ gives $|a|_p\leq\max\{|a+b|_p,|b|_p\}$. Since $|b|_p<|a|_p$, this forces $|a+b|_p=|a|_p$. Interchanging $a,b$ handles the other unequal case, proving

$$
\boxed{|a+b|_p=\max\{|a|_p,|b|_p\}\quad\text{when }|a|_p\ne|b|_p.}
$$

Finally, let $b$ be outside the [open ball](../../../topology.md#open-ball) $B(a,\delta)$, so $d_p(a,b)\geq\delta$. If $d_p(b,c)<\delta$, the preceding unequal-norm identity gives $d_p(a,c)=d_p(a,b)\geq\delta$. Therefore $B(b,\delta)$ also lies outside $B(a,\delta)$. Its complement is [open](../../../topology.md#open-set), so **every p-adic open ball is also closed**, that is, a [clopen set](../../../topology.md#clopen-set).

It helps to separate the [tangent](../../../geometry-and-topology.md#tangent) and the squaring map. Put $t=\tan(z/2)$, with $z=x+iy$. The identity

shows that $0<x<\pi/2$ gives $\operatorname{Re}t>0$ and $|t|<1$. To see that every point of this right half-disc occurs, set

Squaring maps this right half-disc bijectively onto the [unit disc](../../../topology.md#unit-disc) with the interval $(-1,0]$ removed: the argument of $t$ lies between $-\pi/2$ and $\pi/2$, and doubles into $(-\pi,\pi)$. Thus **the desired image is**

$$
\boxed{\{w:|w|<1\}\setminus(-1,0].}

Neither derivative vanishes in the domain, so the composition is also a [conformal map](../../../geometry-and-topology.md#conformal-map) onto that slit disc.

On the parabolic domain with the nonpositive real axis removed, first apply the [principal square root](../../../analysis.md#principal-square-root-of-a-complex-number), then scale by $\pi/2$, and then apply the map of part (b). $$

As $r$ ranges from zero to infinity, these values run once through $(-1,0]$. They fill precisely the slit omitted by the previous mapping, and no point away from the negative real axis has an image in that slit. Therefore **the extended map is a bijection onto the whole unit disc**:

It is [holomorphic](../../../complex-analysis.md#complex-differentiability-at-a-point) throughout, so this bijection is the desired [conformal map from a parabolic domain to the unit disc](../../../geometry-and-topology.md#conformal-map-from-a-parabolic-domain-to-the-unit-disc); in particular $C'(0)=\pi^2/16\ne0$.

## 14A

<h3 id="14a/a">a</h3>

<h4 id="14a/a/solution">Solution</h4>

For the real coefficients of the stated [self-adjoint](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#self-adjoint-operator) [Sturm-Liouville problem](../../../analysis.md#sturm-liouville-problem), define the weighted [inner product](../../../linear-algebra.md#inner-product)

$$
\boxed{\langle u,v\rangle_w=\int_a^b w(x)\overline{u(x)}v(x)\,dx.}
$$

Since $w>0$, a nonzero eigenfunction has positive squared [norm](../../../functional-analysis.md#norm). Let $L y=-(py')'+qy$. [Integration by parts](../../../calculus.md#integration-by-parts) and the [Dirichlet boundary conditions](../../../differential-equation.md#dirichlet-boundary-condition) give

$$
\int_a^b\overline u\,Lv\,dx=\int_a^b\bigl(p\overline{u'}v'+q\overline uv\bigr)\,dx=\int_a^b\overline{Lu}\,v\,dx.
$$

Taking $u=v$ to be an [eigenfunction](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenfunction) shows that its [eigenvalue](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) is real, since $\lambda\int w|u|^2=\int(p|u'|^2+q|u|^2)$ is real. For two eigenfunctions with [eigenvalues](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) $\lambda,\mu$, the same identity gives

$$
(\mu-\lambda)\langle u,v\rangle_w=0.
$$

Hence **distinct eigenvalues imply orthogonality in the weighted inner product**. This is the [orthogonality of Sturm-Liouville eigenfunctions](../../../analysis.md#orthogonality-of-sturm-liouville-eigenfunctions). The calculation assumes the regularity needed for the stated self-adjoint boundary problem, so all boundary terms and integrals are defined.

\boxed{-(x^3y')'-xy=\lambda xy,\qquad p=x^3,\quad q=-x,\quad w=x.}
$$

The [Dirichlet boundary conditions](../../../differential-equation.md#dirichlet-boundary-condition) remain at $1$ and $e$, and the weight $w=x$ is positive on this interval, as required for the [Sturm-Liouville problem](../../../analysis.md#sturm-liouville-problem).

<h4 id="14a/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#14a/b)

<h5 id="14a/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#14a/b/ii)

Set $s=\log x$ and $Y(s)=y(e^s)$, so $0\leq s\leq1$. By the [chain rule](../../../calculus.md#chain-rule), the equation becomes $Y''+2Y'+(1+\lambda)Y=0$. The substitution $Y=e^{-s}v$ reduces it further to

$$
v''+\lambda v=0,\qquad v(0)=v(1)=0.
$$

Multiplication by $\overline v$ and [integration by parts](../../../calculus.md#integration-by-parts) gives $\lambda\int_0^1|v|^2=\int_0^1|v'|^2>0$ for a nonzero solution, ruling out zero and negative [eigenvalues](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue). For $\lambda=k^2>0$, the first boundary condition gives $v=A\sin(ks)$, and the second gives $k=n\pi$, $n=1,2,\ldots$. Thus **all eigenpairs are**

$$
\boxed{\lambda_n=n^2\pi^2,\qquad y_n(x)=\frac1x\sin(n\pi\log x),\quad n\geq1,}
$$

with arbitrary nonzero scalar multiples of each [eigenfunction](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenfunction). These are the complete list since all solutions of the constant-coefficient [differential equation](../../../differential-equation.md) have been considered; the transformed sine system is also complete in the corresponding weighted [Hilbert space](../../../hilbert-space.md).

<h4 id="14a/b/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#14a/b)

<h5 id="14a/b/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#14a/b/iii)

For the weight $w=x$, substitution $x=e^s$ gives

$$
\langle y_m,y_n\rangle_w=\int_1^e\frac{\sin(m\pi\log x)\sin(n\pi\log x)}x\,dx=\int_0^1\sin(m\pi s)\sin(n\pi s)\,ds.
$$

The [product-to-sum formula](../../../geometry-and-topology.md#product-to-sum-formula) makes this zero when $m\ne n$ and $1/2$ when $m=n$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\langle y_m,y_n\rangle_w=\tfrac12\delta_{mn}.}
$$

In particular the [eigenfunctions](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenfunction) are [orthogonal](../../../linear-algebra.md#orthogonal-vectors), and multiplying each by $\sqrt2$ gives an [orthonormal basis](../../../linear-algebra.md#orthonormal-basis) for the weighted space.

Use the normalized [eigenfunctions](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenfunction) of the [infinite square well](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#infinite-square-well),

\phi_n(x)=\sqrt{\frac2a}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{a},\qquad E_n=\frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2ma^2},\quad 0<x<a.

\psi_0=\frac{3\phi_1-\phi_3}{\sqrt{10}}.

By the [Born rule](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#born-rule), the [energy measurement](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#energy-measurement) probabilities are $9/10$ for $E_1$ and $1/10$ for $E_3=9E_1$. $$
\boxed{\langle E\rangle=\frac9{10}E_1+\frac1{10}E_3=\frac{9\pi^2\hbar^2}{10ma^2}.}
$$

The two probabilities sum to one, also checking the [wavefunction normalization](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#wavefunction-normalization).

<h4 id="15b/a/ii">ii</h4>

<h5 id="15b/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

and the [wavefunction](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#wave-function) is zero outside the well. Equivalently, in elementary functions,

<h3 id="15b/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [15B](#15b)

<h4 id="15b/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#15b/b)

For a [bound state](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#bound-state), $0<E<U_0$: the [Hamiltonian operator](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#hamiltonian-quantum-mechanics) is nonnegative, and an energy at or above the exterior potential does not give a nonzero [normalizable wavefunction](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#normalizable-wavefunction). The [Time-independent Schrödinger equation](../../../physics.md#time-independent-schrodinger-equation) and the hard wall at zero therefore give

$$
\psi(x)=A\sin(kx)\quad(0<x<a),\qquad \psi(x)=B e^{-\kappa(x-a)}\quad(x>a),
\qquad k=\frac{\sqrt{2mE}}{\hbar},\quad \kappa=\frac{\sqrt{2m(U_0-E)}}{\hbar}.
$$

Continuity of the [wavefunction](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#wave-function) and its derivative at the finite step gives $k\cot(ka)=-\kappa$. Put $z=ka$ and $z_0=a\sqrt{2mU_0}/\hbar$. The matching equation becomes

$$
z\cot z=-\sqrt{z_0^2-z^2},\qquad 0<z<z_0.
$$

It requires $\cot z<0$, so solutions lie in the intervals $((j+1/2)\pi,(j+1)\pi)$ for $j=0,1,\ldots$. Squaring, with that sign restriction retained, gives $z_0=z/|\sin z|$. On each such interval this function increases strictly from $(j+1/2)\pi$ to infinity: its derivative is $(|\sin z|-z\,\operatorname{sgn}(\sin z)\cos z)/\sin^2z>0$, because the second term in the numerator is positive there. Each interval therefore contributes exactly one [bound state](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#bound-state) if and only if $z_0>(j+1/2)\pi$. These are the [bound-state thresholds for a square well with one hard wall](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#bound-state-thresholds-for-a-square-well-with-one-hard-wall).

**Exactly one normalizable bound state exists if and only if**

$$
\boxed{\frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{8ma^2}<U_0\leq\frac{9\pi^2\hbar^2}{8ma^2}.}
$$

**The printed strict upper inequality misses the threshold case.** At the upper endpoint, the prospective second state has $E=U_0$ and $\kappa=0$. Its exterior solution is constant or linear, hence is not square-integrable unless zero; the zero exterior solution and matching would force the entire solution to vanish. Thus there is still exactly one [bound state](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#bound-state) at that endpoint. The strict upper inequality is valid if threshold values are additionally excluded, but that exclusion is not part of the stated assumptions.

<h3 id="16d/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [16D](#16d)

<h4 id="16d/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#16d/a)

In [electrostatics](../../../electromagnetism.md#electrostatics), the relevant [Maxwell equations](../../../electromagnetism.md#maxwell-equations) reduce to $\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\rho/\varepsilon_0$ and $\nabla\times\mathbf E=0$. For a volume $V$ with outward unit normal $\mathbf n$, integrate the first equation and apply the [divergence theorem](../../../calculus.md#divergence-theorem):

$$
\boxed{\oint_{\partial V}\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf n\,dS=\int_V\nabla\cdot\mathbf E\,dV=\frac1{\varepsilon_0}\int_V\rho\,dV=\frac{Q_V}{\varepsilon_0}.}
$$

This is **the integral form of Gauss's law**, with $Q_V$ the enclosed [electric charge](../../../electromagnetism.md#electric-charge). The zero [curl](../../../calculus.md#curl) is the accompanying condition allowing an [electrostatic potential](../../../electromagnetism.md#electric-potential) locally, and globally when the domain permits one.

<h3 id="16d/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [16D](#16d)

<h4 id="16d/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#16d/b)

By cylindrical symmetry the [electric field](../../../electromagnetism.md#electric-field) of an infinite [line charge](../../../electromagnetism.md#line-charge) is radial, independent of $z$ and angle. Use a coaxial cylindrical [Gaussian surface](../../../electromagnetism.md#gaussian-surface) of radius $r$ and length $L$. The end caps have zero flux, so [Gauss's law](../../../electromagnetism.md#gauss-s-law) gives $2\pi rL E_r=\mu L/\varepsilon_0$. Hence **the field and electrostatic potential are**

$$
\boxed{\mathbf E=\frac{\mu}{2\pi\varepsilon_0r}\mathbf e_r,\qquad \phi(r)=-\frac{\mu}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\log\frac r{r_0}+C.}
$$

Here $r_0>0$ is an arbitrary reference length, and $\mathbf E=-\nabla\phi$. One cannot choose zero [electrostatic potential](../../../electromagnetism.md#electric-potential) at infinity for a single infinite [line charge](../../../electromagnetism.md#line-charge), because its logarithmic potential diverges there; the arbitrary reference handles the additive constant.

<h3 id="16d/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [16D](#16d)

<h4 id="16d/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#16d/c)

For the [conductor in electrostatic equilibrium](../../../electromagnetism.md#conductor-in-electrostatic-equilibrium), the interior [electric field](../../../electromagnetism.md#electric-field) is zero and the [electrostatic potential](../../../electromagnetism.md#electric-potential) is constant. Earthing fixes that constant to zero. Thus **the conductor boundary conditions** at $x=d$ are

$$
\boxed{\phi(d,y,z)=0,\qquad E_y(d^-,y,z)=E_z(d^-,y,z)=0.}
$$

The normal [electric field](../../../electromagnetism.md#electric-field) need not vanish; it is supported by a [surface charge density](../../../electromagnetism.md#surface-charge-density). The outward normal from this conductor into the exterior is $\mathbf n=-\mathbf e_x$, so the normal jump condition is

$$
\mathbf E(d^-)\cdot\mathbf n=\frac\sigma{\varepsilon_0},\qquad E_x(d^-)=-\frac\sigma{\varepsilon_0}.
$$

This sign convention will determine the sign of the induced [surface charge density](../../../electromagnetism.md#surface-charge-density) below.

<h3 id="16d/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [16D](#16d)

<h4 id="16d/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#16d/d)

The [method of images](../../../mathematics.md#method-of-images) uses the real [line charge](../../../electromagnetism.md#line-charge) at the origin and the opposite [image charge](../../../electromagnetism.md#image-charge) at $(2d,0)$. Let

$$
r_1=\sqrt{x^2+y^2},\qquad r_2=\sqrt{(x-2d)^2+y^2}.
$$

Superposing their logarithmic [electrostatic potentials](../../../electromagnetism.md#electric-potential) gives, in the physical region $x<d$,

$$
\boxed{\phi(x,y)=\frac{\mu}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\log\frac{r_2}{r_1}.}
$$

At $x=d$, $r_1=r_2$, so $\phi=0$. Its tangential derivatives also vanish on that plane, giving $E_y=E_z=0$, exactly the [electrostatic boundary conditions at a conductor](../../../electromagnetism.md#electrostatic-boundary-conditions-at-a-conductor). The image lies outside the physical domain, so it creates no extra physical source there. Together with the appropriate behaviour at infinity, [uniqueness of Poisson equation](../../../analysis.md#uniqueness-of-poisson-equation) identifies this field with the grounded-conductor solution.

<h3 id="16d/e">e</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [16D](#16d)

<h4 id="16d/e/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [E](#16d/e)

The [force](../../../classical-mechanics.md#force) on the real [line charge](../../../electromagnetism.md#line-charge) is due to the induced conductor field, represented by the [image charge](../../../electromagnetism.md#image-charge); its own singular field must be omitted. The opposite image at distance $2d$ produces at the origin a field toward the conductor,

$$
\mathbf E_{\rm image}(0)=\frac{\mu}{4\pi\varepsilon_0d}\mathbf e_x.
$$

Multiplying by the charge per unit length gives **the attractive force per unit length**:

$$
\boxed{\frac{\mathbf F}{L}=\frac{\mu^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0d}\mathbf e_x.}
$$

The attraction holds for either sign of $\mu$. The geometry assumes $d>0$, so the real line lies outside the conductor.

<h3 id="16d/f">f</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [16D](#16d)

<h4 id="16d/f/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [F](#16d/f)

Differentiating the [image charge](../../../electromagnetism.md#image-charge) potential gives

$$
E_x=\frac{\mu}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\left(\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}-\frac{x-2d}{(x-2d)^2+y^2}\right).
$$

At the conducting plane, $E_x(d^-)=\mu d/[\pi\varepsilon_0(d^2+y^2)]$. With outward conductor normal $-\mathbf e_x$, the [surface charge density](../../../electromagnetism.md#surface-charge-density) is therefore

$$
\boxed{\sigma(y,z)=-\varepsilon_0 E_x(d^-,y,z)=-\frac{\mu d}{\pi(d^2+y^2)}.}
$$

It is independent of $z$ by translational symmetry. As a check, integrating across the plane gives $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\sigma(y,z)\,dy=-\mu$, the total induced charge per unit length, opposite to the real [line charge](../../../electromagnetism.md#line-charge).

Let $\boldsymbol\omega=\nabla\times\mathbf u$ be the [vorticity](../../../fluid-mechanics.md#vorticity). In component notation, contracting two [Levi-Civita symbols](../../../calculus.md#levi-civita-symbol) gives

$$
(\mathbf u\times\boldsymbol\omega)_i=\varepsilon_{ijk}u_j\varepsilon_{klm}\partial_lu_m=u_j\partial_i u_j-u_j\partial_j u_i.
$$

The first term is $\partial_i(|\mathbf u|^2/2)$ and the second is the $i$th component of the [convective acceleration](../../../continuum-mechanics.md#convective-acceleration). Rearranging proves **the velocity identity**

$$
\boxed{(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u=\nabla\left(\tfrac12|\mathbf u|^2\right)-\mathbf u\times\boldsymbol\omega.}
$$

<h3 id="17c/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [17C](#17c)

<h4 id="17c/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#17c/b)

Parametrize a [streamline](../../../fluid-mechanics.md#streamline) by $\mathbf r(s)$ with $d\mathbf r/ds=\mathbf u(\mathbf r(s))$. The [chain rule](../../../calculus.md#chain-rule) gives

$$
\frac d{ds}H(\mathbf r(s))=\nabla H\cdot\frac{d\mathbf r}{ds}=\mathbf u\cdot\nabla H=0.
$$

Therefore **$H$ is constant along every streamline**. A different regular parametrization merely multiplies this derivative by a scalar and gives the same conclusion. For time-dependent flow, the claim concerns streamlines at a fixed time, rather than [Lagrangian trajectories](../../../continuum-mechanics.md#lagrangian-trajectory).

<h3 id="17c/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [17C](#17c)

<h4 id="17c/c/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#17c/c)

<h5 id="17c/c/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#17c/c/i)

Write the conservative body force per unit mass as $-\nabla\Phi$. For a fluid of constant density $\rho$, the [Euler equations for an inviscid fluid](../../../fluid-mechanics.md#euler-equations-for-an-inviscid-fluid) and the [incompressible flow](../../../fluid-mechanics.md#incompressible-flow) condition are

$$
\partial_t\mathbf u+(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u=-\nabla\left(\frac p\rho+\Phi\right),\qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0.
$$

Using part (a), these become

$$
\partial_t\mathbf u-\mathbf u\times\boldsymbol\omega=-\nabla H,\qquad H=\tfrac12|\mathbf u|^2+\frac p\rho+\Phi.
$$

For [irrotational flow](../../../fluid-mechanics.md#irrotational-flow), locally choose a [velocity potential](../../../fluid-mechanics.md#velocity-potential) with $\mathbf u=\nabla\phi$. Then $\nabla(\partial_t\phi+H)=0$, so **the unsteady Bernoulli integral** is

$$
\boxed{\partial_t\phi+\tfrac12|\mathbf u|^2+\frac p\rho+\Phi=C(t).}
$$

The constant is spatially uniform on a connected region where the [velocity potential](../../../fluid-mechanics.md#velocity-potential) is defined; its time dependence can be absorbed into the additive time-dependent gauge of $\phi$. A global single-valued [velocity potential](../../../fluid-mechanics.md#velocity-potential) requires an appropriate topological condition, such as a [simply connected](../../../algebraic-topology.md#simply-connected-space) domain; without it, the integral is locally valid. In a connected steady irrotational region, $\nabla H=0$ directly gives one spatial Bernoulli constant even without choosing a global potential.

<h4 id="17c/c/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#17c/c)

<h5 id="17c/c/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#17c/c/ii)

In [steady flow](../../../fluid-mechanics.md#steady-flow), the [Euler equations for an inviscid fluid](../../../fluid-mechanics.md#euler-equations-for-an-inviscid-fluid) and part (a) give

$$
\nabla H=\mathbf u\times\boldsymbol\omega,\qquad H=\tfrac12|\mathbf u|^2+\frac p\rho+\Phi.
$$

Taking the dot product with $\mathbf u$ yields $\mathbf u\cdot\nabla H=0$, because a [cross product](../../../vector-space.md#cross-product) is perpendicular to its first factor. Part (b) therefore gives **Bernoulli's quantity along streamlines**:

$$
\boxed{\tfrac12|\mathbf u|^2+\frac p\rho+\Phi=\text{constant on each streamline}.}
$$

For rotational flow these constants can differ between [streamlines](../../../fluid-mechanics.md#streamline), since $\mathbf u\times\boldsymbol\omega$ need not vanish and therefore $\nabla H$ need not be zero. Special rotational flows can still have one global constant when that cross product vanishes; the general conclusion is only constancy along individual [streamlines](../../../fluid-mechanics.md#streamline).

<h4 id="17c/c/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#17c/c)

<h5 id="17c/c/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#17c/c/iii)

Let $\mathbf v$ denote the velocity relative to the uniformly rotating frame, and write $r_\perp^2=x^2+y^2$. For rotation $\boldsymbol\Omega=\Omega\mathbf e_z$, the [Euler equations for an inviscid fluid](../../../fluid-mechanics.md#euler-equations-for-an-inviscid-fluid) in that frame are

$$
\partial_t\mathbf v+(\mathbf v\cdot\nabla)\mathbf v+2\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v=-\nabla\left(\frac p\rho+\Phi-\tfrac12\Omega^2r_\perp^2\right).
$$

The negative quadratic term is the [centrifugal potential](../../../physics.md#centrifugal-potential), whose negative gradient is the outward [centrifugal acceleration](../../../physics.md#centrifugal-acceleration) per unit mass. In [steady flow](../../../fluid-mechanics.md#steady-flow), dotting the equation with $\mathbf v$ eliminates the [Coriolis force](../../../physics.md#coriolis-force), since $\mathbf v\cdot(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v)=0$. The remaining acceleration term is $\mathbf v\cdot\nabla(|\mathbf v|^2/2)$. Thus

$$
\mathbf v\cdot\nabla\left(\tfrac12|\mathbf v|^2+\frac p\rho+\Phi-\tfrac12\Omega^2r_\perp^2\right)=0.
$$

By part (b), **the [steady rotating-frame Bernoulli integral](../../../fluid-mechanics.md#steady-rotating-frame-bernoulli-integral) is**

$$
\boxed{\tfrac12|\mathbf v|^2+\frac p\rho+\Phi-\tfrac12\Omega^2(x^2+y^2)=\text{constant on each relative streamline}.}
$$

Steadiness here is measured in the rotating frame, including the body-force potential used in that frame.

## 18D

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="18d/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [18D](#18d)

<h4 id="18d/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#18d/a)

[Convergence of a numerical method](../../../numerical-analysis.md#convergence-of-a-numerical-method) on a fixed interval $[0,T]$ means that, as the step size tends to zero and the starting approximations tend to the exact initial values,

$$
\boxed{\max_{0\leq t_n\leq T}|y_n-y(t_n)|\longrightarrow0.}
$$

For a vector-valued [ordinary differential equation](../../../differential-equation.md#ordinary-differential-equation), replace the absolute value by a [norm](../../../functional-analysis.md#norm). The interval remains fixed as $h\to0$; a small error at only one step is insufficient.

The [order of a numerical method](../../../numerical-analysis.md#order-of-a-numerical-method) is measured by its defect on a smooth exact solution. With the unscaled residual convention for a [linear multistep method](../../../numerical-analysis.md#linear-multistep-method), order at least $p$ means local residual $O(h^{p+1})$; equivalently, the residual divided by $h$ is $O(h^p)$. Order exactly $p$ means the next coefficient does not vanish in general. For a [zero-stable](../../../numerical-analysis.md#zero-stability) method, under the usual smoothness and [Lipschitz continuity](../../../real-analysis.md#lipschitz-continuity) assumptions and with starting errors $O(h^p)$, this yields [global error](../../../numerical-analysis.md#global-discretization-error) $O(h^p)$ on the fixed interval. Local order by itself does not imply convergence without stability.

<h3 id="18d/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [18D](#18d)

<h4 id="18d/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#18d/b)

Insert an exact solution into the [linear multistep method](../../../numerical-analysis.md#linear-multistep-method) and use $f(t,y(t))=y'(t)$. Its local residual is

$$
R_h(t)=\sum_{\ell=0}^s\rho_\ell y(t+\ell h)-h\sum_{\ell=0}^s\sigma_\ell y'(t+\ell h).
$$

Expanding both terms by [Taylor theorem](../../../calculus.md#taylor-theorem) gives

$$
R_h(t)=C_0y(t)+\sum_{k=1}^p\frac{h^k}{k!}C_k y^{(k)}(t)+O(h^{p+1}),\qquad C_0=\sum_\ell\rho_\ell,\quad C_k=\sum_\ell\ell^k\rho_\ell-k\sum_\ell\ell^{k-1}\sigma_\ell.
$$

Consequently $C_0=\cdots=C_p=0$ is sufficient for order at least $p$. It is also necessary: test the residual on the polynomials $y(t)=1,t,\ldots,t^p$, evaluating at $t=0$. For $y(t)=t^k$, it equals $h^k C_k$ (and equals $C_0$ for the constant), which can be $O(h^{p+1})$ only if $C_k=0$. These functions can be realized as exact solutions of suitable smooth [ordinary differential equations](../../../differential-equation.md#ordinary-differential-equation), so this is a genuine necessity test. Thus **the order conditions are**

$$
\boxed{\sum_{\ell=0}^s\rho_\ell=0,\qquad \sum_{\ell=0}^s\ell^k\rho_\ell=k\sum_{\ell=0}^s\ell^{k-1}\sigma_\ell\quad(1\leq k\leq p).}
$$

This is a statement about local order; [zero-stability](../../../numerical-analysis.md#zero-stability) is the separate condition that turns it into convergent approximation.

<h3 id="18d/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [18D](#18d)

<h4 id="18d/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#18d/c)

The [Dahlquist equivalence theorem](../../../numerical-analysis.md#dahlquist-equivalence-theorem) states that a [linear multistep method](../../../numerical-analysis.md#linear-multistep-method) has [convergence of a numerical method](../../../numerical-analysis.md#convergence-of-a-numerical-method) **if and only if it is consistent and zero-stable**, under the standard assumptions for the [initial value problem](../../../differential-equation.md#initial-value-problem) and convergent starting values. [Consistency of a numerical method](../../../numerical-analysis.md#consistency-of-a-numerical-method) means order at least one, or

$$
\rho(1)=0,\qquad \rho'(1)=\sigma(1),\quad \rho(\zeta)=\sum_{\ell=0}^s\rho_\ell\zeta^\ell,\quad \sigma(\zeta)=\sum_{\ell=0}^s\sigma_\ell\zeta^\ell.
$$

The [root condition for a multistep method](../../../numerical-analysis.md#root-condition-for-a-multistep-method) expresses [zero-stability](../../../numerical-analysis.md#zero-stability): every root of $\rho$ has modulus at most one, and every root with modulus one is simple. Usual hypotheses include a well-posed [ordinary differential equation](../../../differential-equation.md#ordinary-differential-equation) with $f$ continuous in time and [Lipschitz continuous](../../../real-analysis.md#lipschitz-continuity) in the solution variable, a valid step equation, and starting approximations converging to the exact values.

<h3 id="18d/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [18D](#18d)

<h4 id="18d/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#18d/d)

With $\rho_0=a$, $\rho_1=\theta$, $\rho_2=1$, the constant order condition gives $a=-1-\theta$. The next three [order conditions for a linear multistep method](../../../numerical-analysis.md#order-conditions-for-a-linear-multistep-method) give

$$
\theta+2=\sigma_0+\sigma_1+\sigma_2,\qquad \theta+4=2\sigma_1+4\sigma_2,\qquad \theta+8=3\sigma_1+12\sigma_2.
$$

Solving this linear system yields **the unique coefficients of order at least three**:

$$
\boxed{a=-1-\theta,\qquad \sigma_0=\frac{5\theta+4}{12},\quad \sigma_1=\frac{2(\theta+2)}3,\quad \sigma_2=\frac{4-\theta}{12}.}
$$

The characteristic polynomial controlling [zero-stability](../../../numerical-analysis.md#zero-stability) factors as

$$
\rho(\zeta)=\zeta^2+\theta\zeta-1-\theta=(\zeta-1)(\zeta+1+\theta).
$$

The other root is $-1-\theta$. Requiring its modulus to be at most one gives $-2\leq\theta\leq0$, but $\theta=-2$ produces a repeated root at one and violates the [root condition for a multistep method](../../../numerical-analysis.md#root-condition-for-a-multistep-method). At $\theta=0$, the roots $1,-1$ are both simple, so that endpoint is allowed. By the [Dahlquist equivalence theorem](../../../numerical-analysis.md#dahlquist-equivalence-theorem), **the method converges precisely for**

$$
\boxed{-2<\theta\leq0.}
$$

The fourth defect coefficient is $C_4=\theta$, so the method has order exactly three for nonzero $\theta$. At $\theta=0$ it is the two-step Simpson method, of order four; thus the extra-order endpoint has correctly remained in the answer.

## 19H

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="19h/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [19H](#19h)

<h4 id="19h/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#19h/a)

A [statistic](../../../statistical-inference.md#statistic) $T$ is a [sufficient statistic](../../../probability-and-statistics.md#sufficient-statistic) for $\theta$ if the conditional [distribution](../../../distribution-theory.md#distribution-mathematical-analysis) of the data given $T$ does not depend on $\theta$. For a dominated family with density or mass function $f_\theta(x)$, the [Fisher-Neyman factorization theorem](../../../probability-and-statistics.md#fisher-neyman-factorization-theorem) states that this is equivalent to

$$
\boxed{f_\theta(x)=g_\theta(T(x))h(x),}
$$

where $h$ is nonnegative and independent of $\theta$, and $g_\theta$ is nonnegative and depends on the data only through $T$.

For a discrete sample space, suppose first that the factorization holds. If $\mathbb P_\theta(T=t)>0$, summing over the fibre gives $\mathbb P_\theta(T=t)=g_\theta(t)\sum_{T(x)=t}h(x)$, so

$$
\mathbb P_\theta(X=x\mid T=t)=\frac{h(x)}{\sum_{T(z)=t}h(z)}\quad(T(x)=t).
$$

This conditional [distribution](../../../distribution-theory.md#distribution-mathematical-analysis) is independent of $\theta$, proving that $T$ is a [sufficient statistic](../../../probability-and-statistics.md#sufficient-statistic). Conversely, let $q_t(x)$ be the common conditional mass function guaranteed by the [sufficient statistic](../../../probability-and-statistics.md#sufficient-statistic) property, for every fibre attainable under at least one parameter. Set $h(x)=q_{T(x)}(x)$ and $g_\theta(t)=\mathbb P_\theta(T=t)$. Then $\mathbb P_\theta(X=x)=g_\theta(T(x))h(x)$. Fibres never attained under any parameter can be assigned any conditional distribution, since their multiplying probability is always zero. This proves both directions of the discrete criterion, including possible parameter-dependent supports.

<h3 id="19h/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [19H](#19h)

<h4 id="19h/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#19h/b)

The [Rao-Blackwell theorem](../../../probability-and-statistics.md#rao-blackwell-theorem) says that an unbiased finite-variance [estimator](../../../statistical-modelling.md#estimator) $\delta(X)$ of a parameter function $\tau(\theta)$ can be improved using a [sufficient statistic](../../../probability-and-statistics.md#sufficient-statistic) $T$. Define

$$
\delta^*(T)=\mathbb E[\delta(X)\mid T].
$$

Because the conditional [distribution](../../../distribution-theory.md#distribution-mathematical-analysis) given $T$ is parameter-independent, this is a statistic computable without knowing $\theta$. The [tower property of conditional expectation](../../../measure-theory.md#law-of-total-expectation) gives $\mathbb E_\theta\delta^*=\mathbb E_\theta\delta=\tau(\theta)$, so it remains unbiased. The [law of total variance](../../../probability-theory.md#law-of-total-variance) gives

$$
\operatorname{Var}_\theta(\delta)=\operatorname{Var}_\theta(\delta^*)+\mathbb E_\theta[\operatorname{Var}_\theta(\delta\mid T)].
$$

Hence **the improved estimator has no larger variance or mean squared error**:

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Var}_\theta(\delta^*)\leq\operatorname{Var}_\theta(\delta),\qquad \mathbb E_\theta[(\delta^*-\tau(\theta))^2]\leq\mathbb E_\theta[(\delta-\tau(\theta))^2].}
$$

Equality holds precisely when $\delta=\delta^*(T)$ almost surely under the relevant parameter. More generally, even without unbiasedness, [conditional Jensen inequality](../../../measure-theory.md#conditional-jensen-inequality) shows that conditioning on a sufficient statistic cannot increase risk for any convex loss in the estimate, provided the expectations exist. The squared-loss result above is the usual variance form of the theorem.

<h3 id="19h/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [19H](#19h)

<h4 id="19h/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#19h/c)

Write $L=\min_iX_i$, $R=\max_iX_i$ and $M=\max_i|X_i|=\max\{-L,R\}$. The joint [probability density](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#probability-density) factors as

$$
f_\theta(x)=(2\theta)^{-n}\mathbf1_{\{\theta\geq\max_i|x_i|\}}=(2\theta)^{-n}\mathbf1_{\{\theta\geq\max(-L,R)\}}.
$$

By the [Fisher-Neyman factorization theorem](../../../probability-and-statistics.md#fisher-neyman-factorization-theorem), **$T=(L,R)$ is a [sufficient statistic](../../../probability-and-statistics.md#sufficient-statistic)**. The same factorization shows that the smaller [statistic](../../../statistical-inference.md#statistic) $M$ is also a [sufficient statistic](../../../probability-and-statistics.md#sufficient-statistic). This gives the [sufficient statistic for a symmetric uniform sample](../../../probability-and-statistics.md#sufficient-statistic-for-a-symmetric-uniform-sample).

In fact, the [likelihood-ratio criterion for minimal sufficiency](../../../probability-and-statistics.md#likelihood-ratio-criterion-for-minimal-sufficiency) identifies $M$ as a [minimal sufficient statistic](../../../probability-and-statistics.md#minimal-sufficient-statistic): two sample points with the same $M$ have identical likelihoods for all $\theta$; with different $M$, their likelihoods have different parameter supports, and so are not proportional as functions of $\theta$. Equivalently, the condition that $f_\theta(x)=c f_\theta(y)$ for every $\theta$ and some positive constant $c$ holds exactly when their absolute maxima agree.

To see rigorously that $T$ cannot be recovered from $M$ almost surely, fix any $\theta>0$. The sample has a [distribution](../../../distribution-theory.md#distribution-mathematical-analysis) invariant under simultaneous sign reversal. If $T=h(M)$ almost surely, the same identity would hold for the sign-reversed sample on another probability-one set. Since $M$ is unchanged by sign reversal, it would follow that

$$
(L,R)=(-R,-L)\quad\text{almost surely},
$$

and hence $L=-R$ almost surely. But this event has probability zero for a finite sample from a continuous [uniform distribution](../../../continuous-probability-distribution.md#continuous-uniform-distribution): it entails $X_i=-X_j$ for some pair of indices, including the possible case $X_i=0$. Therefore $T$ is not a function of the sufficient statistic $M$. **The pair of extrema is not minimally sufficient**, for every $n\geq1$, even though it is sufficient.

<h3 id="20h/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [20H](#20h)

<h4 id="20h/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#20h/a)

A [Markov chain](../../../markov-process.md#markov-chain) is [irreducible](../../../representation-theory.md#irreducible-representation) if every state can reach every other state in finitely many steps with positive probability. It is a [recurrent Markov chain](../../../markov-process.md#recurrent-markov-chain) if, starting at any state, it returns to that state with probability one; for an irreducible chain this property is shared by all states.

For the [simple symmetric random walk](../../../probability-theory.md#simple-symmetric-random-walk) on $\mathbb Z$, taking $|j-i|$ steps all in the appropriate direction reaches $j$ from $i$ with probability $2^{-|j-i|}>0$. Thus the chain is irreducible. Its return probabilities are

$$
p_{00}^{(2k)}=\binom{2k}{k}2^{-2k},\qquad p_{00}^{(2k+1)}=0.
$$

By [Stirling formula](../../../real-analysis.md#stirling-formula), $p_{00}^{(2k)}\sim1/\sqrt{\pi k}$, so their sum diverges. The standard recurrence criterion $\sum_{n\geq0}p_{00}^{(n)}=\infty$ therefore proves **the walk is recurrent**.

The printed hint's constant is erroneous: the actual limit of $\sqrt{k}\,p_{00}^{(2k)}$ is $1/\sqrt\pi$. Its positivity is what the recurrence argument requires, but the reciprocal is necessary for the correct numerical value.

<h3 id="20h/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [20H](#20h)

<h4 id="20h/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#20h/b)

An irreducible [Markov chain](../../../markov-process.md#markov-chain) has [positive recurrent states](../../../markov-process.md#positive-recurrent-state) if the expected first return time to a state is finite; this then holds for every state. A recurrent chain with infinite mean return time has [null recurrent states](../../../markov-process.md#null-recurrent-state).

An irreducible positive recurrent chain admits a [stationary distribution](../../../markov-process.md#stationary-distribution). If such a distribution $\pi$ existed for this walk, its stationarity equations would give

$$
\pi_j=\tfrac12(\pi_{j-1}+\pi_{j+1})\quad(j\in\mathbb Z).
$$

Thus $\pi_{j+1}-\pi_j=\pi_j-\pi_{j-1}$, so $\pi_j=A+Bj$. Nonnegativity for all positive and negative integers forces $B=0$. A constant sequence cannot sum to one over $\mathbb Z$, so no stationary probability distribution exists. Hence **the walk is null recurrent, not positive recurrent**: all its states are [null recurrent states](../../../markov-process.md#null-recurrent-state). The first-return generating function in part (c) also verifies directly that its mean return time is infinite.

<h3 id="20h/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [20H](#20h)

<h4 id="20h/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#20h/c)

Let $u_n=\mathbb P_0(X_n=0)$ and $f_n=\mathbb P_0(T=n)$. Decomposing at the first return and using the [Markov property](../../../markov-process.md#markov-property) gives the [renewal equation](../../../probability-theory.md#renewal-equation)

$$
u_0=1,\qquad u_n=\sum_{k=1}^n f_k u_{n-k}\quad(n\geq1).
$$

For $0<s<1$, define the [probability generating functions](../../../probability-theory.md#probability-generating-function) $U(s)=\sum_{n\geq0}u_ns^n$ and $F(s)=\sum_{n\geq1}f_ns^n=\mathbb E_0[s^T]$. Absolute convergence, or nonnegative summation, justifies the convolution identity $U=1+FU$. The [binomial series](../../../real-analysis.md#binomial-series) gives

$$
U(s)=\sum_{k\geq0}\binom{2k}{k}\left(\frac{s^2}{4}\right)^k=\frac1{\sqrt{1-s^2}}.
$$

Solving the renewal identity gives **the [first-return generating function of the simple symmetric random walk](../../../probability-theory.md#first-return-generating-function-of-the-simple-symmetric-random-walk)**

$$
\boxed{\mathbb E_0[s^T]=F(s)=1-\sqrt{1-s^2},\qquad 0<s<1.}
$$

As consistency checks, $F(s)\to1$ as $s\uparrow1$, confirming recurrence, while $F'(s)=s/\sqrt{1-s^2}\to\infty$. By monotone convergence of $\sum_n n f_n s^{n-1}$, this last limit is $\mathbb E_0T=\infty$, confirming [null recurrent states](../../../markov-process.md#null-recurrent-state).
