**Transmission spectroscopy, eclipse/emission spectroscopy and direct-imaging spectroscopy** probe atmospheres in complementary geometries.

A [exoplanet transmission spectrum](../../../../../../exoplanet-transmission-spectrum.md) uses the wavelength dependence of primary-transit depth to measure slant-path absorption at the limb. Its long path length gives sensitivity to trace absorbers, but only transiting systems qualify; [exoplanet clouds](../../../../../../exoplanet-cloud.md), stellar heterogeneity and the reference-radius/abundance degeneracy complicate interpretation, and the result samples the terminator rather than the dayside.

An [exoplanet secondary eclipse](../../../../../../exoplanet-secondary-eclipse.md) isolates the dayside thermal/reflected spectrum by removing the planet behind the star. It constrains [brightness temperatures](../../../../../../brightness-temperature.md), molecules and [thermal inversions](../../../../../../inversion-meteorology.md), but the planet/star contrast is small, a suitable orbital geometry is needed, and temperature-abundance degeneracies remain. A [exoplanet thermal phase curve](../../../../../../exoplanet-thermal-phase-curve.md) extends this method to longitudinal information.

[Exoplanet direct imaging](../../../../../../exoplanet-direct-imaging.md) spatially separates a planet from its star and permits repeated spectroscopy without requiring a transit. It is effective for young, luminous, widely separated giant planets, but demands high contrast and angular separation; old small planets close to their stars are difficult. Inferred gravities, radii and masses can depend strongly on atmosphere and evolutionary models.
