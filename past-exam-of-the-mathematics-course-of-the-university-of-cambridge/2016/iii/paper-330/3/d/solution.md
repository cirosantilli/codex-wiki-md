<h1 id="3/d/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [D](../d.md)

For a steady no-source [particle-laden gravity current](../../../../../../particle-laden-gravity-current.md), $B_x=-v_sg'<0$. Simply entraining particle-free ambient fluid increases volume and dilutes [particle volume fraction](../../../../../../particle-volume-fraction.md); it cannot create particle [buoyancy flux](../../../../../../buoyancy-flux.md).

A steady increase can occur if [sediment resuspension](../../../../../../sediment-resuspension.md) or bed erosion adds more suspended particle buoyancy than settling removes, if a tributary or lateral particle source supplies a positive flux, or if the buoyancy per unit particle amount increases through a change of the surrounding fluid's density. Changes of particle properties or another buoyancy source can also alter the flux, but they go beyond the fixed-particle, fixed-reference model. In a model with a source $S_B$,

$$
\partial_t(g'h)+B_x=S_B-v_sg'.
$$

Thus the [buoyancy production in a particle-laden current](../../../../../../buoyancy-production-in-a-particle-laden-current.md) requires $S_B>v_sg'$ in a steady current. In an unsteady current, $B_x$ can be positive even without external addition: local depletion has $\partial_t(g'h)<-v_sg'$, so more buoyancy leaves a section than enters it while stored particles are being released. **An instantaneous spatial flux increase is not by itself proof of particle production.**
