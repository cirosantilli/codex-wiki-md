<h1 id="1/ii/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](../ii.md)

If the gas can cool appreciably below the [virial temperature](../../../../../../virial-temperature.md), [radiative cooling](../../../../../../radiative-cooling.md) removes thermal energy and pressure support. In a [dark matter halo](../../../../../../dark-matter-halo.md) the gas then contracts, dissipating more energy as it falls. Efficient condensation requires the [radiative gas cooling time](../../../../../../optically-thin-gas-cooling-time.md) to be short enough compared with the relevant dynamical or assembly time; merely having an available low-temperature transition does not guarantee that the gas reaches it quickly.

The collisionless [dark matter](../../../../../../dark-matter.md) cannot lose comparable energy through radiation and remains extended. Gas with appreciable conserved [angular momentum](../../../../../../angular-momentum.md) stops radial collapse when rotation supports it, often forming a disk; lower-angular-momentum gas reaches a more compact central region. Cold dense gas can fragment into self-gravitating clouds and form stars if its gravitational instability overcomes remaining support. [Stellar feedback](../../../../../../stellar-feedback.md) subsequently reheats or expels gas and regulates the conversion. **Efficient cooling enables central baryonic condensation and star formation; angular momentum and feedback determine the resulting galaxy.**
