<h1 id="2/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [2](../2.md)

The [method of multiple scales](../../../../../method-of-multiple-scales.md) introduces independent variables for the fast oscillation and its slow modulation, for example $t_0=t$, $T=\epsilon t$, and, when needed, $T_2=\epsilon^2t$. The derivative becomes $d/dt=\partial_{t_0}+\epsilon\partial_T+\epsilon^2\partial_{T_2}+\cdots$. One expands the solution in $\epsilon$ while allowing its leading [amplitudes](../../../../../wave-amplitude.md) and phases to depend on the slow times.

A [solvability condition in the method of multiple scales](../../../../../solvability-condition-in-the-method-of-multiple-scales.md) removes resonant forcing of each fast homogeneous mode. Otherwise a correction contains a [secular term](../../../../../secular-term.md), such as $\epsilon t\sin t$, and an ordinary [asymptotic expansion](../../../../../asymptotic-expansion.md) fails on long times. The resulting slow [amplitude](../../../../../wave-amplitude.md) equations incorporate that accumulated effect in the leading solution. One must still check that the [amplitudes](../../../../../wave-amplitude.md) remain within the assumed weak-coupling regime and that omitted slower effects have not accumulated.
