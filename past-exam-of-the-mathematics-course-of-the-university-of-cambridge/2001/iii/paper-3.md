<h3 id="1/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

For [subgroups](../../../group.md#subgroup) $A,B$ write $[A,B]$ for the [subgroup](../../../group.md#subgroup) generated by their [group commutators](../../../group.md#group-commutator). A [nilpotent group](../../../group-theory.md#nilpotent-group) has a [central series](../../../group-theory.md#central-series): a finite chain $1=G_0\leq G_1\leq\cdots\leq G_c=G$ with $[G_i,G]\leq G_{i-1}$. Equivalently its [upper central series](../../../group-theory.md#upper-central-series), defined by $Z_0=1$ and $Z_{i+1}/Z_i=Z(G/Z_i)$, reaches $G$. Indeed every [central series](../../../group-theory.md#central-series) term $G_i$ lies in $Z_i$ by induction, and the [upper central series](../../../group-theory.md#upper-central-series) itself is central. A third equivalent definition is that the [lower central series](../../../group-theory.md#lower-central-series) $\gamma_1=G$, $\gamma_{i+1}=[\gamma_i,G]$ terminates at $\gamma_{c+1}=1$: reversing this series gives a [central series](../../../group-theory.md#central-series), and induction along any [central series](../../../group-theory.md#central-series) gives $\gamma_{c+1}=1$.

The least such $c$ is the [nilpotency class](../../../group-theory.md#nilpotency-class). Nontrivial [abelian groups](../../../group.md#abelian-group) have class one. Upper-unitriangular $3\times3$ [matrices](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) have class two: their [group commutators](../../../group.md#group-commutator) lie in the central top-right-entry [subgroup](../../../group.md#subgroup), and further [group commutators](../../../group.md#group-commutator) vanish. The same example works over a [finite field](../../../algebra.md#finite-field) or over the integers, so nilpotence is not a finiteness property. [Subgroups](../../../group.md#subgroup) and quotients inherit nilpotence by intersecting or mapping a [central series](../../../group-theory.md#central-series); finite [direct products of groups](../../../group-theory.md#direct-product-of-groups) have class at most the largest factor class. Every [nilpotent group](../../../group-theory.md#nilpotent-group) is soluble, since its [derived series](../../../group-theory.md#derived-series) is contained successively in the [lower central series](../../../group-theory.md#lower-central-series). The converse fails: $S_3$ is soluble but its center is trivial, so it cannot be a nontrivial [nilpotent group](../../../group-theory.md#nilpotent-group). Extensions of [nilpotent groups](../../../group-theory.md#nilpotent-group) need not be nilpotent, as the extension $C_3\triangleleft S_3$ with quotient $C_2$ shows.

To prove the finite classification, first note that every nontrivial [finite p-group](../../../finite-group-theory.md#finite-p-group) has nontrivial center. In its [class equation](../../../group-theory.md#class-equation), every noncentral [conjugacy class](../../../group-theory.md#conjugacy-class) has size divisible by $p$, so the center's order is divisible by $p$. Induct on the [group](../../../group.md) order: the smaller p-group $P/Z(P)$ is nilpotent, and lifting its [central series](../../../group-theory.md#central-series) and adjoining $Z(P)$ proves $P$ nilpotent. A finite [direct product of groups](../../../group-theory.md#direct-product-of-groups) of prime-power-order [groups](../../../group.md) is consequently nilpotent.

Conversely suppose $G$ is finite and nilpotent. We need the [normalizer condition for nilpotent groups](../../../group-theory.md#normalizer-condition-for-nilpotent-groups). For any proper [subgroup](../../../group.md#subgroup) $H<G$, choose the first upper-central term $Z_i$ not contained in $H$. Then $Z_{i-1}\leq H$. Every $z\in Z_i$ commutes with $H$ modulo $Z_{i-1}$, so it normalizes $H$. Choosing $z\notin H$ proves $H<N_G(H)$.

Let $P$ be a [Sylow subgroup](../../../finite-group-theory.md#sylow-subgroup) and put $M=N_G(P)$. Since $P$ is normal in $M$, it is the unique Sylow p-subgroup of $M$. Any element normalizing $M$ must therefore normalize $P$, so $N_G(M)=M$. If $M$ were proper, the [normalizer](../../../group-theory.md#normalizer) condition would contradict this equality. Hence $N_G(P)=G$: every [Sylow subgroup](../../../finite-group-theory.md#sylow-subgroup) is normal. For [Sylow subgroups](../../../finite-group-theory.md#sylow-subgroup) $P,Q$ belonging to different primes, $[P,Q]\leq P\cap Q=1$, so they commute. Their product has order the product of their orders, namely $|G|$, and has trivial intersections between each factor and the product of the others. Multiplication therefore gives

$$
\boxed{G\cong\prod_{p\mid |G|}P_p.}
$$

This proves [finite nilpotent group decomposition](../../../group-theory.md#finite-nilpotent-group-decomposition) in both directions without assuming the classification in the [normalizer](../../../group-theory.md#normalizer) argument. It also shows that a [finite group](../../../group.md#finite-group) is nilpotent exactly when all its [Sylow subgroups](../../../finite-group-theory.md#sylow-subgroup) are normal. The finite hypothesis is essential to the prime-power direct-product statement; an infinite [cyclic group](../../../group.md#cyclic-group) is nilpotent but is not such a finite product.

A [soluble group](../../../group-theory.md#solvable-group) is one whose [derived series](../../../group-theory.md#derived-series) terminates: $G^{(0)}=G$, $G^{(i+1)}=[G^{(i)},G^{(i)}]$, and $G^{(r)}=1$ for some finite $r$. Each factor of this series is abelian. Conversely, if a subnormal series has abelian factors, repeated commutation moves down the series, so the [derived series](../../../group-theory.md#derived-series) terminates. This explains the usual alternative definition in terms of abelian factors. The least $r$ is the derived length.

[Subgroups](../../../group.md#subgroup) are soluble because $H^{(i)}\leq G^{(i)}$; quotients are soluble because a [group homomorphism](../../../group-theory.md#group-homomorphism) maps a derived [subgroup](../../../group.md#subgroup) onto the derived [subgroup](../../../group.md#subgroup) of its image. Solubility is also closed under extensions: if $N\triangleleft G$ has derived length at most $a$ and $G/N$ has length at most $b$, then $G^{(b)}\leq N$ and $G^{(a+b)}=1$. Abelian and [nilpotent groups](../../../group-theory.md#nilpotent-group) are soluble; $S_3$ has derived [subgroup](../../../group.md#subgroup) $A_3$ and derived length two. In a [finite group](../../../group.md#finite-group), solubility is equivalent to every composition factor being cyclic of prime order: simple [soluble groups](../../../group-theory.md#solvable-group) are abelian and therefore cyclic of prime order, while a composition series with these factors builds a [soluble group](../../../group-theory.md#solvable-group) by extensions. This need not give an ambient-normal series with cyclic prime-order factors, which is the stronger property of supersolubility.

A [Hall subgroup](../../../group.md#hall-subgroup) for a prime set $\pi$ is a [subgroup](../../../group.md#subgroup) $H$ whose order uses only primes in $\pi$ and whose index uses only primes outside $\pi$. Its order is the full $\pi$-part of $|G|$. Sylow's theorem is the singleton-prime case; solubility allows all prime sets, although [Hall subgroups](../../../group.md#hall-subgroup) need not be normal, as the order-two [subgroups](../../../group.md#subgroup) of $S_3$ show.

Prove [Hall subgroup existence in soluble groups](../../../group.md#hall-subgroup-existence-in-soluble-groups) by induction on $|G|$. The trivial [group](../../../group.md) is immediate. Choose a nontrivial [minimal normal subgroup](../../../group-theory.md#minimal-normal-subgroup) $N$. It is elementary abelian of some prime characteristic $p$: $N'$ is characteristic in $N$ and normal in $G$, so minimality and solubility force $N'=1$. A nontrivial [Sylow subgroup](../../../finite-group-theory.md#sylow-subgroup) of this finite [abelian group](../../../group.md#abelian-group) is characteristic, making $N$ a p-group. The [subgroup](../../../group.md#subgroup) of pth powers is characteristic and proper, so minimality makes it trivial. This proves [minimal normal subgroups of finite solvable groups are elementary abelian](../../../group-theory.md#minimal-normal-subgroups-of-finite-solvable-groups-are-elementary-abelian).

By induction $G/N$ has a Hall $\pi$-subgroup $\overline H$. Let $K$ be its inverse image. Then $[G:K]$ is a $\pi'$-number. If $p\in\pi$, the whole $K$ is a $\pi$-group and is the desired [Hall subgroup](../../../group.md#hall-subgroup). If $p\notin\pi$, $K/N=Q=\overline H$ has order $s$ prime to $p$. We now prove the required complement existence explicitly, rather than invoking a splitting theorem as a substitute.

Write $N$ additively as an $\mathbb F_p$-vector space. Choose representatives $u(x)$ for $x\in Q$ with $u(1)=1$. Conjugation gives a well-defined action of $Q$ on $N$, because changing a representative by an element of the [abelian group](../../../group.md#abelian-group) $N$ does not change that action. Define $f(x,y)\in N$ by $u(x)u(y)=f(x,y)u(xy)$. Associativity yields

$$
f(x,y)+f(xy,z)=x\cdot f(y,z)+f(x,yz).
$$

Since $s$ is invertible in $\mathbb F_p$, set $c(x)=s^{-1}\sum_{z\in Q}f(x,z)$. Sum the identity over $z$ and use the bijection $z\mapsto yz$ to obtain

$$
f(x,y)=c(x)+x\cdot c(y)-c(xy).
$$

Changing representatives to $u'(x)=(-c(x))u(x)$ removes the multiplication error:

$$
u'(x)u'(y)=u'(xy).
$$

Thus $x\mapsto u'(x)$ is a homomorphic section. Its image $H$ has order $|Q|$, intersects $N$ trivially, and complements $N$ in $K$. This is [coprime splitting over an elementary abelian normal subgroup](../../../group-theory.md#coprime-splitting-over-an-elementary-abelian-normal-subgroup). Finally $|H|$ is a $\pi$-number and $[G:H]=[G:K]|N|$ is a $\pi'$-number, so

$$
\boxed{\text{every finite soluble }G\text{ has a Hall }\pi\text{-subgroup for every prime set }\pi.}
$$

Only existence was needed here; the fuller Hall theorem also gives conjugacy and containment of arbitrary $\pi$-subgroups. Solubility makes the elementary-abelian minimal-normal induction possible and cannot simply be omitted.

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

An [group automorphism](../../../algebra.md#group-automorphism) preserves element orders and sizes of [conjugacy classes](../../../group-theory.md#conjugacy-class). A nonidentity involution in $S_n$ has [cycle type](../../../finite-group-theory.md#cycle-type) $2^k1^{n-2k}$ and class size

$$
\frac{n!}{2^kk!(n-2k)!},\qquad 1\leq k\leq\lfloor n/2\rfloor.
$$

For its class to have the same size as the [transposition](../../../combinatorics.md#transposition-permutation) class, it must satisfy

$$
\frac{(n-2)!}{(n-2k)!}=2^{k-1}k!.
$$

For fixed $k\geq2$ the left side grows strictly with $n$. When $k=2$, it is $(n-2)(n-3)$, which is two at $n=4$ and at least six at $n=5$, never the required four. For $k=3$ equality first occurs at $n=6$, since $4!=2^2\cdot3!$, and thereafter the left side is larger. For $k\geq4$ it is already larger at $n=2k$: the ratio $(2k-2)!/[2^{k-1}k!]$ is $15/4$ at $k=4$, and increases with $k$, its successive ratio being $k(2k-1)/(k+1)>1$. This proves the [symmetric-group involution class-size collision](../../../finite-group-theory.md#symmetric-group-involution-class-size-collision) result. Therefore, for $n\ne6$, every [group automorphism](../../../algebra.md#group-automorphism) maps [transpositions](../../../combinatorics.md#transposition-permutation) to [transpositions](../../../combinatorics.md#transposition-permutation).

Now recover the underlying points from [transpositions](../../../combinatorics.md#transposition-permutation). Two distinct [transpositions](../../../combinatorics.md#transposition-permutation) fail to commute exactly when their two-element supports meet. A maximal family of pairwise intersecting edges of a [complete graph](../../../graph-theory.md#complete-graph) is either a vertex star or a triangle. Indeed, after selecting edges $\{1,2\}$ and $\{1,3\}$, every edge either contains 1 or is $\{2,3\}$; if the latter occurs, all edges lie in that triangle. For $n\geq5$, the stars have size $n-1\geq4$ and triangles size three, so stars are recognized purely by the multiplication structure of $S_n$.

An [group automorphism](../../../algebra.md#group-automorphism) must thus permute the stars, giving a [permutation](../../../combinatorics.md#permutation) $\tau$ of the points. The unique [transposition](../../../combinatorics.md#transposition-permutation) shared by stars $i,j$ is $(i\ j)$, so its image is $(\tau(i)\ \tau(j))$. Since [transpositions](../../../combinatorics.md#transposition-permutation) generate $S_n$, the whole [group automorphism](../../../algebra.md#group-automorphism) is conjugation by $\tau$.

At $n=4$, stars and triangles both have three elements, but their generated [subgroups](../../../group.md#subgroup) distinguish them: the [transpositions](../../../combinatorics.md#transposition-permutation) in a star generate all of $S_4$ (since $(i\ j)=(a\ i)(a\ j)(a\ i)$ for its center $a$), while a triangle generates the $S_3$ fixing the fourth point. The same reconstruction follows. At $n=3$, conjugation by $S_3$ induces every [permutation](../../../combinatorics.md#permutation) of its three [transpositions](../../../combinatorics.md#transposition-permutation) (a [transposition](../../../combinatorics.md#transposition-permutation) swaps the other two, and a three-cycle rotates all three), so a transposition-preserving [group automorphism](../../../algebra.md#group-automorphism) is again inner. At $n=2$ the only [group automorphism](../../../algebra.md#group-automorphism) of $C_2$ is the identity, and $n=0,1$ are trivial (if the empty-set degree is included). Thus

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Out}(S_n)=1\quad\text{for }n\ne6.}
$$

The small-degree adjustments are necessary: a cardinality-only star argument would not cover $n=4$.

<h3 id="3/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

Use the six-point matching geometry. A [duad](../../../combinatorics.md#duad) is an edge of the [complete graph](../../../graph-theory.md#complete-graph) on six points, a [syntheme](../../../combinatorics.md#syntheme) is a [perfect matching](../../../graph-theory.md#perfect-matching) of its three pairs, and a [pentad](../../../combinatorics.md#total-of-synthemes) is a one-factorization: five pairwise edge-disjoint [synthemes](../../../combinatorics.md#syntheme) covering all fifteen [duads](../../../combinatorics.md#duad).

First count the [pentads](../../../combinatorics.md#total-of-synthemes) without assuming their number. There are fifteen [synthemes](../../../combinatorics.md#syntheme): the partner of the first point has five choices, the least remaining point then has three possible partners, and the last pair is forced. For a fixed [syntheme](../../../combinatorics.md#syntheme) $M$, eight others share no edge with it: [inclusion-exclusion](../../../combinatorics.md#inclusion-exclusion-principle) gives $15-3\cdot3+3\cdot1-1=8$. Two edge-disjoint [synthemes](../../../combinatorics.md#syntheme) $M,M'$ have union a six-cycle. The complement of that cycle is a triangular prism, consisting of two triangles with three corresponding cross edges. It has precisely four [perfect matchings](../../../graph-theory.md#perfect-matching): the all-cross matching and three matchings using one cross edge plus one edge from each triangle. The all-cross matching leaves two odd triangles and therefore cannot be completed by two matchings. The other three matchings are pairwise edge-disjoint and partition the prism. Hence $M,M'$ extend to exactly one [pentad](../../../combinatorics.md#total-of-synthemes).

Each [pentad](../../../combinatorics.md#total-of-synthemes) through $M$ uses four of its eight disjoint partners, and every partner gives exactly one completion. Thus $M$ lies in two [pentads](../../../combinatorics.md#total-of-synthemes). Counting incidences gives $15\cdot2/5=6$ [pentads](../../../combinatorics.md#total-of-synthemes) in total. Distinct [pentads](../../../combinatorics.md#total-of-synthemes) cannot share two [synthemes](../../../combinatorics.md#syntheme) because two disjoint [synthemes](../../../combinatorics.md#syntheme) determine their unique completion. Every [syntheme](../../../combinatorics.md#syntheme) therefore identifies a distinct pair of [pentads](../../../combinatorics.md#total-of-synthemes); there are fifteen such pairs, so this incidence map is a bijection.

Permuting the six original points permutes the six [pentads](../../../combinatorics.md#total-of-synthemes), defining a [group homomorphism](../../../group-theory.md#group-homomorphism) $\Phi:S_6\to\operatorname{Sym}(\text{pentads})$. It is faithful. If an element fixes every [pentad](../../../combinatorics.md#total-of-synthemes), it fixes every [syntheme](../../../combinatorics.md#syntheme) by the pair-incidence bijection. A [duad](../../../combinatorics.md#duad) is the unique common edge of two distinct [synthemes](../../../combinatorics.md#syntheme) containing it, so every [duad](../../../combinatorics.md#duad) is fixed as well. A [permutation](../../../combinatorics.md#permutation) fixing all two-element subsets fixes every point, by intersecting $\{i,j\}$ and $\{i,k\}$ for distinct $i,j,k$. The [kernel of a group homomorphism](../../../group-theory.md#kernel-of-a-group-homomorphism) is therefore trivial. Both [groups](../../../group.md) have order $6!$, so labeling the [pentads](../../../combinatorics.md#total-of-synthemes) produces an [group automorphism](../../../algebra.md#group-automorphism) of $S_6$.

It remains to prove this [group automorphism](../../../algebra.md#group-automorphism) is outer. A [transposition](../../../combinatorics.md#transposition-permutation), say $(1\ 2)$, fixes no [pentad](../../../combinatorics.md#total-of-synthemes). If it fixed one, the unique [syntheme](../../../combinatorics.md#syntheme) in that [pentad](../../../combinatorics.md#total-of-synthemes) containing the [duad](../../../combinatorics.md#duad) $\{1,2\}$ would be fixed. Every other [syntheme](../../../combinatorics.md#syntheme) $M'$ in the [pentad](../../../combinatorics.md#total-of-synthemes) contains 1 and 2 in separate edges. Swapping them changes those two edges but leaves the third edge unchanged. Hence $(1\ 2)M'$ is a distinct [syntheme](../../../combinatorics.md#syntheme) sharing an edge with $M'$, impossible for two members of the same [pentad](../../../combinatorics.md#total-of-synthemes). Thus $(1\ 2)$ acts without fixed points on the six [pentads](../../../combinatorics.md#total-of-synthemes) and becomes a product of three disjoint [transpositions](../../../combinatorics.md#transposition-permutation).

[Inner automorphisms](../../../group-theory.md#inner-automorphism) preserve [cycle type](../../../finite-group-theory.md#cycle-type), whereas $\Phi$ sends a single [transposition](../../../combinatorics.md#transposition-permutation) to a triple [transposition](../../../combinatorics.md#transposition-permutation). Consequently

$$
\boxed{\Phi\text{ is an outer automorphism of }S_6.}
$$

This realizes the exceptional class-size coincidence from part (a). It is the full symmetric-group version of the [pentad construction of the exceptional alternating-group automorphism](../../../combinatorics.md#pentad-construction-of-the-exceptional-alternating-group-automorphism), with faithfulness proved directly from incidence rather than borrowed from a simplicity theorem.

Choose $\alpha\in\Omega$. Since $G$ acts regularly, $g\mapsto g\alpha$ identifies $\Omega$ with the underlying set of $G$, and the given action becomes left multiplication $L_g(x)=gx$.

A [permutation](../../../combinatorics.md#permutation) $c$ centralizing all $L_g$ satisfies

$$
c(x)=c(L_x(1))=L_x(c(1))=xc(1).
$$

Conversely every right translation commutes with every left translation. To make the multiplication convention explicit, define $R_g(x)=xg^{-1}$. Then $R_gR_h(x)=xh^{-1}g^{-1}=x(gh)^{-1}=R_{gh}(x)$, using rightmost-first composition. The map $g\mapsto R_g$ is injective and its image is the entire [centralizer](../../../group-theory.md#centralizer), since any possible $c(1)$ can be written $g^{-1}$. Thus

$$
\boxed{C_{\operatorname{Sym}(\Omega)}(G)\cong G.}
$$

This is [centralizer of a regular permutation subgroup](../../../group-theory.md#centralizer-of-a-regular-permutation-subgroup). If one instead writes right multiplication as $x\mapsto xg$, the resulting map is an antihomomorphism; including the inverse avoids suppressing that reversal. Choosing a different base point changes the identification but not the isomorphism type.

<h3 id="4/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#4)

<h4 id="4/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#4/b)

Retain the identification with the [left regular action](../../../group-theory.md#left-regular-action). A [group](../../../group.md) [group automorphism](../../../algebra.md#group-automorphism) $\varphi$ of $G$ satisfies $\varphi L_g\varphi^{-1}=L_{\varphi(g)}$, so it normalizes $L(G)$ and fixes the identity point.

Conversely let a normalizing [permutation](../../../combinatorics.md#permutation) $n$ fix the identity. Its conjugation on $L(G)$ induces a [group](../../../group.md) [group automorphism](../../../algebra.md#group-automorphism) $\varphi$ characterized by $nL_gn^{-1}=L_{\varphi(g)}$. Evaluating at the identity gives $n(g)=\varphi(g)$, so the whole identity stabilizer in the [normalizer](../../../group-theory.md#normalizer) is exactly $\operatorname{Aut}(G)$. For arbitrary $n$, put $t=n(1)$. Then $L_t^{-1}n$ fixes the identity and is an [group automorphism](../../../algebra.md#group-automorphism); thus every element has a unique expression $L_t\varphi$.

The translation [subgroup](../../../group.md#subgroup) is normal in its [normalizer](../../../group-theory.md#normalizer), its intersection with the [group automorphism](../../../algebra.md#group-automorphism) [subgroup](../../../group.md#subgroup) is trivial, and the multiplication rule is

$$
(L_t\varphi)(L_u\psi)=L_{t\varphi(u)}\varphi\psi.
$$

Consequently

$$
\boxed{N_{\operatorname{Sym}(\Omega)}(G)\cong G\rtimes\operatorname{Aut}(G)=\operatorname{Hol}(G).}
$$

The action in this [semidirect product](../../../group-theory.md#semidirect-product) is the natural evaluation of [group automorphisms](../../../algebra.md#group-automorphism) on $G$. This [normalizer](../../../group-theory.md#normalizer) is the [holomorph](../../../group-theory.md#holomorph), realized as all [permutations](../../../combinatorics.md#permutation) $x\mapsto t\varphi(x)$. The decomposition is established from the [regular group action](../../../group-theory.md#regular-group-action) itself rather than only from an order comparison.

<h3 id="4/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#4)

<h4 id="4/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#4/c)

Choose $\alpha\in\Omega$ and identify $\Omega$ with $N$ by $n\mapsto n\alpha$. Normality means that the [point stabilizer](../../../group-theory.md#stabilizer-subgroup) $H_\alpha$ acts on $N$ by conjugation, and

$$
h(n\alpha)=(hnh^{-1})\alpha\qquad(h\in H_\alpha).
$$

Double transitivity therefore says that $H_\alpha$ is transitive on $N\setminus\{1\}$. All nonidentity elements of $N$ have the same order. By [Cauchy's theorem for finite groups](../../../finite-group-theory.md#cauchy-theorem-for-groups), some element has order a prime $p$ dividing $|N|$, so that common order is $p$. Cauchy's theorem excludes every other prime from $|N|$, hence $N$ is a finite p-group.

By the center argument from Q1, $Z(N)\ne1$. The center is characteristic in $N$ and therefore invariant under the conjugation action of $H_\alpha$. Its nonidentity elements form a nonempty invariant subset of the transitive set $N\setminus\{1\}$, so $Z(N)=N$. Thus $N$ is abelian of exponent $p$:

$$
\boxed{N\cong(\mathbb F_p^d,+)\quad\text{for some }d\geq1.}
$$

This is [elementary abelian regular kernels in doubly transitive groups](../../../group-theory.md#elementary-abelian-regular-kernels-in-doubly-transitive-groups); knowing only that all element orders agree would not by itself prove commutativity.

Every $h\in H$ is uniquely $nh_0$ with $n\in N$ and $h_0\in H_\alpha$, because regularity supplies the unique $n$ sending $\alpha$ to $h\alpha$. Hence $H=N\rtimes H_\alpha$. The conjugation action of $H_\alpha$ on $N$ is faithful: an element centralizing $N$ and fixing $\alpha$ fixes every $n\alpha$, and is the identity [permutation](../../../combinatorics.md#permutation). [Group automorphisms](../../../algebra.md#group-automorphism) of an elementary abelian p-group are exactly invertible $\mathbb F_p$-linear maps, so $H_\alpha\leq GL(d,p)$. Choosing a vector-space [basis](../../../vector-space.md#basis) therefore gives

$$
\boxed{H\hookrightarrow\mathbb F_p^d\rtimes GL(d,p)=AGL(d,p).}
$$

Under this embedding, $N$ is the translation [subgroup](../../../group.md#subgroup) and the [point stabilizer](../../../group-theory.md#stabilizer-subgroup) is a linear [subgroup](../../../group.md#subgroup) transitive on nonzero [vectors](../../../vector-space.md#vector). The action is assumed to have at least two points, as usual for a doubly transitive [group](../../../group.md).

## 5

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="5/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#5)

<h4 id="5/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#5/a)

The orbits of a [normal subgroup](../../../group-theory.md#normal-subgroup) form a [block system](../../../group-theory.md#block-system): $g(N\omega)=N(g\omega)$, so every [group](../../../group.md) element permutes these orbits. By primitivity the orbits are either all singletons or one whole orbit. In the singleton case $N$ fixes every point, giving $N\leq G_{[\Omega]}$.

In the other case $N$ is transitive. For each $g\in G$ choose $n\in N$ with $n\alpha=g\alpha$. Then $n^{-1}g\in G_\alpha$, so $G=NG_\alpha$. Work in the quotient $G/N$. If $g=nh$ with $h\in G_\alpha$, the image of $A^g=g^{-1}Ag$ is the image of $A^h$, which equals the image $\overline A$ of $A$ because $A\triangleleft G_\alpha$. All conjugates of $A$ thus have the same image in $G/N$.

By hypothesis those conjugates generate $G$, so the quotient is generated by the single [subgroup](../../../group.md#subgroup) $\overline A$, and equals it. Since $A$ is abelian, $G/N$ is abelian. The [kernel of a group homomorphism](../../../group-theory.md#kernel-of-a-group-homomorphism) of a [group homomorphism](../../../group-theory.md#group-homomorphism) to an [abelian group](../../../group.md#abelian-group) contains the [commutator subgroup](../../../group-theory.md#commutator-subgroup), yielding

$$
\boxed{G'\leq N\quad\text{or}\quad N\leq G_{[\Omega]}.}
$$

This is the normal-subgroup argument behind the [Iwasawa simplicity lemma](../../../finite-group-theory.md#iwasawa-simplicity-lemma). Faithfulness is not needed for this version: retaining the pointwise [kernel of a group homomorphism](../../../group-theory.md#kernel-of-a-group-homomorphism) as the second alternative is essential. In a faithful [primitive group action](../../../group-theory.md#primitive-group-action) it says every nontrivial [normal subgroup](../../../group-theory.md#normal-subgroup) contains $G'$.

<h3 id="5/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#5)

<h4 id="5/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#5/b)

For distinct coordinates $i,j$, let $E_{ij}(t)=I+t e_{ij}$, where $e_{ij}$ is the [matrix unit](../../../vector-space.md#matrix-unit). For $t\ne0$ this is a [transvection](../../../vector-space.md#transvection): it fixes the [hyperplane](../../../vector-space.md#hyperplane) with $j$th coordinate zero pointwise, has [determinant](../../../linear-algebra.md#determinant) one and inverse $E_{ij}(-t)$. We show these [elementary transvection matrices](../../../vector-space.md#elementary-transvection-matrix) generate $SL(n,F)$ over any [field](../../../algebra.md#field).

Let $M\in SL(n,F)$. Row addition is left multiplication by an $E_{ij}(t)$. If the current diagonal pivot is zero, a nonzero entry occurs below it in the same remaining column, since the remaining square block is invertible; adding that row makes the pivot nonzero. Row additions then clear the entries below the pivot. Repeating produces an upper-triangular [matrix](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) with nonzero diagonal. Clearing the entries above the diagonal, starting from the last column, gives a [diagonal matrix](../../../linear-algebra.md#diagonal-matrix) $D=\operatorname{diag}(a_1,\ldots,a_n)$ with $\prod_i a_i=1$. Thus it remains to realize determinant-one [diagonal matrices](../../../linear-algebra.md#diagonal-matrix) using elementary [transvections](../../../vector-space.md#transvection).

In a two-coordinate block put

$$
w(a)=E_{12}(a)E_{21}(-a^{-1})E_{12}(a)
=\begin{pmatrix}0&a\\-a^{-1}&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Direct multiplication gives

$$
\boxed{w(a)w(-1)=\operatorname{diag}(a,a^{-1}).}
$$

The [diagonal matrix](../../../linear-algebra.md#diagonal-matrix) $D$ is the product, for $i=1,\ldots,n-1$, of these blocks on coordinates $i,n$, with entries $a_i,a_i^{-1}$. Their final $n$th entry is $\prod_{i<n}a_i^{-1}=a_n$. Therefore $D$, and hence $M$, is a product of elementary [transvections](../../../vector-space.md#transvection). All row operations used are invertible [transvections](../../../vector-space.md#transvection), and no division except by a nonzero pivot or $a_i$ has been made. This proves

$$
\boxed{SL(n,F)=\langle E_{ij}(t):i\ne j,\ t\in F\rangle.}
$$

The identity element $E_{ij}(0)$ can be omitted from the generating set. This is [transvections generate the special linear group](../../../vector-space.md#transvections-generate-the-special-linear-group); ordinary row scaling alone would not prove it, because such a scaling can leave $SL(n,F)$.

<h3 id="5/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#5)

<h4 id="5/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#5/c)

Let $V=F^n$ and let $G=PSL(n,F)$ act on its one-dimensional subspaces. First identify the [kernel of a group homomorphism](../../../group-theory.md#kernel-of-a-group-homomorphism) in $SL(n,F)$. A [matrix](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) fixing every coordinate line is diagonal, and fixing every line $\langle e_i+e_j\rangle$ forces all diagonal entries equal. Thus the projective [kernel of a group homomorphism](../../../group-theory.md#kernel-of-a-group-homomorphism) is $\{\lambda I:\lambda^n=1\}$. These scalars are central. Conversely a central [matrix](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) commutes with all $E_{ij}(1)$: comparing entries in $Me_{ij}=e_{ij}M$ shows all off-diagonal entries of $M$ vanish and all diagonal entries agree. Therefore the [kernel of a group homomorphism](../../../group-theory.md#kernel-of-a-group-homomorphism) is exactly $Z(SL(n,F))$. This proves [scalar kernel of the projective linear action](../../../group-theory.md#scalar-kernel-of-the-projective-linear-action) and faithfulness of the induced $G$-action.

The action is doubly transitive. Given two ordered pairs of distinct lines, choose two independent representatives of each pair and extend them to [bases](../../../vector-space.md#basis). A [linear map](../../../vector-space.md#linear-map) taking the first [basis](../../../vector-space.md#basis) to the second sends the desired lines correctly. If its [determinant](../../../linear-algebra.md#determinant) is $d\ne1$, postcompose with a diagonal map in the target [basis](../../../vector-space.md#basis) that scales its first [basis](../../../vector-space.md#basis) [vector](../../../vector-space.md#vector) by $d^{-1}$ and fixes all the others. It preserves the two target lines and corrects the [determinant](../../../linear-algebra.md#determinant). Thus an element of $SL(n,F)$ sends either ordered pair to the other. Double transitivity implies primitivity: a block containing two distinct points must contain every point by the transitive point-stabilizer action.

For $\alpha=\langle e_1\rangle$, consider

$$
U=\{I+e_1\varphi:\varphi\in V^*,\ \varphi(e_1)=0\}.
$$

Products add the functionals, so $U$ is abelian. If $g$ fixes $\alpha$, write $ge_1=ae_1$. Then $g(I+e_1\varphi)g^{-1}=I+e_1(a\varphi g^{-1})$, and the new functional still vanishes on $e_1$. Thus the image $A$ of $U$ in $G$ is normal in the [point stabilizer](../../../group-theory.md#stabilizer-subgroup). Its conjugates contain all elementary [transvections](../../../vector-space.md#transvection): changing the center line $\langle e_1\rangle$ to $\langle e_i\rangle$ gives every map $I+e_i\psi$ with $\psi(e_i)=0$, including $E_{ij}(t)$. Part (b) then shows that those conjugates generate $G$.

It remains to prove perfectness, not assume it. Use $[x,y]=x^{-1}y^{-1}xy$. For $n\geq3$, distinct $i,j,k$ give

$$
[E_{ik}(t),E_{kj}(1)]=E_{ij}(t).
$$

Every generator is therefore a [group commutator](../../../group.md#group-commutator), so $SL(n,F)'=SL(n,F)$. For $n=2$ and $|F|>3$, choose $a\in F^\times$ with $a^2\ne1$, possible because a quadratic polynomial has at most two roots. With $D=\operatorname{diag}(a,a^{-1})$,

$$
[D,E_{12}(t)]=E_{12}((1-a^{-2})t),\qquad
[D,E_{21}(t)]=E_{21}((1-a^2)t).
$$

Both coefficients are nonzero, so every upper and lower elementary [transvection](../../../vector-space.md#transvection) is again a [group commutator](../../../group.md#group-commutator). Part (b) proves perfectness in this case as well. Quotients of perfect [groups](../../../group.md) are perfect, hence $G'=G$ in exactly the stated range.

Apply part (a) to the faithful primitive $G$-action and its abelian normal point-stabilizer [subgroup](../../../group.md#subgroup) $A$. Every nontrivial [normal subgroup](../../../group-theory.md#normal-subgroup) contains $G'=G$, so it is the whole [group](../../../group.md). A [transvection](../../../vector-space.md#transvection) is not scalar, showing $G\ne1$. We have proved

$$
\boxed{PSL(n,F)\text{ is simple for }n\geq3,\text{ or }n=2\text{ with }|F|>3.}
$$

The proof works for infinite [fields](../../../algebra.md#field) too and uses no finite-order count. The excluded two-dimensional [fields](../../../algebra.md#field) give the familiar soluble exceptions $PSL(2,2)\cong S_3$ and $PSL(2,3)\cong A_4$.

<h3 id="6/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#6)

<h4 id="6/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#6/a)

Let $q$ be a prime power and let $V=\mathbb F_q^{2m}$ carry a [nondegenerate](../../../linear-algebra.md#nondegenerate-bilinear-form) [alternating bilinear form](../../../linear-algebra.md#alternating-bilinear-form) $B$. The [symplectic group over a finite field](../../../finite-group-theory.md#symplectic-group-over-a-finite-field) is

$$
Sp(V,B)=\{g\in GL(V):B(gv,gw)=B(v,w)\text{ for all }v,w\}.
$$

In a [symplectic basis](../../../linear-algebra.md#symplectic-basis) its [matrix](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) description is $g^{\mathsf T}Jg=J$, where $J=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&I_m\\-I_m&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$. The definition is valid in characteristic two: alternating means $B(v,v)=0$, and then the form is also symmetric.

Count ordered [symplectic bases](../../../linear-algebra.md#symplectic-basis) $(e_1,f_1,\ldots,e_m,f_m)$ with $B(e_i,f_j)=\delta_{ij}$ and all $e$-$e$ and $f$-$f$ pairings zero. There are $q^{2m}-1$ choices for $e_1\ne0$. Nondegeneracy makes $v\mapsto B(e_1,v)$ a nonzero [linear functional](../../../linear-algebra.md#linear-functional), so there are $q^{2m-1}$ choices for $f_1$ satisfying $B(e_1,f_1)=1$.

The span $P=\langle e_1,f_1\rangle$ is [nondegenerate](../../../linear-algebra.md#nondegenerate-bilinear-form). Thus $V=P\oplus P^\perp$, and its [bilinear orthogonal complement](../../../linear-algebra.md#orthogonal-complement-for-a-bilinear-form) is [nondegenerate](../../../linear-algebra.md#nondegenerate-bilinear-form) of dimension $2m-2$. For completeness, a [vector](../../../vector-space.md#vector) in the [radical of a bilinear form](../../../linear-algebra.md#radical-of-a-bilinear-form) of $P^\perp$ is orthogonal both to $P$ and to $P^\perp$, hence to all $V$ and therefore zero. Recursing constructs and counts every [symplectic basis](../../../linear-algebra.md#symplectic-basis). If $b_m$ is their number, $b_m=(q^{2m}-1)q^{2m-1}b_{m-1}$ with $b_0=1$.

The [symplectic group over a finite field](../../../finite-group-theory.md#symplectic-group-over-a-finite-field) acts freely and transitively on [symplectic bases](../../../linear-algebra.md#symplectic-basis): there is a unique [linear map](../../../vector-space.md#linear-map) sending one ordered [basis](../../../vector-space.md#basis) to another, and its equality of [basis](../../../vector-space.md#basis) pairings ensures it preserves $B$. Its order is consequently $b_m$, giving

$$
\boxed{|Sp(2m,q)|=\prod_{i=1}^mq^{2i-1}(q^{2i}-1)=q^{m^2}\prod_{i=1}^m(q^{2i}-1).}
$$

The exponent is $1+3+\cdots+(2m-1)=m^2$. The counting is valid for every prime power $q$, including $q=2$.

<h3 id="6/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#6)

<h4 id="6/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#6/b)

Identify a subset $A\subseteq\Omega$ with its indicator [vector](../../../vector-space.md#vector) $a=(a_1,\ldots,a_n)\in\mathbb F_2^n$. [Symmetric difference](../../../set.md#symmetric-difference) becomes coordinatewise addition modulo two, so the subsets form a [vector space](../../../vector-space.md) of dimension $n$, with the singleton subsets as a [basis](../../../vector-space.md#basis). The proposed form becomes the dot product

$$
B(a,b)=\sum_{i=1}^na_ib_i\in\mathbb F_2.
$$

Distributivity proves bilinearity, and commutativity proves symmetry. Its [Gram matrix](../../../linear-algebra.md#gram-matrix) in the singleton [basis](../../../vector-space.md#basis) is $I_n$, so it is [nondegenerate](../../../linear-algebra.md#nondegenerate-bilinear-form). A [permutation](../../../combinatorics.md#permutation) merely permutes coordinates and preserves the dot product, proving invariance under $S_n$.

Write $u=(1,\ldots,1)$ for the full subset. Its [bilinear orthogonal complement](../../../linear-algebra.md#orthogonal-complement-for-a-bilinear-form) $E=u^\perp$ consists exactly of even-cardinality subsets. If $n\geq2$ is even, $B(u,u)=n=0$ in $\mathbb F_2$, so $\langle u\rangle\leq E$. For any $v\in E$, $B(v,v)=\sum_iv_i^2=\sum_iv_i=0$, so the restricted form is alternating.

Nondegeneracy of the full form gives $E^\perp=\langle u\rangle$; one can also see this directly by testing against all [vectors](../../../vector-space.md#vector) $e_i+e_j$, which forces all coordinates of an element of $E^\perp$ to be equal. Hence the [radical of a bilinear form](../../../linear-algebra.md#radical-of-a-bilinear-form) of the restricted form is

$$
\operatorname{rad}(B|_E)=E\cap E^\perp=\langle u\rangle.
$$

Define the quotient form by $\overline B(v+\langle u\rangle,w+\langle u\rangle)=B(v,w)$. Adding $u$ to either representative does not change the pairing with $E$, so it is well-defined. It is alternating, and a class pairing to zero with all classes has a representative in $\operatorname{rad}(B|_E)=\langle u\rangle$, hence is zero. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\langle\Omega\rangle^\perp/\langle\Omega\rangle\text{ is a nondegenerate alternating space of dimension }n-2.}
$$

Both spaces are $S_n$-invariant, so the coordinate action descends and preserves the quotient form. This is the [symplectic quotient of the binary subset module](../../../finite-group-theory.md#symplectic-quotient-of-the-binary-subset-module). The evenness assumption is essential because otherwise $u$ does not lie in its own [bilinear orthogonal complement](../../../linear-algebra.md#orthogonal-complement-for-a-bilinear-form).

<h3 id="6/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#6)

<h4 id="6/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#6/c)

Set $n=6$ in part (b). The quotient $W=u^\perp/\langle u\rangle$ has dimension four and a [nondegenerate](../../../linear-algebra.md#nondegenerate-bilinear-form) alternating form. The coordinate action therefore defines

$$
\rho:S_6\longrightarrow Sp(W)\cong Sp(4,2).
$$

Prove it is faithful before comparing orders. If a [permutation](../../../combinatorics.md#permutation) lies in the [kernel of a group homomorphism](../../../group-theory.md#kernel-of-a-group-homomorphism), it fixes the quotient class of each two-element subset $A$. That class has exactly the two representatives $A$ and $A+\Omega=\Omega\setminus A$. Their cardinalities are two and four. A coordinate [permutation](../../../combinatorics.md#permutation) preserves cardinality, so it must fix $A$ itself, not exchange it with its complement. Thus it fixes every two-element subset. Intersecting the fixed sets $\{i,j\}$ and $\{i,k\}$ shows it fixes each singleton $\{i\}$, so it is the identity. Hence $\rho$ is injective.

By part (a),

$$
|Sp(4,2)|=2^4(2^2-1)(2^4-1)=16\cdot3\cdot15=720=6!.
$$

An injection between [finite groups](../../../group.md#finite-group) of equal order is surjective. Choosing a [symplectic basis](../../../linear-algebra.md#symplectic-basis) of $W$ identifies the form with the standard one, so

$$
\boxed{Sp(4,2)\cong S_6.}
$$

This realizes the exceptional isomorphism through a concrete binary [permutation](../../../combinatorics.md#permutation) module. Equality of orders alone would not establish it; the faithful form-preserving action supplies the required [group homomorphism](../../../group-theory.md#group-homomorphism).
