<h1 id="3/b/iii/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](../iii.md)

The [cubic amplitude saturation](../../../../../../../cubic-amplitude-saturation.md) equation $R'=R(Q^2-R^2)$ and the [Picard-Lindelöf theorem](../../../../../../../picard-lindelof-theorem.md) imply that positive solutions cannot cross the equilibrium $Q$. If $0<R_0<Q$, then $R$ stays below $Q$ and increases. If $R_0>Q$, then it stays above $Q$ and decreases. The explicit [cubic amplitude saturation](../../../../../../../cubic-amplitude-saturation.md) solution tends to $Q$ in both cases, so

$$
\boxed{|\psi_R(x,t)-\psi_S(x,t)|=|R(t)-Q|\downarrow0.}
$$

The same relation makes the distance a decreasing [monotone function](../../../../../../../monotonic-function.md) in any spatial [norm](../../../../../../../norm.md) for which the plane wave has finite norm, for example on a periodic domain. At large time, $R-Q\sim-\tfrac Q2(Q^2/R_0^2-1)e^{-2Q^2t}$.

The printed monotonicity wording must be interpreted as **monotone amplitude relaxation, or monotone decay of the distance between the waves**. [Complex numbers](../../../../../../../complex-number.md) have no ordinary monotone ordering, and the [real part](../../../../../../../real-part.md) at a fixed point can oscillate. For instance, $k=U=1$, $\mu=2$ and $R_0=1/2$ give $\operatorname{Re}\psi_R(0,t)=R(t)\cos t$, which is not monotone despite the monotone amplitude. This result concerns the common-phase single-mode family, not stability against arbitrary spatial perturbations.
