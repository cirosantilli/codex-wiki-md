<h1 id="1/c/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [C](../c.md)

A natural interpretation of the short-wavelength peak is reflected starlight, while the longer-wavelength peak is planetary [thermal radiation](../../../../../../thermal-radiation.md). A reflected spectrum approximately follows the stellar spectrum multiplied by the wavelength-dependent [geometric albedo](../../../../../../geometric-albedo.md); thermal [radiative flux](../../../../../../radiative-flux.md) approximately follows the planet's [Planck function](../../../../../../planck-function.md), modulated by molecular [opacity](../../../../../../opacity.md). Thus two peaks need not represent two planetary surface [temperatures](../../../../../../temperature.md).

For a quantitative estimate assume both are broad $F_\lambda$ peaks, reflection has a slowly varying [geometric albedo](../../../../../../geometric-albedo.md), and star and planet have approximately [blackbody](../../../../../../blackbody.md) spectral envelopes. [Wien's displacement law](../../../../../../wien-s-displacement-law.md) then gives

$$
T_*\simeq\frac{2898\,\mu\mathrm m\,\mathrm K}{0.7\,\mu\mathrm m}
\simeq4140\,\mathrm K,\qquad
T_p\simeq\frac{2898\,\mu\mathrm m\,\mathrm K}{1.5\,\mu\mathrm m}
\simeq1930\,\mathrm K.
$$

The stellar estimate is compatible at order of magnitude with an old, relatively small main-sequence star; equal age does not mean equal [temperature](../../../../../../temperature.md) to the [Sun](../../../../../../sun.md). Assume the far-infrared signal is thermal and sufficiently long-wavelength that the [Rayleigh-Jeans law](../../../../../../rayleigh-jeans-law.md) applies to both bodies. The [planet-star radius estimate in the Rayleigh-Jeans limit](../../../../../../planet-star-radius-estimate-in-the-rayleigh-jeans-limit.md) gives

$$
\frac{F_p}{F_*}=\left(\frac{R_p}{R_*}\right)^2\frac{T_p}{T_*},\qquad
R_p=\frac{R_\odot}{2}\sqrt{10^{-3}\frac{4140}{1930}},
$$

and therefore

$$
\boxed{R_p\simeq0.023\,R_\odot\simeq1.6\times10^7\,\mathrm m
\simeq2.5\,R_\oplus.}
$$

This is a small volatile-rich-planet-sized estimate, not a Jupiter-sized one. It is conditional: peaks caused by molecular windows, strongly chromatic reflection, or a spectrum expressed as $F_\nu$ rather than $F_\lambda$ do not support those two Wien estimates. Without $T_p/T_*$, the far-infrared ratio only fixes $R_p^2T_p/T_*$.

For a close-in hot planet around a small star, transit-based atmospheric observations are the natural route if the orbital geometry allows them. [Exoplanet transmission spectra](../../../../../../exoplanet-transmission-spectrum.md) gain from the small stellar [radius](../../../../../../radius.md), with a limb signal scaling as $2R_pH/R_*^2$; they probe composition at the [day-night terminator](../../../../../../day-night-terminator.md). The stated $10^{-3}$ thermal contrast also makes [exoplanet secondary eclipse](../../../../../../exoplanet-secondary-eclipse.md) measurements a particularly useful route to the dayside [exoplanet emission spectrum](../../../../../../exoplanet-emission-spectrum.md). Resolving such a close-in small planet by [exoplanet direct imaging](../../../../../../exoplanet-direct-imaging.md) is much harder. **Transit and secondary-eclipse spectroscopy are favoured for a transiting close-in interpretation; the supplied spectrum alone does not determine the orbital geometry or a unique best technique.**
