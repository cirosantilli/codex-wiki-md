<h3 id="1/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1/a)

The [spectrum of a commutative ring](../../../ringed-space.md#spectrum-of-a-commutative-ring) is the set

$$
\operatorname{Spec}R=\{\mathfrak p\subsetneq R:\mathfrak p\text{ is a prime ideal}\}.
$$

For an [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) $I\subseteq R$, set $V(I)=\{\mathfrak p:I\subseteq\mathfrak p\}$. The [Zariski topology](../../../algebraic-geometry.md#zariski-topology) declares precisely these subsets to be [closed sets](../../../topology.md#closed-set). Using arbitrary subsets instead of [ideals](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) gives the same definition, because a [prime ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) contains a subset exactly when it contains the [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) generated by that subset.

We verify the [closed set](../../../topology.md#closed-set) axioms directly. First, $V(0)=\operatorname{Spec}R$ and $V(R)=\varnothing$. For any family of [ideals](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal),

$$
\bigcap_{\lambda}V(I_\lambda)=V\left(\sum_{\lambda}I_\lambda\right),
$$

since containing the sum is equivalent to containing every summand. For two [ideals](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal),

$$
V(I)\cup V(J)=V(IJ).
$$

Indeed, containing either $I$ or $J$ implies containing their [product of ideals](../../../commutative-algebra.md#product-of-ideals). Conversely, if a [prime ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) $\mathfrak p$ contains $IJ$ but does not contain $I$, choose $a\in I\setminus\mathfrak p$. For every $b\in J$, $ab\in\mathfrak p$, so the [prime ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) property forces $b\in\mathfrak p$. Thus $J\subseteq\mathfrak p$.

The displayed identities establish arbitrary intersections and finite unions of [closed sets](../../../topology.md#closed-set), including their empty cases. Therefore **these sets define a topology on $\operatorname{Spec}R$**.

<h3 id="1/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

The induced map on the [spectrum of a commutative ring](../../../ringed-space.md#spectrum-of-a-commutative-ring) is [contraction of an ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#contraction-of-an-ideal):

$$
\boxed{f(\mathfrak q)=\varphi^{-1}(\mathfrak q).}
$$

To see that it is well defined, let $\mathfrak q$ be a [prime ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) of $S$. Its inverse image is an [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) of $R$, and it is proper because $\varphi(1)=1\notin\mathfrak q$. If $ab\in\varphi^{-1}(\mathfrak q)$, then $\varphi(a)\varphi(b)\in\mathfrak q$, whence $\varphi(a)\in\mathfrak q$ or $\varphi(b)\in\mathfrak q$. This proves that the inverse image is a [prime ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal).

For any [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) $I\subseteq R$, its [extension of an ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#extension-of-an-ideal) to $S$ is $IS=\varphi(I)S$. We have

$$
f^{-1}(V_R(I))=\{\mathfrak q:I\subseteq\varphi^{-1}(\mathfrak q)\}=V_S(IS).
$$

Thus inverse images of [Zariski-closed sets](../../../algebraic-geometry.md#zariski-closed-set) are [Zariski-closed sets](../../../algebraic-geometry.md#zariski-closed-set), which proves that **$f$ is a continuous map**.

<h3 id="1/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#1/c)

We first establish the [nilradical as the intersection of prime ideals](../../../commutative-algebra.md#nilradical-as-the-intersection-of-prime-ideals):

$$
\operatorname{Nil}(A)=\bigcap_{\mathfrak p\in\operatorname{Spec}A}\mathfrak p.
$$

Every [nilpotent element](../../../commutative-algebra.md#nilpotent) belongs to every [prime ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal). In the other direction, if $a$ is not nilpotent, the [multiplicative subset](../../../commutative-algebra.md#multiplicatively-closed-set) $T=\{1,a,a^2,\ldots\}$ avoids zero. By the [Zorn lemma](../../../set-theory.md#zorn-s-lemma), there is an [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) $J$ maximal among those disjoint from $T$. It is a [prime ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal): if $uv\in J$ and neither $u$ nor $v$ belongs to $J$, then $J+(u)$ and $J+(v)$ each meet $T$. Multiplying elements in these two intersections gives an element of $T$ lying in $J$, a contradiction. Since $a\notin J$, this proves the identity. The identity also holds for the zero [ring](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ring), with the intersection over an empty family interpreted as the whole [ring](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ring).

A [Zariski-closed set](../../../algebraic-geometry.md#zariski-closed-set) $V_R(I)$ contains the image of $f$ exactly when

$$
I\subseteq\bigcap_{\mathfrak q\in\operatorname{Spec}S}\varphi^{-1}(\mathfrak q)
=\varphi^{-1}(\operatorname{Nil}(S))
=\sqrt{\ker\varphi}.
$$

The last equality follows because $\varphi(r)$ is a [nilpotent element](../../../commutative-algebra.md#nilpotent) exactly when $r^n\in\ker\varphi$ for some $n\geq1$. Consequently the smallest [Zariski-closed set](../../../algebraic-geometry.md#zariski-closed-set) containing the image, namely its [closure](../../../topology.md#closure-topology), is

$$
\overline{f(\operatorname{Spec}S)}=V_R(\sqrt{\ker\varphi})=V_R(\ker\varphi).
$$

This is all of $\operatorname{Spec}R$ precisely when the [kernel of a ring homomorphism](../../../commutative-algebra.md#kernel-of-a-ring-homomorphism) is contained in every [prime ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) of $R$. Hence

$$
\boxed{f(\operatorname{Spec}S)\text{ is dense in }\operatorname{Spec}R
\iff\ker\varphi\subseteq\operatorname{Nil}(R).}
$$

<h3 id="1/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#1/d)

Here the coefficient [fields](../../../algebra.md#field) are $\mathbb R$ and $\mathbb C$. A [polynomial ring](../../../commutative-algebra.md#polynomial-ring) in one variable over a [field](../../../algebra.md#field) is a [Euclidean domain](../../../commutative-algebra.md#euclidean-domain), hence a [principal ideal domain](../../../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal-domain); its nonzero [prime ideals](../../../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) are generated by [irreducible polynomials](../../../polynomial.md#irreducible-polynomial). The [fundamental theorem of algebra](../../../algebra.md#fundamental-theorem-of-algebra) says that all [irreducible polynomials](../../../polynomial.md#irreducible-polynomial) over $\mathbb C$ are linear. Combining it with [complex conjugation](../../../complex-analysis.md#complex-conjugation) shows that the [monic](../../../polynomial.md#monic-polynomial) [irreducible polynomials](../../../polynomial.md#irreducible-polynomial) over $\mathbb R$ are exactly the linear polynomials and quadratics with no real root. Thus

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb R[x]
=\{(0)\}\cup\{(x-a):a\in\mathbb R\}
\cup\{((x-a)^2+b^2):a\in\mathbb R,\ b>0\}.}
$$

Every nonzero [prime ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) in this list is a [maximal ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#maximal-ideal); $(0)$ is a [prime ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) because $\mathbb R[x]$ is an [integral domain](../../../commutative-algebra.md#integral-domain). Likewise,

$$
\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb C[x]=\{(0)\}\cup\{(x-z):z\in\mathbb C\}.
$$

The map is [contraction of an ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#contraction-of-an-ideal) along the inclusion. It sends $(0)$ to $(0)$. For $z=a+ib$, the contraction of $(x-z)$ consists of the real polynomials vanishing at $z$. If $b=0$, the [factor theorem](../../../polynomial.md#factor-theorem) gives the [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) $(x-a)$. If $b\ne0$, a real polynomial vanishing at $z$ also vanishes at its [complex conjugate](../../../complex-analysis.md#complex-conjugate) $\overline z$, so it is divisible by $(x-z)(x-\overline z)=(x-a)^2+b^2$. Division by this real quadratic gives the converse and hence

$$
\boxed{f((x-z))=
\begin{cases}
(x-a),&z=a\in\mathbb R,\\
((x-a)^2+b^2),&z=a+ib,\ b\ne0.
\end{cases}}
$$

Thus each real linear [prime ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) has one preimage, each real quadratic [prime ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) has the two conjugate preimages $(x-a-ib)$ and $(x-a+ib)$, and $(0)$ has only $(0)$ above it. In particular, $f$ is surjective.

<h3 id="2/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#2/a)

Let $T=R\setminus P$. Since $P$ is a [prime ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal), $T$ is a [multiplicative subset](../../../commutative-algebra.md#multiplicatively-closed-set) containing $1$ and excluding $0$. The [localization at a prime ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#localization-at-a-prime-ideal) is

$$
\boxed{R_P=T^{-1}R.}
$$

Concretely, its elements are equivalence classes of pairs $(r,s)\in R\times T$, written $r/s$, with

$$
\frac r s=\frac {r'}{s'}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
u(s'r-sr')=0\text{ for some }u\in T.
$$

This extra multiplier is essential when $R$ has [zero divisors](../../../mathematics.md#zero-divisor). Addition and multiplication are

$$
\frac r s+\frac {r'}{s'}=\frac{rs'+r's}{ss'},\qquad
\frac r s\frac {r'}{s'}=\frac{rr'}{ss'}.
$$

The equivalence criterion is compatible with these operations: after multiplying by the multipliers witnessing changes of representatives, the corresponding numerators agree. The [commutative ring](../../../commutative-algebra.md#commutative-ring) axioms follow from those of $R$; the identity is $1/1$.

The canonical [ring homomorphism](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ring-homomorphism) $\iota:R\to R_P$, $r\mapsto r/1$, supplies the [algebra over a commutative ring](../../../commutative-algebra.md#algebra-over-a-commutative-ring) structure, with scalar action $a(r/s)=ar/s$. It also has the [universal property of localization](../../../commutative-algebra.md#universal-property-of-localization): whenever a [ring homomorphism](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ring-homomorphism) $\psi:R\to A$ sends $T$ to [units](../../../algebra.md#unit-in-a-ring), the unique extension is $r/s\mapsto\psi(r)\psi(s)^{-1}$.

For later use, $R_P$ is a [local ring](../../../commutative-algebra.md#local-ring) with [maximal ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#maximal-ideal) $PR_P$. Reduction of numerators and denominators gives a surjective [ring homomorphism](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ring-homomorphism) $R_P\to\operatorname{Frac}(R/P)$ with kernel $PR_P$, so this [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) is proper and maximal. Every fraction outside it has numerator outside $P$ and is a [unit](../../../algebra.md#unit-in-a-ring), with inverse $s/r$. Thus it is the unique [maximal ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#maximal-ideal).

<h3 id="2/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

The equivalence relation defining the [localization at a prime ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#localization-at-a-prime-ideal) gives

$$
\ker(R\to R_P)=\{r\in R:sr=0\text{ for some }s\notin P\}.
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{R\longrightarrow R_P\text{ is injective }
\iff\text{ every }s\in R\setminus P\text{ annihilates no nonzero element of }R.}
$$

Equivalently, all [zero divisors](../../../mathematics.md#zero-divisor) of $R$ must lie in $P$. To prove necessity, if $s\notin P$ and $sr=0$ with $r\ne0$, then $r/1=0$ in $R_P$, contradicting the hypothesis that the map is [injective](../../../algebra.md#injective-function). Conversely, if every denominator annihilates no nonzero element, the displayed [kernel of a ring homomorphism](../../../commutative-algebra.md#kernel-of-a-ring-homomorphism) is zero, so the map is [injective](../../../algebra.md#injective-function).

An [integral domain](../../../commutative-algebra.md#integral-domain) automatically satisfies this criterion at every [prime ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal), but the criterion itself does not require $R$ to be an [integral domain](../../../commutative-algebra.md#integral-domain).

<h4 id="2/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#2/c)

**No: $R$ cannot contain a nonzero nilpotent element.** We prove the needed principle, [localization detects zero elements](../../../commutative-algebra.md#localization-detects-zero-elements), rather than assume it.

Let $a\ne0$ in $R$. Its [annihilator](../../../module-theory.md#annihilator-ring-theory) $\operatorname{Ann}(a)=\{r:ra=0\}$ is a proper [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal), because $1$ does not annihilate $a$. Every proper [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) is contained in a [maximal ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#maximal-ideal), by the [Zorn lemma](../../../set-theory.md#zorn-s-lemma); choose such a [maximal ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#maximal-ideal) $\mathfrak m$ containing $\operatorname{Ann}(a)$. If $a/1=0$ in $R_{\mathfrak m}$, then some $s\notin\mathfrak m$ satisfies $sa=0$. This would put $s$ in $\operatorname{Ann}(a)\subseteq\mathfrak m$, a contradiction. Thus every nonzero element survives in at least one [localization at a prime ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#localization-at-a-prime-ideal).

Now suppose $a^n=0$. For every [prime ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) $P$, $(a/1)^n=0$ in $R_P$. The assumed absence of nonzero [nilpotent elements](../../../commutative-algebra.md#nilpotent) forces $a/1=0$ in every $R_P$. The preceding argument then forces $a=0$. In the terminology of [reduced rings](../../../commutative-algebra.md#reduced-ring), we have proved

$$
\boxed{R_P\text{ reduced for every prime }P\ \Longrightarrow\ R\text{ reduced}.}
$$

<h3 id="2/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#2/d)

**Yes: $R$ can have nonzero zero divisors.** Let $R=k\times k$, a [direct product of rings](../../../commutative-algebra.md#direct-product-of-rings), where $k$ is any [field](../../../algebra.md#field). The nonzero [idempotents](../../../commutative-algebra.md#idempotent) $e_1=(1,0)$ and $e_2=(0,1)$ satisfy $e_1e_2=0$, so both are [zero divisors](../../../mathematics.md#zero-divisor).

There are exactly two [prime ideals](../../../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal):

$$
P_1=0\times k,\qquad P_2=k\times0.
$$

Indeed, any [prime ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) must contain $e_1$ or $e_2$, since their product is zero. If it contains $e_1$, it contains $k\times0$ and corresponds to a [prime ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) of the [quotient ring](../../../commutative-algebra.md#quotient-ring) $k$, whose only [prime ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) is zero; hence it equals $P_2$. The other case gives $P_1$.

For the [localization at a prime ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#localization-at-a-prime-ideal) $P_1$, every denominator $(u,v)\notin P_1$ has $u\ne0$, and

$$
R_{P_1}\longrightarrow k,\qquad
\frac{(a,b)}{(u,v)}\longmapsto\frac a u
$$

is an isomorphism. It is surjective using constant first-coordinate fractions. It is injective because a numerator with first coordinate zero is annihilated by $e_1\notin P_1$, so its fraction is zero. Interchanging the coordinates proves $R_{P_2}\cong k$.

Thus the [localization at a prime ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#localization-at-a-prime-ideal) is a [field](../../../algebra.md#field) in every case, although $R$ has [zero divisors](../../../mathematics.md#zero-divisor). The obstruction to using the previous part's argument is that the two nonzero factors of a zero product can survive at different [prime ideals](../../../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal).

<h3 id="2/e">e</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/e/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [E](#2/e)

Let $e_j$ be the sequence with $1$ in coordinate $j$ and zero elsewhere. In the [direct product of rings](../../../commutative-algebra.md#direct-product-of-rings) $R=k^{\mathbb N}$, the [ideals](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal)

$$
(e_1)\subsetneq(e_1,e_2)\subsetneq(e_1,e_2,e_3)\subsetneq\cdots
$$

form a strict ascending chain: every element of $(e_1,\ldots,e_n)$ vanishes after coordinate $n$, whereas $e_{n+1}$ does not. Hence **$R$ is not a Noetherian ring**.

Nevertheless, each $a=(a_j)\in R$ has a coordinatewise generalized inverse $b=(b_j)$, defined by $b_j=a_j^{-1}$ when $a_j\ne0$ and $b_j=0$ otherwise. It satisfies

$$
a=a^2b.
$$

This is the defining property of a [Von Neumann regular ring](../../../commutative-algebra.md#von-neumann-regular-ring). Fix any [prime ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) $P$, and take $a\in P$. Since $ab\in P$, $1-ab\notin P$; but

$$
(1-ab)a=0.
$$

Consequently $a/1=0$ in the [localization at a prime ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#localization-at-a-prime-ideal) $R_P$. All elements of $PR_P$ therefore vanish. By the [local ring](../../../commutative-algebra.md#local-ring) description in part (a), $PR_P$ is the unique [maximal ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#maximal-ideal), so $R_P$ is a nonzero [field](../../../algebra.md#field). A [field](../../../algebra.md#field) has only the [ideals](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) zero and itself, and thus is [Noetherian](../../../algebra.md#noetherian-ring).

We have proved the stronger conclusion

$$
\boxed{R_P\text{ is a field for every prime ideal }P\subset R.}
$$

The argument applies to every [prime ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal), without assuming that it comes from a coordinate projection.

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

A $P$-[primary ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#primary-ideal) is a proper [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) $Q$ with [radical of an ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#radical-of-an-ideal) $\sqrt Q=P$, such that $ab\in Q$ and $a\notin Q$ imply $b^n\in Q$ for some $n\geq1$. Equivalently, when $\sqrt Q=P$, the implication is $ab\in Q$, $b\notin P\Rightarrow a\in Q$.

**True for a nonempty finite family.** Let $Q=Q_1\cap\cdots\cap Q_m$, with each $Q_j$ a $P$-[primary ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#primary-ideal). This [intersection of ideals](../../../commutative-algebra.md#intersection-of-ideals) is proper. Its [radical of an ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#radical-of-an-ideal) is $P$: if $r\in P$, choose $n_j$ with $r^{n_j}\in Q_j$ and put $N=\max_j n_j$. Then $r^N\in Q$. Conversely, $r^N\in Q$ implies $r\in\sqrt{Q_j}=P$.

If $ab\in Q$ and $b\notin P$, the [primary ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#primary-ideal) property gives $a\in Q_j$ for every $j$, so $a\in Q$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{Q_1\cap\cdots\cap Q_m\text{ is }P\text{-primary}\quad(m\geq1).}
$$

As usual, the statement about finite intersections excludes the empty intersection, which is the whole [ring](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ring) and is not a [primary ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#primary-ideal).

<h3 id="3/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

**False.** In the [polynomial ring](../../../commutative-algebra.md#polynomial-ring) $R=k[x,y]$ over a [field](../../../algebra.md#field), take

$$
P=(x,y),\qquad I=(x,y^2).
$$

The [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) $P$ is a [maximal ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#maximal-ideal), with [quotient ring](../../../commutative-algebra.md#quotient-ring) $R/P\cong k$. Also

$$
R/I\cong k[y]/(y^2).
$$

In this last [ring](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ring), an element with nonzero constant term is a [unit](../../../algebra.md#unit-in-a-ring), and an element with zero constant term is a multiple of $y$ and has square zero. Thus every [zero divisor](../../../mathematics.md#zero-divisor) is a [nilpotent element](../../../commutative-algebra.md#nilpotent). This is equivalent to $I$ being a [primary ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#primary-ideal): a zero product in the [quotient ring](../../../commutative-algebra.md#quotient-ring) with one nonzero factor forces the other factor to be nilpotent. Its [radical of an ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#radical-of-an-ideal) is $P$, so $I$ is $P$-primary.

However, $y\notin I$, so $I\ne P$. Moreover $x\in I$ while $x\notin P^2$, since every polynomial in $P^2$ has no term of total degree one. For every $n\geq2$, $P^n\subseteq P^2$, so $I\ne P^n$. Hence

$$
\boxed{(x,y^2)\text{ is }(x,y)\text{-primary but is not a power of }(x,y).}
$$

<h3 id="3/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#3/c)

**True, without a Noetherian hypothesis.** By definition, the [symbolic power](../../../commutative-algebra.md#symbolic-power) is the [contraction of an ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#contraction-of-an-ideal)

$$
P^{(n)}=P^nR_P\cap R,
$$

where the intersection notation means inverse image under $R\to R_P$, even if this map is not [injective](../../../algebra.md#injective-function). In the [local ring](../../../commutative-algebra.md#local-ring) $A=R_P$, put $\mathfrak m=PR_P$. Then $P^nR_P=\mathfrak m^n$, and $\sqrt{\mathfrak m^n}=\mathfrak m$: every element of $\mathfrak m$ has its $n$th power in $\mathfrak m^n$, whereas a [unit](../../../algebra.md#unit-in-a-ring) cannot have a power in this proper [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal).

The [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) $\mathfrak m^n$ is $\mathfrak m$-primary. Indeed, if $uv\in\mathfrak m^n$ and $v\notin\mathfrak m$, then $v$ is a [unit](../../../algebra.md#unit-in-a-ring), so $u\in\mathfrak m^n$. Its [contraction of an ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#contraction-of-an-ideal) is therefore $P$-primary: its [radical of an ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#radical-of-an-ideal) contracts to $P$, and the same implication applies to the images of any two elements of $R$. Thus $P^{(n)}$ is always $P$-primary, proving the reverse implication immediately when $P^n=P^{(n)}$.

For the other implication, suppose $P^n$ is $P$-primary. Membership in the contraction can be expressed by clearing denominators:

$$
r\in P^{(n)}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
sr\in P^n\text{ for some }s\notin P.
$$

For completeness, if $r/1=a/t$ with $a\in P^n$ and $t\notin P$, equality of fractions gives $u(tr-a)=0$ for some $u\notin P$, so $(ut)r=ua\in P^n$; the converse follows by inverting $s$. Since $s\notin\sqrt{P^n}=P$, the [primary ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#primary-ideal) property forces $r\in P^n$. The inclusion $P^n\subseteq P^{(n)}$ always holds, giving

$$
\boxed{P^n\text{ is }P\text{-primary}\iff P^n=P^{(n)}.}
$$

**False: existence holds, but uniqueness fails.** First, the [Lasker–Noether theorem](../../../module-theory.md#lasker-noether-theorem) supplies existence; here is an argument from the preceding part. If some proper [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) in a [Noetherian ring](../../../algebra.md#noetherian-ring) were not a finite intersection of [irreducible ideals](../../../commutative-algebra.md#irreducible-ideal), the [ascending chain condition](../../../algebra.md#ascending-chain-condition) would let us choose such an [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) $I$ maximal under inclusion. It is not irreducible, so $I=J\cap K$ with both $J$ and $K$ strictly larger than $I$. By maximality, both are finite intersections of [irreducible ideals](../../../commutative-algebra.md#irreducible-ideal), and therefore so is $I$, a contradiction. Part (d) makes each factor a [primary ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#primary-ideal). The whole [ring](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ring) is represented by the empty intersection if that convention is needed.

For a counterexample to uniqueness, work in $R=k[x,y]$ over a [field](../../../algebra.md#field) $k$ and put $I=(x^2,xy)$. The [Hilbert basis theorem](../../../algebra.md#hilbert-basis-theorem) makes $R$ a [Noetherian ring](../../../algebra.md#noetherian-ring). There are two distinct [minimal primary decompositions](../../../commutative-algebra.md#minimal-primary-decomposition):

$$
\boxed{(x^2,xy)=(x)\cap(x^2,y)=(x)\cap(x^2,y-x).}
$$

The [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) $(x)$ is a [prime ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal), hence a [primary ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#primary-ideal). Each of the other factors has [quotient ring](../../../commutative-algebra.md#quotient-ring) isomorphic to $k[x]/(x^2)$, by substituting respectively $y=0$ and $y=x$. As in part (b), each is $(x,y)$-primary.

To check both intersections, let $Q_c=(x^2,y-cx)$, for $c=0,1$. Substitution identifies $R/Q_c$ with $k[x]/(x^2)$. The class of $xg(x,y)$ is zero exactly when $g(0,0)=0$, equivalently when $g\in(x,y)$. Therefore $(Q_c:x)=(x,y)$ and

$$
(x)\cap Q_c=x(Q_c:x)=x(x,y)=I.
$$

The two $Q_c$ are different, since $y$ belongs to $Q_0$ but has nonzero class $x$ modulo $Q_1$. Both decompositions are irredundant: $x\notin Q_c$, while $y-cx\notin(x)$. Applying the [radical of an ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#radical-of-an-ideal) operation to the two components in either decomposition gives $(x)$ and $(x,y)$, respectively.

The [second uniqueness theorem for primary decomposition](../../../commutative-algebra.md#second-uniqueness-theorem-for-primary-decomposition) explains the distinction: the component at an [isolated prime of a primary decomposition](../../../commutative-algebra.md#isolated-prime-of-a-primary-decomposition) is unique, but [embedded primary components](../../../commutative-algebra.md#embedded-primary-component) need not be. The counterexample varies an embedded component.

**A [ring](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ring) is a [Noetherian ring](../../../algebra.md#noetherian-ring) if it satisfies the [ascending chain condition](../../../algebra.md#ascending-chain-condition) on [ideals](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal)**: every chain

$$
I_1\subseteq I_2\subseteq I_3\subseteq\cdots
$$

eventually becomes constant. Equivalently, every [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) has a finite [generating set of an ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#generating-set-of-an-ideal). To see this equivalence, if an [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) were not finitely generated, one could repeatedly choose an element outside the [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) generated by the preceding choices, producing a strictly ascending chain. Conversely, the union of an ascending chain is an [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal); if it is generated by finitely many elements, all of them lie in one term of the chain, and every later term equals that term.

**A [ring](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ring) is an [Artinian ring](../../../algebra.md#artinian-ring) if it satisfies the [descending chain condition](../../../algebra.md#descending-chain-condition) on [ideals](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal)**: every chain

$$
I_1\supseteq I_2\supseteq I_3\supseteq\cdots
$$

eventually becomes constant. These are stabilization conditions on all [ideals](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal), not merely on [principal ideals](../../../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal).

**Yes: $R[[x]]$ is Noetherian.** We prove [Noetherianity of a formal power series ring](../../../commutative-algebra.md#noetherianity-of-a-formal-power-series-ring) directly, using only the equivalence in part (a). Let $A=R[[x]]$, a [formal power series ring](../../../commutative-algebra.md#formal-power-series), and fix an [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) $I\subseteq A$. For $n\geq0$, let $C_n$ be the set of coefficients of $x^n$ in elements of $I\cap x^nA$. It is an [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) of $R$, because coefficient extraction is additive and respects multiplication by constants. Multiplication by $x$ gives

$$
C_0\subseteq C_1\subseteq C_2\subseteq\cdots.
$$

Since $R$ is [Noetherian](../../../algebra.md#noetherian-ring), there is an $N$ with $C_n=C_N$ for all $n\geq N$. Each $C_n$, for $0\leq n\leq N$, has a finite [generating set of an ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#generating-set-of-an-ideal) $a_{n1},\ldots,a_{nr_n}$. Choose $f_{nj}\in I\cap x^nA$ whose coefficient of $x^n$ is $a_{nj}$. If $C_n=0$, use no generators at that index.

We claim that these finitely many $f_{nj}$ generate $I$ over $A$. Given $f\in I$, start with $g_0=f$ and recursively construct $g_n\in I\cap x^nA$. For $n<N$, express the coefficient of $x^n$ in $g_n$ as $\sum_jc_{nj}a_{nj}$, with $c_{nj}\in R$, and subtract $\sum_jc_{nj}f_{nj}$ to obtain $g_{n+1}\in I\cap x^{n+1}A$. For $n\geq N$, use $C_n=C_N$ and subtract

$$
\sum_jc_{nj}x^{n-N}f_{Nj}
$$

to cancel that coefficient. Every step involves a finite sum and leaves a residual still in $I$.

For each $j$, define the [formal power series](../../../commutative-algebra.md#formal-power-series)

$$
h_j(x)=\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}c_{nj}x^{n-N}\in A.
$$

The residual after the $n$th cancellation lies in $x^{n+1}A$, so comparison of each coefficient gives the exact identity

$$
f=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\sum_jc_{nj}f_{nj}+\sum_jh_j(x)f_{Nj}.
$$

The right side is a finite $A$-linear combination of the chosen generators. No assertion that arbitrary [ideals](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) are closed under limits is being used: the equality is verified coefficient by coefficient, and the multipliers $h_j$ belong to $A$. Thus every [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) of $A$ is finitely generated, proving

$$
\boxed{R\text{ Noetherian}\ \Longrightarrow\ R[[x]]\text{ Noetherian}.}
$$

**A [Dedekind domain](../../../commutative-algebra.md#dedekind-domain) is a [Noetherian ring](../../../algebra.md#noetherian-ring) $D$ which is an [integral domain](../../../commutative-algebra.md#integral-domain), is an [integrally closed domain](../../../commutative-algebra.md#integrally-closed-domain), and has [Krull dimension](../../../commutative-algebra.md#krull-dimension) one**. Explicitly, every [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) is finitely generated; every element of the [fraction field](../../../commutative-algebra.md#field-of-fractions) that is an [integral element](../../../commutative-algebra.md#integral-element) over $D$ already belongs to $D$; and every nonzero [prime ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) is a [maximal ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#maximal-ideal), with $D$ itself not a [field](../../../algebra.md#field).

With this convention, an equivalent local characterization is that $D$ is a [Noetherian](../../../algebra.md#noetherian-ring) [integral domain](../../../commutative-algebra.md#integral-domain) that is not a [field](../../../algebra.md#field) and whose [localization at a prime ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#localization-at-a-prime-ideal) $D_P$ is a [discrete valuation ring](../../../commutative-algebra.md#discrete-valuation-ring) for every nonzero [prime ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) $P$. The [integrally closed domain](../../../commutative-algebra.md#integrally-closed-domain) condition rules out singularities such as those represented by the subring $k[t^2,t^3]\subset k[t]$, where $t$ is integral but missing.

Some conventions also include [fields](../../../algebra.md#field) by allowing [Krull dimension](../../../commutative-algebra.md#krull-dimension) at most one; the conclusions about [ideals](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) below remain valid under that convention.

<h3 id="5/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#5)

<h4 id="5/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#5/b)

The [unique factorization of ideals in a Dedekind domain](../../../commutative-algebra.md#unique-factorization-of-ideals-in-a-dedekind-domain) states that every nonzero proper [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) $I$ of a [Dedekind domain](../../../commutative-algebra.md#dedekind-domain) $D$ has a factorization

$$
\boxed{I=P_1^{n_1}\cdots P_r^{n_r},}
$$

where the $P_j$ are distinct nonzero [prime ideals](../../../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) and the $n_j$ are positive integers. The [prime ideals](../../../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) and their exponents are unique up to reordering. The unit [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) $D$ is the empty product; the zero [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) is excluded.

Write $v_P(I)$ for the [prime-ideal valuation in a Dedekind domain](../../../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal-valuation-in-a-dedekind-domain), namely the exponent of $P$ in this factorization, with zero assigned when $P$ is absent. The associated containment rule is

$$
I\subseteq J\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad v_P(I)\geq v_P(J)\text{ for every nonzero prime ideal }P.
$$

Larger exponents give smaller [ideals](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal), just as higher divisibility gives smaller [principal ideals](../../../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal) in $\mathbb Z$. The theorem concerns unique factorization of [ideals](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal); it does not assert unique factorization of elements or that these [ideals](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) are principal.

<h3 id="5/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#5)

<h4 id="5/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#5/c)

Let $D$ be a [Dedekind domain](../../../commutative-algebra.md#dedekind-domain). The zero [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) is generated by $0$, and $D$ is generated by $1$, so consider a nonzero proper [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) $I$. Choose $0\ne a\in I$. We use these consequences of the [unique factorization of ideals in a Dedekind domain](../../../commutative-algebra.md#unique-factorization-of-ideals-in-a-dedekind-domain): every nonzero [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) has finitely many nonzero [prime-ideal valuations in a Dedekind domain](../../../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal-valuation-in-a-dedekind-domain); containment reverses the valuations as in part (b); and

$$
v_P(J+K)=\min\{v_P(J),v_P(K)\}.
$$

The last formula follows from the containment rule: $J+K$ is the smallest [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) containing both $J$ and $K$, and the product with the indicated minimum exponents is exactly that [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal).

Let $T$ be the finite set of [prime ideals](../../../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) dividing $(a)$. For each $P\in T$, set $r_P=v_P(I)$. Since $(a)\subseteq I$, we have $r_P\leq v_P((a))$. The containment rule also gives $I\not\subseteq P^{r_P+1}$, so choose

$$
b_P\in I\setminus P^{r_P+1}.
$$

Distinct nonzero [prime ideals](../../../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) of $D$ are distinct [maximal ideals](../../../commutative-algebra.md#maximal-ideal) and hence [comaximal ideals](../../../commutative-algebra.md#comaximal-ideals); their positive powers are also comaximal. Indeed, if $u+v=1$ with $u\in P$, $v\in Q$, for positive integers $m,n$ expanding $(u+v)^{m+n-1}$ shows $1\in P^m+Q^n$, since each term contains either at least $m$ factors of $u$ or at least $n$ factors of $v$. We use the [Chinese remainder theorem](../../../mathematics.md#chinese-remainder-theorem) in its ideal form: for pairwise comaximal [ideals](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) $J_1,\ldots,J_s$, the map $D\to\prod_jD/J_j$ is surjective. Apply it to the powers $P^{r_P+1}$ to choose $e_P\in D$ with

$$
e_P\equiv1\pmod{P^{r_P+1}},\qquad
e_P\equiv0\pmod{Q^{r_Q+1}}\quad(Q\in T,\ Q\ne P).
$$

Then $b=\sum_{P\in T}e_Pb_P$ belongs to $I$ and satisfies $b\equiv b_P\pmod{P^{r_P+1}}$ for every $P\in T$. Thus $b\notin P^{r_P+1}$, whereas $b\in I\subseteq P^{r_P}$, proving $v_P((b))=r_P$.

For $P\in T$, therefore,

$$
v_P((a,b))=\min\{v_P((a)),v_P((b))\}=r_P.
$$

For $P\notin T$, $v_P((a))=0$, and $(a)\subseteq I$ forces $v_P(I)=0$ as well. Hence $(a,b)$ and $I$ have the same valuation at every nonzero [prime ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal). Uniqueness of their factorizations gives

$$
\boxed{I=(a,b).}
$$

This proves [two generators for an ideal in a Dedekind domain](../../../commutative-algebra.md#two-generators-for-an-ideal-in-a-dedekind-domain); in fact the first nonzero generator $a\in I$ can be chosen in advance.

Take

$$

We justify that this is a [Dedekind domain](../../../commutative-algebra.md#dedekind-domain). It is an [integral domain](../../../commutative-algebra.md#integral-domain) inside the [number field](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#number-field) $K=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-5})$. As an additive group it is free of rank two over $\mathbb Z$. The subgroup theorem for [finitely generated abelian groups](../../../group.md#finitely-generated-abelian-group) says that every subgroup of such a group is finitely generated. Thus every [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) has finitely many additive generators, and those same elements generate it as a $D$-ideal. This proves that $D$ is [Noetherian](../../../algebra.md#noetherian-ring).

We use the standard formula for the [ring of integers of a quadratic field](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#ring-of-integers-of-a-quadratic-field): for squarefree $d$, it is $\mathbb Z[(1+\sqrt d)/2]$ if $d\equiv1\pmod4$, and $\mathbb Z[\sqrt d]$ otherwise. Since $-5\equiv3\pmod4$, $D$ is the [ring of integers of a number field](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#ring-of-integers) $K$, meaning all elements of $K$ integral over $\mathbb Z$. We also use transitivity of [integral extensions](../../../commutative-algebra.md#integral-extension). If an element of $K$ is integral over $D$, it is integral over $\mathbb Z$ because $D$ is integral over $\mathbb Z$, and therefore belongs to $D$. Thus $D$ is an [integrally closed domain](../../../commutative-algebra.md#integrally-closed-domain).

Every nonzero [prime ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) $P$ is maximal. Indeed, choose $0\ne\alpha=a+b\sqrt{-5}\in P$. Multiplying by its [algebraic conjugate](../../../algebra.md#conjugate-element-field-theory) puts the nonzero integer $N(\alpha)=a^2+5b^2$ in $P$. Hence the [integral domain](../../../commutative-algebra.md#integral-domain) $D/P$ is a quotient of the finite [ring](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ring) $D/N(\alpha)D$, and is finite. Every finite [integral domain](../../../commutative-algebra.md#integral-domain) is a [field](../../../algebra.md#field), since multiplication by a nonzero element is injective and therefore surjective. The element $2$ has no inverse in $D$, since $1/2\notin\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-5}]$, so $D$ is not a [field](../../../algebra.md#field). Consequently $(0)$ is not maximal, and a [maximal ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#maximal-ideal) above it is nonzero. Together with the preceding result, this gives [Krull dimension](../../../commutative-algebra.md#krull-dimension) exactly one.

Finally, consider the [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal)

$$
I=(2,1+\sqrt{-5}).
$$

Reduction modulo $2$ with $\sqrt{-5}\mapsto1$ identifies $D/I$ with $\mathbb F_2$, so the additive index $[D:I]$ is $2$. If $I$ were the [principal ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal) $(a+b\sqrt{-5})$, multiplication by its nonzero generator would have integer matrix

$$
\begin{pmatrix}a&-5b\\b&a\end{pmatrix}
$$

in the basis $1,\sqrt{-5}$. The index of its image is the absolute determinant, so

$$
2=[D:I]=a^2+5b^2.
$$

There are no integer solutions: $b\ne0$ makes the right side at least $5$, while $b=0$ would require $a^2=2$. Thus $I$ is a [nonprincipal ideal in the integers adjoined a square root of minus five](../../../algebra.md#nonprincipal-ideal-in-the-integers-adjoined-a-square-root-of-minus-five), and **$D$ is a Dedekind domain that is not a principal ideal domain**.

<h3 id="6/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#6)

<h4 id="6/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#6/a)

The [Krull dimension](../../../commutative-algebra.md#krull-dimension) of a [commutative ring](../../../commutative-algebra.md#commutative-ring) $R$ is

$$
\boxed{\dim R=\sup\{n:\mathfrak p_0\subsetneq\mathfrak p_1\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq\mathfrak p_n
\text{ is a chain of prime ideals of }R\}.}
$$

The length counts strict inclusions, so a chain with $n+1$ [prime ideals](../../../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) has length $n$. The supremum may be infinite. A [field](../../../algebra.md#field), for example, has just the [prime ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) $(0)$ and therefore has [Krull dimension](../../../commutative-algebra.md#krull-dimension) zero. For the zero [ring](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ring), the [spectrum of a commutative ring](../../../ringed-space.md#spectrum-of-a-commutative-ring) is empty; under the convention $\sup\varnothing=-\infty$, its dimension is $-\infty$.

<h3 id="6/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#6)

<h4 id="6/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#6/b)

For a [discrete valuation ring](../../../commutative-algebra.md#discrete-valuation-ring) $A$,

$$
\boxed{\dim A=1.}
$$

Let $\pi$ be a [uniformizer](../../../commutative-algebra.md#uniformizer), so its unique nonzero [maximal ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#maximal-ideal) is $\mathfrak m=(\pi)$. We use the defining description of a [discrete valuation ring](../../../commutative-algebra.md#discrete-valuation-ring): it is a non-field [local ring](../../../commutative-algebra.md#local-ring) that is a [principal ideal domain](../../../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal-domain), and every nonzero element is $u\pi^n$ for a [unit](../../../algebra.md#unit-in-a-ring) $u$ and an integer $n\geq0$.

Every nonzero proper [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) is therefore $(\pi^n)$ for some $n\geq1$. Such an [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) is a [prime ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) only when $n=1$: for $n\geq2$, $\pi\pi^{n-1}\in(\pi^n)$ while neither factor belongs to $(\pi^n)$. The [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) $(\pi)$ is prime because it is maximal. Also $(0)$ is prime because $A$ is an [integral domain](../../../commutative-algebra.md#integral-domain). Thus

$$
\operatorname{Spec}A=\{(0),\mathfrak m\},\qquad (0)\subsetneq\mathfrak m,
$$

and the longest strict chain has length one.

<h3 id="6/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#6)

<h4 id="6/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#6/c)

For a subring $S\subseteq R$ with the same identity, $R$ is an [integral extension](../../../commutative-algebra.md#integral-extension) of $S$ if every $r\in R$ is an [integral element](../../../commutative-algebra.md#integral-element) over $S$: there exist $n\geq1$ and $s_0,\ldots,s_{n-1}\in S$ such that

$$
\boxed{r^n+s_{n-1}r^{n-1}+\cdots+s_1r+s_0=0.}
$$

The requirement that the polynomial be [monic](../../../polynomial.md#monic-polynomial) is essential. Merely satisfying some polynomial equation over $S$ is insufficient: for instance $1/2\in\mathbb Q$ satisfies $2T-1=0$ over $\mathbb Z$, but is not integral over $\mathbb Z$. To see this directly, a hypothetical [monic](../../../polynomial.md#monic-polynomial) equation for $1/2$, multiplied by $2^n$, would say that an odd integer $1$ plus a sum of even integers is zero.

The degree and coefficients may depend on $r$. Integrality does not by itself assert that the entire [ring](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ring) $R$ is a finite $S$-[module](../../../module-theory.md#module-mathematics); it does imply that the subalgebra $S[r]$ is generated as an $S$-[module](../../../module-theory.md#module-mathematics) by $1,r,\ldots,r^{n-1}$.

<h3 id="6/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#6)

<h4 id="6/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#6/d)

We use three theorems for an [integral extension](../../../commutative-algebra.md#integral-extension) $S\subseteq R$, none requiring [Noetherian](../../../algebra.md#noetherian-ring) hypotheses. The [Lying-over theorem](../../../commutative-algebra.md#lying-over-theorem) says that each [prime ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) $\mathfrak p$ of $S$ is $\mathfrak q\cap S$ for some [prime ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) $\mathfrak q$ of $R$. The [going-up theorem](../../../commutative-algebra.md#going-up-theorem) says that if $\mathfrak p\subseteq\mathfrak p'$ in $S$ and $\mathfrak q\cap S=\mathfrak p$, there is $\mathfrak q'\supseteq\mathfrak q$ with $\mathfrak q'\cap S=\mathfrak p'$. The [incomparability theorem for integral extensions](../../../commutative-algebra.md#incomparability-theorem-for-integral-extensions) says that comparable [prime ideals](../../../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) of $R$ with the same contraction to $S$ are equal.

Take any strict chain

$$
\mathfrak q_0\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq\mathfrak q_n
$$

in $R$. The [contraction of an ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#contraction-of-an-ideal) operation gives $\mathfrak p_j=\mathfrak q_j\cap S$, which are [prime ideals](../../../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) of $S$, by the proof in Question 1(b), and remain strictly increasing by the [incomparability theorem for integral extensions](../../../commutative-algebra.md#incomparability-theorem-for-integral-extensions). Thus every chain length in $R$ occurs in $S$, giving $\dim R\leq\dim S$.

Conversely, take any strict chain $\mathfrak p_0\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq\mathfrak p_n$ in $S$. Use the [Lying-over theorem](../../../commutative-algebra.md#lying-over-theorem) to choose $\mathfrak q_0$ over $\mathfrak p_0$, then repeatedly use the [going-up theorem](../../../commutative-algebra.md#going-up-theorem) to obtain $\mathfrak q_0\subseteq\cdots\subseteq\mathfrak q_n$ with the prescribed contractions. Each inclusion must be strict, since its contractions are distinct. Hence every chain length in $S$ occurs in $R$, giving $\dim S\leq\dim R$.

Taking suprema proves [integral extensions preserve Krull dimension](../../../commutative-algebra.md#integral-extensions-preserve-krull-dimension):

$$
\boxed{\dim R=\dim S.}
$$

The argument works equally well when the dimensions are infinite, since it compares all finite chain lengths.

**No.** In the [Gaussian integers](../../../commutative-algebra.md#gaussian-integer), choose

$$
P=(2+i)\subset\mathbb Z[i],\qquad Q=P\cap\mathbb Z=(5).
$$

Indeed, substituting $i=-2$ gives $\mathbb Z[i]/(2+i)\cong\mathbb Z/5\mathbb Z$, so $P$ is a [prime ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) and its contraction is $(5)$. Put $\beta=2-i$. Modulo $P$, $\beta$ becomes $4\ne0$ in $\mathbb F_5$, so $\beta\notin P$. Therefore

$$
\alpha=\frac1{2-i}=\frac{2+i}{5}\in\mathbb Z[i]_P.
$$

We show that $\alpha$ is not an [integral element](../../../commutative-algebra.md#integral-element) over $\mathbb Z_{(5)}$, using a direct reduction argument. Write $B=\mathbb Z_{(5)}[i]$, viewed inside $\mathbb Q(i)$. There is a [ring homomorphism](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ring-homomorphism)

$$
\rho:B\longrightarrow\mathbb F_5,\qquad i\longmapsto2,
$$

which reduces rational coefficients modulo $5$. It is well defined because denominators in $\mathbb Z_{(5)}$ are not divisible by $5$, and $2^2+1=0$ in $\mathbb F_5$. In particular, $\rho(\beta)=0$ and $\rho(1)=1$.

If $\alpha$ satisfied a [monic](../../../polynomial.md#monic-polynomial) equation over $\mathbb Z_{(5)}$, say

$$
\alpha^n+a_{n-1}\alpha^{n-1}+\cdots+a_0=0,
$$

multiplication by $\beta^n$ would give an equality in $B$:

$$
1+a_{n-1}\beta+a_{n-2}\beta^2+\cdots+a_0\beta^n=0.
$$

Applying $\rho$ gives $1=0$ in $\mathbb F_5$, a contradiction. Consequently

$$
\boxed{\mathbb Z[i]_{(2+i)}\text{ is not integral over }\mathbb Z_{(5)}.}
$$

This exhibits why [localization of an integral extension at a prime need not be integral](../../../commutative-algebra.md#localization-of-an-integral-extension-at-a-prime-need-not-be-integral). Localizing both [rings](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ring) by the same [multiplicative subset](../../../commutative-algebra.md#multiplicatively-closed-set) preserves an [integral extension](../../../commutative-algebra.md#integral-extension), but $\mathbb Z[i]_P$ inverts additional elements, including $2-i$, whose image at the other [prime ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) $(2-i)$ is zero. The original extension is integral because $i$ satisfies the [monic](../../../polynomial.md#monic-polynomial) equation $T^2+1=0$: the [subalgebra generated by finitely many integral elements](../../../commutative-algebra.md#subalgebra-generated-by-finitely-many-integral-elements) property applies to $\mathbb Z[i]$.
