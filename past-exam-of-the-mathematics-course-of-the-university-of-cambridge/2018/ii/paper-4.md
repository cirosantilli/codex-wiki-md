## 1G

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 4](paper-4.md)

<h3 id="1g/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1G](#1g)

Suppose first that a simple [continued fraction](../../../number-theory.md#continued-fraction) is eventually periodic. Its periodic tail $\xi$ is fixed by the [Möbius transformation](../../../group-theory.md#mobius-transformation) represented by the [continued-fraction matrix](../../../number-theory.md#continued-fraction-matrix) of one full period:

$$
\xi=\frac{a\xi+b}{c\xi+d}.
$$

Thus

$$
c\xi^2+(d-a)\xi-b=0.
$$

The infinite tail is not [rational](../../../number-theory.md#rational-number), since a rational simple continued fraction terminates. Hence $\xi$ is a [quadratic irrational](../../../algebra.md#quadratic-irrational-number), and the finite initial block expresses the original number as another rational Möbius transform of $\xi$. Therefore **every eventually periodic simple continued fraction represents a quadratic irrational**.

For the purely periodic fraction $x=[7,7,7,\ldots]$,

$$
x=7+\frac1x,
$$

so $x^2-7x-1=0$. Positivity selects

$$
\boxed{x=\frac{7+\sqrt{53}}2.}
$$

Apply the [continued-fraction algorithm](../../../number-theory.md#continued-fraction-algorithm) to $\sqrt{23}$:

$$
\sqrt{23}
=4+\frac1{\frac{\sqrt{23}-4}{1}}
=4+\cfrac1{1+\cfrac1{3+\cfrac1{1+\cfrac1{8+\cdots}}}}.
$$

The complete quotient then repeats, giving

$$
\boxed{\sqrt{23}=[4;\overline{1,3,1,8}].}
$$

The [convergents](../../../number-theory.md#continued-fraction-convergent) through one term before the final $8$ are

$$
4,\quad5,\quad\frac{19}{4},\quad\frac{24}{5}.
$$

The last of these satisfies the [Pell equation](../../../number-theory.md#pell-equation)

$$
24^2-23\cdot5^2=576-575=1,
$$

so a positive solution is

$$
\boxed{(x,y)=(24,5).}
$$

## 2F

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 4](paper-4.md)

<h3 id="2f/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2F](#2f)

Let

$$
S=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{m_n}{n!}.
$$

Assume for a contradiction that $S=a/b$ is [rational](../../../number-theory.md#rational-number). For every $N\geq b$, both $N!S$ and

$$
N!\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{m_n}{n!}
$$

are [integers](../../../number-theory.md#integer), because the $m_n$ are integers. Their positive difference

$$
R_N=N!\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}\frac{m_n}{n!}
=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}
\frac{m_{N+k}}{(N+1)\cdots(N+k)}
$$

must therefore be a positive integer.

On the other hand, [concavity](../../../real-analysis.md#concave-function) of $x^\alpha$ for $0\leq\alpha<1$ gives $(N+k)^\alpha\leq N^\alpha+k^\alpha\leq N^\alpha+k$. Since $(N+1)\cdots(N+k)\geq(N+1)^k$,

$$
0<R_N
\leq A\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{N^\alpha+k}{(N+1)^k}
=A\left(N^{\alpha-1}+\frac{N+1}{N^2}\right)
\longrightarrow0.
$$

Eventually $0<R_N<1$, contradicting its integrality. Hence

$$
\boxed{\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{m_n}{n!}\text{ is irrational}.}
$$

The integrality assumption is essential. For example, take $\alpha=0$, $A=2$, and

$$
m_n=\frac2{e-1}\qquad(n\geq1).
$$

Then $1<m_n<2$, but the [exponential series](../../../calculus.md#exponential-series) gives

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{m_n}{n!}
=\frac2{e-1}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{n!}=2.
$$

Thus **the result does not remain true for arbitrary real $m_n$**.

## 3H

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 4](paper-4.md)

<h3 id="3h/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3H](#3h)

A binary [linear-feedback shift register](../../../coding-theory.md#linear-feedback-shift-register) of length $L$ stores $L$ bits. At each clock it outputs one bit, shifts the state, and inserts a fixed [linear combination](../../../vector-space.md#linear-combination) over $\mathbb F_2$ of the stored bits. Equivalently, its output obeys a recurrence

$$
s_n=c_1s_{n-1}+\cdots+c_Ls_{n-L}.
$$

The [Berlekamp-Massey algorithm](../../../coding-theory.md#berlekamp-massey-algorithm) processes the observed symbols from left to right. It maintains the shortest current connection polynomial $C(D)$, its degree $L$, and a saved polynomial from the most recent degree increase. At step $n$ it computes the discrepancy

$$
d_n=s_n+\sum_{i=1}^{L}c_i s_{n-i}.
$$

If $d_n=0$, the current recurrence already predicts the new symbol. If $d_n\ne0$, a shifted multiple of the saved polynomial is added to $C$ to cancel the discrepancy; when $2L\leq n$, the algorithm also replaces $L$ by $n+1-L$ and saves the old polynomial.

For the observed binary sequence, the nonzero-discrepancy updates are

$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
n&L&C(D)\\ \hline
1&2&1+D^2\\
4&3&1+D^2+D^3\\
6&4&1+D^3+D^4.
\end{array}
$$

All discrepancies through $n=11$ then vanish. Thus the shortest recurrence is

$$
\boxed{s_n=s_{n-3}+s_{n-4}\pmod2,}
$$

or, equivalently, $s_{n+4}=s_{n+1}+s_n$. The connection polynomial is $1+D^3+D^4$, and with the reciprocal convention the [feedback polynomial](../../../coding-theory.md#feedback-polynomial) is

$$
\boxed{x^4+x+1.}
$$

## 4G

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 4](paper-4.md)

<h3 id="4g/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4G](#4g)

<h4 id="4g/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#4g/a)

Fix an effective enumeration $f_{e,n}$ of the $n$-ary [partial recursive functions](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#computable-function).

The [S-m-n theorem](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#smn-theorem) says that program inputs can be compiled into an index: for every $m,n\geq1$ there is a total recursive $s_n^m$ such that

$$
f_{s_n^m(e,x_1,\ldots,x_m),n}(y_1,\ldots,y_n)
\simeq f_{e,m+n}(x_1,\ldots,x_m,y_1,\ldots,y_n).
$$

Here $\simeq$ means that either both sides are undefined or both are defined and equal.

The [recursion theorem](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#kleene-s-recursion-theorem) says that every total recursive transformation $h$ of program indices has a semantic fixed point: some $e$ satisfies

$$
f_{e,1}\simeq f_{h(e),1}.
$$

The [Rice theorem](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#rice-s-theorem) says that every nontrivial [extensional property of programs](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#extensional-property-of-programs) computed by partial recursive functions is undecidable.

<h3 id="4g/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4G](#4g)

<h4 id="4g/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#4g/b)

By the [S-m-n theorem](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#smn-theorem), there is a total recursive function $h$ such that

$$
f_{h(x),1}(y)\simeq g(x,y)
$$

for all $x,y$. Apply the [recursion theorem](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#kleene-s-recursion-theorem) to $h$. It supplies an index $e$ for which

$$
f_{e,1}\simeq f_{h(e),1}.
$$

Consequently

$$
\boxed{f_{e,1}(y)\simeq g(e,y)\quad\text{for every }y\in\mathbb N.}
$$

No. The property

$$
\mathcal P(f)\iff|\operatorname{dom}f|=9

\boxed{\text{there is no algorithm that decides whether }|W_n|=9.}
$$

## 5J

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 4](paper-4.md)

<h3 id="5j/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [5J](#5j)

With Control as the reference group, the fitted [normal linear model](../../../statistical-modelling.md#normal-linear-model) has mean

$$
\mathbb E[\mathrm{Weight}]
=\beta_0+\beta_{\rm Time}t+\beta_{\rm Treat}I_{\rm Treat}
+\beta_{\rm Int}tI_{\rm Treat}+\text{cage effect}.
$$

Thus the control-group rate is $\beta_{\rm Time}$, while the treatment-group rate is $\beta_{\rm Time}+\beta_{\rm Int}$. The estimates are

$$
\boxed{\widehat{\text{control slope}}=-0.006023,}
$$

and

$$
\boxed{\widehat{\text{treatment slope}}
=-0.006023-0.173515=-0.179538}
$$

weight units per day. The control slope has $t=-0.477$ and $p=0.63334$, so **there is no statistically significant evidence of weight loss over time in the control group** at conventional [significance levels](../../../statistical-modelling.md#significance-level).

The plotted [regression residuals](../../../probability-and-statistics.md#regression-residual) for each mouse form smooth time patterns rather than an unstructured cloud: nearby observations on the same mouse have similar residuals, and the mice also show persistent individual offsets. This is strong evidence of [autocorrelation](../../../time-series.md#autocorrelation) and omitted mouse-level variation. The ordinary linear model treats all 400 measurements as independent after accounting for cage, so it commits [pseudoreplication](../../../statistical-modelling.md#pseudoreplication) and will generally underestimate the coefficient [standard errors](../../../statistical-inference.md#standard-error). The reported $t$-tests and $p$-values should therefore **not be trusted**. A [random-intercept linear mixed model](../../../statistical-modelling.md#random-intercept-linear-mixed-model) with a within-mouse correlation structure would respect the repeated-measures design.

## 6C

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 4](paper-4.md)

<h3 id="6c/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [6C](#6c)

Let

$$
F(N)=\frac{rN}{(1+bN)^2}.
$$

At low density, $F(N)/N\to r$, so $r$ is the density-independent per-generation multiplication factor. The parameter $b$ has units of inverse population and sets the density scale at which crowding suppresses recruitment.

The [fixed points](../../../function.md#fixed-point) satisfy $N=F(N)$. Besides extinction $N=0$, for $r>1$ there is the positive fixed point

$$
\boxed{N_*=\frac{\sqrt r-1}{b}.}
$$

Since

$$
F'(N)=\frac{r(1-bN)}{(1+bN)^3},
$$

its multiplier is

$$
F'(N_*)=\frac{2-\sqrt r}{\sqrt r}=\frac2{\sqrt r}-1.
$$

For every $r>1$, $-1<F'(N_*)<1$, so [fixed point stability for an iteration](../../../dynamical-systems.md#fixed-point-stability-for-an-iteration) proves that $N_*$ is **locally asymptotically stable**.

Scale the population by $x_\tau=bN_\tau$. Then

$$
x_{\tau+1}=f(x_\tau),\qquad f(x)=\frac{rx}{(1+x)^2}.
$$

The map increases on $0<x<1$, decreases on $x>1$, and has global maximum $f(1)=r/4$. Starting from $x_1=1$ therefore gives

$$
x_2=\frac r4=:U,
\qquad
x_3=f(U)=\frac{4r^2}{(4+r)^2}=:L.
$$

For $r>4$, writing $s=\sqrt r>2$ gives

$$
L-1=\frac{(s-2)(s+2)(3s^2+4)}{(s^2+4)^2}>0,
\qquad
(s-1)-L=\frac{(s-2)^3(s^2+s+2)}{(s^2+4)^2}>0.
$$

Thus $1<L<N_*b=s-1<U$. Since $f$ decreases on $[L,U]$, $f(U)=L$ and $f(L)>f(s-1)=s-1>L$, while the global maximum gives $f(L)\leq U$. Hence $f([L,U])\subseteq[L,U]$. The [cobweb plot](../../../dynamical-systems.md#cobweb-plot) consequently remains in this invariant interval after the first iterate, and both endpoints are attained at $\tau=2,3$:

$$
\boxed{\frac{4r^2}{(4+r)^2b}\leq N_\tau\leq\frac r{4b}\qquad(\tau\geq2).}
$$

The printed bound cannot include $\tau=1$, because $N_1=1/b<L/b$ when $r>4$.

## 7B

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 4](paper-4.md)

<h3 id="7b/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [7B](#7b)

For

$$
y''+P(z)y'+Q(z)y=0,
$$

a finite point $z_0$ is a [regular singular point](../../../complex-analysis.md#regular-singular-point) when $(z-z_0)P(z)$ and $(z-z_0)^2Q(z)$ extend holomorphically to $z_0$. A singularity at infinity is classified by applying this criterion after the change of variable $w=1/z$.

For the displayed [Bessel differential equation](../../../analysis.md#bessel-differential-equation),

$$
P(z)=\frac1z,
\qquad
Q(z)=1-\frac1{4z^2}.
$$

At $z=0$, the functions $zP(z)=1$ and $z^2Q(z)=z^2-1/4$ are holomorphic, so zero is regular singular. To inspect infinity, write $Y(w)=y(1/w)$. The equation becomes

$$
Y''+\frac1wY'
+\left(\frac1{w^4}-\frac1{4w^2}\right)Y=0.
$$

Here $w^2Q(w)=w^{-2}-1/4$ is not holomorphic at zero, so infinity is an [irregular singular point](../../../complex-analysis.md#irregular-singular-point). Thus

$$
\boxed{z=0\text{ is regular singular, while }z=\infty\text{ is irregular singular}.}
$$

Set $y=z^{-1/2}f$. Direct differentiation makes every lower-order term cancel:

$$
z^2y''+zy'+\left(z^2-\frac14\right)y
=z^{3/2}(f''+f).
$$

Hence $f''+f=0$, and on any domain carrying a branch of $\sqrt z$ two linearly independent solutions are

$$
\boxed{y_1(z)=\frac{\cos z}{\sqrt z},
\qquad
y_2(z)=\frac{\sin z}{\sqrt z}.}
$$

Near the regular singular point, these behave as $z^{-1/2}$ and $z^{1/2}$, the two [Frobenius exponents](../../../complex-analysis.md#frobenius-method). At infinity their combinations are $z^{-1/2}e^{\pm iz}$; after $w=1/z$ these contain $e^{\pm i/w}$, the exponential behavior characteristic of an irregular singular point.

## 8B

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 4](paper-4.md)

<h3 id="8b/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [8B](#8b)

Let a one-parameter infinitesimal transformation be $\delta q_i=\epsilon X_i(q,t)$ and suppose its change in the [Lagrangian](../../../calculus-of-variations.md#lagrangian) is a total derivative,

$$
\delta\mathcal L=\epsilon\frac{dF}{dt}.
$$

With [canonical momentum](../../../classical-mechanics.md#canonical-momentum) $p_i=\partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot q_i$, the chain rule and the [Euler-Lagrange equations](../../../analysis.md#euler-lagrange-equation) give, along a motion,

$$
\delta\mathcal L
=\epsilon\sum_i\left(
\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial q_i}X_i
+p_i\frac{dX_i}{dt}\right)
=\epsilon\frac d{dt}\sum_i p_iX_i.
$$

Comparison with the assumed total derivative proves [Noether theorem](../../../calculus-of-variations.md#noether-theorem):

$$
\boxed{J=\sum_i p_iX_i-F\quad\text{is conserved}.}
$$

The given Lagrangian has no explicit time dependence, so its [energy function](../../../classical-mechanics.md#energy-function) is conserved:

$$
\boxed{E=\dot x\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\dot x}
+\dot y\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\dot y}-\mathcal L
=\frac{\dot x^2+\dot y^2}{2y^2}+V\!\left(\frac xy\right).}
$$

It is also invariant under the dilation $(x,y)\mapsto(e^\epsilon x,e^\epsilon y)$: the velocities and $y$ all scale by the same factor, while $x/y$ is unchanged. The generator is $(X_x,X_y)=(x,y)$ and $F=0$. Since

$$
p_x=\frac{\dot x}{y^2},
\qquad
p_y=\frac{\dot y}{y^2},
$$

Noether's theorem supplies the second first integral

$$
\boxed{D=xp_x+yp_y=\frac{x\dot x+y\dot y}{y^2}.}

The [Fermat-Euler theorem](../../../number-theory.md#euler-s-theorem) states that

$$
\gcd(a,n)=1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad a^{\varphi(n)}\equiv1\pmod n.
$$

Indeed, if $r_1,\ldots,r_{\varphi(n)}$ are the reduced residue classes modulo $n$, multiplication by $a$ permutes them. Therefore

$$
\prod_i ar_i\equiv\prod_i r_i\pmod n.
$$

The product $\prod_i r_i$ is invertible modulo $n$, so cancellation gives the theorem.

Now let $p$ be prime. If $k\equiv1\pmod{p-1}$, then for $p\nmid b$, [Fermat little theorem](../../../number-theory.md#fermat-little-theorem) gives $b^{k-1}\equiv1\pmod p$, while $p\mid b$ makes $b^k\equiv b\equiv0$. Thus $b^k\equiv b$ for every integer $b$.

Conversely, assume this congruence holds for every $b$. Choose a [primitive root](../../../number-theory.md#primitive-root-modulo-n) $g$ modulo $p$. Then $g^{k-1}\equiv1\pmod p$. Since $g$ has order $p-1$, one has $p-1\mid k-1$. Hence

$$
\boxed{b^k\equiv b\pmod p\text{ for every }b
\iff k\equiv1\pmod{p-1}.}
$$

<h3 id="11g/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [11G](#11g)

<h4 id="11g/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#11g/b)

An odd composite $N$ coprime to $b$ is a [Fermat pseudoprime](../../../number-theory.md#fermat-pseudoprime) to base $b$ when $b^{N-1}\equiv1\pmod N$. It is a [Carmichael number](../../../number-theory.md#carmichael-number) when this congruence holds for every $b$ coprime to $N$.

Suppose a Carmichael number has $p^a\Vert N$ with $a\geq2$. The group $(\mathbb Z/p^a\mathbb Z)^*$ is cyclic, so choose a residue of order

$$
\varphi(p^a)=p^{a-1}(p-1).
$$

By the [Chinese remainder theorem](../../../mathematics.md#chinese-remainder-theorem), extend it to an integer $b$ that is congruent to $1$ modulo every other prime-power factor of $N$. Then $\gcd(b,N)=1$, and the Carmichael congruence forces the order of $b$ modulo $p^a$ to divide $N-1$. In particular $p\mid N-1$, contradicting $p\mid N$. Therefore every Carmichael number is [square-free](../../../number-theory.md#square-free-integer).

Now assume $N$ is square-free. If $p-1\mid N-1$ for every $p\mid N$, then for every $b$ coprime to $N$, [Fermat little theorem](../../../number-theory.md#fermat-little-theorem) gives

$$
b^{N-1}\equiv1\pmod p
$$

for each $p\mid N$. The [Chinese remainder theorem](../../../mathematics.md#chinese-remainder-theorem) then gives the congruence modulo $N$, so $N$ is Carmichael.

Conversely, if $N$ is Carmichael, fix $p\mid N$. Choose a [primitive root](../../../number-theory.md#primitive-root-modulo-n) $g$ modulo $p$ and use the Chinese remainder theorem to choose $b\equiv g\pmod p$ and $b\equiv1\pmod{N/p}$. Then $b^{N-1}\equiv1\pmod p$, so the order $p-1$ of $g$ divides $N-1$. This proves the [Korselt criterion](../../../number-theory.md#korselt-criterion) in the required form:

$$
\boxed{N\text{ is Carmichael}\iff
N\text{ is square-free and }p-1\mid N-1\text{ for every }p\mid N.}
$$

A Carmichael number cannot have one prime factor because it is composite. If $N=pq$ with distinct primes $p<q$, the condition $q-1\mid pq-1$ implies $q-1\mid p-1$, which is impossible. Hence **every Carmichael number has at least three prime factors**.

The $u^2/v$ term makes $u$ locally autocatalytic, while increasing $v$ suppresses this production. Thus **$u$ is the activator and $v$ is the inhibitor**.

$$
\frac{u^2}{v}-2bu=0,
\qquad
u^2-v=0.
$$

Hence $u=2bv$ and $v=u^2$, giving

$$
\boxed{(u_*,v_*)=\left(\frac1{2b},\frac1{4b^2}\right).}
$$

The reaction [Jacobian matrix](../../../calculus.md#jacobian-matrix) there is

$$
J=
\begin{pmatrix}
2b&-4b^2\\
1/b&-1
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\operatorname{tr}J=2b-1,
\qquad
\det J=2b.
$$

Because $b>0$, the [trace-determinant stability criterion](../../../dynamical-systems.md#trace-determinant-stability-criterion) shows that the spatially uniform reaction kinetics are stable exactly when

$$
\boxed{0<b<\frac12.}
$$

For a perturbation proportional to $e^{\lambda t+ikx}$, put $q=k^2$. Its growth rates are the eigenvalues of

$$
J-q
\begin{pmatrix}d&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The trace is $2b-1-(d+1)q<0$ in the reaction-stable regime, while the determinant is

$$
\Delta(q)=dq^2+(d-2b)q+2b.
$$

A [Turing instability](../../../diffusion-equation.md#turing-instability) occurs exactly when this upward-opening quadratic is negative for some $q>0$. By the [two-species diffusion-driven instability criterion](../../../diffusion-equation.md#two-species-diffusion-driven-instability-criterion), this requires

$$
d<2b,
\qquad
(2b-d)^2>8bd.
$$

Together these reduce to

$$
\boxed{0<b<\frac12,\qquad 0<d<(6-4\sqrt2)b.}
$$

Thus the unstable region in the $(b,d)$-plane lies below the straight line $d=(6-4\sqrt2)b$ and to the left of $b=1/2$.

Inside this region, the unstable squared-wavenumber band is

$$
\boxed{\frac{2b-d-\sqrt{(2b-d)^2-8bd}}{2d}
<k^2<
\frac{2b-d+\sqrt{(2b-d)^2-8bd}}{2d}.}
$$

At the bifurcation boundary the two roots coincide, so

$$
k_c^2=\frac{2b-d}{2d}.
$$

Substituting $d=(6-4\sqrt2)b$ yields

$$
\boxed{k_c=\sqrt{1+\sqrt2}.}

Define the [canonical momenta](../../../classical-mechanics.md#canonical-momentum)

$$
p_i=\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\dot q_i}.
$$

When the velocity Hessian is nonsingular, these equations can be inverted for $\dot q_i(q,p,t)$, and the [Hamiltonian](../../../classical-mechanics.md#hamiltonian) is the [Legendre transform](../../../convex-optimization.md#convex-conjugate)

$$
\boxed{H(q,p,t)=\sum_i p_i\dot q_i-\mathcal L(q,\dot q,t).}
$$

Its differential is

$$
dH=\sum_i\dot q_i\,dp_i
-\sum_i\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial q_i}\,dq_i
-\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial t}\,dt,
$$

because the $p_i\,d\dot q_i$ terms cancel. The [Euler-Lagrange equation](../../../analysis.md#euler-lagrange-equation) identifies $\partial\mathcal L/\partial q_i=\dot p_i$, so coefficient comparison gives [Hamilton's equations](../../../classical-mechanics.md#hamilton-s-equations)

$$
\boxed{\dot q_i=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i},
\qquad
\dot p_i=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i}.}
$$

For the [symmetric top](../../../classical-mechanics.md#symmetric-top), the momenta are

$$
p_\theta=A\dot\theta,
\qquad
p_\psi=B(\dot\psi+\dot\phi\cos\theta),
\qquad
p_\phi=A\dot\phi\sin^2\theta+p_\psi\cos\theta.
$$

Thus

$$
\dot\theta=\frac{p_\theta}{A},
\qquad
\dot\phi=\frac{p_\phi-p_\psi\cos\theta}{A\sin^2\theta},
\qquad
\dot\psi=\frac{p_\psi}{B}-\dot\phi\cos\theta.
$$

The Legendre transform gives

$$
\boxed{H=
\frac{p_\theta^2}{2A}
+\frac{(p_\phi-p_\psi\cos\theta)^2}{2A\sin^2\theta}
+\frac{p_\psi^2}{2B}
+Mgl\cos\theta.}
$$

Both $\phi$ and $\psi$ are [cyclic coordinates](../../../classical-mechanics.md#cyclic-coordinate), so $p_\phi$ and $p_\psi$ are conserved. The Hamiltonian has no explicit time dependence and is also conserved. Since $H$ is independent of $\phi,\psi$,

$$
\{H,p_\phi\}=\{H,p_\psi\}=\{p_\phi,p_\psi\}=0
$$

under the [Poisson bracket](../../../classical-mechanics.md#poisson-bracket). Hence three independent [first integrals in involution](../../../classical-mechanics.md#first-integrals-in-involution) are

$$
\boxed{H,\qquad p_\phi,\qquad p_\psi.}
$$

## 16G

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 4](paper-4.md)

<h3 id="16g/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [16G](#16g)

The [epsilon-recursion theorem](../../../set-theory.md#epsilon-recursion-theorem) says that for every definable operation $G$ assigning a set $G(x,h)$ to each set $x$ and each function $h$ with domain $x$, there is a unique class function $F$ such that

$$
\boxed{F(x)=G(x,F\mathbin{\upharpoonright}x)}
$$

for every set $x$.

For uniqueness, suppose $F$ and $F'$ both satisfy the recursion. If they agree on every $y\in x$, then their restrictions to $x$ agree and hence

$$
F(x)=G(x,F\mathbin{\upharpoonright}x)
=G(x,F'\mathbin{\upharpoonright}x)=F'(x).
$$

The [epsilon induction](../../../set-theory.md#epsilon-induction) principle therefore gives $F=F'$.

For existence, call a function $f$ coherent on a transitive set $T$ when

$$
f(z)=G(z,f\mathbin{\upharpoonright}z)\qquad(z\in T).
$$

The same epsilon-induction argument shows that any two coherent functions agree on the intersection of their transitive domains. Assuming coherent functions have been constructed below every member of $x$, their compatible union defines all values below $x$; adjoining the value prescribed by $G(x,\cdot)$ extends the construction to $x$. Epsilon induction establishes this construction for every $x$. The compatible local functions therefore unite to the required class function $F$.

A relation $r$ on $x$ is [well-founded](../../../set-theory.md#well-founded-relation) when every nonempty subset of $x$ has an element with no $r$-predecessor in that subset. It is [extensional](../../../set-theory.md#extensional-relation) when

$$
\{b:b\,r\,a\}=\{b:b\,r\,a'\}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad a=a'.
$$

The [Mostowski collapse theorem](../../../set-theory.md#mostowski-collapse-theorem) states that every well-founded extensional relation $(x,r)$ is isomorphic to membership on a unique [transitive set](../../../set-theory.md#transitive-set) $M$. Define recursively

$$
\pi(a)=\{\pi(b):b\,r\,a\}.
$$

Well-founded recursion makes $\pi$ well defined. Epsilon-style induction along $r$, together with extensionality, shows that $\pi$ is injective. By definition,

$$
b\,r\,a\iff\pi(b)\in\pi(a),
$$

so $\pi$ is an isomorphism from $(x,r)$ onto $(M,\in)$, where $M=\operatorname{ran}\pi$. If $z\in\pi(a)\in M$, then $z=\pi(b)$ for some $b\,r\,a$, so $z\in M$; hence $M$ is transitive. If another collapse $\pi'$ has transitive range, induction gives $\pi'(a)=\{\pi'(b):b\,r\,a\}=\pi(a)$, proving uniqueness.

Finally, suppose $x$ is finite. Its collapse $M$ is a finite transitive set, so every member of $M$ is [hereditarily finite](../../../set-theory.md#hereditarily-finite-set). Therefore every well-founded extensional relation on $x$ collapses to a transitive subset of $V_\omega$.

Conversely, let $x$ be infinite. Choose the initial infinite ordinal $\kappa$ equinumerous with $x$. The successor ordinal $\kappa+1$ has the same cardinality as $\kappa$. Pulling its membership relation back along a bijection $x\to\kappa+1$ gives a well-founded extensional relation on $x$ whose collapse is $\kappa+1$. But $\kappa\in\kappa+1$ is not hereditarily finite, so $\kappa+1\nsubseteq V_\omega$. Consequently,

$$
\boxed{\text{the required sets }x\text{ are exactly the finite sets}.}
$$

For the existence and bound, write $R(r,s)$ for the least $N$ forcing either a red $K_r$ or a blue $K_s$. Given a vertex $v$ of $K_{R(r-1,s)+R(r,s-1)}$, the [pigeonhole principle](../../../algebra.md#pigeonhole-principle) says that at least $R(r-1,s)$ incident edges are red or at least $R(r,s-1)$ are blue. In the first case those red neighbours contain a red $K_{r-1}$, which joins to $v$ to make a red $K_r$, or a blue $K_s$; the second case is symmetric. Hence

$$
R(r,s)\leq R(r-1,s)+R(r,s-1).
$$

Starting from $R(1,s)=R(r,1)=1$ and using [Pascal's identity](../../../combinatorics.md#pascal-s-rule) gives the [binomial upper bound for a Ramsey number](../../../graph-theory.md#binomial-upper-bound-for-a-ramsey-number)

$$
R(r,s)\leq\binom{r+s-2}{r-1}.
$$

Therefore

$$
R(s)\leq\binom{2s-2}{s-1}<\sum_{j=0}^{2s-2}\binom{2s-2}{j}=2^{2s-2}<4^s,
$$

which proves both existence and the required estimate.

To determine $R(3)$, color a [cycle graph](../../../graph-theory.md#cycle-graph) $C_5$ red and its [complement graph](../../../graph-theory.md#complement-graph), also a $C_5$, yellow. This colors $K_5$ without a monochromatic triangle, so $R(3)>5$. At any vertex of a red-yellow $K_6$, at least three of the five incident edges have one color. If an edge among their three other endpoints has that color, it completes a monochromatic triangle with the chosen vertex; if none does, those three endpoints themselves form a triangle of the other color. Thus $R(3)\leq6$, and

$$
\boxed{R(3)=6}.
$$

The same local argument proves uniqueness on $K_5$. No vertex can have three incident edges of one color: their other endpoints would either contain an edge of that color or form a triangle of the other color. Every vertex therefore has [vertex degree](../../../graph-theory.md#degree-graph-theory) at most two in each color, and because the two degrees sum to four, each is exactly two. Each color class is consequently a [regular graph](../../../graph-theory.md#regular-graph) of degree two on five vertices, hence a single $C_5$. Up to relabelling, the coloring is exactly the cycle-and-complement coloring above.

Finally, the [monochromatic-triangle counting formula](../../../graph-theory.md#monochromatic-triangle-counting-formula) gives, for a coloring of $K_6$ with red degrees $d_v$,

$$
M=\binom63-\frac12\sum_vd_v(5-d_v)
\geq20-\frac12(6\cdot6)=2.
$$

The bound is attained by coloring all edges of $K_{3,3}$ red and the two complementary copies of $K_3$ yellow: there are no red triangles and exactly two yellow triangles. The least requested number is therefore

$$
\boxed{n=2}.
$$

## 18I

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 4](paper-4.md)

<h3 id="18i/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [18I](#18i)

Because $K$ has [characteristic](../../../algebra.md#characteristic-of-a-field) $p$, the [prime field](../../../algebra.md#prime-field) $\mathbb F_p$ lies in $K$, and

$$
f(\alpha+c)=(\alpha+c)^p-(\alpha+c)+a=f(\alpha)+c^p-c=0
$$

for every $c\in\mathbb F_p$. These are all $p$ roots. Moreover $f'(t)=-1$, so $f$ is a [separable polynomial](../../../galois-theory.md#separable-polynomial).

Let $m(t)$ be the [minimal polynomial of an algebraic element](../../../galois-theory.md#minimal-polynomial-of-an-algebraic-element) of $\alpha$ over $K$. Translation by $c\in\mathbb F_p$ sends an irreducible factor $g(t)$ of $f(t)$ to the irreducible factor $g(t-c)$. The [additive group](../../../group.md#additive-group) of $\mathbb F_p$ therefore acts on the irreducible factors, and every orbit has size $1$ or $p$. An orbit of size $p$ would already contribute $p\deg m$ to the degree-$p$ polynomial $f$, forcing $\deg m=1$. Then $\alpha\in K$, all roots $\alpha+c$ lie in $K$, and $L=K$, contrary to the hypothesis.

Thus $m(t-c)=m(t)$ for every $c\in\mathbb F_p$. If $1\leq d=\deg m<p$, comparison of the coefficient of $t^{d-1}$ in $m(t-c)-m(t)$ for $c\ne0$ gives $-dc$ times the nonzero leading coefficient, a contradiction. Hence $\deg m=p$, and since $m$ is a monic divisor of the monic degree-$p$ polynomial $f$, we have $m=f$. Thus **$f$ is irreducible over $K$**.

All roots $\alpha+c$ already belong to $K(\alpha)$, so the [splitting field](../../../galois-theory.md#splitting-field) is

$$
\boxed{L=K(\alpha)}.
$$

As the splitting field of a separable polynomial, $L/K$ is a [Galois extension](../../../galois-theory.md#finite-galois-extension). For each $c\in\mathbb F_p$, irreducibility gives a distinct $K$-automorphism

$$
\sigma_c(\alpha)=\alpha+c,
\qquad
\sigma_c\sigma_d=\sigma_{c+d}.
$$

These exhaust the $p=[L:K]$ automorphisms. This is an [Artin–Schreier extension](../../../galois-theory.md#artin-schreier-extension), and

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)\cong(\mathbb F_p,+)\cong C_p}.
$$

Now let $n=m=p$ be [prime](../../../number-theory.md#prime-number) and put $\alpha=\sqrt[p]{p}$ and $A=\mathbb Z[\alpha]$. The [Eisenstein criterion](../../../commutative-algebra.md#eisenstein-criterion) applied at $p$ gives $[\mathbb Q(\alpha):\mathbb Q]=p$, while the [discriminant of elements of a number field](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#discriminant-of-elements-of-a-number-field) of its power basis is

$$
|\operatorname{disc}(1,\alpha,\ldots,\alpha^{p-1})|
=|N(f'(\alpha))|
=|N(p\alpha^{p-1})|
=p^{2p-1},

where $f(X)=X^p-p$. By the [discriminant-index formula for an integral lattice](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#discriminant-index-formula-for-an-integral-lattice), every prime dividing $[\mathcal O_L:A]$ must therefore be $p$.

Suppose $A\ne\mathcal O_L$. The finite [abelian group](../../../group.md#abelian-group) $\mathcal O_L/A$ is then a nontrivial [finite p-group](../../../finite-group-theory.md#finite-p-group), so it contains an element of order $p$. Consequently there is an $x\in\mathcal O_L\setminus A$ such that

$$
x=\frac{g(\alpha)}p,
\qquad
g(X)=\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}c_kX^k\in\mathbb Z[X],
$$

with some $c_k$ not divisible by $p$.

The polynomial $X^p-p$ is Eisenstein, so [total ramification from an Eisenstein polynomial](../../../commutative-algebra.md#total-ramification-from-an-eisenstein-polynomial) gives a unique prime ideal $P$ above $p$ with normalized [discrete valuation](../../../commutative-algebra.md#discrete-valuation)

$$
v_P(\alpha)=1,
\qquad
v_P(p)=p.
$$

Let $k_0$ be the least index for which $p\nmid c_{k_0}$. The term $c_{k_0}\alpha^{k_0}$ has valuation $k_0<p$; every earlier term has valuation at least $p$, and every later term with coefficient prime to $p$ has a distinct, larger valuation. The non-Archimedean valuation therefore gives

$$
v_P(g(\alpha))=k_0<p,
\qquad
v_P(x)=k_0-p<0.
$$

This contradicts $x\in\mathcal O_L$, since an [algebraic integer](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#algebraic-integer) has nonnegative valuation at every [prime ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal). Thus $A=\mathcal O_L$, and

$$
\boxed{\mathcal O_L=\mathbb Z[\sqrt[p]{p}]}.
$$

Represent a class in $H_k(K)$ by a [chain cycle](../../../homology.md#chain-cycle) $z\in Z_k(K)$. z=m+n,
\qquad m\in C_k(M),\quad n\in C_k(N).

Because $\partial z=0$, we have

$$

The left side lies in $C_{k-1}(M)$ and the right side in $C_{k-1}(N)$, so this common chain is supported in $L=M\cap N$. It is a cycle because $\partial^2=0$. The [connecting homomorphism](../../../homology.md#connecting-homomorphism) is therefore

\boxed{\partial_*[z]=[\partial m]=[-\partial n]\in H_{k-1}(L)}.

Changing the decomposition or the representative changes $\partial m$ only by a boundary in $L$, which is why the construction descends to [homology classes](../../../homology.md#homology-class).

## 22F

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 4](paper-4.md)

<h3 id="22f/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [22F](#22f)

<h4 id="22f/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#22f/a)

Choose a countable [dense subset](../../../topology.md#dense-set) $\{x_1,x_2,\ldots\}$ of the [normed vector space](../../../functional-analysis.md#normed-vector-space) $X$. For each fixed $j$, the scalar sequence $(f_n(x_j))$ is bounded because

$$
|f_n(x_j)|\leq\|f_n\|\,\|x_j\|\leq\|x_j\|.
$$

Repeated use of the [Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem](../../../real-analysis.md#bolzano-weierstrass-theorem) followed by a [diagonal subsequence argument](../../../real-analysis.md#diagonal-subsequence-argument) produces a subsequence $(f_n)_{n\in\Lambda}$ for which $f_n(x_j)$ converges for every $j$.

Let $D$ be the linear span of the $x_j$. On every $x\in D$, define

$$
f(x)=\lim_{\substack{n\in\Lambda\\n\to\infty}}f_n(x).
$$

Finite linear combinations show that $f:D\to\mathbb R$ is linear, and passage to the limit in $|f_n(x)|\leq\|x\|$ gives $|f(x)|\leq\|x\|$. Thus $f$ is a [bounded linear functional](../../../topological-vector-space.md#continuous-linear-functional) on the dense linear subspace $D$ and extends uniquely to an element of the [dual space](../../../linear-algebra.md#dual-space) $X^*$ with $\|f\|\leq1$.

For arbitrary $x\in X$ and $y\in D$,

$$
|f_n(x)-f(x)|
\leq|f_n(x-y)|+|f_n(y)-f(y)|+|f(y-x)|
\leq2\|x-y\|+|f_n(y)-f(y)|.
$$

First choose $y$ close to $x$, then let $n\in\Lambda$ tend to infinity. This proves

$$
\boxed{f_n(x)\longrightarrow f(x)\quad\text{for every }x\in X}.
$$

This is the [Sequential Banach-Alaoglu theorem for a separable predual](../../../functional-analysis.md#sequential-banach-alaoglu-theorem-for-a-separable-predual).

<h4 id="22f/b/solution">Solution</h4>

The fixed-point space $I=\ker(U-I)$ is closed. If $y\in I$ and $w=Ux-x\in W$, then [unitarity](../../../vector-space.md#unitary-operator) gives

$$
\langle y,w\rangle
=\langle y,Ux\rangle-\langle y,x\rangle
=\langle U^{-1}y,x\rangle-\langle y,x\rangle=0,
$$

so $I\perp W$.

Conversely, $z\in W^\perp$ exactly when

$$
0=\langle z,Ux-x\rangle
=\langle U^*z-z,x\rangle
$$

for every $x\in H$. Thus $U^*z=z$, equivalently $Uz=z$, and $z\in I$. Hence $W^\perp=I$, so the [double orthogonal complement](../../../hilbert-space.md#double-orthogonal-complement) identity gives

$$
\overline W=(W^\perp)^\perp=I^\perp.
$$

The [orthogonal decomposition by a closed subspace](../../../hilbert-space.md#orthogonal-decomposition-by-a-closed-subspace) now yields

$$
\boxed{H=I\oplus\overline W}.
$$

Define the [Cesaro average](../../../real-analysis.md#cesaro-mean)

$$
A_n=\frac1n\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}U^i.
$$

For $y\in I$, $A_ny=y$. For a generator $w=Uz-z$ of $W$, the sum telescopes:

$$
A_nw=\frac{U^nz-z}{n},
\qquad
\|A_nw\|\leq\frac{2\|z\|}{n}\longrightarrow0.
$$

Moreover $\|A_n\|\leq1$, so approximation extends this convergence from $W$ to $\overline W$. Writing $x=Px+(I-P)x$ according to the orthogonal decomposition gives the [Von Neumann mean ergodic theorem](../../../vector-space.md#von-neumann-mean-ergodic-theorem)

$$
\boxed{\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac1n\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}U^ix=Px}.
$$

<h4 id="22f/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#22f/c)

Fix $z_0\in S^1$, and let $\delta_{z_0}\in C(S^1)^*$ be the [point evaluation functional](../../../topological-vector-space.md#point-evaluation-functional) $\delta_{z_0}(f)=f(z_0)$. Define orbit averages in the dual space by

$$
\mu_n=\frac1n\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}(T^*)^j\delta_{z_0}.
$$

Each $\mu_n$ has norm at most one and satisfies $\mu_n(1_{S^1})=1$. The space $C(S^1)$ is separable, so part (a) gives a subsequence converging pointwise to some $\mu\in C(S^1)^*$. In particular,

$$
\mu(1_{S^1})=1.
$$

For every $f\in C(S^1)$, the [Cesaro average](../../../real-analysis.md#cesaro-mean) telescopes at the endpoints:

$$
\mu_n(Tf)-\mu_n(f)
=\frac{\delta_{z_0}(T^nf)-\delta_{z_0}(f)}n,
$$

whose absolute value is at most $2\|f\|_\infty/n$. Passing to the convergent subsequence proves

$$
\boxed{\mu(Tf)=\mu(f)\quad\text{for every }f\in C(S^1)}.
$$

Thus $\mu$ is an [invariant functional of a composition operator](../../../vector-space.md#invariant-functional-of-a-composition-operator).

It need not be unique when $T$ is not the [identity operator](../../../vector-space.md#identity-operator). For the nonidentity [homeomorphism](../../../topology.md#homeomorphism) $\tau(z)=\overline z$, both $1$ and $-1$ are fixed. Hence the two distinct point evaluations

$$
\delta_1(f)=f(1),
\qquad
\delta_{-1}(f)=f(-1)
$$

have value one on $1_{S^1}$ and are invariant under $T$. Therefore **nonidentity does not imply uniqueness**.

Set

\end{aligned}

The boundary term vanishes because $h$, and hence $g$, has [compact support](../../../function.md#compact-support).

Because $|g|^2=|h|^2e^{-\Phi}$, this is exactly the required estimate.

## 24I

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 4](paper-4.md)

<h3 id="24i/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [24I](#24i)

Let $H$ denote a [hyperplane-section divisor of a projective plane curve](../../../cartier-divisor.md#hyperplane-section-divisor-of-a-projective-plane-curve). The [adjunction formula](../../../complex-geometry.md#adjunction-formula) for a smooth degree-$d$ [projective plane curve](../../../algebraic-geometry.md#projective-plane-curve) states

$$
K_C\sim(d-3)H|_C.
$$

Since $\deg(H|_C)=d$, this gives the [canonical degree of a smooth plane curve](../../../algebraic-topology.md#canonical-degree-of-a-smooth-plane-curve)

$$
\boxed{\deg K_C=d(d-3)}.
$$

Combining this with $\deg K_C=2g-2$ gives the [genus of a smooth plane curve](../../../algebraic-geometry.md#genus-of-a-smooth-plane-curve)

$$
\boxed{g(C)=\frac{(d-1)(d-2)}2}.
$$

[Homogenization](../../../projective-space.md#homogenization-algebra) of the affine equation gives the [projective completion](../../../projective-space.md#projective-completion)

$$
C=V(F)\subset\mathbb P^2,
\qquad
F(X,Y,Z)=X^3Z+XY^3+YZ^3.
$$

This is the [plane model y plus x cubed plus xy cubed equals zero of the Klein quartic](../../../algebraic-geometry.md#plane-model-y-plus-x-cubed-plus-xy-cubed-equals-zero-of-the-klein-quartic): after the coordinate relabelling $[A:B:C]=[Y:X:Z]$, its equation is $A^3B+B^3C+C^3A=0$.

Its first partial derivatives are

$$
F_X=3X^2Z+Y^3,
\qquad
F_Y=3XY^2+Z^3,
\qquad
F_Z=X^3+3YZ^2.
$$

If one of $X,Y,Z$ vanishes at a common zero of these three derivatives, the displayed equations successively force all three coordinates to vanish, which is impossible in [projective space](../../../projective-space.md). If $XYZ\ne0$, multiplying the three derivative equations gives

$$
X^3Y^3Z^3=-27X^3Y^3Z^3,
$$

again a contradiction. The [Jacobian criterion](../../../algebraic-geometry.md#jacobian-criterion) therefore proves that **$C$ is smooth**.

The rational function $y=Y/Z$ defines a [rational map of projective varieties](../../../algebraic-geometry.md#rational-map-of-projective-varieties) $C\dashrightarrow\mathbb P^1$. Because $C$ is a [smooth projective curve](../../../projective-space.md#smooth-projective-curve), it extends uniquely to a morphism

$$
\pi:C\longrightarrow\mathbb P^1.
$$

For a generic finite value of $y$, its fibre is given by the cubic

$$
x^3+y^3x+y=0,
$$

so the [degree of a holomorphic map](../../../complex-analysis.md#degree-of-a-holomorphic-map) is $3$. The [discriminant of a depressed cubic](../../../galois-theory.md#discriminant-of-a-depressed-cubic) is

$$
\Delta(y)=-4y^9-27y^2=-y^2(4y^7+27).
$$

At $y=0$ the fibre consists of $(0,0)$. Since $F_y(0,0)=1$, the [holomorphic implicit function theorem](../../../calculus.md#holomorphic-implicit-function-theorem) gives

$$
y=-x^3+O(x^{10}),
$$

so $x$ is a [local coordinate](../../../complex-analysis.md#local-coordinate) and $\pi$ has [ramification index of a holomorphic map](../../../complex-analysis.md#ramification-index-of-a-holomorphic-map) $3$ there. Each of the seven distinct roots of $4y^7+27$ gives one double, but not triple, root of the cubic in $x$, hence seven further ramification points of index $2$.

It remains to inspect the points at infinity. They are

$$
P=[1:0:0],
\qquad
Q=[0:1:0].
$$

Near $Q$, set $u=X/Y$ and $v=Z/Y$. The equation becomes $u^3v+u+v^3=0$, so $u=-v^3+O(v^{10})$, while $y=1/v$ has a [simple pole](../../../isolated-singularity.md#simple-pole). Thus $e_Q=1$. Near $P$, set $s=Y/X$ and $t=Z/X$. Now $t+s^3+st^3=0$, so $t=-s^3+O(s^{10})$ and

$$
y=\frac{s}{t}=-s^{-2}(1+O(s^7)).
$$

Thus $y$ has a [double pole](../../../isolated-singularity.md#double-pole) at $P$ and $e_P=2$. These calculations are the [ramification of the x-coordinate on the Klein quartic](../../../algebraic-geometry.md#ramification-of-the-x-coordinate-on-the-klein-quartic) after the coordinate relabelling above.

The total ramification contribution is

$$
(3-1)+7(2-1)+(2-1)=10.
$$

The [Riemann-Hurwitz formula](../../../complex-analysis.md#riemann-hurwitz-formula) for the degree-three map to the [projective line](../../../finite-group-theory.md#projective-line) now gives

$$
2g(C)-2=3(-2)+10=4,
$$

and therefore

$$
\boxed{g(C)=3}.
$$

A twice differentiable curve $\gamma:I\to S$ is a [geodesic](../../../riemannian-geometry.md#geodesic) when its velocity is parallel along the curve:

$$
\frac{D\dot\gamma}{dt}=0.
$$

For a surface embedded in $\mathbb R^3$, the [ambient acceleration criterion for a surface geodesic](../../../riemannian-geometry.md#ambient-acceleration-criterion-for-a-surface-geodesic) gives the equivalent condition

$$
\boxed{\ddot\gamma(t)\perp T_{\gamma(t)}S\quad(t\in I)}.
$$

The parameter $t$ is then an [affine parameter](../../../riemannian-geometry.md#affine-parameter), and $|\dot\gamma|$ is constant.

<h3 id="25i/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [25I](#25i)

<h4 id="25i/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#25i/b)

Take the [circular cylinder](../../../differential-geometry.md#circular-cylinder)

$$
S=\{(R\cos\theta,R\sin\theta,z):\theta\in\mathbb R, z\in\mathbb R\},
\qquad R>0.
$$

Its [first fundamental form](../../../differential-geometry.md#first-fundamental-form) is $R^2d\theta^2+dz^2$. The universal covering parametrization $\mathbb R^2\to S$ is therefore a [local isometry](../../../differential-geometry.md#local-isometry) from the [Euclidean plane](../../../geometry-and-topology.md#euclidean-plane), after scaling the first coordinate. By [geodesic preservation by a local isometry](../../../differential-geometry.md#geodesic-preservation-by-a-local-isometry), every geodesic on the cylinder lifts to a straight line defined for all $t\in\mathbb R$. Thus **the cylinder is geodesically complete**. It is noncompact because $z$ is unbounded, and it is not a plane because one of its [principal curvatures](../../../second-fundamental-form.md#principal-curvature) is $1/R$.

<h3 id="25i/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [25I](#25i)

<h4 id="25i/c/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#25i/c)

<h5 id="25i/c/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#25i/c/i)

Suppose that a connected smooth surface $\widetilde S\subset\mathbb R^3$ properly contains $S$. Since $S$ and $\widetilde S$ have the same dimension, $S$ is open in $\widetilde S$. Choose a point of its relative boundary. Inside a sufficiently small compact [normal neighbourhood](../../../riemannian-geometry.md#normal-neighbourhood) in $\widetilde S$, choose $q\in S$ and a closest point $p\in\widetilde S\setminus S$. A minimizing $\widetilde S$-geodesic from $q$ to $p$ stays in $S$ before its endpoint, since encountering the complement earlier would contradict the choice of $p$.

The restriction of this curve is consequently a geodesic of $S$ with a finite maximal endpoint at $p$. On the other hand, the [Gaussian curvature](../../../second-fundamental-form.md#gaussian-curvature) of $S$ is the restriction of the smooth Gaussian curvature of $\widetilde S$, so it remains bounded near $p$. This contradicts condition $(*)$. Hence

$$
\boxed{\widetilde S=S},
$$

so $S$ is an [inextendible embedded surface](../../../differential-geometry.md#inextendible-embedded-surface). This is the [curvature-blowup criterion for inextendibility of a surface](../../../riemannian-geometry.md#curvature-blowup-criterion-for-inextendibility-of-a-surface).

<h4 id="25i/c/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#25i/c)

<h5 id="25i/c/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#25i/c/ii)

For an example satisfying $(*)$, consider the [incomplete surface z equals r to three halves](../../../riemannian-geometry.md#incomplete-surface-z-equals-r-to-three-halves)

$$
S_*=\{(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta,r^{3/2}):r>0\}.
$$

A [meridian of a surface of revolution](../../../differential-geometry.md#meridian-of-a-surface-of-revolution) reaches the missing origin in the finite length

$$
\int_0^{r_0}\sqrt{1+\frac94r}\,dr,
$$

so $S_*$ is [geodesically incomplete](../../../riemannian-geometry.md#geodesic-incompleteness). The [curvatures of a parametrized surface of revolution](../../../differential-geometry.md#curvatures-of-a-parametrized-surface-of-revolution) give

$$
K(r)=\frac{9}{8r(1+9r/4)^2}\longrightarrow+\infty
\qquad(r\downarrow0).
$$

By [constant speed of an affinely parametrized geodesic](../../../riemannian-geometry.md#constant-speed-of-an-affinely-parametrized-geodesic), a geodesic with a finite endpoint has finite length and converges in $\mathbb R^3$. The [finite-dimensional continuation criterion](../../../differential-equation.md#finite-dimensional-continuation-criterion) rules out an endpoint in a compact subset of $S_*$, and infinity is infinitely far away. It must approach the origin, so $(*)$ holds.

The answer to the second question is **no**. The [punctured circular cone](../../../differential-geometry.md#punctured-circular-cone)

$$
S_0=\{(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta,r):r>0\}
$$

is incomplete because a generator reaches the missing vertex in finite time. It is inextendible: a proper connected smooth extension would have to add the origin, but the tangent planes approaching the origin depend on $\theta$ and therefore cannot be the continuous tangent planes of a smooth surface. Yet

$$
K\equiv0
$$

by the [Gaussian curvature of a cone away from its vertex](../../../second-fundamental-form.md#gaussian-curvature-of-a-cone-away-from-its-vertex). Thus this incomplete inextendible surface does not satisfy $(*)$.

## 27J

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 4](paper-4.md)

<h3 id="27j/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [27J](#27j)

Let $\mathcal F_n=\sigma(X_1,\ldots,X_n)$ be the [natural filtration](../../../stochastic-process.md#natural-filtration). An integer-valued random variable $M$ is a [stopping time](../../../martingale.md#stopping-time) with respect to $(X_i)$ when

$$
\boxed{\{M\leq n\}\in\mathcal F_n\quad\text{for every }n}.
$$

Thus whether one has stopped by time $n$ can be decided from the first $n$ observations, without seeing future variables. Equivalently, $\{M\geq i\}\in\mathcal F_{i-1}$.

Write the stopped sum as

$$
\sum_{i=1}^M X_i=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}X_i\mathbf1_{\{M\geq i\}}.
$$

The event $\{M\geq i\}$ depends only on $X_1,\ldots,X_{i-1}$, so its indicator and $X_i$ are [independent random variables](../../../random-variable.md#independent-random-variables). Since $\mathbb E|X_1|<\infty$ and $\mathbb EM<\infty$, [Tonelli theorem](../../../measure-theory.md#tonelli-theorem) gives

$$
\mathbb E\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}|X_i|\mathbf1_{\{M\geq i\}}
=\mathbb E|X_1|\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mathbb P(M\geq i)
=\mathbb E|X_1|\,\mathbb EM<\infty.
$$

We may consequently exchange expectation and summation. Using the [tail-sum formula](../../../probability-theory.md#tail-sum-formula) for $M$ proves [Wald's equation](../../../probability-theory.md#wald-s-equation):

$$
\boxed{
\mathbb E\left[\sum_{i=1}^M X_i\right]
=\sum_{i\geq1}\mu\,\mathbb P(M\geq i)
=\mu\,\mathbb EM
}.
$$

For the [renewal process](../../../probability-theory.md#renewal-process), put $S_n=X_1+\cdots+X_n$ and $N_t=\max\{n:S_n\leq t\}$. The random variable $N_t+1$ is a stopping time. Since $S_{N_t+1}>t$, Wald's equation gives

$$
\mu\bigl(m(t)+1\bigr)=\mathbb ES_{N_t+1}>t,
$$

and hence

$$
\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{m(t)}t\geq\frac1\mu.
$$

For $c>0$, set $Y_i=X_i\wedge c$ and $\mu_c=\mathbb EY_i$. Wald's equation still applies to the stopping time $N_t+1$ and the independent variables $Y_i$. Because $S_{N_t}\leq t$,

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{N_t+1}Y_i
\leq S_{N_t}+c
\leq t+c.
$$

Therefore

$$
\mu_c\bigl(m(t)+1\bigr)\leq t+c,
\qquad
\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{m(t)}t\leq\frac1{\mu_c}.
$$

By the [monotone convergence theorem](../../../measure-theory.md#monotone-convergence-theorem), $\mu_c\uparrow\mu$ as $c\to\infty$. Combining the bounds proves the [elementary renewal theorem](../../../probability-theory.md#elementary-renewal-theorem)

$$
\boxed{\frac{m(t)}t\longrightarrow\frac1\mu}.
$$

For a fixed word over $q$ equally likely symbols, the [waiting time for a word in independent uniform symbols](../../../martingale.md#waiting-time-for-a-word-in-independent-uniform-symbols) has mean $\sum q^k$, where $k$ runs through the lengths of the [borders of a word](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#border-of-a-word), including the full word. The word `lava' has no proper nonempty border, so
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E\tau_{\mathtt{lava}}=100^4=100\,000\,000}.
$$

The word `aa' has borders of lengths $1$ and $2$, so

$$
\boxed{\mathbb E\tau_{\mathtt{aa}}=100+100^2=10\,100}.
$$

For a direct check, let $E_0$ be the expected remaining time with no trailing `a' and $E_1$ with one trailing `a'. Then

$$
E_0=1+\frac1{100}E_1+\frac{99}{100}E_0,
\qquad
E_1=1+\frac{99}{100}E_0,
$$

which gives $E_0=10\,100$.

After subtracting $F(x,t)$, dividing by $\Delta t$ and taking the [limit](../../../calculus.md#limit-of-a-function), the [Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#hamilton-jacobi-bellman-equation) is

<h3 id="30k/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [30K](#30k)

<h4 id="30k/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#30k/b)

Completing the square in the [Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#hamilton-jacobi-bellman-equation) shows that the minimizing feedback is

$$
u^*(x,t)=-\frac{F_x(x,t)}{2c_t}.
$$

This is a [linear-quadratic optimal control](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#linear-quadratic-optimal-control) problem, so set $F(x,t)=a(t)x^2$. The equation and terminal condition become

$$
a'(t)=\frac{a(t)^2}{c_t},
\qquad a(h)=1.
$$

This [Riccati equation](../../../analysis.md#riccati-equation) is separable and gives

$$
\frac1{a(t)}=1+\int_t^h\frac{ds}{c_s}.
$$

Consequently the general optimal feedback law is

$$
\boxed{u^*(x,t)=-\frac{x}{c_t\left(1+\displaystyle\int_t^h ds/c_s\right)}}.
$$

When $c_t=c$ is constant,

$$
F(x,t)=\frac{c}{c+h-t}x^2,
\qquad
\boxed{u^*(x,t)=-\frac{x}{c+h-t}}.
$$

Along the corresponding optimal trajectory, $x_s=x(c+h-s)/(c+h-t)$, so the open-loop control is constant: $u_s^*=-x/(c+h-t)$ for $s\in[t,h]$.

The intended integral representation of the [modified Bessel function](../../../analysis.md#modified-bessel-function) is

$$
I_0(x)=\frac1\pi\int_0^\pi e^{x\cos\theta}\,d\theta.
$$

[Differentiation under the integral sign](../../../analysis.md#differentiation-under-the-integral-sign) gives

$$
I_0'(x)=\frac1\pi\int_0^\pi\cos\theta\,e^{x\cos\theta}\,d\theta,
\qquad
I_0''(x)=\frac1\pi\int_0^\pi\cos^2\theta\,e^{x\cos\theta}\,d\theta.
$$

Since

$$
\frac d{d\theta}\left(\sin\theta\,e^{x\cos\theta}\right)
=\left(\cos\theta-x\sin^2\theta\right)e^{x\cos\theta},
$$

[integration by parts](../../../calculus.md#integration-by-parts) and the vanishing endpoint term yield $xI_0''+I_0'-xI_0=0$.

For large positive $x$, [Laplace's method](../../../analysis.md#laplace-s-method) localizes the integral near the endpoint $\theta=0$. Put $\theta=u/\sqrt{x}$ and use

$$
x\cos\theta=x-\frac{u^2}{2}+\frac{u^4}{24x}+O(x^{-2}u^6).
$$

Extending the exponentially small tail to infinity and evaluating the resulting [Gaussian integrals](../../../calculus.md#gaussian-integral) gives

$$
I_0(x)=\frac{e^x}{\pi\sqrt{x}}
\left(\int_0^\infty e^{-u^2/2}du
+\frac1{24x}\int_0^\infty u^4e^{-u^2/2}du+O(x^{-2})\right),
$$

and therefore

$$
\boxed{I_0(x)\sim\frac{e^x}{\sqrt{2\pi x}}
\left(1+\frac1{8x}+O(x^{-2})\right)}.
$$

Now write $y=x^{-1/2}w$. Direct differentiation gives

$$
y'=x^{-1/2}w'-\frac12x^{-3/2}w,
\qquad
y''=x^{-1/2}w''-x^{-3/2}w'+\frac34x^{-5/2}w,
$$

so substitution into the [ordinary differential equation](../../../differential-equation.md#ordinary-differential-equation) gives

$$
\boxed{w''+\left(\frac1{4x^2}-1\right)w=0}.
$$

For the [Liouville–Green approximation](../../../analysis.md#wkb-approximation), put $Q(x)=1-1/(4x^2)$. The two approximate solutions are

$$
w_\pm(x)\sim Q(x)^{-1/4}
\exp\left(\pm\int^x\sqrt{Q(s)}\,ds\right),
$$

so at leading order $y_\pm(x)\sim x^{-1/2}e^{\pm x}$. The growing solution agrees with the preceding [asymptotic expansion](../../../analysis.md#asymptotic-expansion), including its leading normalization $I_0(x)\sim e^x/\sqrt{2\pi x}$.

## 32E

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 4](paper-4.md)

<h3 id="32e/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [32E](#32e)

An [interval map](../../../dynamical-systems.md#interval-map) has a [horseshoe for an interval map](../../../dynamical-systems.md#horseshoe-for-an-interval-map) when two disjoint subintervals are each mapped across a common interval. It is [Glendinning-chaotic](../../../dynamical-systems.md#glendinning-chaos) when one of its positive iterates has such a horseshoe.

Let the points of the three-cycle be $x_1<x_2<x_3$ and put $I_0=[x_1,x_2]$, $I_1=[x_2,x_3]$. There are two possible cyclic orders. If $x_1\mapsto x_2\mapsto x_3\mapsto x_1$, the [intermediate value theorem](../../../calculus.md#intermediate-value-theorem) gives

$$
I_0\longrightarrow I_1,
\qquad I_1\longrightarrow I_0,
\qquad I_1\longrightarrow I_1
$$

for the [interval covering relation](../../../dynamical-systems.md#interval-covering-relation). Thus there are two closed covering walks of length two based at $I_1$. In the reverse cyclic order there are instead the arrows $I_0\to I_0$, $I_0\to I_1$ and $I_1\to I_0$, again giving two closed walks of length two. The [horseshoe from two closed covering walks](../../../dynamical-systems.md#horseshoe-from-two-closed-covering-walks) therefore shows that $F^2$ has a horseshoe in either case, so $F$ is chaotic.

The [Sharkovsky theorem](../../../dynamical-systems.md#sharkovskii-s-theorem) orders the positive integers as

$$
3\prec5\prec7\prec\cdots
\prec2\cdot3\prec2\cdot5\prec\cdots
\prec2^2\cdot3\prec\cdots
\prec\cdots\prec2^3\prec2^2\prec2\prec1,
$$

and says that a cycle of a given period forces cycles of every period to its right.

Suppose $N=2^rq$ with $q>1$ odd. Then $F^{2^r}$ has a $q$-cycle. The [Sharkovsky theorem](../../../dynamical-systems.md#sharkovskii-s-theorem) gives that map a six-cycle, and squaring it produces a three-cycle. Hence $F^{2^{r+1}}$ has a three-cycle and an iterate of $F$ has a horseshoe. Thus

$$
\boxed{N\text{ not a power of two and an }N\text{-cycle}\implies F\text{ is Glendinning-chaotic}.}
$$

A horseshoe contains the symbolic dynamics of the [Bernoulli shift](../../../measure-theory.md#bernoulli-shift). Periodic binary words give cycles of every symbolic period; after translating from an iterate back to $F$, this supplies cycles of many periods that are not powers of two.

For the [logistic map](../../../dynamical-systems.md#logistic-map), a ten-cycle would imply chaos because $10$ is not a power of two. Hence there is no ten-cycle for $\mu<\mu_\infty$. A three-cycle forces every period, so a ten-cycle exists for $\mu>1+\sqrt8$. The stated facts alone give no conclusion for the intermediate range:

$$
\boxed{\mu<\mu_\infty:\ 	ext{no ten-cycle};
\qquad \mu>1+\sqrt8:\ 	ext{a ten-cycle exists}.}
$$

A three-dimensional [Bravais lattice](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#bravais-lattice) is the set of all integer combinations of three linearly independent primitive vectors. Its [reciprocal lattice](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#reciprocal-lattice) is

$$
\Lambda^*=\{G:G\mathbin{\cdot}R\in2\pi\mathbb Z
\text{ for every }R\in\Lambda\},
$$

and the first [Brillouin zone](../../../quantum-theory.md#brillouin-zone) is the [Wigner-Seitz cell](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#wigner-seitz-cell) of the origin in reciprocal space.

For the stated [FCC lattice](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#face-centered-cubic-lattice), the primitive-cell volume is

$$
\Omega=a_1\mathbin{\cdot}(a_2\times a_3)=\frac{a^3}{4}.
$$

The reciprocal-basis formula gives

$$
\boxed{
 b_1=\frac{2\pi}{a}(-e_1+e_2+e_3),\quad
 b_2=\frac{2\pi}{a}(e_1-e_2+e_3),\quad
 b_3=\frac{2\pi}{a}(e_1+e_2-e_3)}.
$$

A reciprocal primitive cell, and hence the [Brillouin zone](../../../quantum-theory.md#brillouin-zone), has volume

$$
\boxed{\frac{(2\pi)^3}{\Omega}=\frac{32\pi^3}{a^3}}.
$$

In the first [Born approximation](../../../quantum-theory.md#born-approximation), with momentum transfer $q=k-k'$, the [scattering amplitude](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#scattering-amplitude) is

$$
f_V(k;k')=-\frac{m}{2\pi\hbar^2}
\int_{\mathbb R^3}e^{iq\cdot r}V(r)\,d^3r.
$$

For the spherical shell, align the polar axis with $q$ and use its radial [Dirac delta function](../../../distribution-theory.md#dirac-delta-function):

$$
\int e^{iq\cdot r}V_0\delta(r-d)\,d^3r
=4\pi V_0d^2\frac{\sin(qd)}{qd}.
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{\widetilde f_V(k;k')
=-\frac{2mV_0d^2}{\hbar^2}\frac{\sin(qd)}{qd}}.
$$

Translation of each atomic potential multiplies its transform by a phase, so the [crystal lattice structure factor](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#crystal-lattice-structure-factor) gives

$$
f_{V_\Lambda}(k;k')
=\widetilde f_V(k;k')\sum_{R\in\Lambda}e^{iq\cdot R}.
$$

For an infinite lattice, the lattice [Poisson summation formula](../../../fourier-analysis.md#poisson-summation-formula) turns the sum into

$$
\sum_{R\in\Lambda}e^{iq\cdot R}
=\frac{(2\pi)^3}{\Omega}
\sum_{G\in\Lambda^*}\delta^{(3)}(q-G).
$$

Thus the amplitude vanishes away from reciprocal-lattice momentum transfers.

In [elastic scattering](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#elastic-scattering), $|k|=|k'|$ and hence

$$
|q|=2k\sin\frac\theta2.
$$

The two shortest relevant reciprocal-vector lengths of this [FCC lattice](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#face-centered-cubic-lattice) are

$$
|G_2|=\frac{2\pi\sqrt3}{a},
\qquad |G_1|=\frac{4\pi}{a}.
$$

Both are kinematically available when $k>2\pi/a$. Assigning their scattering angles $\theta_2$ and $\theta_1$, respectively, gives

$$
\boxed{\frac{\sin(\theta_1/2)}{\sin(\theta_2/2)}
=\frac{|G_1|}{|G_2|}=\frac2{\sqrt3}}.
$$

$$

<h4 id="35a/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#35a/b)

<h5 id="35a/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#35a/b/ii)

At $B=0$,

$$
M=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\b&a\end{pmatrix},
\qquad a=e^{\beta J},\quad b=e^{-\beta J},
$$

with eigenvalues $\lambda_+=a+b=2\cosh(\beta J)$ and $\lambda_-=a-b=2\sinh(\beta J)$. Diagonalizing gives

$$
\boxed{M^p=\frac12
\begin{pmatrix}
\lambda_+^p+\lambda_-^p&\lambda_+^p-\lambda_-^p\\
\lambda_+^p-\lambda_-^p&\lambda_+^p+\lambda_-^p
\end{pmatrix}}.
$$

Substitution in part (i), together with $Z=\lambda_+^N+\lambda_-^N$, gives

$$
\langle s_1s_i\rangle
=\frac{\lambda_-^{i-1}\lambda_+^{N-i+1}
+\lambda_+^{i-1}\lambda_-^{N-i+1}}
{\lambda_+^N+\lambda_-^N}.
$$

Since $\lambda_-/\lambda_+=\tanh(\beta J)$, the required [One-dimensional Ising correlation function](../../../statistical-physics.md#one-dimensional-ising-correlation-function) is

$$
\boxed{\langle s_1s_i\rangle
=\frac{\tanh^{i-1}(\beta J)+\tanh^{N-i+1}(\beta J)}
{1+\tanh^N(\beta J)}}.
$$

## 36D

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 4](paper-4.md)

<h3 id="36d/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [36D](#36d)

<h4 id="36d/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#36d/a)

The [electric polarization](../../../electromagnetism.md#polarization-density) $P$ is electric dipole moment per unit volume. A polarized body acts as the bound volume and [surface charge density](../../../electromagnetism.md#surface-charge-density)

$$
\rho_{\rm b}=-\nabla\mathbin{\cdot}P,
\qquad \sigma_{\rm b}=P\mathbin{\cdot}n.
$$

Indeed, summing the [scalar potential](../../../quantum-field-theory.md#scalar-potential) of its dipoles and integrating by parts gives

$$
\phi(x)=\frac1{4\pi\epsilon_0}
\int_V P(x')\mathbin{\cdot}\nabla'\frac1{|x-x'|}\,d^3x'
$$



$$
=\frac1{4\pi\epsilon_0}
\left[
\int_V\frac{-\nabla'\mathbin{\cdot}P(x')}{|x-x'|}\,d^3x'
+\int_S\frac{P(x')\mathbin{\cdot}n}{|x-x'|}\,dS'
\right].
$$

This is exactly the potential of $\rho_{\rm b}$ and $\sigma_{\rm b}$.

For the central free charge, [Gauss's law](../../../electromagnetism.md#gauss-s-law) for the [electric displacement field](../../../electromagnetism.md#electric-displacement-field) gives

$$
D(r)=\frac{q}{4\pi r^2}\hat r,
\qquad E=\frac D\epsilon,
\qquad P=D-\epsilon_0E
=\left(1-\frac{\epsilon_0}{\epsilon}\right)
\frac{q}{4\pi r^2}\hat r.
$$

Thus on the sphere,

$$
\boxed{\sigma_{\rm b}
=\left(1-\frac{\epsilon_0}{\epsilon}\right)
\frac{q}{4\pi R^2}}.
$$

<h3 id="36d/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [36D](#36d)

<h4 id="36d/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#36d/b)

The [magnetization](../../../electromagnetism.md#magnetization) $M$ is magnetic dipole moment per unit volume. It produces bound volume and [surface current density](../../../electromagnetism.md#surface-current-density)

$$
J_{\rm b}=\nabla\times M,
\qquad K_{\rm b}=M\times n.
$$

Starting from the [vector potential](../../../calculus.md#vector-potential) of magnetic dipoles and integrating by parts gives

$$
A(x)=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}
\left[
\int_V\frac{\nabla'\times M(x')}{|x-x'|}\,d^3x'
+\int_S\frac{M(x')\times n}{|x-x'|}\,dS'
\right],
$$

which has precisely the form generated by those bound currents.

Write the cylinder radius as $R$ and use cylindrical radius $\rho$. [Ampère's law](../../../electromagnetism.md#ampere-s-circuital-law) for the free axial current gives, inside the material,

$$
H(\rho)=\frac{I}{2\pi\rho}\hat\phi,
\qquad
M=\frac B{\mu_0}-H
=\left(\frac\mu{\mu_0}-1\right)
\frac{I}{2\pi\rho}\hat\phi.
$$

At $\rho=R$ this is tangential to the cylinder. Since $n=\hat r$,

$$
\boxed{K_{\rm b}=M\times\hat r
=-\left(\frac\mu{\mu_0}-1\right)
\frac{I}{2\pi R}\hat z},
$$

and

$$
\boxed{M(R)=\left(\frac\mu{\mu_0}-1\right)
\frac{I}{2\pi R}\hat\phi}.
$$

For $\mu>\mu_0$, the bound surface current therefore runs opposite to the free wire current.

## 37E

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 4](paper-4.md)

<h3 id="37e/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [37E](#37e)

<h4 id="37e/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#37e/a)

For a slowly moving particle in the [weak-field approximation](../../../general-relativity.md#weak-field-approximation), $dt/d\tau=1+O(\Phi,v^2)$ and terms containing two spatial velocities may be neglected. The spatial [geodesic equation](../../../riemannian-geometry.md#geodesic-equation) reduces to

$$
\frac{d^2x^i}{dt^2}=-\Gamma^i{}_{00}.
$$

For $g_{00}=-(1+2\Phi)$, the [Levi-Civita connection](../../../general-relativity.md#levi-civita-connection) is

$$
\Gamma^i{}_{00}
=\frac12g^{ij}(-\partial_jg_{00})
=\partial_i\Phi+O(\Phi^2).
$$

Hence the [Newtonian limit of general relativity](../../../general-relativity.md#newtonian-limit) is

$$
\boxed{\frac{d^2\mathbf x}{dt^2}=-\nabla\Phi}.
$$

<h3 id="37e/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [37E](#37e)

<h4 id="37e/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#37e/b)

<h5 id="37e/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#37e/b/i)

Along the radial curve at fixed $t,\theta,\varphi$, the spatial line element is $dS=\sqrt{1+2M/r}\,dr$. To first order,

$$
S=\int_{r_1}^{r_2}\left(1+\frac Mr\right)dr,
$$

so

$$
\boxed{S=(r_2-r_1)+M\log\frac{r_2}{r_1}}.
$$

<h4 id="37e/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#37e/b)

<h5 id="37e/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#37e/b/ii)

A radial [null geodesic](../../../special-relativity.md#null-geodesic) satisfies

$$
0=-\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)dt^2
+\left(1+\frac{2M}{r}\right)dr^2,
$$

so for the outgoing ray

$$
\frac{dt}{dr}=1+\frac{2M}{r}+O(M^2/r^2).
$$

The coordinate-time interval is therefore

$$
\Delta t=(r_2-r_1)+2M\log\frac{r_2}{r_1}.
$$

The stationary observer at $r_2$ measures [proper time](../../../special-relativity.md#proper-time) $dT=\sqrt{1-2M/r_2}\,dt$. Hence

$$
\boxed{T=(r_2-r_1)+2M\log\frac{r_2}{r_1}
-\frac{M}{r_2}(r_2-r_1)}.
$$

<h4 id="37e/b/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#37e/b)

<h5 id="37e/b/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#37e/b/iii)

Put $\Delta r=r_2-r_1$. Expanding the quotient $S/T$ consistently to first order gives

$$
v_{\rm eff}
=1+\frac{M}{r_2}
-\frac{M}{\Delta r}\log\frac{r_2}{r_1}.
$$

Thus

$$
\boxed{v_{\rm eff}
=1+\frac{M}{r_2}
-\frac{M}{r_2-r_1}\log\frac{r_2}{r_1}}.
$$

Because $\log(r_2/r_1)/(r_2-r_1)\to1/r_2$ as $r_1\to r_2$,

$$
\boxed{\lim_{r_1\to r_2}v_{\rm eff}=1}.
$$

This local limit is the expected speed of a massless particle measured in a freely falling [local inertial frame](../../../physics.md#local-inertial-frame); the finite-interval effective velocity combines nonlocal length and clock comparisons.

## 38C

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 4](paper-4.md)

<h3 id="38c/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [38C](#38c)

The exact gap between the plane and the circle is

$$
h(x)=h_0+a-\sqrt{a^2-x^2}.
$$

In the narrow region relevant to [lubrication theory](../../../viscous-fluid-flow.md#lubrication-theory),

$$
\boxed{h(x)\simeq h_0+\frac{x^2}{2a}},
$$

so changes occur over the streamwise scale $x=O(\sqrt{ah_0})$; below use $x=\sqrt{2ah_0}X$.

Choose the positive $x$ direction to be the direction of the cylinder surface velocity $U=a\Omega$ in the gap, and write $\mu=\rho\nu$ for the [dynamic viscosity](../../../fluid-mechanics.md#dynamic-viscosity). The lubrication velocity profile and [volume flux](../../../fluid-mechanics.md#volumetric-flow-rate) per unit axial length are

$$
u(y)=\frac{p_x}{2\mu}y(y-h)+\frac{Uy}{h},
\qquad
Q=\int_0^hu\,dy=-\frac{h^3p_x}{12\mu}+\frac{Uh}{2}.
$$

Since $Q$ is constant and the pressure returns to the same ambient value as $x\to\pm\infty$, $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p_xdx=0$. Therefore

$$
Q=\frac U2
\frac{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}h^{-2}dx}
{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}h^{-3}dx}.
$$

Using $h=h_0(1+X^2)$ and the supplied integrals gives

$$
\boxed{Q=\frac23Uh_0=\frac23a\Omega h_0},
$$

in the direction of the cylinder surface motion.

The [shear stress](../../../viscous-fluid-flow.md#shear-stress) exerted by the fluid on the cylinder is opposite to

$$
\mu u_y(h)=\frac h2p_x+\frac{\mu U}{h}.
$$

Eliminating $p_x$ and inserting $Q=2Uh_0/3$ gives

$$
\boxed{\tau(x)=-4\mu U\left(\frac1h-\frac{h_0}{h^2}\right)},
$$

where the sign is relative to $U$. Since

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
\left(\frac1h-\frac{h_0}{h^2}\right)dx
=\frac\pi2\sqrt{\frac{2a}{h_0}},
$$

the resisting [torque](../../../classical-mechanics.md#torque) per unit axial length is

$$
\boxed{T=a\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\tau\,dx
=-2\sqrt2\,\pi\rho\nu\Omega
\frac{a^{5/2}}{h_0^{1/2}}}.
$$

Finally, the ratio of streamwise fluid inertia $U^2/\sqrt{ah_0}$ to transverse viscous acceleration $\nu U/h_0^2$ is of order

$$
\frac{a^{1/2}\Omega h_0^{3/2}}{\nu}.
$$

The stated restriction makes this lubrication-scale [Reynolds number](../../../fluid-mechanics.md#reynolds-number) small, justifying neglect of inertia.

## 39C

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 4](paper-4.md)

<h3 id="39c/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [39C](#39c)

For a [Fourier mode](../../../fourier-analysis.md#fourier-mode) $e^{i(kx-\omega t)}$, the wave equation gives

$$
-(1+k^2)^2\omega^2+k^4=0.
$$

Taking the positive-frequency branch,

$$
\boxed{\omega(k)=\frac{k^2}{1+k^2}}.
$$

For $k>0$, the [phase velocity and group velocity](../../../wave-equation.md#phase-velocity-and-group-velocity) are

$$
c_p(k)=\frac{k}{1+k^2},
\qquad
c_g(k)=\frac{2k}{(1+k^2)^2}.
$$

The frequency rises from zero and approaches $1$; $c_p$ reaches its maximum at $k=1$, while

$$
c_g'(k)=\frac{2(1-3k^2)}{(1+k^2)^3}
$$

shows that the front velocity is

$$
V_m=c_g(1/\sqrt3)=\frac{3\sqrt3}{8}.
$$

Thus the wave at the front has

$$
\boxed{k=1/\sqrt3}.
$$

Moreover $c_g/c_p=2/(1+k^2)$, so the packet outruns its crests for $0<k<1$, the speeds agree at $k=1$, and the crests outrun the packet for $k>1$.

The initial conditions give the exact superposition

$$
\varphi(x,t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}A(k)
\left[e^{i(kx-\omega(k)t)}+e^{i(kx+\omega(k)t)}\right]dk.
$$

The condition $A^*(-k)=A(k)$ makes this solution real. For $V=x/t$ with $0<V<V_m$, the equation

$$
\boxed{V=\omega'(\alpha)=\frac{2\alpha}{(1+\alpha^2)^2}}
$$

has two positive roots $0<\alpha_1<1/\sqrt3<\alpha_2$. The second exponential has the corresponding stationary points $-\alpha_1,-\alpha_2$. Since

$$
\omega''(k)=\frac{2(1-3k^2)}{(1+k^2)^3},
$$

the [stationary phase method](../../../analysis.md#stationary-phase-method) and pairing of complex-conjugate contributions give

$$
\boxed{
\varphi(Vt,t)\sim2\operatorname{Re}\sum_{j=1}^2
\left(\frac{2\pi}{t|\omega''(\alpha_j)|}\right)^{1/2}
A(\alpha_j)
\exp\left\{it[\alpha_jV-\omega(\alpha_j)]
-\frac{i\pi}{4}\operatorname{sgn}\omega''(\alpha_j)\right\}
}.
$$

For $-V_m<V<0$, the same picture is reflected and the stationary points have negative wavenumber. For $|V|>V_m$ there are no real stationary points, so a smooth localized spectrum gives a much smaller nonstationary tail outside the wave front.

## 40E

<h4 id="40e/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#40e/a)

Let $y_r^{(E)}=y_{2r}$ and $y_r^{(O)}=y_{2r+1}$. Since $\omega_{2m}^2=\omega_m$,

$$
x_\ell=\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}\omega_m^{r\ell}y_{2r}
+\omega_{2m}^{\ell}\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}\omega_m^{r\ell}y_{2r+1}.
$$

Hence, for $0\leq\ell<m$, the [even--odd fast Fourier transform recursion](../../../numerical-analysis.md#even-odd-fast-fourier-transform-recursion) is

$$
\boxed{x_\ell=x_\ell^{(E)}+\omega_{2m}^{\ell}x_\ell^{(O)},
\qquad
x_{\ell+m}=x_\ell^{(E)}-\omega_{2m}^{\ell}x_\ell^{(O)}}.
$$

Only one twiddle-factor multiplication is needed for each value of $\ell$.

<h3 id="40e/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [40E](#40e)

<h4 id="40e/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#40e/b)

The radix-two [Fast Fourier transform](../../../numerical-analysis.md#cooley-tukey-fft-algorithm) recursively applies part (a) until it reaches one-point transforms, then combines adjacent even and odd transforms in butterfly operations. For $n=8$, the decomposition tree is

$$
\mathcal F_8^{-1}
\longrightarrow2\mathcal F_4^{-1}
\longrightarrow4\mathcal F_2^{-1}
\longrightarrow8\mathcal F_1^{-1},
$$

followed by the reverse sequence of twiddle-factor butterfly combinations. This is the usual three-stage FFT network.
