## 1G

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="1g/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1G](#1g)

An [arithmetic function](../../../number-theory.md#arithmetic-function) $f$ is [multiplicative](../../../number-theory.md#multiplicative-function) if $f(mn)=f(m)f(n)$ whenever $m$ and $n$ are [coprime integers](../../../number-theory.md#coprime-integers). If $g(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}f(d)$ and $(m,n)=1$, every positive [integer divisor](../../../number-theory.md#divisor) of $mn$ is uniquely $d_1d_2$ with $d_1\mid m$ and $d_2\mid n$. Hence

$$
g(mn)=\sum_{d_1\mid m}\sum_{d_2\mid n}f(d_1d_2)
=\left(\sum_{d_1\mid m}f(d_1)\right)
 \left(\sum_{d_2\mid n}f(d_2)\right)
=g(m)g(n),
$$

so $g$ is [multiplicative](../../../number-theory.md#multiplicative-function).

The [Möbius function](../../../number-theory.md#mobius-function) is defined by $\mu(1)=1$, $\mu(n)=0$ if the [square](../../../number-theory.md#square-number) of a [prime number](../../../number-theory.md#prime-number) divides $n$, and $\mu(n)=(-1)^r$ if $n$ is a product of $r$ distinct [prime numbers](../../../number-theory.md#prime-number). Coprime integers have disjoint sets of [prime factors](../../../number-theory.md#prime-factor), so this definition immediately gives $\mu(mn)=\mu(m)\mu(n)$ when $(m,n)=1$.

For $n=p_1^{a_1}\cdots p_r^{a_r}>1$, only the [square-free](../../../number-theory.md#square-free-integer) divisors contribute, and therefore

$$
\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)=\prod_{j=1}^r(1-1)=0.
$$

For $n=1$ the sum is $1$. Thus $\mathbf 1*\mu=\varepsilon$ in the language of [Dirichlet convolution](../../../number-theory.md#dirichlet-convolution). Convolving $g=\mathbf 1*f$ with $\mu$ gives the [Möbius inversion formula](../../../number-theory.md#mobius-inversion-formula)

$$
f(n)=(\mu*g)(n)=\sum_{e\mid n}\mu(e)g(n/e).
$$

If $f(n)=n$, its [divisor sum](../../../number-theory.md#divisor-sum) is the [sum-of-divisors function](../../../number-theory.md#sum-of-divisors-function). Conversely, if $g(n)=n$, [Möbius inversion](../../../number-theory.md#mobius-inversion-formula) gives

$$
f(n)=\sum_{e\mid n}\mu(e)\frac ne=\varphi(n),
$$

using the standard divisor identity for the [Euler totient function](../../../number-theory.md#euler-totient-function). Therefore the two requested answers are

$$
\boxed{g(n)=\sigma_1(n),\qquad f(n)=\varphi(n).}
$$

## 2F

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="2f/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2F](#2f)

One form of the [Baire category theorem](../../../topological-analysis.md#baire-category-theorem) says that a nonempty [complete metric space](../../../topological-analysis.md#complete-metric-space) cannot be a countable union of closed [nowhere dense sets](../../../topological-analysis.md#nowhere-dense-set).

For each nonnegative integer $n$, put

$$
Z_n=\{z\in\mathbb C:f^{(n)}(z)=0\}.
$$

Because $f$ is an [entire function](../../../complex-analysis.md#entire-function), every [derivative](../../../calculus.md#derivative) $f^{(n)}$ is continuous and $Z_n$ is closed. No $f^{(n)}$ is identically zero: if $f^{(n)}\equiv0$ for some $n$, repeated integration shows that $f$ is a [polynomial](../../../polynomial.md). By the [identity theorem](../../../complex-analysis.md#identity-theorem), the zero set of the nonzero [holomorphic function](../../../complex-analysis.md#holomorphic-function) $f^{(n)}$ has empty interior. Thus each $Z_n$ is [nowhere dense](../../../topological-analysis.md#nowhere-dense-set).

The [complex plane](../../../complex-analysis.md#complex-plane) is complete, so the [Baire category theorem](../../../topological-analysis.md#baire-category-theorem) gives

$$
\mathbb C\ne\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}Z_n.
$$

Choose $z_0$ outside this union. Then $f^{(n)}(z_0)\ne0$ for every $n$, and hence every coefficient $f^{(n)}(z_0)/n!$ in the [Taylor series](../../../calculus.md#taylor-series) about $z_0$ is nonzero. **Such a point $z_0$ therefore exists.**

## 3H

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="3h/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3H](#3h)

The relation $g(X)h(X)=X^7-1$ makes this a length-seven binary [cyclic code](../../../coding-theory.md#cyclic-code). Since $\deg g=3$, the [generator polynomial of a cyclic code](../../../coding-theory.md#generator-polynomial-of-a-cyclic-code) gives

$$
\dim C=7-\deg g=4.
$$

The polynomial $g$ itself is a codeword of [Hamming weight](../../../coding-theory.md#hamming-weight) three, so the [minimum distance](../../../coding-theory.md#minimum-distance-of-a-code) is at most three. A nonzero word of weight one cannot vanish at the nonzero element $\alpha$. If a weight-two word $X^i+X^j$ vanished there, then $\alpha^{i-j}=1$; because $\alpha$ has order seven and $0\leq i,j<7$, this forces $i=j$. Hence there are no nonzero words of weight below three, and

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{rank}C=4,\qquad d(C)=3.}
$$

The equation $g(\alpha)=0$ gives $\alpha^3=\alpha^2+1$ in the [finite field](../../../algebra.md#finite-field) $\mathbb F_8$, and therefore

$$
\alpha^4=\alpha(\alpha^2+1)=\alpha^3+\alpha
=\alpha^2+\alpha+1=r(\alpha).
$$

By the [root-evaluation syndrome for a cyclic code](../../../coding-theory.md#root-evaluation-syndrome-for-a-cyclic-code), this is the syndrome of the single error $e(X)=X^4$. Since $d(C)=3$, [minimum-distance decoding](../../../coding-theory.md#minimum-distance-decoding) uniquely corrects one error. Subtracting and adding agree over $\mathbb F_2$, so the decoded codeword is

$$
\boxed{c(X)=r(X)+X^4=X^4+X^2+X+1=(X+1)g(X).}
$$

## 4G

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="4g/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4G](#4g)

<h4 id="4g/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#4g/a)

A [context-free grammar](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#context-free-grammar) is in [Chomsky normal form](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#chomsky-normal-form) when every [production rule](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#production-rule) has one of the forms

$$
A\to BC,\qquad A\to a,
$$

where $A,B,C$ are [nonterminal symbols](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#nonterminal-symbol) and $a$ is a [terminal symbol](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#terminal-symbol). If the language contains the [empty word](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#empty-word), one may additionally permit $S_0\to\epsilon$, with the start symbol $S_0$ absent from all right-hand sides.

<h3 id="4g/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4G](#4g)

<h4 id="4g/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#4g/b)

The standard [conversion of a context-free grammar to Chomsky normal form](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#conversion-of-a-context-free-grammar-to-chomsky-normal-form) proceeds as follows:

[ Add](https://ourbigbook.com/-/topic/add) a fresh start symbol $S_0$ with $S_0\to S$.  
[ Find](https://ourbigbook.com/-/topic/find) the nullable nonterminals, add the variants obtained by omitting nullable occurrences, and remove all [epsilon productions](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#epsilon-production). Omit $S_0\to\epsilon$ as well because the requested language excludes $\epsilon$.  
[ Remove](https://ourbigbook.com/-/topic/remove) every [unit production](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#unit-production) $A\to B$ by copying the non-unit productions reachable from $B$ to $A$.  
[ Remove](https://ourbigbook.com/-/topic/remove) nonterminals that are unreachable from $S_0$ or cannot derive a terminal word.  
[ In](https://ourbigbook.com/-/topic/in) every right-hand side of length at least two, replace a terminal $a$ by a fresh nonterminal $T_a$ with $T_a\to a$.  
[ Replace](https://ourbigbook.com/-/topic/replace) each right-hand side of length greater than two by a chain of binary productions using fresh nonterminals.

Each remaining production is of the required binary or terminal form, and these transformations preserve every nonempty generated word. Thus **$\mathcal L(G_{\rm Chom})=\mathcal L(G)\setminus\{\epsilon\}$.**

<h3 id="4g/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4G](#4g)

<h4 id="4g/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#4g/c)

The original grammar generates

$$
\mathcal L(G)=\{a^n b c^n:n\geq0\},
$$

because each use of $S\to ASc$ adds one $a$ on the left and one $c$ on the right, and $S\to B\to b$ terminates the derivation.

Introduce nonterminals $S_0,X,C$ and use

$$
\begin{aligned}
S_0&\to AX\mid b,\\
S&\to AX\mid b,\\
X&\to SC,\\
A&\to a,\\
C&\to c.
\end{aligned}
$$

Every production is in [Chomsky normal form](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#chomsky-normal-form). The production $X\to SC$ completes one matched $a,c$ pair, while either start-like symbol terminates with $b$, so the grammar generates exactly the same language. **These productions are a valid $G_{\rm Chom}$.**

## 5J

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="5j/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [5J](#5j)

Treat the cell counts as independent [Poisson random variables](../../../discrete-probability-distribution.md#poisson-distribution). Under independence of Month and Hospital, the [independence log-linear model for a two-way contingency table](../../../statistical-modelling.md#independence-log-linear-model-for-a-two-way-contingency-table) is

$$
\log\mu_{ij}=\lambda+\alpha_i+\beta_j,
$$

with no Month–Hospital interaction. Compare its [Poisson deviance](../../../statistical-modelling.md#poisson-deviance) with the appropriate upper quantile of a [chi-squared distribution](../../../probability-theory.md#chi-squared-distribution). This is the [likelihood-ratio test of independence in a contingency table](../../../statistical-modelling.md#likelihood-ratio-test-of-independence-in-a-contingency-table).

The approximation assumes independent counts with correctly specified Poisson means, identifiable parameters, and sufficiently large fitted cell means for the asymptotic chi-squared law to be accurate. Equivalently, one may condition on the margins and use the corresponding multinomial sampling formulation.

For the $12\times3$ month table there are $36$ cells and $1+11+2=14$ independent model parameters, so the residual [degrees of freedom](../../../classical-mechanics.md#degree-of-freedom) are

$$
(12-1)(3-1)=22.
$$

Since

$$
28.51836<\chi^2_{22,0.99}=40.29,
$$

**model 1 does not reject Month–Hospital independence at the $1\%$ level.**

After combining months into four quarters, the table is $4\times3$, giving

$$
(4-1)(3-1)=6
$$

degrees of freedom. Now

$$
17.9181>\chi^2_{6,0.99}=16.81,
$$

so **model 2 rejects Quarter–Hospital independence at the $1\%$ level.**

There is no contradiction. Relabelling twelve months as four quarters aggregates the [contingency table](../../../statistical-modelling.md#contingency-table), changes the null hypothesis, and reduces the degrees of freedom. The quarter-level hospital pattern is coherent enough to cross the much lower six-degree-of-freedom threshold even though the more detailed month-level omnibus test does not. This is an instance of how [aggregation can change a contingency-table independence test](../../../statistical-modelling.md#aggregation-can-change-a-contingency-table-independence-test).

## 6C

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="6c/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [6C](#6c)

For a radial function, the equation is

$$
n_t=\frac D r\frac{\partial}{\partial r}
\left(rn\frac{\partial n}{\partial r}\right).
$$

Therefore, assuming the population density and radial flux decay at infinity and are regular at the origin,

$$
\frac d{dt}\left(2\pi\int_0^\infty nr\,dr\right)
=2\pi D\left[rn n_r\right]_{0}^{\infty}=0.
$$

Thus $N$ is conserved; it is the total population obtained by integrating density over the plane. This is the global form of the equation's [conservation law](../../../physics.md#conservation-law).

Set

$$
n(r,t)=\left(\frac N{Dt}\right)^{1/2}f(x),
\qquad
x=\frac r{(NDt)^{1/4}}.
$$

Then

$$
n_t=\left(\frac N D\right)^{1/2}t^{-3/2}
\left(-\frac12f-\frac14xf'\right),
$$

while the radial diffusion term has the same prefactor and equals

$$
D\nabla\cdot(n\nabla n)
=\left(\frac N D\right)^{1/2}t^{-3/2}
\frac1x\frac d{dx}(xff').
$$

Equating them and multiplying by $x$ gives

$$
\frac d{dx}\left(xff'+\frac14x^2f\right)=0,
$$

so the proposed ansatz is a [similarity solution](../../../partial-differential-equation.md#similarity-solution).

Regularity at $x=0$ makes the constant of integration zero. Wherever $f>0$,

$$
f'=-\frac x4,
\qquad
f(x)=\frac{x_0^2-x^2}{8}
$$

for some $x_0>0$. The nonnegative weak solution continues as $f=0$ once this parabola reaches zero:

$$
\boxed{f(x)=\left[\frac{x_0^2-x^2}{8}\right]_+.}
$$

This is the [two-dimensional Barenblatt profile for quadratic porous-medium diffusion](../../../diffusion-equation.md#two-dimensional-barenblatt-profile-for-quadratic-porous-medium-diffusion); its degenerate diffusivity permits [compact support](../../../function.md#compact-support).

Indeed, conservation requires

$$
1=2\pi\int_0^\infty f(x)x\,dx
=\frac{\pi x_0^4}{16},
$$

so $x_0=(16/\pi)^{1/4}$ if desired. At time $t$ the populated disk has radius $x_0(NDt)^{1/4}$ and hence area

$$
\boxed{A(t)=\pi x_0^2(NDt)^{1/2}.}
$$

## 7B

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="7b/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [7B](#7b)

On the slit plane $\mathbb C\setminus[-a,a]$, choose

$$
F(z)=\sqrt{z-a}\sqrt{z+a}
$$

so that $F(z)\sim z$ as $z\to\infty$. This [branch cut](../../../analysis.md#branch-cut) gives, for $-a<x<a$, the boundary values

$$
F_+(x)=i\sqrt{a^2-x^2},
\qquad
F_-(x)=-i\sqrt{a^2-x^2}.
$$

Integrate $F$ over the boundary of a large disk with a thin slit removed. The upper bank is traversed from $-a$ to $a$ and the lower bank from $a$ to $-a$, so together they contribute

$$
2i\int_{-a}^{a}\sqrt{a^2-x^2}\,dx.
$$

At infinity the [Laurent series](../../../analysis.md#laurent-series) is

$$
F(z)=z\left(1-\frac{a^2}{z^2}\right)^{1/2}
=z-\frac{a^2}{2z}+O(z^{-3}),
$$

and hence the counterclockwise large-circle integral tends to $-\pi i a^2$. By the [Cauchy integral theorem](../../../complex-analysis.md#cauchy-s-integral-theorem), the large-circle and slit-boundary contributions sum to zero. Therefore

$$
-\pi i a^2+2i\int_{-a}^{a}\sqrt{a^2-x^2}\,dx=0,
$$

which yields

$$
\boxed{\int_{-a}^{a}\sqrt{a^2-x^2}\,dx=\frac{\pi a^2}{2}.}
$$

## 8B

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="8b/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [8B](#8b)

The kinetic energy of the three unit masses is $\frac12\sum_i\dot x_i^2$. Expanding the potential gives

$$
V=\frac12\left(3\sum_{i=1}^3x_i^2-2\sum_{i<j}x_ix_j\right).
$$

Thus the [small-oscillation mass and stiffness matrices](../../../classical-mechanics.md#small-oscillation-mass-and-stiffness-matrices) are

$$
\boxed{
T=I_3,\qquad
V=
\begin{pmatrix}
3&-1&-1\\
-1&3&-1\\
-1&-1&3
\end{pmatrix}
=4I_3-J,
}
$$

where $J$ is the all-ones matrix.

The vector $(1,1,1)^T$ is an [eigenvector](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvector) of the stiffness matrix with [eigenvalue](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) $1$. Every vector orthogonal to it has coordinate sum zero and is an eigenvector with eigenvalue $4$. Since $T=I$, the [generalized eigenvalue problem for small oscillations](../../../classical-mechanics.md#generalized-eigenvalue-problem-for-small-oscillations) gives $\omega^2$ equal to these eigenvalues. One orthonormal choice of [normal modes](../../../wave-equation.md#normal-mode) is

$$
\begin{array}{c|c}
\text{mode}&\text{angular frequency}\\ \hline
\dfrac1{\sqrt3}(1,1,1)&1\\[4pt]
\dfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,-1,0)&2\\[4pt]
\dfrac1{\sqrt6}(1,1,-2)&2.
\end{array}
$$

The last two may be replaced by any orthonormal basis of the sum-zero plane because the frequency is degenerate. Hence **the normal frequencies are $\boxed{1,2,2}$.**

In the very early universe, assume species $i$ is in [thermal equilibrium](../../../thermodynamics.md#thermal-equilibrium), is [ultrarelativistic](../../../special-relativity.md#ultrarelativistic-particle) so that $kT\gg m_ic^2$ and $E(p)\simeq pc$, and has negligible [chemical potential](../../../thermodynamics.md#chemical-potential), $\mu_i\simeq0$. Its occupation factor is then the [Bose-Einstein distribution](../../../statistical-physics.md#bose-einstein-distribution) for a boson or the [Fermi-Dirac distribution](../../../statistical-physics.md#fermi-dirac-distribution) for a fermion.

Substitute $E=pc$ and set $y=pc/(kT)$. Since $p=(kT/c)y$ and $dp=(kT/c)\,dy$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\epsilon_i
&=\frac{4\pi g_i}{h^3}\int_0^\infty
\frac{pc\,p^2\,dp}{e^{pc/(kT)}\mp1}\\
&=\boxed{\frac{4\pi g_i}{(hc)^3}(kT)^4
\int_0^\infty\frac{y^3}{e^y\mp1}\,dy}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<h3 id="9b/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [9B](#9b)

<h4 id="9b/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#9b/b)

For a bosonic degree of freedom, the supplied integral gives

$$
I_B=\int_0^\infty\frac{y^3}{e^y-1}\,dy=\frac{\pi^4}{15}.
$$

For a fermionic degree of freedom, use

$$
\frac1{e^y+1}=\frac1{e^y-1}-\frac2{e^{2y}-1}.
$$

Changing variable $u=2y$ in the second integral gives

$$
I_F=I_B-\frac18I_B=\frac78I_B.
$$

This is the energy-density case of the [fermion-to-boson thermal integral ratio](../../../statistical-physics.md#fermion-to-boson-thermal-integral-ratio).

Summing the result of part (a) over all particle species therefore gives

$$
\epsilon
=\frac{4\pi g_*}{(hc)^3}(kT)^4\frac{\pi^4}{15}
=\boxed{\frac{4\pi^5}{15(hc)^3}g_*(kT)^4},
$$

where the [effective number of relativistic energy degrees of freedom](../../../cosmology.md#effective-number-of-relativistic-energy-degrees-of-freedom) is

$$
\boxed{g_*=\sum_{\rm bosons}g_i+\frac78\sum_{\rm fermions}g_i.}
$$

Apply a [Pauli X gate](../../../quantum-theory.md#pauli-x-gate) and then a [Hadamard gate](../../../quantum-theory.md#hadamard-gate) to the third qubit, and apply Hadamard gates to the first two. This prepares

$$
\left(\frac12\sum_{x\in B_2}|x\rangle\right)|-\rangle,
\qquad
|-\rangle=\frac{|0\rangle-|1\rangle}{\sqrt2}.
$$

One application of the [Boolean quantum oracle](../../../quantum-theory.md#boolean-quantum-oracle) now gives, by [quantum phase kickback](../../../quantum-theory.md#phase-kickback),

$$
U_f\left(\frac12\sum_x|x\rangle|-\rangle\right)
=\frac12\sum_x(-1)^{f(x)}|x\rangle|-\rangle
=|f\rangle|-\rangle.
$$

The answer qubit is unentangled and may be ignored. If all three qubits must be restored to the displayed register form, applying $H$ and then $X$ to the answer qubit maps $|-\rangle$ back to $|0\rangle$. Thus **the first register is exactly $|f\rangle$ after one use of $U_f$.**

|f\rangle

\otimes

## 11G

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="11g/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [11G](#11g)

Write an integral [binary quadratic form](../../../number-theory.md#binary-quadratic-form) as $[a,b,c]=ax^2+bxy+cy^2$, with [discriminant of a binary quadratic form](../../../number-theory.md#discriminant-of-a-binary-quadratic-form) $D=b^2-4ac$. It is positive definite when $a>0$ and $D<0$. Two forms are [equivalent](../../../number-theory.md#proper-equivalence-of-binary-quadratic-forms) if one is obtained from the other by an integral linear change of variables whose matrix lies in $\operatorname{SL}_2(\mathbb Z)$. A positive definite form is [reduced](../../../number-theory.md#reduced-positive-definite-binary-quadratic-form) when

$$
|b|\leq a\leq c,
$$

with $b\geq0$ when $|b|=a$ or $a=c$.

To reduce a form, apply a unimodular shear $x\mapsto x+ny$ with $n$ chosen so that the new middle coefficient has $|b'|\leq a$. If the new final coefficient $c'$ is smaller than $a$, exchange the variables, using a determinant-one rotation, so that the positive leading coefficient decreases from $a$ to $c'$. Repeating must stop because a positive integer cannot decrease indefinitely. At termination $|b|\leq a\leq c$, and elementary sign changes impose the boundary convention. This is the [reduction algorithm for a positive definite binary quadratic form](../../../number-theory.md#reduction-algorithm-for-a-positive-definite-binary-quadratic-form), and proves that every positive definite form is equivalent to a reduced one.

The displayed forms have discriminant $1-36=-35$, so the intended discriminant is $-35$. For any reduced form of this discriminant, [enumeration of reduced binary quadratic forms](../../../number-theory.md#enumeration-of-reduced-binary-quadratic-forms) gives

$$
1\leq a\leq\sqrt{\frac{35}{3}}<4,\qquad |b|\leq a,
\qquad c=\frac{b^2+35}{4a}\in\mathbb Z.
$$

The boundary convention and direct checking of $a=1,2,3$ leave only

$$
\boxed{f_1=[1,1,9]=x^2+xy+9y^2,\qquad
f_2=[3,1,3]=3x^2+xy+3y^2.}
$$

Suppose first that a prime $p>7$ is represented by $f_1$. The identity

$$
4f_1(x,y)=(2x+y)^2+35y^2
$$

shows modulo both $5$ and $7$ that $4p$ is a nonzero square. Hence

$$
\left(\frac p5\right)=\left(\frac p7\right)=1.
$$

Similarly,

$$
12f_2(x,y)=(6x+y)^2+35y^2.
$$

Modulo $5$, this says $2p$ is a square, while modulo $7$ it says $5p$ is a square. Since $(2/5)=(5/7)=-1$, the [multiplicativity of the Legendre symbol](../../../number-theory.md#multiplicativity-of-the-legendre-symbol) gives

$$
\left(\frac p5\right)=\left(\frac p7\right)=-1.
$$

Conversely, suppose the two displayed [Legendre symbols](../../../number-theory.md#legendre-symbol) have the same sign. By [quadratic reciprocity](../../../number-theory.md#quadratic-reciprocity),

$$
\left(\frac{-35}{p}\right)
=\left(\frac p5\right)\left(\frac p7\right)=1.
$$

Choose an odd integer $B$ satisfying $B^2\equiv-35\pmod p$. Since $B$ is odd, $B^2+35$ is also divisible by $4$, so

$$
F=\left[p,B,\frac{B^2+35}{4p}\right]
$$

is a positive definite integral form of discriminant $-35$ that represents $p$. Reducing $F$ yields either $f_1$ or $f_2$, and equivalence preserves represented integers. The necessary symbol calculation above determines which one: the positive pair selects $f_1$, and the negative pair selects $f_2$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{
p\text{ is represented by }f_i
\iff
\left(\frac p5\right)=\left(\frac p7\right)
=
\begin{cases}
+1,&i=1,\\
-1,&i=2.
\end{cases}}
$$

## 12G

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="12g/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [12G](#12g)

<h4 id="12g/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#12g/a)

The [pumping lemma for regular languages](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#pumping-lemma-for-regular-languages) states that for every [regular language](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#regular-language) $L$ there is an integer $N$ such that every $w\in L$ with $|w|\geq N$ can be written

$$
w=xyz
$$

with

$$
|xy|\leq N,\qquad |y|\geq1,\qquad
xy^rz\in L\quad\text{for every }r\geq0.
$$

To prove it, let a [deterministic finite automaton](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#deterministic-finite-automaton) with $N$ states recognize $L$. During the reading of the first $N$ symbols of an accepted word $w$, the automaton visits $N+1$ states, including the initial state. By the [pigeonhole principle](../../../algebra.md#pigeonhole-principle), two of them agree, say $q_i=q_j$ with $0\leq i<j\leq N$. Let $x$ be the first $i$ symbols, $y$ the next $j-i$ symbols, and $z$ the remaining suffix. Reading $y$ is a nonempty closed path from $q_i$ to itself. Traversing this path any number $r\geq0$ of times and then reading $z$ therefore reaches the same accept state. This proves all three assertions.

<h3 id="12g/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [12G](#12g)

<h4 id="12g/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#12g/b)

<h5 id="12g/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#12g/b/i)

Because $D$ is minimal, every state is [accessible](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#accessible-state-of-a-deterministic-finite-automaton). Choose a word $u$ taking the initial state to $p$, and let the nonempty word $v$ label the closed path $\gamma$. If a word $w$ took $p$ to an accept state, then

$$
uv^rw\in\mathcal L(D)\qquad(r=0,1,2,\ldots).
$$

These words have strictly increasing lengths because $v\ne\epsilon$, contradicting the assumption that $\mathcal L(D)$ is finite. Hence **no accept state is reachable from $p$.**

<h4 id="12g/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#12g/b)

<h5 id="12g/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#12g/b/ii)

Suppose a path took $p$ to a state $q$. Part (i) implies that no accept state is reachable from either $p$ or $q$. Thus every possible continuation is rejected from both states, so $p$ and $q$ are [indistinguishable states of a deterministic finite automaton](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#indistinguishable-states-of-a-deterministic-finite-automaton). Distinct indistinguishable states cannot occur in a [minimal deterministic finite automaton](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#minimal-deterministic-finite-automaton). Therefore $q=p$, and **there is no path from $p$ to any other state.**

Equivalently, a [cycle state in a minimal deterministic finite automaton for a finite language](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#cycle-state-in-a-minimal-deterministic-finite-automaton-for-a-finite-language) is the unique rejecting sink.

## 13C

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="13c/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [13C](#13c)

<h4 id="13c/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#13c/a)

Define the [Multivariate Fokker-Planck probability current](../../../probability-theory.md#multivariate-fokker-planck-probability-current) by

$$
\boxed{J_i=A_iP-\frac12\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}(B_{ij}P).}
$$

Then

$$
-\frac{\partial J_i}{\partial x_i}
=-\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}(A_iP)
+\frac12\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x_i\partial x_j}(B_{ij}P),
$$

so the [Fokker-Planck equation](../../../probability-theory.md#fokker-planck-equation) is exactly the [continuity equation](../../../physics.md#continuity-equation) $\partial_tP+\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=0$.

<h3 id="13c/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [13C](#13c)

<h4 id="13c/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#13c/b)

Integrating the continuity equation over state space and applying the [divergence theorem](../../../calculus.md#divergence-theorem) gives

$$
\frac d{dt}\int P\,dV
=-\int\nabla\cdot\mathbf J\,dV
=-\int_{\partial V}\mathbf J\cdot d\mathbf S.
$$

Assume the [probability density function](../../../continuous-probability-distribution.md#probability-density-function) and its derivatives decay sufficiently rapidly at infinity, or impose a [zero-flux boundary condition](../../../probability-theory.md#zero-flux-boundary-condition). The boundary integral then vanishes, so $\int P\,dV$ is constant. Since it is initially one, **the distribution remains normalized for all time.**

<h3 id="13c/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [13C](#13c)

<h4 id="13c/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#13c/c)

Assume again that all boundary terms vanish. Multiplying the [Fokker-Planck equation](../../../probability-theory.md#fokker-planck-equation) by $x_k$ and integrating by parts gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac d{dt}\langle x_k\rangle
&=-\int x_k\,\partial_i(A_iP)\,dV
+\frac12\int x_k\,\partial_i\partial_j(B_{ij}P)\,dV\\
&=\int\delta_{ik}A_iP\,dV
=\boxed{\langle A_k\rangle}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The diffusion term vanishes after the second integration by parts.

Similarly, $\partial_i(x_kx_l)=\delta_{ik}x_l+\delta_{il}x_k$ and

$$
\partial_i\partial_j(x_kx_l)
=\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}.
$$

Because $B$ is symmetric,

$$
\boxed{
\frac d{dt}\langle x_kx_l\rangle
=\langle x_lA_k+x_kA_l+B_{kl}\rangle.}
$$

These are the [First two moments of a multivariate Fokker-Planck equation](../../../probability-theory.md#first-two-moments-of-a-multivariate-fokker-planck-equation).

<h3 id="13c/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [13C](#13c)

<h4 id="13c/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#13c/d)

With $A_i=a_{ij}x_j$ and constant $B_{ij}=b_{ij}$, the [Fokker-Planck probability current](../../../probability-theory.md#fokker-planck-probability-current) becomes

$$
\boxed{J_i=a_{ij}x_jP-\frac12b_{ij}\frac{\partial P}{\partial x_j}.}
$$

In the stationary state we impose the stronger zero-current condition $J_i=0$, corresponding to [detailed balance](../../../markov-process.md#detailed-balance).

<h3 id="13c/e">e</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [13C](#13c)

<h4 id="13c/e/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [E](#13c/e)

For

$$
P(\mathbf x)=Z^{-1}\exp\left(-\frac12D_{ij}x_ix_j\right)
$$

with symmetric $D$,

$$
\frac{\partial P}{\partial x_j}=-D_{jk}x_kP.
$$

Substitution into the current from part (d) yields

$$
J_i=\left(a_{ik}+\frac12b_{ij}D_{jk}\right)x_kP.
$$

It vanishes for all $\mathbf x$ exactly when

$$
\boxed{a+\frac12bD=0,\qquad D=-2b^{-1}a.}
$$

Thus the stated [multivariate normal distribution](../../../probability-and-statistics.md#multivariate-normal-distribution) is a stationary solution whenever the zero-current assumption makes $D$ symmetric positive definite. This is the [Stationary Gaussian density for a linear Fokker-Planck equation](../../../probability-theory.md#stationary-gaussian-density-for-a-linear-fokker-planck-equation).

<h3 id="13c/f">f</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [13C](#13c)

<h4 id="13c/f/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [F](#13c/f)

Comparison with the [multivariate normal density](../../../probability-and-statistics.md#multivariate-normal-density) shows that $D$ is its [precision matrix](../../../variance.md#precision-matrix). Since the mean is zero,

$$
\boxed{C_{ij}=\langle x_ix_j\rangle=(D^{-1})_{ij}.}
$$

Multiplying $a+\frac12bD=0$ on the right by $C=D^{-1}$ gives $aC=-b/2$. Taking the transpose and using the symmetry of $b$ and $C$ gives $Ca^T=-b/2$. Hence the stationary covariance obeys the [Continuous Lyapunov equation](../../../control-theory.md#continuous-lyapunov-equation)

$$
\boxed{aC+Ca^T+b=0.}
$$

The same equation follows directly by setting the time derivative of the second-moment equation from part (c) to zero.

## 14B

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="14b/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [14B](#14b)

<h4 id="14b/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#14b/a)

At the centre, spherical symmetry and regularity require a finite central density $\rho(0)=\rho_c$ and

$$
\left.\frac{dP}{dr}\right|_{r=0}=0.
$$

At the stellar surface $r=R$, the pressure vanishes:

$$
\boxed{P(R)=0.}
$$

For a [stellar polytrope](../../../stellar-structure.md#stellar-polytrope) these become the dimensionless conditions $\theta(0)=1$, $\theta'(0)=0$, and the surface is the first zero $\theta(\xi_1)=0$; specifying $\rho_c$ fixes the overall scale.

<h3 id="14b/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [14B](#14b)

<h4 id="14b/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#14b/b)

With the [polytropic equation of state](../../../astrophysical-fluid-dynamics.md#polytropic-equation-of-state) and substitutions

$$
P=K\rho_c^{1+1/n}\theta^{n+1},
\qquad
\rho=\rho_c\theta^n,
\qquad
r=a\xi,
$$

we have

$$
\frac1\rho\frac{dP}{dr}
=(n+1)K\rho_c^{1/n}\frac{d\theta}{dr}.
$$

The pressure-support equation therefore becomes

$$
(n+1)K\rho_c^{1/n}\frac1{r^2}
\frac d{dr}\left(r^2\frac{d\theta}{dr}\right)
=-4\pi G\rho_c\theta^n.
$$

Choose

$$
\boxed{a^2=\frac{(n+1)K}{4\pi G}\rho_c^{(1-n)/n}.}
$$

Changing from $r$ to $\xi$ then yields the [Lane-Emden equation](../../../nonlinear-analysis.md#lane-emden-equation)

$$
\boxed{\frac1{\xi^2}\frac d{d\xi}
\left(\xi^2\frac{d\theta}{d\xi}\right)=-\theta^n.}
$$

These are the [Lane-Emden variables for a stellar polytrope](../../../stellar-structure.md#lane-emden-variables-for-a-stellar-polytrope).

<h3 id="14b/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [14B](#14b)

<h4 id="14b/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#14b/c)

Using the [enclosed mass](../../../stellar-structure.md#enclosed-mass) integral, $R=a\xi_1$, and the Lane-Emden equation,

$$
\begin{aligned}
M
&=4\pi\int_0^R\rho(r)r^2\,dr\\
&=4\pi a^3\rho_c\int_0^{\xi_1}\theta^n\xi^2\,d\xi\\
&=4\pi a^3\rho_c
\left[-\xi^2\theta'(\xi)\right]_{\xi=\xi_1}
=4\pi a^3\rho_cY_n.
\end{aligned}
$$

Substituting the value of $a$ from part (b) gives the [Lane-Emden mass formula](../../../stellar-structure.md#lane-emden-mass-formula)

$$
\boxed{
M=\frac1{2\sqrt\pi}
\left(\frac{(n+1)K}{G}\right)^{3/2}
\rho_c^{(3-n)/(2n)}Y_n,
\qquad
Y_n=-\xi_1^2\theta'(\xi_1).}
$$

<h3 id="14b/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [14B](#14b)

<h4 id="14b/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#14b/d)

Since $R=a\xi_1$, the scaling in part (b) gives

$$
\rho_c^{(1-n)/n}
=\frac{4\pi G}{(n+1)K}\frac{R^2}{\xi_1^2}.
$$

Substitute this into $M=4\pi(R/\xi_1)^3\rho_cY_n$. After collecting powers,

$$
\boxed{M=A_nK^{n/(n-1)}R^{(3-n)/(1-n)},}
$$

where

$$
\boxed{
A_n=4\pi Y_n
\left(\frac{n+1}{4\pi G}\right)^{n/(n-1)}
\xi_1^{(3-n)/(n-1)}.}
$$

This is the [polytropic mass-radius relation](../../../stellar-structure.md#polytropic-mass-radius-relation).

An $n=3$ polytrope models a [white dwarf](../../../stellar-astrophysics.md#white-dwarf) whose electrons are an ultrarelativistic degenerate gas. For $n=3$, the radius exponent vanishes and the mass is independent of both $R$ and $\rho_c$. This fixed limiting value is the [Chandrasekhar mass](../../../stellar-astrophysics.md#chandrasekhar-limit): increasing the central density can no longer support a larger mass, so a cold nonrotating white dwarf above the limit cannot remain in hydrostatic equilibrium.

## 16G

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="16g/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [16G](#16g)

The [compactness theorem](../../../mathematical-logic.md#compactness-theorem) for first-order logic says that a set $T$ of first-order sentences has a model if and only if every finite subset of $T$ has a model. The forward implication is immediate. Conversely, if every finite subset is satisfiable, then $T$ is syntactically consistent: any proof of a contradiction would use only finitely many premises. The [Godel completeness theorem](../../../mathematical-logic.md#godel-s-completeness-theorem) then gives a model of $T$.

The [Upward Lowenheim-Skolem theorem](../../../mathematical-logic.md#upward-lowenheim-skolem-theorem) says that a first-order theory with an infinite model has arbitrarily large models. Given an infinite model of $T$ and any cardinal $\kappa$, enlarge the language by constants $c_\alpha$ for $\alpha<\kappa$ and add

$$
c_\alpha\ne c_\beta\qquad(\alpha\ne\beta).
$$

Every finite subset can interpret its finitely many constants as distinct elements of the original infinite model. By the [compactness theorem](../../../mathematical-logic.md#compactness-theorem), the enlarged theory has a model containing at least $\kappa$ distinct elements; its reduct is a model of $T$.

Finally, suppose a set $S$ and a finite set $T$ axiomatize the same class. Let $\tau$ be the conjunction of the sentences in $T$. Since $S\models\tau$, the set $S\cup\{\neg\tau\}$ is unsatisfiable. Compactness gives a finite $S_0\subseteq S$ such that $S_0\models\tau$. Because $\tau\models S$, we also have $S_0\models S$, while $S\models S_0$ is immediate. Therefore **yes: the finite subset $S_0$ axiomatizes the same theory.** This is [finite subaxiomatization from an equivalent finite theory](../../../mathematical-logic.md#finite-subaxiomatization-from-an-equivalent-finite-theory).

## 17I

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="17i/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [17I](#17i)

A $k$-colouring assigns one of $k$ colours to every vertex so that adjacent vertices have different colours. The [chromatic number](../../../graph-theory.md#chromatic-number) $\chi(G)$ is the least such $k$. An [independent set](../../../graph-theory.md#independent-set-graph-theory) contains no adjacent pair, and the [independence number](../../../graph-theory.md#independence-number) $\alpha(G)$ is the maximum size of such a set.

For a concrete first example, start with the five-cycle $C_5$ and apply the [Mycielski construction](../../../graph-theory.md#mycielskian) $r-2$ times. Each application raises the chromatic number by one and preserves triangle-freeness. The resulting graph has chromatic number $r+1>r$ and contains no triangle, hence no [complete graph](../../../graph-theory.md#complete-graph) $K_r$ for $r\geq3$.

We next prove the stronger high-[girth](../../../graph-theory.md#girth) assertion by the [probabilistic method](../../../probabilistic-combinatorics.md#probabilistic-method). For a large integer $n$, take

$$
G\sim G(n,p),\qquad p=n^{-1+1/(2g)}.
$$

If $X$ counts cycles of lengths $3,\ldots,g$, then

$$
\mathbb EX
\leq\sum_{j=3}^g\frac{n^jp^j}{2j}
=O\left((np)^g\right)
=O(n^{1/2}).
$$

By the [first moment method](../../../probability-inequality.md#first-moment-method), with probability tending to one $X<n/2$.

Let $m=\lfloor n/(2k)\rfloor$. The expected number of independent sets of size $m$ is at most

$$
\binom nm(1-p)^{\binom m2}
\leq
\left(\frac{en}{m}\right)^m
\exp\left(-\frac{pm(m-1)}2\right)
\longrightarrow0,
$$

because $pm^2=\Theta(n^{1+1/(2g)})$ dominates $m\log(en/m)=O(n)$. Hence with positive probability $G$ has fewer than $n/2$ short cycles and $\alpha(G)<m$.

Delete one vertex from each cycle of length at most $g$. The remaining graph $H$ has no such cycle, has at least $n/2$ vertices, and still has $\alpha(H)<n/(2k)$. Since each colour class is independent,

$$
\boxed{\chi(H)\geq\frac{|V(H)|}{\alpha(H)}\geq k.}
$$

This proves that there are [graphs of arbitrarily high girth and chromatic number](../../../graph-theory.md#graphs-of-arbitrarily-high-girth-and-chromatic-number).

For the final claim, begin instead with

$$
G\sim G(N,p),\qquad N=2n,\qquad p=N^{-0.69}.
$$

The expected number $T$ of triangles is

$$
\mathbb ET=\binom N3p^3=O(N^{0.93})=o(n),
$$

so with probability tending to one $T<n$. Put $m=\lceil n^{0.7}\rceil$. The expected number of independent $m$-sets is bounded by

$$
\binom Nm(1-p)^{\binom m2}
\leq\exp\left(
m\log\frac{eN}{m}-\frac{pm(m-1)}2
\right)\longrightarrow0,
$$

because the positive term is $O(n^{0.7}\log n)$ whereas the negative term has order $n^{0.71}$.

Thus some such $G$ has fewer than $n$ triangles and no independent set of size $m$. Delete one vertex from every triangle; more than $n$ vertices remain and the graph is triangle-free. Take any induced subgraph on exactly $n$ vertices. Vertex deletion cannot increase the [independence number](../../../graph-theory.md#independence-number), so the resulting graph satisfies

$$
\boxed{\alpha(G)<n^{0.7}.}
$$

This is the [triangle-free graph with sub-power independence number](../../../graph-theory.md#triangle-free-graph-with-sub-power-independence-number) construction.

Put $F=L^G$ and $n=|G|$. The [automorphism-count bound for a finite field extension](../../../galois-theory.md#automorphism-count-bound-for-a-finite-field-extension) gives

$$
n\leq[L:F].
$$

For the reverse inequality, let $x_1,\ldots,x_m$ be linearly independent over $F$. We claim that the $n\times m$ matrix

$$
M=(g(x_j))_{g\in G,\,1\leq j\leq m}
$$

has linearly independent columns over $L$. If not, choose a nonzero relation

$$
\sum_jc_jg(x_j)=0\qquad\text{for every }g\in G
$$

with the fewest nonzero coefficients, and normalize one coefficient to $c_1=1$. Applying $h\in G$ and reindexing the rows gives another relation with coefficients $h(c_j)$. Subtracting eliminates the first term, so minimality forces $h(c_j)=c_j$ for every $h$ and $j$. Thus every $c_j\in F$. The row for the identity automorphism then contradicts the $F$-linear independence of the $x_j$.

Therefore $m\leq n$. Taking an $F$-basis of $L$ gives $[L:F]\leq n$, and hence

$$
\boxed{[L:L^G]=|G|.}
$$

This proves the degree assertion in the [Artin fixed-field theorem](../../../galois-theory.md#artin-fixed-field-theorem).

Consequently $[L^G:E]=1$, so

<h3 id="19i/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [19I](#19i)

The [row orthogonality relations for a character table](../../../representation-theory.md#row-orthogonality-relations-for-a-character-table) state that for irreducible characters $\chi,\psi$,

$$
\boxed{\sum_{g\in G}\chi(g)\overline{\psi(g)}
=|G|\delta_{\chi\psi}.}
$$

Equivalently, if $c$ runs through representatives of the conjugacy classes $C$,

$$
\sum_C|C|\chi(c)\overline{\psi(c)}
=|G|\delta_{\chi\psi}.
$$

Fix an irreducible $\chi$. The sum of the elements in a conjugacy class $C$ is central in $\mathbb CG$, so [Schur lemma](../../../representation-theory.md#schur-s-lemma) says that it acts in the representation affording $\chi$ by the scalar

$$
\omega_\chi(C)=\frac{|C|\chi(c)}{\chi(1)}.
$$

This [central character value of a conjugacy-class sum](../../../representation-theory.md#central-character-value-of-a-conjugacy-class-sum) is an [algebraic integer](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#algebraic-integer): the class sum acts by a matrix with integer entries on the [regular representation](../../../representation-theory.md#regular-representation), and $\omega_\chi(C)$ is one of its eigenvalues. Also $\overline{\chi(c)}$ is an algebraic integer because character values are sums of roots of unity.

Row orthogonality with $\psi=\chi$ now gives

$$
\frac{|G|}{\chi(1)}
=\sum_C
\frac{|C|\chi(c)}{\chi(1)}\overline{\chi(c)}
=\sum_C\omega_\chi(C)\overline{\chi(c)}.
$$

The right-hand side is an algebraic integer. The left-hand side is rational, and every rational algebraic integer is an integer. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\chi(1)\mid|G|.}
$$

<h3 id="19i/i">i</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [19I](#19i)

<h4 id="19i/i/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#19i/i)

Let $|G|=p^2$. By [irreducible character degree divides the group order](../../../representation-theory.md#irreducible-character-degree-divides-the-group-order), each irreducible degree divides $p^2$. A degree is at most $\sqrt{|G|}=p$ by the [sum of squares of irreducible degrees](../../../representation-theory.md#sum-of-squares-of-irreducible-degrees). If a degree $p$ occurred, its square would already consume the entire sum $p^2$, leaving no room for the trivial character. Hence every irreducible degree is one. A finite group has only linear irreducible characters exactly when it is abelian, so **every group of order $p^2$ is abelian.**

<h3 id="19i/ii">ii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [19I](#19i)

<h4 id="19i/ii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#19i/ii)

For $p=2$, every group of order four is abelian. Now let $p$ be odd. Degree divisibility and the sum-of-squares formula show that every nonlinear irreducible character has degree two: the other divisors $p$ and $2p$ have squares larger than $2p$.

Let $a$ be the number of linear characters and $b$ the number of degree-two characters. The linear characters are precisely the characters of the [abelianization](../../../group-theory.md#abelianization), so

$$
a=|G/G'|\mid2p.
$$

The degree-sum formula gives

$$
a+4b=2p,
$$

so $a\equiv2\pmod4$. Among the divisors of $2p$, this forces $a=2$ or $a=2p$. In the latter case $b=0$ and all degrees are one. In the former,

$$
b=\frac{2p-2}{4}=\frac{p-1}{2}.
$$

Thus the degrees are

$$
\boxed{1,1,\underbrace{2,\ldots,2}_{(p-1)/2}}
$$

unless they are all one.

<h3 id="19i/iii">iii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [19I](#19i)

<h4 id="19i/iii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#19i/iii)

Suppose a finite simple group had an irreducible representation $\rho$ of degree two. Its kernel is normal and cannot be the whole group, so $\rho$ is faithful. A nonabelian simple group has no nontrivial linear character; consequently $\det\rho$ is trivial and $\rho(G)\subseteq\operatorname{SL}_2(\mathbb C)$.

Since $2\mid|G|$ by degree divisibility, [Cauchy's theorem for finite groups](../../../finite-group-theory.md#cauchy-theorem-for-groups) gives an involution $x\in G$. A nonidentity matrix of order two in $\operatorname{SL}_2(\mathbb C)$ has both eigenvalues $-1$, hence equals $-I$. Thus $\rho(x)=-I$ is central in $\rho(G)$. Faithfulness makes $x$ central in $G$, contradicting the trivial centre of a nonabelian simple group. Therefore **no finite simple group has an irreducible character of degree two.**

<h3 id="19i/iv">iv</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [19I](#19i)

<h4 id="19i/iv/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#19i/iv)

Let $|G|=pq$ with primes $p>q$ and $G$ nonabelian. The [Sylow theorems](../../../finite-group-theory.md#sylow-theorems) give $n_p\mid q$ and $n_p\equiv1\pmod p$, so $n_p=1$ and the Sylow $p$-subgroup $P$ is normal. Also $n_q\mid p$ and $n_q\equiv1\pmod q$. If $n_q=1$, both Sylow subgroups are normal and $G$ is their abelian direct product, a contradiction. Hence $n_q=p$, and

$$
\boxed{q\mid p-1.}
$$

Because $G/P$ is abelian, the [commutator subgroup](../../../group-theory.md#commutator-subgroup) $G'$ lies in $P$. It is nontrivial because $G$ is nonabelian, and $|P|=p$ is prime, so $G'=P$. Therefore

$$
|G/G'|=q,
$$

and $G$ has exactly $q$ [linear characters](../../../representation-theory.md#linear-character).

Every nonlinear irreducible degree divides $pq$. Its square is at most $pq$, so the only possibility is degree $q$. If there are $b$ such characters, the [sum of squares of irreducible degrees](../../../representation-theory.md#sum-of-squares-of-irreducible-degrees) gives

$$
pq=q+bq^2,
\qquad
b=\frac{p-1}{q}.
$$

The number of irreducible characters equals the number of conjugacy classes, so

$$
\boxed{k(G)=q+\frac{p-1}{q}.}
$$

These are the [irreducible characters of a nonabelian group of order p q](../../../representation-theory.md#irreducible-characters-of-a-nonabelian-group-of-order-p-q).

\phi(a_i)=1\ (i>1).
$$

Its kernel determines a connected regular [four-sheeted cover of a wedge of circles and a real projective plane](../../../algebraic-topology.md#four-sheeted-cover-of-a-wedge-of-circles-and-a-real-projective-plane), say $\widetilde X_n\to X_n$.

Use the CW structure on $X_n$ with one vertex, one edge for every $a_i$ and for $b$, and one two-cell attached along $b^2$. In the regular cover, the degree-two cellular chain group is

$$
C_2(\widetilde X_n)\cong\mathbb Z[V_4].
$$

The [cellular boundary formula](../../../homology.md#cellular-boundary-formula) gives the lifted attaching word $b^2$ the boundary

$$
\partial_2(c)=(1+v)c
$$

in the $b$-edge summand of $C_1$. Therefore

$$
\ker\partial_2
=\mathbb Z(1-v)\oplus\mathbb Z(u-uv)
\cong\mathbb Z^2.
$$

There are no three-cells, so

$$
\boxed{H_2(\widetilde X_n;\mathbb Z)\cong\mathbb Z^2.}
$$

If $X_n$ were homotopy equivalent to a compact connected surface $S$, the subgroup corresponding to this cover would produce a connected four-sheeted cover $\widetilde S$ homotopy equivalent to $\widetilde X_n$. A finite cover of a compact surface is again a compact connected surface. But the [integral top homology of a compact connected surface](../../../topology.md#integral-top-homology-of-a-compact-connected-surface) has rank at most one, contradicting the displayed rank two. Hence **$X_n$ is not homotopy equivalent to a compact connected surface for any $n\geq1$.**

## 21F

<h3 id="21f/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [21F](#21f)

<h4 id="21f/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#21f/a)

Let $(T_m)$ be Cauchy in the [operator norm](../../../continuous-dual-space.md#operator-norm) on $\mathcal B(X,Y)$. For each $x\in X$,

$$
\lVert T_mx-T_lx\rVert
\leq\lVert T_m-T_l\rVert\,\lVert x\rVert,
$$

so $(T_mx)$ is Cauchy in the [Banach space](../../../banach-space.md) $Y$. Define

$$
Tx=\lim_{m\to\infty}T_mx.
$$

Pointwise passage to the limit shows that $T$ is linear. A norm-Cauchy sequence is bounded, say $\lVert T_m\rVert\leq M$, and hence

$$
\lVert Tx\rVert\leq M\lVert x\rVert.
$$

Thus $T$ is bounded. Finally, for fixed $m$,

$$
\lVert(T_m-T)x\rVert
=\lim_{l\to\infty}\lVert(T_m-T_l)x\rVert
\leq\left(\limsup_{l\to\infty}\lVert T_m-T_l\rVert\right)\lVert x\rVert.
$$

The right-hand coefficient tends to zero with $m$, so $T_m\to T$ in operator norm. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\mathcal B(X,Y)\text{ is a Banach space}.}
$$

This is the [Banach space of bounded linear operators](../../../topological-vector-space.md#banach-space-of-bounded-linear-operators).

<h3 id="21f/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [21F](#21f)

<h4 id="21f/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#21f/b)

For $x\in X$, choose $x_m\in D$ with $x_m\to x$ and define

$$
\widetilde Tx=\lim_{m\to\infty}Tx_m.
$$

The sequence on the right is Cauchy because

$$
\lVert Tx_m-Tx_l\rVert\leq\lVert T\rVert\lVert x_m-x_l\rVert,
$$

and it converges because $Y$ is complete. The same estimate shows that the limit is independent of the approximating sequence, is linear, and satisfies

$$
\lVert\widetilde Tx\rVert\leq\lVert T\rVert\lVert x\rVert.
$$

Its norm is at most $\lVert T\rVert$, while restriction to $D$ gives the reverse inequality, so

$$
\boxed{\lVert\widetilde T\rVert=\lVert T\rVert.}
$$

Any two continuous extensions agree on the dense set $D$ and hence everywhere, proving uniqueness. This is the [extension of a bounded linear operator from a dense subspace](../../../topological-vector-space.md#extension-of-a-bounded-linear-operator-from-a-dense-subspace).

Completeness of $X$ was never used, so the result remains true when $X$ is incomplete. Completeness of $Y$ is essential. For example, with $D=c_{00}\subset X=\ell^2$, let the codomain be the incomplete normed space $Y=c_{00}$ with the $\ell^2$ norm and let $T$ be the identity. An extension would have to send every limit in $\ell^2$ to itself and therefore could not take values in $c_{00}$. This is the [failure of dense-subspace extension into an incomplete codomain](../../../topological-vector-space.md#failure-of-dense-subspace-extension-into-an-incomplete-codomain).

<h3 id="21f/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [21F](#21f)

<h4 id="21f/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#21f/c)

For every finite scalar sequence $a=(a_n)$ with $|a_n|\leq1$, define a bounded linear functional on the [continuous dual space](../../../continuous-dual-space.md) $X^*$ by

$$
T_a(f)=\sum_na_nf(x_n).
$$

For each fixed $f\in X^*$, the hypothesis gives

$$
\sup_a|T_a(f)|
=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|f(x_n)|<\infty,
$$

where the supremum is over all finite choices and the phases $a_n$ may be chosen to align the complex numbers $f(x_n)$.

The dual $X^*$ is a [Banach space](../../../banach-space.md) even when $X$ is incomplete. The [Uniform boundedness principle](../../../banach-space.md#uniform-boundedness-principle) therefore gives a constant $C$ such that

$$
\sup_a\lVert T_a\rVert\leq C.
$$

For each $N$, choose $a_n$ for $n\leq N$ so that $a_nf(x_n)=|f(x_n)|$. Then

$$
\sum_{n=1}^N|f(x_n)|
=|T_a(f)|
\leq C\lVert f\rVert.
$$

Letting $N\to\infty$ proves

$$
\boxed{\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|f(x_n)|\leq C\lVert f\rVert
\quad\text{for every }f\in X^*.}
$$

This is the [uniform bound from pointwise absolute summability of dual evaluations](../../../banach-space.md#uniform-bound-from-pointwise-absolute-summability-of-dual-evaluations).

## 22F

<h3 id="22f/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [22F](#22f)

<h4 id="22f/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#22f/a)

For $1\leq p<\infty$, the [Lebesgue space](../../../measure-theory.md#lp-space) $L^p(X)$ consists of equivalence classes modulo equality almost everywhere of measurable functions satisfying

$$
\lVert f\rVert_p
=\left(\int_X|f|^p\,d\mu\right)^{1/p}<\infty.
$$

The space $L^\infty(X)$ consists of essentially bounded measurable functions modulo equality almost everywhere, with norm

$$
\lVert f\rVert_\infty=\operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{x\in X}|f(x)|.
$$

Let $1\leq p<q<\infty$ and put $r=q/p>1$. By [Hölder's inequality](../../../functional-analysis.md#holder-inequality),

$$
\int_X|f|^p
\leq
\left(\int_X|f|^{pr}\right)^{1/r}
\mu(X)^{1-1/r}.
$$

Taking $p$th roots gives

$$
\boxed{\lVert f\rVert_p
\leq\mu(X)^{1/p-1/q}\lVert f\rVert_q.}
$$

For $q=\infty$, directly integrating $|f|^p\leq\lVert f\rVert_\infty^p$ gives the same endpoint estimate. Hence $L^q(X)\subseteq L^p(X)$ when $\mu(X)<\infty$, as in [Lp inclusion on a finite measure space](../../../measure-theory.md#lp-inclusion-on-a-finite-measure-space).

<h3 id="22f/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [22F](#22f)

<h4 id="22f/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#22f/b)

Let $q$ be the [Holder conjugate exponent](../../../functional-analysis.md#conjugate-exponents) of $p$. On $\mathbb R^n$ with Lebesgue measure, the [duality of Lp spaces](../../../continuous-dual-space.md#duality-of-lp-spaces) is

$$
\boxed{(L^p)^*\cong L^q\qquad(1\leq p<\infty),}
$$

under the pairing $\Lambda_g(f)=\int fg$. Thus $(L^1)^*=L^\infty$, while for $1<p<\infty$ the dual exponent satisfies $1/p+1/q=1$.

A Banach space is [reflexive](../../../functional-analysis.md#reflexive-banach-space) when its canonical map into its bidual is surjective. The spaces $L^p(\mathbb R^n)$ are reflexive exactly for

$$
\boxed{1<p<\infty.}
$$

For these exponents, applying $L^p$ duality twice identifies the bidual with $L^p$. The endpoint spaces $L^1$ and $L^\infty$ are not reflexive; for example, disjoint unit cubes give closed copies of $\ell^1$ and $\ell^\infty$, respectively.

Integration gives an isometric embedding $L^1\hookrightarrow(L^\infty)^*$, but it is not onto. On the subspace

$$
V=\mathbb C1\oplus C_0(\mathbb R^n)\subset L^\infty,
$$

define $\Lambda(c+h)=c$, the limit at infinity. Since $|\Lambda(v)|\leq\lVert v\rVert_\infty$, the [Hahn-Banach theorem](../../../functional-analysis.md#hahn-banach-theorem) extends $\Lambda$ to all of $L^\infty$. If this extension were integration against some $g\in L^1$, its vanishing on every compactly supported bounded function would force $g=0$ almost everywhere, but $\Lambda(1)=1$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{(L^\infty)^*\ne L^1.}
$$

This is the [Dual of L infinity is larger than L1](../../../continuous-dual-space.md#dual-of-l-infinity-is-larger-than-l1).

<h3 id="22f/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [22F](#22f)

<h4 id="22f/c/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#22f/c)

<h5 id="22f/c/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#22f/c/i)

First suppose $1\leq p<\infty$. Choose a subsequence so that

$$
\lVert f_{n_k}-f\rVert_p^p\leq2^{-k}.
$$

By [Tonelli theorem](../../../measure-theory.md#tonelli-theorem),

$$
\int_X\sum_{k=1}^\infty|f_{n_k}-f|^p\,d\mu
=\sum_{k=1}^\infty\lVert f_{n_k}-f\rVert_p^p
<\infty.
$$

Hence the sum inside the integral is finite almost everywhere, which forces $|f_{n_k}(x)-f(x)|\to0$ almost everywhere.

If $p=\infty$, choose representatives and remove the countable union of the null sets on which

$$
|f_n-f|>\lVert f_n-f\rVert_\infty.
$$

Outside that null set, norm convergence bounds the pointwise difference and the entire sequence converges to $f$. Thus in every case **an almost-everywhere convergent subsequence exists**, as stated in [Almost-everywhere convergent subsequence from Lp convergence](../../../measure-theory.md#almost-everywhere-convergent-subsequence-from-lp-convergence).

<h4 id="22f/c/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#22f/c)

<h5 id="22f/c/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#22f/c/ii)

Regard the set-theoretic inclusion

$$
I:L^q(X)\longrightarrow L^p(X)
$$

as a linear map between Banach spaces. Its graph is closed. Indeed, if $f_n\to f$ in $L^q$ and $f_n\to g$ in $L^p$, part (i) gives an almost-everywhere convergent subsequence for the first convergence and then a further subsequence converging almost everywhere for the second; hence $f=g$ almost everywhere. The [closed graph theorem](../../../functional-analysis.md#closed-graph-theorem) therefore gives

$$
\lVert f\rVert_p\leq C\lVert f\rVert_q.
$$

Let $E_R=X\cap B(0,R)$. It has finite measure, and $\mu(E_R)\uparrow\mu(X)$. Applying the bound to $\mathbf1_{E_R}$ gives, for finite $q$,

$$
\mu(E_R)^{1/p}
\leq C\mu(E_R)^{1/q},
$$

so $\mu(E_R)^{1/p-1/q}\leq C$. For $q=\infty$, it gives $\mu(E_R)^{1/p}\leq C$. In either case the measures $\mu(E_R)$ are uniformly bounded. Continuity from below now yields

$$
\boxed{\mu(X)<\infty.}
$$

This proves the converse summarized by [Lq inclusion implies finite measure on a Euclidean Borel subset](../../../measure-theory.md#lq-inclusion-implies-finite-measure-on-a-euclidean-borel-subset).

<h3 id="23f/solution">Solution</h3>

For a nonconstant [holomorphic map](../../../complex-analysis.md#holomorphic-map) $f:X\to Y$ between compact connected [Riemann surfaces](../../../complex-analysis.md#riemann-surfaces), its [degree](../../../complex-analysis.md#degree-of-a-holomorphic-map) is

$$
\deg f=\sum_{x\in f^{-1}(y)}e_x,
$$

where $e_x$ is the [ramification index of a holomorphic map](../../../complex-analysis.md#ramification-index-of-a-holomorphic-map) at $x$. The [valency theorem](../../../complex-analysis.md#valency-theorem) makes this sum independent of $y\in Y$. The [Riemann-Hurwitz formula](../../../complex-analysis.md#riemann-hurwitz-formula) is

$$
\boxed{2g_X-2=(\deg f)(2g_Y-2)+\sum_{x\in X}(e_x-1).}
$$

Write $E=\mathbb C/\Lambda$. The map $\psi(z+\Lambda)=-z+\Lambda$ is well-defined and [biholomorphic](../../../complex-analysis.md#biholomorphism), with $\psi^2=\operatorname{id}$. Its fixed points satisfy

$$
z+\Lambda=-z+\Lambda
\iff2z\in\Lambda.
$$

They are therefore indexed by $\tfrac12\Lambda/\Lambda\cong(\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z)^2$, so there are four, as in the [negation map on a one-dimensional complex torus](../../../complex-geometry.md#negation-map-on-a-one-dimensional-complex-torus).

For each $x\in E'$ choose a small open disc $U_x$ for which $U_x\cap\psi(U_x)=\varnothing$. The projection restricts to a homeomorphism

$$
p|_{U_x}:U_x\longrightarrow p(U_x).
$$

Use $(p|_{U_x})^{-1}$ followed by a holomorphic coordinate on $U_x$ as a chart on $p(U_x)$. Choosing $\psi(U_x)$ instead changes this coordinate by the holomorphic map induced by $\psi$, so the transition functions are holomorphic. These charts make $S'$ a [Riemann surface](../../../complex-analysis.md#riemann-surfaces) and $p:E'\to S'$ a holomorphic double [covering map](../../../algebraic-topology.md#covering-space).

At each omitted fixed point, a centred local coordinate changes by $z\mapsto-z$, so the invariant quotient coordinate is $w=z^2$. Hence the extension has degree two and exactly four ramification points, each with $e_x=2$. Since the [complex torus](../../../complex-geometry.md#complex-torus) $E$ has genus one, [Riemann--Hurwitz](../../../complex-analysis.md#riemann-hurwitz-formula) gives

$$
0=2(2g_S-2)+4.
$$

Thus **$\boxed{g_S=0}$**, in agreement with the general [quotient of a one-dimensional complex torus by negation](../../../complex-geometry.md#quotient-of-a-one-dimensional-complex-torus-by-negation).

<h3 id="24i/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [24I](#24i)

<h4 id="24i/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#24i/b)

Choose a point $Q\in E$. Since $g(E)=1$, a nonzero regular differential has an effective divisor of degree $2g-2=0$, so $K_E\sim0$. The [Riemann-Roch theorem](../../../algebraic-geometry.md#riemann-roch-theorem) therefore gives

$$
\ell(nQ)=n\qquad(n\geq1).
$$

Choose

$$
x\in L(2Q)\setminus L(Q),
\qquad
y\in L(3Q)\setminus L(2Q).
$$

The [rational functions](../../../isolated-singularity.md#rational-function) $x$ and $y$ have their only poles at $Q$, of orders two and three respectively.

The seven functions

$$
1,x,y,x^2,xy,x^3,y^2
$$

belong to the six-dimensional [Riemann-Roch space](../../../algebraic-geometry.md#riemann-roch-space) $L(6Q)$, so they satisfy a nontrivial linear relation. Comparing pole orders shows that the coefficients of both $x^3$ and $y^2$ are nonzero. After rescaling, the relation has the [Weierstrass form](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#weierstrass-equation-of-an-elliptic-curve)

$$
y^2+a_1xy+a_3y=x^3+a_2x^2+a_4x+a_6.
$$

The divisor $3Q$ is [very ample](../../../cartier-divisor.md#very-ample-divisor), so its complete linear system embeds $E$ in $\mathbb P^2$ and the displayed relation cuts out its image; this is the [Weierstrass construction from Riemann-Roch in genus one](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#weierstrass-construction-from-riemann-roch-in-genus-one).

Because $\operatorname{char}k\ne2$, completing the square removes the terms linear in $y$, and because $\operatorname{char}k\ne3$, translating $x$ removes the quadratic term. Thus the equation becomes

$$
y^2=x^3+Ax+B.
$$

Since $k$ is [algebraically closed](../../../algebra.md#algebraically-closed-field), the cubic factors as

$$
x^3+Ax+B=(x-\lambda_1)(x-\lambda_2)(x-\lambda_3).
$$

Smoothness of $E$ says that the [elliptic-curve discriminant](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#elliptic-curve-discriminant) is nonzero, equivalently the three roots are distinct. Hence

$$
\boxed{E\cong\{y^2=(x-\lambda_1)(x-\lambda_2)(x-\lambda_3)\}\subset\mathbb P^2.}
$$

↑ **Parent:** [24I](#24i)

<h4 id="24i/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#24i/c)

Define

$$
\alpha:E\longrightarrow\operatorname{Cl}^0(E),
\qquad
\alpha(P)=[P-Q].
$$

If $D$ is a [divisor class](../../../algebraic-geometry.md#divisor-class) of degree zero, [Riemann--Roch](../../../algebraic-geometry.md#riemann-roch-theorem) gives $\ell(D+Q)=1$, because $K_E\sim0$ and the divisor $-D-Q$ has negative degree. A nonzero section therefore yields

$$
D+Q\sim P
$$

for some point $P$, proving that $\alpha$ is surjective. If $\alpha(P)=\alpha(P')$, then $P-P'$ is a [principal divisor](../../../algebraic-geometry.md#principal-divisor-on-an-algebraic-curve). For $P\ne P'$ its defining function would have one simple pole and give a degree-one map $E\to\mathbb P^1$, impossible for a genus-one curve. Thus $P=P'$. The map and its inverse are regular, so this proves the [Abel-Jacobi map of a genus-one curve](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#abel-jacobi-map-of-a-genus-one-curve) is an isomorphism.

Transport the addition of the [divisor class group](../../../algebraic-geometry.md#divisor-class-group) through $\alpha$: define $P\boxplus R$ by

$$
[P\boxplus R-Q]=[P-Q]+[R-Q].
$$

Then $Q$ is the identity, and this is the [chord-and-tangent group law](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#chord-and-tangent-group-law) on the plane cubic.

For the displayed equation, inversion is $(x,y)\mapsto(x,-y)$. Thus $P\boxplus P=Q$ exactly when $P=-P$. Besides the point at infinity $Q$, this requires $y=0$. Therefore the complete set of two-torsion points is

$$
\boxed{Q,\quad(\lambda_1,0),\quad(\lambda_2,0),\quad(\lambda_3,0).}
$$

For a local parametrization $\phi(u,v)$, write the [first fundamental form](../../../differential-geometry.md#first-fundamental-form) and [second fundamental form](../../../second-fundamental-form.md) as

$$
I=E\,du^2+2F\,du\,dv+G\,dv^2,
\qquad
II=e\,du^2+2f\,du\,dv+g\,dv^2.
$$

The [Gaussian curvature](../../../second-fundamental-form.md#gaussian-curvature) is

$$
\boxed{K=\frac{eg-f^2}{EG-F^2}.}
$$

To prove the [Theorema Egregium](../../../differential-geometry.md#theorema-egregium), set $g_{ij}=\langle\phi_i,\phi_j\rangle$ and decompose

$$
\phi_{ij}=\Gamma^k_{ij}\phi_k+h_{ij}N.
$$

Differentiating $g_{ij}$ and solving for the tangential coefficients gives

$$
\Gamma_{ijk}
=\frac12\left(\partial_i g_{jk}+\partial_jg_{ik}-\partial_kg_{ij}\right),
$$

so every [Christoffel symbol](../../../riemannian-geometry.md#christoffel-symbol) depends only on $E,F,G$ and their first derivatives. Equality of mixed third derivatives yields the [Gauss equation](../../../second-fundamental-form.md#gauss-equation)

$$
R_{1212}=h_{11}h_{22}-h_{12}^2=eg-f^2.
$$

The [Riemann curvature tensor](../../../general-relativity.md#riemann-curvature-tensor) is formed from first derivatives and quadratic products of the Christoffel symbols, and hence from the first and second derivatives of $E,F,G$. Consequently

$$
\boxed{K=\frac{R_{1212}}{\det(g_{ij})}
=\frac{R_{1212}}{EG-F^2}}
$$

depends only on the first fundamental form.

**No.** The missing point is not assumed to complete $S$ as a smooth surface, so the hypotheses of the global [Gauss-Bonnet theorem](../../../differential-geometry.md#gauss-bonnet-theorem) do not apply to $S\cup\{p\}$.

For a concrete counterexample, rotate a smooth simple arc $(r(s),z(s))$, parametrized by arc length for $0\leq s<L$, about the $z$-axis. Arrange that it starts smoothly on the axis,

$$
r(0)=0,\qquad r'(0)=1,
$$

stays in $r>0$ for $0<s<L$, and converges to another point of the axis as $s\uparrow L$, with

$$
r'(s)\longrightarrow-a,\qquad0\leq a<1.
$$

The endpoint is omitted. The resulting [surface of revolution](../../../differential-geometry.md#surface-of-revolution) is diffeomorphic to $\mathbb S^2$ minus one point, and adjoining its limiting endpoint makes the set compact.

For an arc-length surface of revolution,

$$
K=-\frac{r''}{r},
\qquad
dA=r\,ds\,d\theta.
$$

Therefore the [total Gaussian curvature of a punctured surface with a singular compactification point](../../../differential-geometry.md#total-gaussian-curvature-of-a-punctured-surface-with-a-singular-compactification-point) is

$$
\int_SK\,dA
=-2\pi\int_0^Lr''(s)\,ds
=2\pi(1+a),
$$

which can take values different from $4\pi$. Compactness of the one-point closure alone therefore does not determine the integral.

<h3 id="27j/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [27J](#27j)

Immediately after departure $n$, either $Q_n>0$ and one of those customers starts the next service, or $Q_n=0$ and the next arrival both initiates service and is not counted among those waiting after it. During that service $A_n$ further customers arrive. Thus the [embedded departure chain of an M-G-1 queue](../../../queueing-theory.md#embedded-departure-chain-of-an-m-g-1-queue) obeys

$$
\boxed{Q_{n+1}=A_n+Q_n-h(Q_n),\qquad h(x)=\min\{1,x\}.}
$$

Conditional on the next service time $S_{n+1}=s$, the [Poisson process](../../../probability-theory.md#poisson-process) property gives

$$
A_n\mid S_{n+1}=s\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda s).
$$

The [law of total expectation](../../../measure-theory.md#law-of-total-expectation) and [law of total variance](../../../probability-theory.md#law-of-total-variance) therefore give

$$
\boxed{\mathbb EA_n=\lambda\mathbb ES=\rho,}
$$

and

$$
\boxed{\mathbb EA_n^2
=\mathbb E[\lambda S+(\lambda S)^2]
=\rho+\lambda^2\mathbb ES^2.}
$$

In equilibrium let $Q$ have the common distribution of $Q_n,Q_{n+1}$ and put $H=\mathbf1_{\{Q>0\}}=h(Q)$. The fresh pair $(A_n,S_{n+1})$ is independent of $Q_n$. Taking expectations in the recursion gives

$$
0=\rho-\mathbb EH,
\qquad\text{so}\qquad
\mathbb P(Q>0)=\rho.
$$

Because $QH=Q$ and $H^2=H$, stationarity of the second moment gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
0
&=\mathbb E[(Q-H+A)^2-Q^2]\\
&=-2\mathbb EQ+\rho
+2\rho(\mathbb EQ-\rho)
+\rho+\lambda^2\mathbb ES^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

Solving,

$$
\boxed{\mathbb EQ
=\rho+\frac{\lambda^2\mathbb ES^2}{2(1-\rho)}.}
$$

In stationarity, level crossing together with [Poisson arrivals see time averages](../../../queueing-theory.md#poisson-arrivals-see-time-averages) identifies this as the mean number $L$ in the shop. The [Little law](../../../queueing-theory.md#little-s-law) gives $L=\lambda(W_q+\mathbb ES)$, where $W_q$ is waiting time before service. Since $\rho=\lambda\mathbb ES$,

$$
\boxed{W_q=\frac{\lambda\mathbb ES^2}{2(1-\rho)}.}
$$

This is the [Pollaczek-Khinchine mean formula](../../../queueing-theory.md#pollaczek-khinchine-mean-formula).

The finite-horizon discrete-time [fundamental theorem of asset pricing](../../../mathematical-finance.md#fundamental-theorem-of-asset-pricing) states that the market has no [arbitrage](../../../mathematical-finance.md#arbitrage) if and only if there exists a probability measure $\mathbb Q$ equivalent to $\mathbb P$ under which every integrable discounted asset price $X^i$ is a [martingale](../../../martingale.md). <h3 id="29k/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [29K](#29k)

<h4 id="29k/c/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#29k/c)

<h5 id="29k/c/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#29k/c/i)

Condition (i) says that each discounted risky price $X^i$ is an integrable $\mathbb Q$-martingale. For a bounded predictable risky holding process $\theta$, self-financing gives the [discounted wealth equation in discrete time](../../../mathematical-finance.md#discounted-wealth-equation-in-discrete-time)

$$
V_t=V_0+\sum_{s=1}^t
\theta_s\mathbin{\cdot}(X_s-X_{s-1}).
$$

Each increment is integrable and

$$
\mathbb E_{\mathbb Q}[
\theta_s\mathbin{\cdot}(X_s-X_{s-1})
\mid\mathcal F_{s-1}]
=\theta_s\mathbin{\cdot}
\mathbb E_{\mathbb Q}[X_s-X_{s-1}\mid\mathcal F_{s-1}]
=0.
$$

Hence $V$ is a $\mathbb Q$-martingale, proving **(i) $\Rightarrow$ (ii)**.

<h4 id="29k/c/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#29k/c)

<h5 id="29k/c/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#29k/c/ii)

If $V$ is a $\mathbb Q$-martingale, then

$$
\mathbb E_{\mathbb Q}[V_T]
=\mathbb E_{\mathbb Q}[V_0]
=V_0,
$$

because $\mathcal F_0$ is $\mathbb P$-trivial and equivalence makes it $\mathbb Q$-trivial as well. Thus **(ii) $\Rightarrow$ (iii)**.

<h4 id="29k/c/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#29k/c)

<h5 id="29k/c/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#29k/c/iii)

Assume (iii). Fix $t\in\{1,\ldots,T\}$, a coordinate $i$, and an event $A\in\mathcal F_{t-1}$. Choose the bounded predictable risky strategy

$$
\theta_s=\mathbf1_Ae_i\mathbf1_{\{s=t\}},
$$

and choose the numéraire holding to make the strategy self-financing with $V_0=0$. Its terminal discounted value is

$$
V_T=\mathbf1_A(X_t^i-X_{t-1}^i).
$$

Condition (iii) yields

$$
\mathbb E_{\mathbb Q}[
\mathbf1_A(X_t^i-X_{t-1}^i)]=0
$$

for every $A\in\mathcal F_{t-1}$. The defining property of [conditional expectation](../../../measure-theory.md#conditional-expectation) implies

$$
\mathbb E_{\mathbb Q}[X_t^i\mid\mathcal F_{t-1}]
=X_{t-1}^i.
$$

Every coordinate is therefore a $\mathbb Q$-martingale, so **(iii) $\Rightarrow$ (i)**. This proves the [martingale characterization by bounded self-financing strategies](../../../mathematical-finance.md#martingale-characterization-by-bounded-self-financing-strategies).

<h3 id="29k/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [29K](#29k)

<h4 id="29k/d/solution">Solution</h4>

The set $\{\dot V=0\}$ is $z=0$. A trajectory remaining there must also have $\dot z=y=0$, so its largest invariant subset is the origin. In the [phase plane](../../../dynamical-systems.md#phase-plane), $\dot x=0$ on $y=0$ and $\dot y=0$ on $x+y=0$; every nonstationary trajectory crosses the nested level curves of $V$ inward and tends to the origin.

$$

$$

$$
J_*=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&1\\
\mu_1-3x_*^2&\mu_2-3x_*^2
\end{pmatrix},
\quad
\det J_*=3x_*^2-\mu_1,
\quad

$$

A [stationary bifurcation](../../../dynamical-systems.md#stationary-bifurcation) occurs when a real [eigenvalue](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) passes through zero, hence on

$$
\boxed{\mu_1=0.}
$$

A [Hopf bifurcation](../../../dynamical-systems.md#hopf-bifurcation) requires zero trace and positive determinant. At the origin this gives

$$
\boxed{\mu_2=0,\quad\mu_1<0,}
$$

and on either nonzero branch it gives

$$
\boxed{\mu_2=3\mu_1,\quad\mu_1>0.}
$$

Thus the Hopf locus consists of the negative $\mu_1$-axis and the ray $\mu_2=3\mu_1$ in the first quadrant; the stationary locus is the $\mu_2$-axis.

Now set $\mu_2=-1$, write $\mu=\mu_1$, and append $\dot\mu=0$. At $(x,y,\mu)=(0,0,0)$ the [extended centre manifold for a parameter](../../../dynamical-systems.md#extended-centre-manifold-for-a-parameter) is tangent to the $(x,\mu)$-plane, so put $y=h(x,\mu)$. Its [centre-manifold invariance equation](../../../dynamical-systems.md#centre-manifold-invariance-equation) is

$$
h_xh=\mu x-h-(x+h)^3.
$$

Under the ordering $\mu=O(x^2)$, solving through cubic order gives

$$
\boxed{y=h(x,\mu)=\mu x-x^3+O(x^5),
\qquad
\dot x=\mu x-x^3+O(x^5).}
$$

This is the [pitchfork bifurcation normal form](../../../dynamical-systems.md#pitchfork-bifurcation-normal-form): $x=0$ is stable for $\mu<0$ and unstable for $\mu>0$, while the two stable branches $x=\pm\sqrt\mu$ emerge for $\mu>0$. The bifurcation is therefore a **supercritical pitchfork bifurcation**.

The asymptotic [scattering wavefunction](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#scattering-wavefunction) is

$$
\boxed{\psi(\mathbf r)\sim e^{ikz}+f(\theta)\frac{e^{ikr}}r.}
$$

In each [partial wave](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#partial-wave), the [Partial-wave S-matrix](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#partial-wave-s-matrix) is the ratio of its outgoing coefficient to its incoming coefficient. With the conventions in the question,

$$
S_l=1+2if_l.
$$

For [elastic scattering](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#elastic-scattering) by a real central potential, [unitarity](../../../vector-space.md#unitary-operator) gives $|S_l|=1$, so $S_l=e^{2i\delta_l}$ for a real [scattering phase shift](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#scattering-phase-shift) $\delta_l$. Hence

$$
\boxed{f_l=\frac{S_l-1}{2i}=e^{i\delta_l}\sin\delta_l.}
$$

Using the [Orthogonality of Legendre polynomials](../../../differential-equation.md#orthogonality-of-legendre-polynomials) in $\sigma_T=\int|f(\theta)|^2d\Omega$ yields the [partial-wave total scattering cross-section](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#partial-wave-total-scattering-cross-section)

$$
\boxed{\sigma_T=\frac{4\pi}{k^2}\sum_{l=0}^\infty(2l+1)\sin^2\delta_l.}
$$

For the [S wave](../../../wave-equation.md#s-wave), write the reduced radial wavefunction as

$$
u_{<}(r)=A\sinh(\kappa r),
\qquad
u_{>}(r)=B\sin(kr+\delta_0),
\qquad
\kappa=\sqrt{\gamma^2-k^2},
$$

where regularity at $r=0$ selected the hyperbolic sine. Continuity of $u$ and $u'$ at $r=a$, equivalently [logarithmic-derivative matching](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#logarithmic-derivative-matching), gives

$$
k\cot(ka+\delta_0)=\kappa\coth(\kappa a),
$$

or

$$
\boxed{\frac{\tan(ka+\delta_0)}{ka}
=\frac{\tanh(\sqrt{\gamma^2-k^2}\,a)}{\sqrt{\gamma^2-k^2}\,a}.}
$$

For $ka\ll1$, let the [scattering length](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#scattering-length-from-a-partial-wave-s-matrix) be $a_s=-\lim_{k\to0}\tan\delta_0/k$. Expanding the matching relation gives

$$
a-a_s=\frac{\tanh(\gamma a)}\gamma,
\qquad
a_s=a-\frac{\tanh(\gamma a)}\gamma.
$$

Only the S wave contributes at leading order, so

$$
\boxed{\sigma_T^{(0)}\sim4\pi
\left(a-\frac{\tanh(\gamma a)}\gamma\right)^2.}
$$

In the [hard-sphere limit](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#hard-sphere-limit) $\gamma a\to\infty$, penetration is suppressed, $a_s\to a$, and

$$
\boxed{\sigma_T^{(0)}\to4\pi a^2.}
$$

This is four times the geometric area $\pi a^2$, a wave effect associated with diffraction from an impenetrable sphere.

For each one-particle state $i$, a [boson](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#boson) occupation number can be any $n_i\geq0$. $$

The noninteracting modes factorize, so

$$
\mathcal Z=\prod_i\frac1{1-e^{-\beta(\epsilon_i-\mu)}},
\qquad
\boxed{\log\mathcal Z=-\sum_i\log\left(1-e^{-\beta(\epsilon_i-\mu)}\right).}

Convergence requires $\mu<\min_i\epsilon_i$. Since the lowest energy is zero, the limiting maximum [chemical potential](../../../thermodynamics.md#chemical-potential) is

\boxed{\mu_{\max}=0.}

\boxed{A^\mu(t,\mathbf x)=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}

\qquad
t_{\rm ret}=t-\frac{|\mathbf x-\mathbf x'|}{c}.}

For the point particle, $\tau_*(x)$ is its unique retarded [proper time](../../../special-relativity.md#proper-time), characterized by

$$
\boxed{R^\mu(\tau_*)R_\mu(\tau_*)=0,\qquad R^0(\tau_*)>0.}

$$

$$

Because the displacement from the retarded source point to the observation point is orthogonal to this velocity, $|R^\nu\dot y_\nu|=\gamma cD$, while $\dot y^\mu=\gamma(c,\mathbf v)$. The factors of $\gamma$ cancel, leaving

$$
\boxed{A^\mu(t,0,0,z)=\frac{\mu_0q}{4\pi D}

$$

## 39C

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="39c/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [39C](#39c)

For each incompressible [Stokes flow](../../../stokes-flow.md) without body force, $\partial_j\sigma_{ij}=0$ and

$$
\sigma_{ij}=-p\delta_{ij}+2\mu e_{ij},
\qquad
e_{ij}=\frac12(\partial_i u_j+\partial_j u_i).
$$

The [divergence theorem](../../../calculus.md#divergence-theorem) gives

$$
\int_Su_i^{(1)}\sigma_{ij}^{(2)}n_j\,dS
=\int_V\partial_j u_i^{(1)}\sigma_{ij}^{(2)}\,dV.
$$

Incompressibility removes the pressure term, antisymmetric velocity-gradient parts do not contract with the symmetric stress, and the remaining integrand is $2\mu e_{ij}^{(1)}e_{ij}^{(2)}$, symmetric under $1\leftrightarrow2$. This proves the [Lorentz reciprocal theorem for Stokes flow](../../../stokes-flow.md#lorentz-reciprocal-theorem-for-stokes-flow):

$$
\boxed{\int_Su_i^{(1)}\sigma_{ij}^{(2)}n_j\,dS
=\int_Su_i^{(2)}\sigma_{ij}^{(1)}n_j\,dS.}
$$

By [Linearity of Stokes flow](../../../stokes-flow.md#linearity-of-stokes-flow), the drag on a translating body is linear in its velocity, so $F_i=A_{ij}U_j$. Apply the reciprocal theorem to two translation solutions with velocities $\mathbf U^{(1)}$ and $\mathbf U^{(2)}$. Their surface velocities are constant and the far-field contribution vanishes, yielding

$$
U_i^{(1)}A_{ij}U_j^{(2)}=U_i^{(2)}A_{ij}U_j^{(1)}.
$$

Since the two vectors are arbitrary,

$$
\boxed{A_{ij}=A_{ji}.}
$$

Thus the [hydrodynamic resistance matrix](../../../stokes-flow.md#hydrodynamic-resistance-matrix) is symmetric and, for fixed fluid viscosity, depends only on body geometry.

A straight rod has fore-aft symmetry about its centre. Gravity acts through that centre, so the translational-rotational coupling and applied torque vanish. [Kinematic reversibility of Stokes flow](../../../stokes-flow.md#kinematic-reversibility-of-stokes-flow) and [Uniqueness of Stokes flow](../../../stokes-flow.md#uniqueness-of-stokes-flow) then rule out spontaneous rotation: the inclination $\theta$ remains constant.

Let the speed for perpendicular translation be $U_0$. The given factor of two makes the parallel speed $2U_0$ for the same force component. Resolving gravity parallel and perpendicular to the rod gives

$$
U_\parallel=2U_0\cos\theta,
\qquad
U_\perp=U_0\sin\theta.
$$

Resolving these velocities horizontally and vertically gives

$$
U_H=U_0\sin\theta\cos\theta,
\qquad
U_V=U_0(1+\cos^2\theta).
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{\tan\phi=\frac{U_H}{U_V}
=\frac{\sin\theta\cos\theta}{1+\cos^2\theta}.}
$$

Let a slowly varying wave have phase $S(\mathbf x,t)$, [wavevector](../../../continuum-mechanics.md#wavevector) $\mathbf k=\nabla S$, and [angular frequency](../../../classical-mechanics.md#angular-frequency) $\omega=-\partial_tS$. The local [dispersion relation](../../../wave-equation.md#dispersion-relation) is the Hamilton-Jacobi equation $\omega=\Omega(\mathbf k;\mathbf x,t)$. Equality of mixed derivatives, followed along a curve with velocity $\dot{\mathbf x}=\nabla_{\mathbf k}\Omega$, gives the [Hamiltonian ray-tracing equations](../../../continuum-mechanics.md#hamiltonian-ray-tracing-equations)

$$
\boxed{\frac{dx_i}{dt}=\frac{\partial\Omega}{\partial k_i},
\qquad
\frac{dk_i}{dt}=-\frac{\partial\Omega}{\partial x_i},
\qquad
\frac{d\omega}{dt}=\frac{\partial\Omega}{\partial t}.}
$$

Here $d/dt=\partial_t+\dot{\mathbf x}\mathbin{\cdot}\nabla$ is the total derivative evaluated along a ray, and $\dot{\mathbf x}$ is the [group velocity](../../../wave-equation.md#group-velocity).

For $\Omega=kc(z)$, $\omega$, $k_x$, and $k_y$ are conserved. If $\psi$ is the angle between the ray and the $z$-axis, then $k\sin\psi=(k_x^2+k_y^2)^{1/2}$ and $k=\omega/c$. Hence [Snell's law](../../../partial-differential-equation.md#snell-s-law) is

$$
\boxed{\frac{\sin\psi}{c}=\frac{(k_x^2+k_y^2)^{1/2}}\omega=\text{constant}.}
$$

For the ray launched with $\mathbf k=k(\cos\phi,0,\sin\phi)$ at $z=0$, this constant is $|\cos\phi|/c_0$. Therefore

$$
\sin\psi=|\cos\phi|(1+\beta^2z^2)\leq1,
$$

which proves

$$
\boxed{|z|\leq z_m=\frac1\beta
\left(\frac1{|\cos\phi|}-1\right)^{1/2}.}
$$

For $0<\phi<\pi/2$, write $a=\cos\phi$. Since the ray and wavevector are parallel,

$$
\left(\frac{dz}{dx}\right)^2
=\left[\frac1{a(1+\beta^2z^2)}\right]^2-1.
$$

Near the upper [turning point](../../../classical-mechanics.md#turning-point), put $z=z_m-\zeta$. Expansion gives

$$
\left(\frac{dz}{dx}\right)^2\simeq4a\beta^2z_m\zeta.
$$

If $x=x_m$ at the turn, integration yields the local ray path

$$
\boxed{z\simeq z_m-a\beta^2z_m(x-x_m)^2.}
$$

The ray is tangent to the line $z=z_m$, turns smoothly, crosses $z=0$, and repeats symmetrically between $-z_m$ and $z_m$. Increasing $\phi$ decreases $z_m$, while $\phi\downarrow0$ sends the turning level outward.

## 41E

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="41e/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [41E](#41e)

<h4 id="41e/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#41e/a)

Let $\delta_x^2u_m=u_{m-1}-2u_m+u_{m+1}$. For an exact smooth solution of the [diffusion equation](../../../diffusion-equation.md), the [second-order central difference](../../../finite-difference.md#second-order-central-difference) gives

$$
\delta_x^2u(x,t)=h^2u_{xx}(x,t)+\frac{h^4}{12}u_{xxxx}(x,t)+O(h^6).
$$

Since the [Courant number](../../../finite-difference.md#courant-number) $\mu=k/h^2$ is fixed, $h^2=O(k)$. Taylor expansion in time gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
u(t+k)-u(t)&=ku_t+\frac{k^2}{2}u_{tt}+O(k^3),\\
\frac32\mu\delta_x^2u(t)-\frac12\mu\delta_x^2u(t-k)
&=ku_{xx}+\frac{k^2}{2}u_{xxt}+O(kh^2)+O(k^3).
\end{aligned}
$$

Using $u_t=u_{xx}$ and $u_{tt}=u_{xxt}$ cancels the leading terms. Because $kh^2=O(k^2)$, the residual, and hence the [local truncation error](../../../numerical-analysis.md#local-truncation-error) in the convention of the question, is

$$
\boxed{O(k^2).}
$$

<h3 id="41e/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [41E](#41e)

<h4 id="41e/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#41e/b)

For a [discrete Fourier mode](../../../numerical-analysis.md#discrete-fourier-mode) $u_m^n=r^ne^{im\theta}$, put

$$
q=4\mu\sin^2\frac\theta2,
\qquad 0\leq q\leq4\mu.
$$

The recurrence becomes

$$
r^{n+1}=\left(1-\frac32q\right)r^n+\frac12qr^{n-1},
$$

so its [amplification polynomial of a multilevel finite difference scheme](../../../finite-difference.md#amplification-polynomial-of-a-multilevel-finite-difference-scheme) is

$$
p(r)=r^2-\left(1-\frac32q\right)r-\frac12q.
$$

Its roots are real. Moreover,

$$
p(1)=q,
\qquad
p(-1)=2(1-q),
\qquad
\frac{r_1+r_2}{2}=\frac12-\frac34q.
$$

The root-location criterion in the question therefore puts both roots in $[-1,1]$ exactly when $0\leq q\leq1$. [Fourier stability analysis](../../../finite-difference.md#fourier-stability-analysis) for every [wavenumber](../../../wave-equation.md#wavenumber) is equivalent to $4\mu\leq1$, so

$$
\boxed{\text{the scheme is stable if and only if }\mu\leq\frac14.}
$$

<h3 id="41e/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [41E](#41e)

<h4 id="41e/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#41e/c)

For the same Fourier mode, the [leapfrog finite-difference scheme for the diffusion equation](../../../finite-difference.md#leapfrog-finite-difference-scheme-for-the-diffusion-equation) gives

$$
r^{n+1}=r^{n-1}-2qr^n,
\qquad
p(r)=r^2+2qr-1.
$$

The two amplification roots are

$$
r_\pm=-q\pm\sqrt{1+q^2}.
$$

For every nonzero mode $q>0$, one has

$$
|r_-|=q+\sqrt{1+q^2}>1.
$$

Thus every $\mu>0$ admits a growing Fourier mode, and

$$
\boxed{\text{the leapfrog scheme is unstable for every }\mu>0.}
$$
