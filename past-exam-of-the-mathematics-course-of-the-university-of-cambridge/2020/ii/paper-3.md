## 1H

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="1h/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1H](#1h)

Put $e=(N-1)/2$. The odd integer $N$ is an [Euler pseudoprime](../../../number-theory.md#euler-pseudoprime) to the [coprime](../../../number-theory.md#coprime-integers) base $b$ when

$$
b^e\equiv\left(\frac bN\right)\pmod N,
$$

where $(b/N)$ is the [Jacobi symbol](../../../number-theory.md#jacobi-symbol).

Let $G=(\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z)^\times$ be the [unit group](../../../algebra.md#unit-group) of [residue classes](../../../number-theory.md#residue-class) modulo $N$, and partition it into the set $E$ of bases satisfying the displayed congruence and the set $W$ of [Euler witnesses](../../../number-theory.md#euler-witness) that do not. If $b_0\in W$, multiplication by $b_0$ maps $E$ [injectively](../../../algebra.md#injective-function) into $W$: indeed, if $b\in E$ and $bb_0$ also lay in $E$, then the [multiplicativity of the Jacobi symbol](../../../number-theory.md#multiplicativity-of-the-jacobi-symbol) would give

$$
b^eb_0^e\equiv\left(\frac bN\right)\left(\frac {b_0}N\right)\pmod N.
$$

Cancelling the invertible congruence for $b$ would say that $b_0\in E$, a contradiction. Hence $|W|\geq |E|$, so at least half of the [coprime](../../../number-theory.md#coprime-integers) bases are witnesses.

It remains to construct one witness for every odd [composite number](../../../number-theory.md#composite-number) $N$. If $N$ is not [squarefree](../../../number-theory.md#squarefree-integer), choose an odd [prime number](../../../number-theory.md#prime-number) $p$ with $p^2\mid N$. The [Chinese remainder theorem](../../../mathematics.md#chinese-remainder-theorem) gives a unit $b$ such that $b\equiv1+p\pmod {p^a}$ for the full power $p^a\mid N$ and $b\equiv1$ modulo every other prime-power factor. The [binomial theorem](../../../combinatorics.md#binomial-theorem) gives

$$
b^e\equiv(1+p)^e\equiv1+ep\pmod {p^2}.
$$

Since $p\nmid e$, this is neither $1$ nor $-1$ modulo $p^2$, whereas the [Jacobi symbol](../../../number-theory.md#jacobi-symbol) is always $1$ or $-1$. Thus $b$ is a witness.

If $N$ is squarefree and composite, choose distinct primes $p,q\mid N$. By the [Chinese remainder theorem](../../../mathematics.md#chinese-remainder-theorem), choose $b$ to be a [quadratic nonresidue](../../../number-theory.md#quadratic-nonresidue) modulo $p$ and to satisfy $b\equiv1$ modulo every other prime divisor of $N$. Then $(b/N)=-1$, while $b^e\equiv1\pmod q$, so the Euler congruence again fails. This proves the [existence of an Euler witness for every odd composite integer](../../../number-theory.md#existence-of-an-euler-witness-for-every-odd-composite-integer).

## 2H

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="2h/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2H](#2h)

The [polynomial Runge theorem](../../../complex-analysis.md#polynomial-runge-theorem) says that if $K\subset\mathbb C$ is [compact](../../../topology.md#compact-space), $\mathbb C\setminus K$ is [connected](../../../geometry-and-topology.md#connected-space), and $f$ is [holomorphic](../../../complex-analysis.md#holomorphic-function) on a neighbourhood of $K$, then for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is a [polynomial](../../../polynomial.md) $p$ such that $\sup_{z\in K}|p(z)-f(z)|<\varepsilon$.

For an explicit approximation on the left semicircle $K=\{z:|z|=1,\operatorname{Re}z\leq0\}$, define

$$
p_n(z)=-\frac14\sum_{j=0}^n\sum_{k=0}^{n^2}\binom{j+k}{k}\left(\frac z4\right)^k.
$$

The two [power-series expansions](../../../real-analysis.md#power-series)

$$
\frac1z=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-4)^j}{(z-4)^{j+1}},
\qquad
\frac1{(z-4)^{j+1}}=\frac1{(-4)^{j+1}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\binom{j+k}{k}\left(\frac z4\right)^k
$$

produce this formula. The first is a [geometric series](../../../real-analysis.md#geometric-series) whose ratio has modulus at most $4/\sqrt{17}<1$ on $K$. The second converges uniformly on the whole [unit circle](../../../complex-analysis.md#complex-unit-circle); the elementary bound $\binom{j+k}{k}\leq2^{j+k}$ shows that truncating it at $k=n^2$ gives a total error tending to zero even after summing over $0\leq j\leq n$. Consequently $p_n\to1/z$ [uniformly](../../../real-analysis.md#uniform-convergence) on $K$. This is an [explicit polynomial approximation of the reciprocal on the left semicircle](../../../complex-analysis.md#explicit-polynomial-approximation-of-the-reciprocal-on-the-left-semicircle).

**No such sequence exists on the punctured unit circle $S=\{z:|z|=1,z\ne1\}$.** If polynomials $p_n$ converged uniformly there, they would be [Uniformly Cauchy](../../../real-analysis.md#uniformly-cauchy-sequence). Continuity gives

$$
\sup_{|z|=1}|p_n(z)-p_m(z)|=\sup_{z\in S}|p_n(z)-p_m(z)|,
$$

so they would converge uniformly on the entire unit circle. The [uniform limit theorem](../../../real-analysis.md#uniform-limit-theorem) would force the value at the missing point to be $1$, and hence the limit would be $1/z$ on the full circle. But the [Cauchy integral theorem](../../../complex-analysis.md#cauchy-s-integral-theorem) gives $\oint p_n(z)\,dz=0$ for every $n$, while [uniform convergence and contour integration](../../../complex-analysis.md#uniform-convergence-and-contour-integration) would imply

$$
0=\lim_{n\to\infty}\oint p_n(z)\,dz=\oint\frac{dz}{z}=2\pi i,
$$

a contradiction. This is the [polynomial approximation obstruction on a punctured circle](../../../complex-analysis.md#polynomial-approximation-obstruction-on-a-punctured-circle).

## 3I

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="3i/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3I](#3i)

Use [Shamir's secret sharing](../../../coding-theory.md#shamir-s-secret-sharing) over the [finite field](../../../algebra.md#finite-field) $\mathbb F_p$. The Chair independently chooses $a_1,\ldots,a_{r-1}$ from the [uniform distribution on a finite set](../../../discrete-probability-distribution.md#discrete-uniform-distribution) $\mathbb F_p$ and forms the random [polynomial](../../../polynomial.md)

$$
f(X)=N+a_1X+\cdots+a_{r-1}X^{r-1}\in\mathbb F_p[X].
$$

Choose distinct nonzero $x_1,\ldots,x_s\in\mathbb F_p$, with $s$ equal to the number of discs to be issued, and inscribe $(x_i,f(x_i))$ on disc $i$.

Any $r$ discs give $r$ distinct values of a polynomial of degree at most $r-1$. Their [Vandermonde determinant](../../../galois-theory.md#vandermonde-determinant) is nonzero, so [polynomial interpolation](../../../numerical-analysis.md#polynomial-interpolation) recovers $f$ uniquely and hence recovers the secret as $N=f(0)$.

Conversely, fix any $r-1$ observed discs and any candidate secret $n\in\mathbb F_p$. Interpolating through those $r-1$ points and $(0,n)$ gives exactly one polynomial of degree at most $r-1$. Thus every candidate $n$ is compatible with the observed discs in exactly the same number of ways. Because the coefficients were chosen [uniformly](../../../discrete-probability-distribution.md#discrete-uniform-distribution), the [conditional distribution](../../../probability-theory.md#conditional-distribution) of the secret is unchanged after seeing any $r-1$ discs. This proves the [Perfect secrecy of Shamir's threshold scheme](../../../coding-theory.md#perfect-secrecy-of-shamir-s-threshold-scheme).

## 4F

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="4f/i">i</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4F](#4f)

<h4 id="4f/i/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#4f/i)

A [context-free grammar](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#context-free-grammar) is a quadruple $G=(V,\Sigma,P,S)$ consisting of a finite set $V$ of [nonterminal symbols](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#nonterminal-symbol), a disjoint finite [alphabet](../../../information-theory.md#alphabet) $\Sigma$ of [terminal symbols](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#terminal-symbol), a start symbol $S\in V$, and a finite set $P$ of [productions](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#production-rule) $A\to\alpha$ with $A\in V$ and $\alpha\in(V\cup\Sigma)^*$. A sentence is a terminal [word over an alphabet](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#string) $w\in\Sigma^*$ with $S\Rightarrow^*w$, and the generated [formal language](../../../computer-science.md#formal-language) is $\mathcal L(G)=\{w\in\Sigma^*:S\Rightarrow^*w\}$.

The first language is [context-free](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#context-free-language). For example, the productions

$$
S\to aaSbb\mid\varepsilon
$$

generate exactly $\{a^{2m}b^{2m}:m\geq0\}$: each recursive production adds two $a$s to the left and two $b$s to the right.

<h3 id="4f/ii">ii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4F](#4f)

<h4 id="4f/ii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#4f/ii)

The language $L=\{a^{m^2}b^{m^2}:m\geq0\}$ is not [context-free](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#context-free-language). Suppose it had [pumping length](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#pumping-length) $p$ in the [pumping lemma for context-free languages](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#pumping-lemma-for-context-free-languages), and apply the lemma to $w=a^{p^2}b^{p^2}$. Write $w=uvxyz$ with $|vxy|\leq p$, $|vy|>0$, and $uv^ixy^iz\in L$ for every $i\geq0$.

Let $\alpha$ and $\beta$ be the respective numbers of $a$s and $b$s in $vy$. If $\alpha\ne\beta$, pumping with $i=0$ or $i=2$ destroys equality of the two block lengths. Hence a valid decomposition would require $\alpha=\beta=d>0$. Pumping with $i=2$ would then produce $a^{p^2+d}b^{p^2+d}$, where $1\leq d\leq p$. But

$$
p^2<p^2+d<(p+1)^2,
$$

so $p^2+d$ is not a [square number](../../../number-theory.md#square-number). This contradiction proves the [square-count language is not context-free](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#square-count-language-is-not-context-free).

## 5J

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="5j/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [5J](#5j)

<h4 id="5j/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#5j/a)

Conditional independence makes the [log-likelihood](../../../statistical-modelling.md#log-likelihood)

$$
\ell(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^n\log f_{x_i}(Y_i;\theta).
$$

A [maximum-likelihood estimator](../../../statistical-modelling.md#maximum-likelihood-estimator) is any $\widehat\theta\in\operatorname*{argmax}_{\theta\in\Theta}\ell(\theta)$. If the model has $q$ fitted real parameters, its [Akaike information criterion](../../../statistical-modelling.md#akaike-information-criterion) is

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{AIC}=-2\ell(\widehat\theta)+2q.}
$$

<h3 id="5j/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [5J](#5j)

<h4 id="5j/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#5j/b)

For the [normal linear model](../../../statistical-modelling.md#normal-linear-model), the [Gaussian likelihood](../../../statistical-modelling.md#gaussian-likelihood) gives

$$
\ell(\beta,\sigma^2)
=-\frac n2\log(2\pi\sigma^2)
-\frac1{2\sigma^2}\|Y-X\beta\|^2.
$$

Maximization over $\beta$ gives the [ordinary least squares](../../../statistical-modelling.md#ordinary-least-squares) estimator $\widehat\beta$, and maximization over the variance gives

$$
\widehat\sigma^2=\frac1n\|Y-X\widehat\beta\|^2.
$$

At this value the quadratic term in $-2\ell$ is $n$. There are $p$ [regression coefficients](../../../linear-regression.md#regression-coefficient) and one variance parameter, so substitution into the [Akaike information criterion](../../../statistical-modelling.md#akaike-information-criterion) yields

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{AIC}=n\bigl(1+\log(2\pi\widehat\sigma^2)\bigr)+2(p+1).}
$$

<h3 id="5j/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [5J](#5j)

<h4 id="5j/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#5j/c)

The [hat matrix](../../../statistical-modelling.md#hat-matrix) $P$ is an [orthogonal projection](../../../hilbert-space.md#orthogonal-projection) of rank $p$, so $I-P$ is an orthogonal projection of rank $n-p$. Because $\varepsilon/\sigma$ is a standard [multivariate normal distribution](../../../probability-and-statistics.md#multivariate-normal-distribution) vector, [Cochran's theorem](../../../statistical-modelling.md#cochran-s-theorem) gives

$$
\frac{n\widehat\sigma^2}{\sigma^2}
=\frac{\|(I-P)\varepsilon\|^2}{\sigma^2}
\sim\chi^2_{n-p}.
$$

Thus $\widehat\sigma^2\overset d=(\sigma^2/n)\chi^2_{n-p}$. Substituting this into the preceding formula gives the [Distribution of the Akaike information criterion in a normal linear model](../../../statistical-modelling.md#distribution-of-the-akaike-information-criterion-in-a-normal-linear-model):

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{AIC}\overset d=
n\log(\chi^2_{n-p})
+n\bigl(\log(2\pi\sigma^2/n)+1\bigr)+2(p+1).}
$$

<h4 id="6b/i/solution">Solution</h4>

Adding the three equations of the [SIR model with waning immunity](../../../mathematical-biology.md#sir-model-with-waning-immunity) gives

$$
\frac{d}{dt}(S+I+R)=0.
$$

**Hence [conservation of population](../../../mathematical-biology.md#conservation-of-population) holds: $N=S+I+R$ is an [integral of motion](../../../classical-mechanics.md#integral-of-motion).**

<h3 id="6b/ii">ii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [6B](#6b)

<h4 id="6b/ii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#6b/ii)

At an [endemic equilibrium](../../../mathematical-biology.md#endemic-equilibrium), $I^*>0$, so $0=\dot I=I^*(\beta S^*-\gamma)$ forces

$$
S^*=\frac\gamma\beta.
$$

The equation $\dot R=0$ gives $R^*=\gamma I^*/\alpha$. The [conservation law](../../../physics.md#conservation-law) $S^*+I^*+R^*=N$ therefore yields

$$
I^*=\frac{\alpha(\beta N-\gamma)}{\beta(\alpha+\gamma)},
\qquad
R^*=\frac{\gamma(\beta N-\gamma)}{\beta(\alpha+\gamma)}.
$$

A positive endemic state exists exactly when $\beta N>\gamma$, equivalently when the [basic reproduction number](../../../mathematical-biology.md#basic-reproduction-number) $\mathcal R_0=\beta N/\gamma$ exceeds one.

<h3 id="6b/iii">iii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [6B](#6b)

<h4 id="6b/iii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#6b/iii)

Eliminate $R=N-S-I$ to obtain the [two-dimensional autonomous differential equation](../../../dynamical-systems.md#autonomous-system-mathematics)

$$
\dot S=\alpha(N-S-I)-\beta SI,
\qquad
\dot I=\beta SI-\gamma I.
$$

Its [Jacobian matrix](../../../calculus.md#jacobian-matrix) at the endemic equilibrium is

$$
J_*=
\begin{pmatrix}
-\alpha-\beta I^*&-\alpha-\gamma\\
\beta I^*&0
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Since $I^*>0$,

$$
\operatorname{tr}J_*=-(\alpha+\beta I^*)<0,
\qquad
\det J_*=\beta I^*(\alpha+\gamma)>0.
$$

The [trace-determinant stability criterion](../../../dynamical-systems.md#trace-determinant-stability-criterion) shows that both [eigenvalues](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) have negative real part. The [Endemic equilibrium of the SIR model with waning immunity](../../../mathematical-biology.md#endemic-equilibrium-of-the-sir-model-with-waning-immunity) is therefore locally [asymptotically stable](../../../dynamical-systems.md#asymptotic-stability).

<h4 id="7e/i/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#7e/i)

For each nonzero [period lattice](../../../complex-analysis.md#period-lattice) point $\omega$, the [binomial series](../../../real-analysis.md#binomial-series) gives

$$
\frac1{(z-\omega)^2}-\frac1{\omega^2}
=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(k+1)\frac{z^k}{\omega^{k+2}}.
$$

Pairing $\omega$ with $-\omega$ cancels every odd power. Hence the [Laurent series](../../../analysis.md#laurent-series) of the [Weierstrass elliptic function](../../../complex-analysis.md#weierstrass-elliptic-function) is

$$
\wp(z)=\frac1{z^2}
+3z^2\sum_{\omega\ne0}\omega^{-4}
+5z^4\sum_{\omega\ne0}\omega^{-6}+O(z^6)
=\frac1{z^2}+\frac{g_2}{20}z^2+\frac{g_3}{28}z^4+O(z^6),
$$

which is the stated [coefficient formula](../../../complex-analysis.md#laurent-coefficients-of-the-weierstrass-elliptic-function).

<h3 id="7e/ii">ii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [7E](#7e)

<h4 id="7e/ii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#7e/ii)

Set

$$
F(z)=\wp'(z)^2-4\wp(z)^3+g_2\wp(z)+g_3.
$$

This is an [elliptic function](../../../complex-analysis.md#elliptic-function). Write $a=g_2/20$ and $b=g_3/28$ in the expansion from part (i). At a lattice point, the coefficient of $z^{-2}$ in $F$ is $g_2-20a=0$, and its constant coefficient is $g_3-28b=0$; there are no other singular terms. Every apparent pole is therefore a [removable singularity](../../../isolated-singularity.md#removable-singularity), and $F$ extends to an entire elliptic function with value zero at the lattice points.

An entire elliptic function is bounded on a [fundamental parallelogram](../../../complex-analysis.md#fundamental-parallelogram-of-a-period-lattice) and hence everywhere. By [Liouville theorem](../../../complex-analysis.md#liouville-theorem) it is constant, and its value at zero is zero. Thus $F\equiv0$, proving the [Weierstrass elliptic differential equation](../../../complex-analysis.md#weierstrass-elliptic-differential-equation)

$$
\boxed{\wp'(z)^2=4\wp(z)^3-g_2\wp(z)-g_3.}
$$

## 8B

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="8b/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [8B](#8b)

The repulsive force is $\mathbf F=k\mathbf r/r^3=-\nabla(k/r)$, so the [Hamiltonian](../../../classical-mechanics.md#hamiltonian) is

$$
H=\frac{\mathbf p^2}{2m}+\frac kr.
$$

The [Hamilton's equations](../../../classical-mechanics.md#hamilton-s-equations) give $\dot{\mathbf r}=\mathbf p/m$ and $\dot{\mathbf p}=k\mathbf r/r^3$. Consequently

$$
\dot{\mathbf L}
=\dot{\mathbf r}\times\mathbf p+\mathbf r\times\dot{\mathbf p}=\mathbf0,
$$

so the [angular momentum](../../../classical-mechanics.md#angular-momentum) is conserved. Moreover,

$$
\dot{\mathbf p}\times\mathbf L
=k\left(\frac{\mathbf r(\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf p)}{r^3}-\frac{\mathbf p}{r}\right)
=-mk\frac{d\widehat{\mathbf r}}{dt}.
$$

It follows that the [Laplace-Runge-Lenz vector](../../../classical-mechanics.md#laplace-runge-lenz-vector) $\mathbf A=\mathbf p\times\mathbf L+mk\widehat{\mathbf r}$ is conserved. By the [Hamiltonian conservation law](../../../classical-mechanics.md#hamiltonian-conservation-law), these time derivatives are exactly $\{\mathbf L,H\}$ and $\{\mathbf A,H\}$, so both [Poisson brackets](../../../classical-mechanics.md#poisson-bracket) vanish.

The [integrals of motion](../../../classical-mechanics.md#integral-of-motion) are the energy $H$, the components of $\mathbf L$, and the components of $\mathbf A$, subject to

$$
\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf L=0,
\qquad
A^2=m^2k^2+2mHL^2.
$$

Taking the [dot product](../../../linear-algebra.md#dot-product) of $\mathbf A$ with $\widehat{\mathbf r}$ gives

$$
A\cos\theta=\frac{L^2}{r}+mk.
$$

Therefore the [polar equation of a repulsive inverse-square orbit](../../../classical-mechanics.md#polar-equation-of-a-repulsive-inverse-square-orbit) is

$$
r=\frac{L^2/(mk)}{(A/(mk))\cos\theta-1}
=\frac{\lambda}{e\cos\theta-1},
$$

where $\lambda=L^2/(mk)\geq0$ and $e=A/(mk)\geq0$. For a nonradial physical scattering orbit $H>0$, the identity above gives $e>1$, as expected for a [hyperbolic Kepler orbit](../../../classical-mechanics.md#hyperbolic-kepler-orbit).

## 11H

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="11h/i">i</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [11H](#11h)

<h4 id="11h/i/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#11h/i)

For an odd [prime number](../../../number-theory.md#prime-number) $p$, the [Legendre symbol](../../../number-theory.md#legendre-symbol) is

$$
\left(\frac xp\right)=
\begin{cases}
0,&p\mid x,\\
1,&x\not\equiv0\pmod p\text{ is a <quadratic residue>},\\
-1,&x\text{ is a <quadratic nonresidue>}.
\end{cases}
$$

If $p\nmid x$, [multiplicativity of the Legendre symbol](../../../number-theory.md#multiplicativity-of-the-legendre-symbol) gives

$$
\left(\frac xp\right)\left(\frac{x^{-1}}p\right)
=\left(\frac1p\right)=1.
$$

Both factors belong to $\{1,-1\}$, so they are equal. Thus $(x^{-1}/p)=(x/p)$.

<h3 id="11h/ii">ii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [11H](#11h)

<h4 id="11h/ii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#11h/ii)

[Gauss lemma](../../../riemannian-geometry.md#gauss-s-lemma-riemannian-geometry) states that if $p\nmid a$ and $m$ of the least positive residues of

$$
a,2a,\ldots,\frac{p-1}{2}a
$$

exceed $p/2$, then $(a/p)=(-1)^m$.

To prove it, replace each residue exceeding $p/2$ by its negative, obtaining numbers $r_1,\ldots,r_{(p-1)/2}$ in $\{1,\ldots,(p-1)/2\}$. No two of them are equal modulo $p$: such an equality would give $ia\equiv\pm ja\pmod p$, hence $i=j$ in the allowed range. They therefore permute $1,\ldots,(p-1)/2$, and multiplication gives

$$
a^{(p-1)/2}\left(\frac{p-1}{2}\right)!
\equiv(-1)^m\left(\frac{p-1}{2}\right)!\pmod p.
$$

Cancel the nonzero factorial and apply [Euler's criterion](../../../number-theory.md#euler-s-criterion) to obtain the lemma.

For $a=-1$, all $(p-1)/2$ least positive residues $p-1,p-2,\ldots,(p+1)/2$ exceed $p/2$. Hence the [first supplementary law for quadratic reciprocity](../../../number-theory.md#first-supplementary-law-for-quadratic-reciprocity) is

$$
\boxed{\left(\frac{-1}{p}\right)=(-1)^{(p-1)/2}.}
$$

<h3 id="11h/iii">iii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [11H](#11h)

<h4 id="11h/iii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#11h/iii)

Write $\chi(x)=(x/p)$. Choose a [quadratic nonresidue](../../../number-theory.md#quadratic-nonresidue) $a$. Multiplication by $a$ permutes $\mathbb F_p$, while [multiplicativity of the Legendre symbol](../../../number-theory.md#multiplicativity-of-the-legendre-symbol) gives $\chi(ax)=-\chi(x)$. Therefore

$$
\sum_{x\in\mathbb F_p}\chi(x)
=\sum_{x\in\mathbb F_p}\chi(ax)
=-\sum_{x\in\mathbb F_p}\chi(x),
$$

so the [complete Legendre-symbol sum](../../../number-theory.md#complete-legendre-symbol-sum) is zero.

Since $4x(x+1)=(2x+1)^2-1$ and $\chi(4)=1$, the bijection $x\mapsto2x+1$ reduces the next sum to $\sum_y\chi(y^2-1)$. Count pairs $(y,t)\in\mathbb F_p^2$ satisfying $t^2=y^2-1$. On one hand their number is

$$
\sum_y\bigl(1+\chi(y^2-1)\bigr).
$$

On the other hand $(y-t)(y+t)=1$, and every $u\in\mathbb F_p^\times$ gives exactly one pair by $y-t=u$, $y+t=u^{-1}$. There are therefore $p-1$ pairs, proving the [quadratic Legendre-symbol correlation](../../../number-theory.md#quadratic-legendre-symbol-correlation)

$$
\sum_{x\in\mathbb F_p}\chi(x(x+1))=-1.
$$

For $1\leq z\leq p-2$, the indicator that both $z$ and $z+1$ are quadratic residues is $(1+\chi(z))(1+\chi(z+1))/4$. Summing and using the two identities above gives the [number of consecutive nonzero quadratic residues modulo an odd prime](../../../number-theory.md#number-of-consecutive-nonzero-quadratic-residues-modulo-an-odd-prime)

$$
\frac14\left(p-2-\chi(-1)-1-1\right)
=\boxed{\frac{p-4-(-1)^{(p-1)/2}}4}.
$$

## 12F

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="12f/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [12F](#12f)

A [deterministic finite automaton](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#deterministic-finite-automaton) is a tuple $(Q,\Sigma,\delta,q_0,F)$ with finite state set $Q$, finite input alphabet $\Sigma$, transition function $\delta:Q\times\Sigma\to Q$, initial state $q_0$, and accepting-state set $F\subseteq Q$. It accepts a word when the [extended transition function of a deterministic finite automaton](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#extended-transition-function-of-a-deterministic-finite-automaton) carries $q_0$ to a state in $F$. A [regular language](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#regular-language) is a language accepted by some deterministic finite automaton.

The [pumping lemma for regular languages](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#pumping-lemma-for-regular-languages) says that if a regular language is accepted by an automaton with $N$ states, every accepted word $w$ of length at least $N$ has a decomposition $w=xyz$ with $|xy|\leq N$, $|y|>0$, and $xy^iz$ in the language for every $i\geq0$. Indeed, among the $N+1$ states visited before and after the first $N$ symbols, two are equal by the [pigeonhole principle](../../../algebra.md#pigeonhole-principle). The intervening nonempty word $y$ labels a loop, which may be traversed any number of times without changing the final accepting state.

In [base two](../../../number-theory.md#binary-numeral-system), the powers of two have representations $10^n$, so their language is the [regular expression](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#regular-expression) $10^*$ and is regular.

Suppose instead that their [base-ten representations](../../../number-theory.md#decimal-representation) formed a regular language. Apply the pumping lemma to a sufficiently long decimal power of two $xyz$, and put $d=|y|>0$. Pumping gives decimal integers $a_i$ represented by $xy^iz$, all powers of two. A direct place-value calculation shows that

$$
a_{i+1}-10^d a_i=C
$$

for a constant $C$ independent of $i$. The lengths, and hence the exponents in $a_i=2^{n_i}$, tend to infinity. Dividing by $a_i$ gives

$$
2^{n_{i+1}-n_i}-10^d=\frac C{2^{n_i}}.
$$

For large $i$, the right side has absolute value less than one while the left side is an integer, so it must vanish. This would make a power of two equal to $10^d=2^d5^d$, impossible for $d>0$ by [unique prime factorization](../../../number-theory.md#fundamental-theorem-of-arithmetic). Thus the decimal powers of two are not a [regular language](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#regular-language).

## 13B

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="13b/i">i</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [13B](#13b)

<h4 id="13b/i/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#13b/i)

Removing the death term with $u(x,t)=e^{\mu t}n(x,t)$ gives the [heat equation](../../../diffusion-equation.md#heat-equation) $u_t=Du_{xx}$ with initial data $N\delta(x)$. The [heat kernel](../../../diffusion-equation.md#heat-kernel) therefore yields

$$
n(x,t)=\frac{N}{\sqrt{4\pi Dt}}
\exp\left(-\frac{x^2}{4Dt}-\mu t\right).
$$

For each fixed $x$, the exponential mortality factor and diffusive prefactor show that $n(x,t)\to0$ as $t\to\infty$. Its spatial integral is $Ne^{-\mu t}$, the expected number of surviving larvae.

<h3 id="13b/ii">ii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [13B](#13b)

<h4 id="13b/ii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#13b/ii)

Because $e(x,0)=0$, the egg density is the accumulated deposition

$$
e(x,t)=\lambda\int_0^t n(x,s)\,ds.
$$

It is convenient to use the [Fourier transform](../../../analysis.md#fourier-transform). The larval equation gives

$$
\widehat n(k,t)=N e^{-(Dk^2+\mu)t},
\qquad
\widehat e(k,\infty)=\frac{N\lambda}{Dk^2+\mu}.
$$

Applying the stated inverse-transform integral with $\alpha=\sqrt{\mu/D}$ gives the [remnant density from diffusion with mortality and deposition](../../../mathematical-biology.md#remnant-density-from-diffusion-with-mortality-and-deposition)

$$
\boxed{e(x,\infty)=\frac{N\lambda}{2\sqrt{D\mu}}
\exp\left(-|x|\sqrt{\frac\mu D}\right).}
$$

<h3 id="13b/iii">iii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [13B](#13b)

<h4 id="13b/iii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#13b/iii)

Each larva undergoes [diffusion](../../../diffusion-equation.md) over its exponentially distributed lifetime of mean $1/\mu$ and deposits eggs at rate $\lambda$ wherever it is found. The characteristic [diffusion length](../../../mathematical-biology.md#diffusion-length) before death is $\sqrt{D/\mu}$, which explains the exponential spatial decay. Integrating the remnant over space gives

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e(x,\infty)\,dx=\frac{N\lambda}{\mu}.
$$

This is also immediate physically: each of the $N$ larvae lives for mean time $1/\mu$ and therefore lays an expected $\lambda/\mu$ eggs. The eggs remain as a stationary record because they neither die nor diffuse.

## 14D

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="14d/i">i</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [14D](#14d)

<h4 id="14d/i/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#14d/i)

Expand the [density contrast](../../../linear-cosmological-density-perturbation.md#density-contrast) in [Fourier modes](../../../fourier-analysis.md#fourier-mode), $\delta(\mathbf x,t)=\int\delta_{\mathbf k}(t)e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x}\,d^3k$. In the static universe each mode satisfies

$$
\ddot\delta_{\mathbf k}+c_s^2(k^2-k_J^2)\delta_{\mathbf k}=0.
$$

For $k>k_J$ there are [sound waves](../../../fluid-mechanics.md#acoustic-wave)

$$
\delta_{\mathbf k}=A_{\mathbf k}e^{i\omega_k t}+B_{\mathbf k}e^{-i\omega_k t},
\qquad
\omega_k=c_s\sqrt{k^2-k_J^2}.
$$

For $k<k_J$, [Jeans instability](../../../linear-cosmological-density-perturbation.md#jeans-instability) gives growing and decaying exponentials with rate $c_s\sqrt{k_J^2-k^2}$. At the [Jeans wavenumber](../../../linear-cosmological-density-perturbation.md#jeans-wavenumber) the two solutions are constant and linear in time.

<h3 id="14d/ii">ii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [14D](#14d)

<h4 id="14d/ii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#14d/ii)

On long wavelengths the gradient term is negligible. The [Friedmann equation](../../../cosmology.md#friedmann-equations) implies

$$
c_s^2k_J^2=\frac{4\pi G\bar\rho}{c^2}=\frac32H^2.
$$

During [matter domination](../../../linear-cosmological-density-perturbation.md#matter-domination), $a\propto t^{2/3}$ and $H=2/(3t)$, so the [linear growth equation](../../../linear-cosmological-density-perturbation.md#linear-growth-equation) becomes

$$
\ddot\delta+\frac4{3t}\dot\delta-\frac2{3t^2}\delta=0.
$$

The [power-law ansatz](../../../differential-equation.md#power-law-ansatz) $\delta\propto t^s$ gives $s=2/3$ or $s=-1$. Since $a\propto t^{2/3}$, the growing and decaying modes are

$$
\boxed{\delta_+\propto a,\qquad \delta_-\propto a^{-3/2}}.
$$

<h3 id="14d/iii">iii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [14D](#14d)

<h4 id="14d/iii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#14d/iii)

Under the stated approximation the perturbation equation is

$$
\ddot\delta+2H\dot\delta=0,
\qquad
\dot\delta\propto a^{-2}.
$$

During [radiation domination](../../../linear-cosmological-density-perturbation.md#radiation-domination), $a\propto t^{1/2}$, so $\dot\delta\propto t^{-1}$ and

$$
\delta=C_1+C_2\log t=C_1+2C_2\log a.
$$

Matter perturbations therefore have only [logarithmic growth of matter perturbations during radiation domination](../../../linear-cosmological-density-perturbation.md#logarithmic-growth-of-matter-perturbations-during-radiation-domination).

In a [curvature-dominated universe](../../../cosmology.md#curvature-dominated-universe), $a\propto t$, so $\dot\delta\propto t^{-2}$ and

$$
\delta=C_1+C_2t^{-1}=C_1+C_2a^{-1}.
$$

The nondecaying mode is constant: curvature domination gives [freezing of matter perturbations during curvature domination](../../../linear-cosmological-density-perturbation.md#freezing-of-matter-perturbations-during-curvature-domination), rather than continued growth.

<h5 id="15c/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#15c/b/i)

Starting from $|0^n\rangle|0^n\rangle$, the first layer of [Hadamard gates](../../../quantum-theory.md#hadamard-gate) gives

$$
2^{-n/2}\sum_{x\in B_n}|x\rangle|0^n\rangle.
$$

The [Boolean quantum oracle](../../../quantum-theory.md#boolean-quantum-oracle) then gives

$$
2^{-n/2}\sum_{x\in B_n}|x\rangle|f(x)\rangle.
$$

Using the [Walsh-Hadamard transform](../../../quantum-theory.md#walsh-hadamard-transform) identity

$$
H^{\otimes n}|x\rangle
=2^{-n/2}\sum_{y\in B_n}(-1)^{x\cdot y}|y\rangle,
$$

the final state is

$$
\boxed{2^{-n}\sum_{x,y\in B_n}(-1)^{x\cdot y}|y\rangle|f(x)\rangle.}
$$

<h4 id="15c/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#15c/b)

<h5 id="15c/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#15c/b/ii)

When $z=0^n$, the function $f$ is injective, so the states $|f(x)\rangle$ are distinct [computational basis](../../../quantum-theory.md#computational-basis) states and hence form an [orthonormal set](../../../linear-algebra.md#orthonormal-set). Therefore, for arbitrary signs $\varphi(x,y)$,

$$
\boxed{\left\|\sum_x\varphi(x,y)|f(x)\rangle\right\|^2
=\sum_x|\varphi(x,y)|^2
=2^n
=\left\|\sum_x\varphi(x,y)|x\rangle\right\|^2.}
$$

<h4 id="15c/b/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#15c/b)

<h5 id="15c/b/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#15c/b/iii)

In the final state, the second-register amplitude vector associated with outcome $y$ is

$$
2^{-n}\sum_x(-1)^{x\cdot y}|f(x)\rangle.
$$

For injective $f$, part (ii) says that its squared norm is $2^{-2n}2^n=2^{-n}$. By the [Born rule for a product-basis measurement](../../../quantum-theory.md#born-rule-for-a-product-basis-measurement), every $y\in B_n$ is therefore observed with probability $2^{-n}$: the input register is uniformly distributed.

<h4 id="15c/b/iv">iv</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#15c/b)

<h5 id="15c/b/iv/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#15c/b/iv)

Now let $z\ne0^n$ and choose one representative $x$ from each pair $\{x,x\mathbin\oplus z\}$. Since $f(x)=f(x\mathbin\oplus z)$ and

$$
(-1)^{(x\mathbin\oplus z)\cdot y}
=(-1)^{x\cdot y}(-1)^{z\cdot y},
$$

the unnormalized second-register amplitude for outcome $y$ is

$$
\sum_{\text{pairs }[x]}(-1)^{x\cdot y}
\bigl(1+(-1)^{z\cdot y}\bigr)|f(x)\rangle.
$$

If $y\cdot z=1$, every paired contribution cancels, so the probability is zero. If $y\cdot z=0$, every coefficient has magnitude two. The $2^{n-1}$ distinct pair values give orthogonal states, so the squared norm before the overall $2^{-n}$ factor is $4\cdot2^{n-1}=2^{n+1}$. The probability is therefore

$$
2^{-2n}2^{n+1}=2^{-(n-1)}.
$$

**Thus one run samples uniformly from the $2^{n-1}$ strings in the [orthogonal complement over the binary field](../../../quantum-theory.md#orthogonal-complement-over-the-binary-field) $\{y:y\cdot z=0\}$, which is the sampling step in [Simon's algorithm](../../../quantum-theory.md#simon-s-algorithm).**

## 16H

<h3 id="16h/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [16H](#16h)

A [class in set theory](../../../set-theory.md#class-set-theory) in the model $V$ is a collection

$$
C=\{x\in V:V\models\varphi(x,a_1,\ldots,a_k)\}
$$

defined by a first-order formula with parameters $a_i\in V$. A [set-theoretic class function](../../../set-theory.md#set-theoretic-class-function) is a definable class relation $F$ for which every input in its domain has exactly one output. Informally, the [Axiom schema of replacement](../../../set-theory.md#axiom-schema-of-replacement) says that the image $F``a$ of any set $a$ under any such function class is again a set.

Define the class function $F:\omega\to V$ by the [natural-number recursion theorem](../../../set-theory.md#natural-number-recursion-theorem),

$$
F(0)=\omega,
\qquad
F(n+1)=\mathcal P(F(n)).
$$

Then $F(n)=z_n$, and [Replacement](../../../set-theory.md#axiom-schema-of-replacement) applied to the set $\omega$ gives

$$
y=F``\omega=\{z_n:n\in\omega\}
$$

as a set.

Call a set small when it injects into some $z_n$. Every [natural number](../../../arithmetic.md#natural-number) $m$ is finite, and every member of $\operatorname{TC}(\{m\})$ is finite, so each injects into $z_0=\omega$. Hence $m\in\mathbf{HS}$. The set $\omega$ itself injects into $z_0$, and its hereditary members are natural numbers, so $\omega\in\mathbf{HS}$.

We next prove $z_n\in\mathbf{HS}$ by [mathematical induction](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#mathematical-induction). The case $z_0=\omega$ was just proved. If $z_n\in\mathbf{HS}$ and $u\in z_{n+1}=\mathcal P(z_n)$, then $u\subseteq z_n$, so inclusion injects $u$ into $z_n$ and makes $u$ small. Every set below $u$ in its [transitive closure](../../../set-theory.md#transitive-closure) is already below $z_n$ and is small by the induction hypothesis. The set $z_{n+1}$ itself injects into $z_{n+1}$. Thus every member of $\operatorname{TC}(\{z_{n+1}\})$ is small.

By [Cantor theorem](../../../set.md#cantor-s-theorem), $|z_{n+1}|>|z_n|$, so the $z_n$ are distinct and $z_n\mapsto n$ injects $y$ into $\omega=z_0$. Thus $y$ is small. Every other member of $\operatorname{TC}(\{y\})$ belongs to $\operatorname{TC}(\{z_n\})$ for some $n$, and is small by the preceding paragraph. Therefore $y\in\mathbf{HS}$. This proves the [finite-power-set hereditary-small construction](../../../set-theory.md#finite-power-set-hereditary-small-construction).

The structure $(\mathbf{HS},\in)$ is not a model of ZF because it fails the [Axiom of union](../../../set-theory.md#axiom-of-union). If $\bigcup y$ were small, it would inject into some $z_m$. But $z_{m+1}\subseteq\bigcup y$, since $z_{m+1}\in y$, so restriction would inject

$$
z_{m+1}=\mathcal P(z_m)\longrightarrow z_m.
$$

Together with the singleton injection $z_m\to\mathcal P(z_m)$, the [Cantor-Schröder-Bernstein theorem](../../../set-theory.md#cantor-schroder-bernstein-theorem) would produce a bijection, contradicting [Cantor theorem](../../../set.md#cantor-s-theorem). Hence $\bigcup y$ is not small and therefore does not belong to $\mathbf{HS}$. Since $y\in\mathbf{HS}$ has no union inside the class, the Union axiom fails.

## 17G

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="17g/i">i</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [17G](#17g)

<h4 id="17g/i/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#17g/i)

[Turan theorem](../../../graph-theory.md#turan-s-theorem) states that among all $N$-vertex graphs containing no $K_{r+1}$, the maximum number of [edges](../../../graph-theory.md#edge-of-a-graph) is attained by the [Turán graph](../../../graph-theory.md#turan-graph) $T_r(N)$, the [complete multipartite graph](../../../graph-theory.md#complete-multipartite-graph) whose $r$ part sizes differ by at most one.

Here is the [Zykov symmetrization](../../../graph-theory.md#zykov-symmetrization) proof. Start with an extremal $K_{r+1}$-free graph. If nonadjacent vertices $u,v$ have different neighbourhoods, replace the lower-degree vertex by a clone of the higher-degree one: delete all its incident edges and join it to precisely the neighbours of the other vertex. This does not decrease the number of edges and cannot create a $K_{r+1}$, because a clique using the clone corresponds to one using the original vertex. Repeating the operation, with the standard tie-breaking that merges equal-neighbourhood classes, produces a complete multipartite extremal graph. It has at most $r$ nonempty parts, since choosing one vertex from each part gives a clique.

If two part sizes satisfy $a\geq b+2$, moving one vertex from the larger part to the smaller changes the number of cross-edges by

$$
a-b-1>0.
$$

**Thus an extremal partition has exactly $r$ parts whose sizes differ by at most one. This is $T_r(N)$ and proves the theorem. For $r=2$ it is [Mantel theorem](../../../graph-theory.md#mantel-theorem): a triangle-free graph on $N$ vertices has at most $\lfloor N^2/4\rfloor$ edges.**

<h3 id="17g/ii">ii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [17G](#17g)

<h4 id="17g/ii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#17g/ii)

Here $|V(G)|=2n$ and $e(G)=n^2+1$. By [Mantel theorem](../../../graph-theory.md#mantel-theorem), a [triangle-free graph](../../../graph.md#triangle-free-graph) of this order has at most

$$
\left\lfloor\frac{(2n)^2}{4}\right\rfloor=n^2
$$

edges, so $G$ contains a [triangle in a graph](../../../graph.md#triangle-in-a-graph).

If $n=2$, then $G$ has four vertices and five of the six possible edges, so it is $K_4$ with one edge deleted. Exactly the two triangles containing neither both endpoints of the deleted edge remain. Hence $G$ contains two triangles.

<h3 id="17g/iii">iii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [17G](#17g)

<h4 id="17g/iii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#17g/iii)

Let $t(G)$ be the number of [triangles in a graph](../../../graph.md#triangle-in-a-graph). Count incidences $(e,\Delta)$ in which the edge $e$ lies in the triangle $\Delta$. Every edge contributes at least one incidence by hypothesis, while every triangle contributes exactly three. Therefore

$$
3t(G)\geq e(G)=n^2+1,
\qquad
 t(G)\geq\frac{n^2+1}{3}.
$$

This is the [edge-triangle incidence bound](../../../graph.md#edge-triangle-incidence-bound).

<h3 id="17g/iv">iv</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [17G](#17g)

<h4 id="17g/iv/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#17g/iv)

If the edge $uv$ lies in no triangle, no vertex can be adjacent to both endpoints; hence the [neighbourhoods](../../../graph.md#vertex-neighbourhood) satisfy $\Gamma(u)\cap\Gamma(v)=\varnothing$.

Suppose $|\Gamma(u)|+|\Gamma(v)|=2n$. The two disjoint neighbourhoods then partition $V(G)$. If both were [independent](../../../graph-theory.md#independent-set-graph-theory), every edge would run between these two parts, so $G$ would be [bipartite](../../../graph-theory.md#bipartite-graph) and

$$
e(G)\leq|\Gamma(u)|\,|\Gamma(v)|
\leq\left(\frac{|\Gamma(u)|+|\Gamma(v)|}{2}\right)^2=n^2,
$$

contrary to $e(G)=n^2+1$. Thus at least one neighbourhood contains an edge, and that edge forms a triangle with $u$ or $v$.

We now prove by induction on $n\geq2$ the [Triangle lower bound one edge above the Mantel threshold](../../../graph-theory.md#triangle-lower-bound-one-edge-above-the-mantel-threshold): every graph on $2n$ vertices with at least $n^2+1$ edges has at least $n$ triangles. Part (ii) is the base case. For $n\geq3$, if every edge lies in a triangle, part (iii) gives

$$
t(G)\geq\frac{n^2+1}{3}\geq n.
$$

Otherwise choose an edge $uv$ lying in no triangle and put $s=d(u)+d(v)\leq2n$. Deleting $u,v$ removes $s-1$ edges, so the graph $G'=G-u-v$ has

$$
e(G')=n^2+2-s\geq(n-1)^2+1.
$$

If $s=2n$, induction gives at least $n-1$ triangles in $G'$, while the nonindependence proved above supplies a further triangle through $u$ or $v$.

If $s\leq2n-1$, then $e(G')\geq(n-1)^2+2$. Induction first supplies a triangle $\Delta$ in $G'$. Delete one edge of $\Delta$; the remaining graph still has at least $(n-1)^2+1$ edges, so induction supplies $n-1$ triangles avoiding that edge. Together with $\Delta$, these give at least $n$ distinct triangles in $G$.

## 19F

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="19f/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [19F](#19f)

For subgroups $H,K\leq G$ and an $H$-representation $W$, [Mackey restriction formula](../../../representation-theory.md#mackey-restriction-formula) is

$$
\operatorname{Res}_K^G\operatorname{Ind}_H^G W
\cong
\bigoplus_{x\in K\backslash G/H}
\operatorname{Ind}_{K\cap xHx^{-1}}^K
\operatorname{Res}_{K\cap xHx^{-1}}^{xHx^{-1}}({}^xW).
$$

[Frobenius reciprocity](../../../representation-theory.md#frobenius-reciprocity) states

$$
\langle\operatorname{Ind}_H^G\chi,\psi\rangle_G
=\langle\chi,\operatorname{Res}_H^G\psi\rangle_H.
$$

Applying these with $K=H$ gives

$$
\left\langle\operatorname{Ind}_H^G\chi,
\operatorname{Ind}_H^G\chi\right\rangle_G
=
\sum_{x\in H\backslash G/H}
\left\langle
\operatorname{Res}_{H\cap xHx^{-1}}^H\chi,
\operatorname{Res}_{H\cap xHx^{-1}}^{xHx^{-1}}({}^x\chi)
\right\rangle.
$$

The identity double coset contributes one exactly when $W$ is irreducible, and every summand is a nonnegative integer. This proves [Mackey irreducibility criterion](../../../representation-theory.md#mackey-irreducibility-criterion): the induced representation is irreducible exactly when $W$ is irreducible and every nonidentity double-coset summand vanishes.

Now take $G=S_n$, $H=S_{n-1}$, and $x=(n-1\ n)\notin H$. Then

$$
H\cap xHx^{-1}=S_{n-2},
$$

the subgroup fixing both $n-1$ and $n$. Conjugation by $x$ fixes every element of this subgroup, so the two representations appearing in the corresponding Mackey summand are both $\operatorname{Res}_{S_{n-2}}^{S_{n-1}}W$. Their inner product is positive because this restriction is nonzero. Hence the [Mackey irreducibility criterion](../../../representation-theory.md#mackey-irreducibility-criterion) fails whenever $W$ is irreducible. If $W$ is reducible, induction preserves its direct-sum decomposition, so the induced representation is again reducible. Therefore $\operatorname{Ind}_{S_{n-1}}^{S_n}W$ is never irreducible for $n\geq2$.

## 21I

<h4 id="21i/a/solution">Solution</h4>

For a bounded [linear operator](../../../vector-space.md#linear-operator) $T:H\to H$, the [spectrum](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#spectrum-functional-analysis) is

$$
\sigma(T)=\{\lambda\in\mathbb C:T-\lambda I\text{ has no bounded inverse}\},
$$

while the [point spectrum](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#point-spectrum) is the set of [eigenvalues](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue), equivalently the $\lambda$ for which $T-\lambda I$ is not injective.

A [compact operator](../../../compact-operator.md) maps the unit ball to a relatively compact set. A [self-adjoint operator](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#self-adjoint-operator) satisfies $T=T^*$, or $\langle Tx,y\rangle=\langle x,Ty\rangle$ for all $x,y\in H$. A [finite-rank operator](../../../compact-operator.md#finite-rank-operator) has finite-dimensional image.

<h3 id="21i/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [21I](#21i)

<h4 id="21i/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#21i/b)

Compactness gives a stronger statement than the one requested. Since $T(B_H)$ has compact closure, finitely many open balls of radius $\delta$ cover it. Choose their centres from $T(B_H)$ and let $E$ be their finite-dimensional linear span. Then

$$
\sup_{\|x\|\leq1}\operatorname{dist}(Tx,E)<\delta.
$$

For the [orthogonal projection](../../../hilbert-space.md#orthogonal-projection) $P_E$, best approximation in a Hilbert space gives

$$
\|(I-P_E)Tx\|<\delta
\qquad(\|x\|\leq1).
$$

In particular this holds for every vector $e_n$ in the given [orthonormal basis](../../../linear-algebra.md#orthonormal-basis).

The operator $P_ET$ has image in $E$ and hence has [finite rank](../../../compact-operator.md#finite-rank-operator), while

$$
\|T-P_ET\|
=\sup_{\|x\|\leq1}\|(I-P_E)Tx\|<\delta.
$$

**Thus every compact operator is an [operator norm](../../../continuous-dual-space.md#operator-norm) limit of finite-rank operators. This is the [finite-rank approximation theorem for compact operators on a Hilbert space](../../../compact-operator.md#finite-rank-approximation-theorem-for-compact-operators-on-a-hilbert-space).**

(S-\lambda I)x_E=y_E-Sx_\perp.
$$

For $x=x_E+x_\perp$ we obtain $(S-\lambda I)x=y$. Hence $S-\lambda I$ is surjective. This is the [finite-rank Fredholm alternative](../../../compact-operator.md#finite-rank-fredholm-alternative).

<h3 id="21i/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [21I](#21i)

<h4 id="21i/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#21i/d)

Write $A=T-\lambda I$. We first prove that $\operatorname{im}A$ is dense. Otherwise some nonzero $y$ lies in its [orthogonal complement](../../../hilbert-space.md#orthogonal-complement), so

$$
A^*y=0,
\qquad T^*y=\overline\lambda y.
$$

By part (b), choose finite-dimensional spaces $E_j$ containing $y$ such that $S_j=P_{E_j}T\to T$ in [operator norm](../../../continuous-dual-space.md#operator-norm). Since

$$
S_j^*y=T^*P_{E_j}y=T^*y=\overline\lambda y,
$$

the operator $S_j^*-\overline\lambda I$ is not injective. Therefore $S_j-\lambda I$ is not surjective; the contrapositive of part (c) makes $\lambda$ an eigenvalue of $S_j$. Choose unit vectors $x_j$ with $S_jx_j=\lambda x_j$. Then

$$
\|Tx_j-\lambda x_j\|
\leq\|T-S_j\|\longrightarrow0.
$$

Compactness gives a subsequence for which $Tx_j$ converges. Since $\lambda\ne0$, the displayed relation makes $x_j$ converge to a unit vector $x$, and continuity gives $Tx=\lambda x$, contradicting the hypothesis. Thus $\operatorname{im}A$ is dense.

Next suppose $A$ were not bounded below, or equivalently that its [lower norm](../../../functional-analysis.md#lower-norm-of-an-operator) were zero. There would be unit vectors $x_j$ with $Ax_j\to0$, hence

$$
Tx_j=\lambda x_j+o(1).
$$

Again a convergent subsequence of $Tx_j$ forces the corresponding $x_j$ to converge to a unit vector $x$ satisfying $Ax=0$, another contradiction. Therefore some $c>0$ satisfies

$$
\|(T-\lambda I)x\|\geq c\|x\|.
$$

This lower bound makes $\operatorname{im}A$ closed: if $Ax_j$ converges, then $x_j$ is Cauchy and its limit maps to the same limit. Since the image is both dense and closed, it is all of $H$. We have proved the [Fredholm alternative for a compact operator](../../../compact-operator.md#fredholm-alternative): every nonzero spectral value of a compact operator is an eigenvalue.

The [continuous dual space](../../../continuous-dual-space.md) $X'$ consists of all bounded linear maps $\Lambda:X\to\mathbb C$, with

and $\|\Lambda_h\|=\|h\|_q$.

[Weak-star convergence](../../../weak-topology.md#weak-star-topology) means pointwise convergence on the predual:

$$
\Lambda_j(x)\longrightarrow\Lambda(x)
\qquad\text{for every }x\in X.
$$

For each $x\in X$, its image $J_Xx\in X''$ under the [canonical embedding into the bidual](../../../functional-analysis.md#canonical-embedding-into-the-bidual) satisfies $(J_Xx)(\Lambda)=\Lambda(x)$. Thus weak convergence implies weak-star convergence, and altogether

$$
\text{norm convergence}\ \Longrightarrow\
\text{weak convergence}\ \Longrightarrow\
\text{weak-star convergence}.
$$

For the requested example, let

$$
f_j(x)=\sin(jx)\in L^\infty(\mathbb R).
$$

The [Riemann-Lebesgue lemma](../../../fourier-analysis.md#riemann-lebesgue-lemma) gives $f_j\mathrel{\stackrel{*}{\rightharpoonup}}0$ in $(L^1)'$. Since

$$
f_j(x)^2=\frac12-\frac12\cos(2jx),
$$

the same lemma gives $f_j^2\mathrel{\stackrel{*}{\rightharpoonup}}1/2$. Hence $f=0$ and $g=1/2\ne f^2$, exhibiting the [failure of weak-star convergence to commute with squaring](../../../weak-topology.md#failure-of-weak-star-convergence-to-commute-with-squaring).

<h3 id="22i/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [22I](#22i)

<h4 id="22i/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#22i/c)

On $C_c^0(\mathbb R^n)$ equipped with the $L^\infty$ norm, point evaluation is linear and satisfies

$$
|\Lambda f|=|f(0)|\leq\|f\|_\infty,
$$

with equality for a compactly supported function equal to one near zero. Thus $\|\Lambda\|=1$. The [Hahn-Banach theorem](../../../functional-analysis.md#hahn-banach-theorem) extends it, without increasing its norm, to a continuous functional $\widetilde\Lambda$ on $L^\infty(\mathbb R^n)$.

This extension cannot be represented by an $L^1$ function. If $h\in L^1$ satisfied $\widetilde\Lambda(f)=\int fh$, choose continuous functions $0\leq f_k\leq1$ with $f_k(0)=1$ and support in the ball of radius $1/k$. Then

$$
1=\widetilde\Lambda(f_k)=\int f_kh\longrightarrow0
$$

by the [absolute continuity of the Lebesgue integral](../../../calculus.md#absolute-continuity-of-the-lebesgue-integral), a contradiction. Therefore $(L^\infty)'$ strictly contains the functionals represented by $L^1$; this is a [singular functional on L infinity](../../../functional-analysis.md#singular-functional-on-l-infinity).

Finally, $L^p(\mathbb R^n)$ is a [reflexive Banach space](../../../functional-analysis.md#reflexive-banach-space) exactly when

$$
1<p<\infty.
$$

The endpoint spaces $L^1$ and $L^\infty$ are not reflexive.

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="23f/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [23F](#23f)

For the [period lattice](../../../complex-analysis.md#period-lattice) $\Lambda=\langle\lambda,\mu\rangle$, define the [Weierstrass elliptic function](../../../complex-analysis.md#weierstrass-elliptic-function) by

$$
\wp(z)=\frac1{z^2}
+\sum_{\omega\in\Lambda\setminus\{0\}}
\left(\frac1{(z-\omega)^2}-\frac1{\omega^2}\right).
$$

To prove [Normal convergence of the Weierstrass elliptic-function series](../../../complex-analysis.md#normal-convergence-of-the-weierstrass-elliptic-function-series), let $K\subset\mathbb C\setminus\Lambda$ be compact and choose $R$ with $|z|\leq R$ on $K$. For $|\omega|>2R$,

$$
\left|\frac1{(z-\omega)^2}-\frac1{\omega^2}\right|
=\left|\frac{2\omega z-z^2}{\omega^2(z-\omega)^2}\right|
\leq\frac{C_K}{|\omega|^3}.
$$

The comparison series $\sum_{\omega\ne0}|\omega|^{-3}$ converges by the stated lattice-sum criterion. The [Weierstrass M-test](../../../probability-and-statistics.md#weierstrass-m-test) gives uniform convergence on $K$; since $K$ was arbitrary, the series converges locally uniformly away from $\Lambda$. The [locally uniform convergence of holomorphic functions](../../../complex-analysis.md#locally-uniform-convergence-of-holomorphic-functions) makes its sum holomorphic there, and the displayed principal term gives a double pole at every lattice point.

The function $\wp$ is even and satisfies

$$
\wp(z)=\wp(w)
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
z\equiv\pm w\pmod\Lambda.
$$

The three nonzero [two-torsion points](../../../complex-analysis.md#two-torsion-point-of-a-complex-torus) $z_1,z_2,z_3$ are distinct modulo $\Lambda$ and each equals its own negative modulo $\Lambda$. Therefore no two can have the same $\wp$-value, so $e_1,e_2,e_3$ are distinct. Equivalently, $\wp$ is a degree-two branched map and each half-lattice point already occupies its fiber with multiplicity two.

For a transitive example, take the [equianharmonic lattice](../../../complex-analysis.md#equianharmonic-lattice)

$$
\Lambda=\mathbb Z+\mathbb Z\omega,
\qquad \omega=e^{2\pi i/3},
$$

and define $\theta([z])=[\omega z]$. Multiplication by $\omega$ preserves $\Lambda$, so $\theta:\mathbb C/\Lambda\to\mathbb C/\Lambda$ is a [conformal equivalence](../../../geometry-and-topology.md#conformal-equivalence). On the nonzero half-lattice points it acts by

$$
\left[\frac12\right]
\longmapsto\left[\frac\omega2\right]
\longmapsto\left[\frac{1+\omega}{2}\right]
\longmapsto\left[\frac12\right],
$$

because $\omega^2=-1-\omega$. Thus it acts transitively.

Let the [smooth projective curve](../../../projective-space.md#smooth-projective-curve) be the [projective completion](../../../projective-space.md#projective-completion) of $f(x,y)=0$, of [degree](../../../polynomial.md#degree-of-a-polynomial) $d$, and suppose after interchanging $x$ and $y$ if necessary that $f_y=\partial f/\partial y$ is nonzero. A basis of its [holomorphic differential forms](../../../complex-geometry.md#holomorphic-differential-form) is

$$
\left\{
x^iy^j\frac{dx}{f_y}:i,j\geq0,\ i+j\leq d-3
\right\}.
$$

Equivalently one may use $-x^iy^jdy/f_x$. These expressions agree on their common domain because differentiating $f(x,y)=0$ gives $f_xdx+f_ydy=0$. The allowed [monomials](../../../polynomial.md#monomial) are precisely the homogeneous polynomials of degree at most $d-3$, in accordance with the [adjunction formula](../../../complex-geometry.md#adjunction-formula) $K_C\cong\mathcal O_C(d-3)$.

<h3 id="24f/ii">ii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [24F](#24f)

<h4 id="24f/ii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#24f/ii)

Put

$$
F=X_0^4-X_1^4-X_2^4.
$$

By the [Jacobian criterion](../../../algebraic-geometry.md#jacobian-criterion), a [projective plane curve](../../../algebraic-geometry.md#projective-plane-curve) is singular only at a common projective zero of $F$ and all its [partial derivatives](../../../calculus.md#partial-derivative). Here

F_{X_0}=4X_0^3,\qquad
F_{X_1}=-4X_1^3,\qquad
F_{X_2}=-4X_2^3.

Their simultaneous vanishing forces $X_0=X_1=X_2=0$, which is not a point of [projective space](../../../projective-space.md). Hence the Fermat quartic $C$ is [nonsingular](../../../projective-space.md#smooth-projective-curve). In particular it is a [smooth plane quartic](../../../algebraic-geometry.md#smooth-plane-quartic) and has [geometric genus](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#geometric-genus) $(4-1)(4-2)/2=3$.

<h3 id="24f/iv">iv</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [24F](#24f)

<h4 id="24f/iv/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#24f/iv)

Choose a point $p\in C$ and project from $p$ to a line not containing $p$. Every line through $p$ meets the quartic in four points counted with [intersection multiplicity](../../../algebraic-geometry.md#intersection-multiplicity) by [Bézout theorem](../../../algebraic-geometry.md#bezout-s-theorem); removing the fixed intersection at $p$ leaves a generic fibre of three points. This [projection from a point on a plane curve](../../../algebraic-geometry.md#projection-from-a-point-on-a-plane-curve) therefore defines a nonconstant morphism

$$
C\longrightarrow\mathbb P^1
$$

of degree three, so the [gonality](../../../algebraic-geometry.md#gonality) is at most three.

It cannot be one, because a degree-one morphism between [smooth projective curves](../../../projective-space.md#smooth-projective-curve) is an [isomorphism of algebraic varieties](../../../algebraic-geometry.md#isomorphism-of-algebraic-varieties), whereas $C$ has genus three and the [projective line](../../../finite-group-theory.md#projective-line) has genus zero. It cannot be two either: that would make $C$ a [hyperelliptic curve](../../../algebraic-geometry.md#hyperelliptic-curve). The [canonical map of a hyperelliptic curve](../../../algebraic-geometry.md#canonical-map-of-a-hyperelliptic-curve) is not a [closed embedding](../../../ringed-space.md#closed-immersion), but for a [smooth plane quartic](../../../algebraic-geometry.md#smooth-plane-quartic) the [canonical map of a smooth plane curve](../../../algebraic-geometry.md#canonical-map-of-a-smooth-plane-curve) is exactly its given embedding $C\hookrightarrow\mathbb P^2$, since $K_C\cong\mathcal O_C(1)$. This contradiction proves

$$
\boxed{d=\operatorname{gon}(C)=3.}
$$

<h3 id="25i/a">a</h3>

<h4 id="25i/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#25i/a)

Suppose $S$ lies in the closed ball of radius $R$ centred at $c$. By [compactness](../../../topology.md#compact-space), the continuous function $q\mapsto|q-c|$ attains its maximum $r\leq R$ at some $p\in S$. The sphere of radius $r$ about $c$ is a [supporting sphere](../../../second-fundamental-form.md#supporting-sphere) tangent to $S$ at $p$.

Put $N=(p-c)/r$. For any unit vector $v\in T_pS$, choose a surface curve $\alpha$ with $\alpha(0)=p$ and $\alpha'(0)=v$. Since $|\alpha(t)-c|^2$ has a local maximum at zero,

$$
0\geq
\left.\frac{d^2}{dt^2}|\alpha(t)-c|^2\right|_{t=0}
=2+2r\langle\alpha''(0),N\rangle.
$$

The scalar $\langle\alpha''(0),N\rangle$ is the [normal curvature](../../../second-fundamental-form.md#normal-curvature) in direction $v$, so every normal curvature is at most $-1/r$ with this choice of [unit normal](../../../differential-geometry.md#unit-normal). Applying this to [principal directions](../../../second-fundamental-form.md#principal-direction) shows that both [principal curvatures](../../../second-fundamental-form.md#principal-curvature) satisfy $k_i\leq-1/r$. Therefore

$$
K(p)=k_1k_2\geq\frac1{r^2}\geq\frac1{R^2}>0.
$$

This proves both assertions, including the existence of an [elliptic point](../../../second-fundamental-form.md#elliptic-point) on every compact regular surface.

<h3 id="25i/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [25I](#25i)

<h4 id="25i/b/solution">Solution</h4>

Choose an [orthonormal basis](../../../linear-algebra.md#orthonormal-basis) $(e_1,e_2)$ of $T_pS$ and write $e(\theta)=\cos\theta\,e_1+\sin\theta\,e_2$. On a punctured disc on which the [exponential map](../../../riemannian-geometry.md#exponential-map-riemannian-geometry) is a [diffeomorphism](../../../geometry-and-topology.md#diffeomorphism), the map

$$
X(r,\theta)=\exp_p\bigl(r e(\theta)\bigr),
\qquad 0<r<\varepsilon,
$$

defines [geodesic polar coordinates](../../../riemannian-geometry.md#geodesic-polar-coordinates) centred at $p$. The curves $r\mapsto X(r,\theta)$ are unit-speed [geodesics](../../../riemannian-geometry.md#geodesic), so the first coefficient of the [first fundamental form](../../../differential-geometry.md#first-fundamental-form) is

$$
E=\langle X_r,X_r\rangle=1.
$$

The [Gauss lemma](../../../riemannian-geometry.md#gauss-s-lemma-riemannian-geometry) says that radial and angular coordinate vectors are orthogonal, hence

$$
F=\langle X_r,X_\theta\rangle=0.
$$

The angular variation $J=X_\theta$ is a [Jacobi field](../../../general-relativity.md#jacobi-field) along each radial geodesic, with initial data

$$
J(0)=0,
\qquad
D_rJ(0)=e'(\theta),
\qquad |e'(\theta)|=1.
$$

Since $G=\langle X_\theta,X_\theta\rangle=|J|^2$, smooth dependence of the [geodesic flow](../../../riemannian-geometry.md#geodesic-flow) gives

$$
\sqrt{G(r,\theta)}=|J(r)|=r+o(r).
$$

Consequently

$$
\lim_{r\to0}G(r,\theta)=0,
\qquad
\lim_{r\to0}(\sqrt G)_r(r,\theta)=1,
$$

as required.

<h4 id="25i/c/solution">Solution</h4>

Put $h(r,\theta)=\sqrt{G(r,\theta)}$. The [Jacobi equation in geodesic polar coordinates](../../../riemannian-geometry.md#jacobi-equation-in-geodesic-polar-coordinates) and the initial conditions from part (b) are

$$
h_{rr}=-Kh,
\qquad h(0,\theta)=0,
\qquad h_r(0,\theta)=1.
$$

Inside a geodesic polar coordinate ball, $h>0$. If $K\leq0$, then $h_{rr}\geq0$, so $h_r\geq1$ and therefore $h(r,\theta)\geq r$. The [Riemannian area element](../../../differential-geometry.md#area-element-of-a-surface) is $h\,dr\,d\theta$, whence

$$
\operatorname{Area}B(p,\varepsilon_0)
=\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^{\varepsilon_0}h(r,\theta)\,dr\,d\theta
\geq\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^{\varepsilon_0}r\,dr\,d\theta
=\pi\varepsilon_0^2.
$$

**Thus nonpositive [Gaussian curvature](../../../second-fundamental-form.md#gaussian-curvature) makes such a ball at least as large as the [Euclidean disk](../../../topology.md#disk-mathematics) of the same radius.**

Again set $h=\sqrt G$ and fix $\theta$. For $0<\alpha<C^{-1/2}$ define

$$
f(r)=h(r,\theta)-\alpha\sin(\sqrt C\,r).

$$
f''+Cf=(C-K)h\geq0,
\qquad f(0)=0,

\frac d{dr}
\left(f'\sin(\sqrt C r)-\sqrt C f\cos(\sqrt C r)\right)

The expression in parentheses vanishes at zero, and hence

$$
\left(\frac f{\sin(\sqrt C r)}\right)'\geq0.

Its limit at zero is $(1-\alpha\sqrt C)/\sqrt C>0$, so $f>0$. Letting $\alpha\uparrow C^{-1/2}$ yields the [one-dimensional Rauch comparison inequality](../../../riemannian-geometry.md#one-dimensional-rauch-comparison-inequality)

h(r,\theta)\geq\frac{\sin(\sqrt C r)}{\sqrt C}
\qquad(0\leq r<\pi/\sqrt C).

It follows that

$$
\operatorname{Area}B(p,\varepsilon)
\geq\frac{2\pi}{C}\bigl(1-\cos(\sqrt C\,\varepsilon)\bigr).

\frac{2(1-\cos x)}{x^2}\longrightarrow1

as $x\to0$. Given $0<\gamma<1$, choose $x_0\in(0,\pi)$ such that $2(1-\cos x)/x^2\geq\gamma$ for $0<x\leq x_0$, and set $\varepsilon_0=x_0/\sqrt C$. Then every geodesic polar coordinate ball of radius $\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_0$ satisfies

## 26K

<h4 id="26k/a/solution">Solution</h4>

A [measure-preserving transformation](../../../measure-theory.md#measure-preserving-transformation) $T$ on a [probability space](../../../probability-theory.md#probability-space) $(X,\mathcal A,m)$ is [ergodic](../../../measure-theory.md#ergodicity) if every [invariant event](../../../measure-theory.md#invariant-sigma-algebra) $A\in\mathcal A$, meaning

$$
T^{-1}A=A
$$

up to an $m$-null set, satisfies $m(A)\in\{0,1\}$. Thus the [invariant sigma-algebra](../../../measure-theory.md#invariant-sigma-algebra) is trivial modulo null sets.

<h3 id="26k/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [26K](#26k)

<h4 id="26k/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#26k/b)

The [Kolmogorov zero-one law](../../../probability-theory.md#kolmogorov-s-zero-one-law) says that for [independent random variables](../../../random-variable.md#independent-random-variables) $(Y_n)_{n\geq0}$, every event in the [tail sigma-algebra](../../../probability-theory.md#tail-sigma-algebra)

$$
\mathcal T=\bigcap_{N\geq0}\sigma(Y_N,Y_{N+1},\ldots)
$$

has [probability](../../../probability-theory.md#probability-measure) zero or one.

In the [canonical model of an independent and identically distributed sequence](../../../probability-theory.md#canonical-model-of-an-independent-and-identically-distributed-sequence), the sample space is a sequence space $E^{\mathbb N}$ with the product law, $Y_n$ is the $n$th coordinate, and the left [Bernoulli shift](../../../measure-theory.md#bernoulli-shift) $S$ satisfies $Y_n\circ S=Y_{n+1}$. If $A$ is shift-invariant, then for every $N$,

$$
A=S^{-N}A\in\sigma(Y_N,Y_{N+1},\ldots),
$$

so $A\in\mathcal T$. The zero-one law therefore says that the canonical one-sided i.i.d. shift is [ergodic](../../../measure-theory.md#ergodicity).

<h3 id="26k/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [26K](#26k)

<h4 id="26k/c/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#26k/c)

<h5 id="26k/c/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#26k/c/i)

For a [Borel set](../../../measure-theory.md#borel-set) $A\subseteq[0,1]$, the two inverse branches of the [tent map](../../../dynamical-systems.md#tent-map) are $y\mapsto y/2$ and $y\mapsto1-y/2$. Apart from their immaterial common endpoint,

$$
T^{-1}A
=\frac A2\ \cup\ \left(1-\frac A2\right).
$$

Each branch scales [Lebesgue measure](../../../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-measure) by $1/2$, and their images lie in opposite half-intervals. Hence

$$
m(T^{-1}A)=\frac12m(A)+\frac12m(A)=m(A).
$$

Also $m([0,1])=1$, so $m$ is an invariant probability measure.

<h5 id="26k/c/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#26k/c/ii)

The event $\{X_n=1\}$ is $T^{-n}(0,1/2)$. Since $T^n$ has $2^n$ affine branches of slope magnitude $2^n$, this preimage is, up to endpoints, a disjoint union of $2^n$ intervals of length $2^{-(n+1)}$ and has measure $1/2$.

More generally, for every binary word $(a_0,\ldots,a_{N-1})$, its [itinerary cylinder](../../../dynamical-systems.md#itinerary-cylinder)

$$
C(a_0,\ldots,a_{N-1})
=\{x:X_0(x)=a_0,\ldots,X_{N-1}(x)=a_{N-1}\}
$$

is, up to finitely many endpoints, one monotonicity interval of $T^N$ and has measure $2^{-N}$. Therefore

$$
m(C(a_0,\ldots,a_{N-1}))
=\prod_{j=0}^{N-1}m(X_j=a_j),
$$

so the $X_n$ are [independent and identically distributed random variables](../../../random-variable.md#independent-and-identically-distributed-random-variables), each with the [Bernoulli distribution](../../../discrete-probability-distribution.md#bernoulli-distribution) of parameter $1/2$.

The finite itinerary cylinders have diameters at most $2^{-N}$. Their endpoints form a dense set, so their unions generate the [Borel sigma-algebra](../../../measure-theory.md#borel-sigma-algebra) after [completion](../../../measure-theory.md#completion-of-a-measure) by null sets. Thus

$$
\sigma(X_0,X_1,\ldots)=\mathcal A
$$

in the standard probability-space convention that σ-algebras are identified modulo null sets.

There is a small literal endpoint defect in the uncompleted formulation of the question: $0$, $1$, and $2/3$ all have the all-zero itinerary, so $\sigma(X_0,X_1,\ldots)$ cannot separate these Borel singletons. Consequently the displayed equality is false as an equality of raw Borel σ-algebras with the stated open interval, but it is true after completion and modulo null sets, which is the version used in the ergodicity argument.

**Thus $A$ belongs to the [tail sigma-algebra](../../../probability-theory.md#tail-sigma-algebra) of the i.i.d. sequence $(X_n)$. A [renewal-reward process](../../../probability-theory.md#renewal-reward-process) consists of i.i.d. regenerative cycles $(\xi_n,R_n)$, where $\xi_n>0$ is the $n$th cycle length and $R_n$ is the reward accumulated during that cycle. If $0<\mathbb E\xi_1<\infty$ and $\mathbb E|R_1|<\infty$, the [renewal-reward theorem](../../../probability-theory.md#renewal-reward-theorem) states that the cumulative reward $Z(t)$ satisfies

$$
\frac{Z(t)}t\longrightarrow\frac{\mathbb E R_1}{\mathbb E\xi_1}
$$

almost surely, with the usual negligible-remainder condition for an unfinished cycle.

For the machine, let $X\sim\operatorname{Exp}(\lambda)$ be its working lifetime after a repair. The next repair occurs at the first inspection following failure, so one repair-to-repair cycle has length

$$
\xi=m\left\lceil\frac Xm\right\rceil,
$$

while its working-time reward is $R=X$. Since an [exponential distribution](../../../continuous-probability-distribution.md#exponential-distribution) of rate $\lambda$ has mean $1/\lambda$,

$$
\mathbb ER=\frac1\lambda.
$$

By the [tail-sum formula](../../../probability-theory.md#tail-sum-formula) for the positive integer-valued variable $\lceil X/m\rceil$,

$$
\mathbb E\left\lceil\frac Xm\right\rceil
=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbb P(X>km)
=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda km}
=\frac1{1-e^{-\lambda m}}.
$$

Therefore

$$
\mathbb E\xi=\frac{m}{1-e^{-\lambda m}},
$$

and the [long-run proportion of time for which the machine is working](../../../probability-theory.md#long-run-availability-under-periodic-inspection) is

$$
\boxed{\frac{\mathbb ER}{\mathbb E\xi}
=\frac{1-e^{-\lambda m}}{\lambda m}}.
$$

## 28J

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="28j/i">i</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [28J](#28j)

<h4 id="28j/i/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#28j/i)

Let $K(t,\mathord\cdot)$ be the [Markov kernel](../../../markov-process.md#markov-kernel) of the [Metropolis–Hastings algorithm](../../../statistical-inference.md#metropolis-hastings-algorithm). Away from the possibility of remaining at $t$, its transition density is

$$
k(t,s)=q(s\mid t)\rho(t,s).
$$

Multiplication by the target density gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mu(t)k(t,s)
&=\mu(t)q(s\mid t)
\min\left\{\frac{\mu(s)q(t\mid s)}{\mu(t)q(s\mid t)},1\right\}\\
&=\min\{\mu(s)q(t\mid s),\mu(t)q(s\mid t)\}\\
&=\mu(s)k(s,t).
\end{aligned}
$$

This is [detailed balance](../../../markov-process.md#detailed-balance) for every pair of distinct states.

Write

$$
r(t)=1-\int_\Theta k(t,s)\,ds
$$

for the rejection probability, so

$$
K(t,ds)=k(t,s)\,ds+r(t)\delta_t(ds).
$$

For every measurable $B\subseteq\Theta$, detailed balance and the [Tonelli theorem](../../../measure-theory.md#tonelli-theorem) give

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_\Theta K(t,B)\mu(t)\,dt
&=\int_B\int_\Theta \mu(t)k(t,s)\,dt\,ds
 +\int_B\mu(t)r(t)\,dt\\
&=\int_B\mu(s)\left[\int_\Theta k(s,t)\,dt+r(s)\right]ds\\
&=\int_B\mu(s)\,ds.
\end{aligned}
$$

**Hence $\mu$ is an [invariant distribution](../../../markov-process.md#stationary-distribution) of the chain.**

<h3 id="28j/ii">ii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [28J](#28j)

<h4 id="28j/ii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#28j/ii)

Let $L(t)=f(x,t)$ be the [likelihood function](../../../statistical-modelling.md#likelihood-function). Since the [prior distribution](../../../statistical-inference.md#prior-probability) is $N(0,I_p)$, the [posterior density](../../../statistical-inference.md#posterior-density) has the form

$$
\mu(t)=\frac1ZL(t)\phi(t),
\qquad
\phi(t)=(2\pi)^{-p/2}e^{-\lVert t\rVert^2/2},
$$

where $Z$ is the [normalizing constant](../../../statistical-inference.md#bayesian-model-evidence).

Choose $0<\delta\leq1/2$ and use the [Gaussian autoregressive proposal reversible with respect to a standard normal distribution](../../../statistical-inference.md#gaussian-autoregressive-proposal-reversible-with-respect-to-a-standard-normal-distribution)

$$
s=\sqrt{1-2\delta}\,t+\sqrt{2\delta}\,Z_0,
\qquad Z_0\sim N(0,I_p).
$$

Thus

$$
q(\mathord\cdot\mid t)
=N\!\left(\sqrt{1-2\delta}\,t,2\delta I_p\right),
$$

whose [covariance matrix](../../../variance.md#covariance-matrix) is the required $2\delta I_p$. Reversibility with respect to the [standard normal distribution](../../../probability-theory.md#standard-normal-distribution) says

$$
\phi(t)q(s\mid t)=\phi(s)q(t\mid s),
$$

so

$$
\frac{q(t\mid s)}{q(s\mid t)}=\frac{\phi(t)}{\phi(s)}.
$$

The [Metropolis–Hastings acceptance probability](../../../statistical-inference.md#metropolis-hastings-acceptance-probability) therefore reduces to

$$
\rho(t,s)
=\min\left\{
\frac{L(s)\phi(s)}{L(t)\phi(t)}
\frac{\phi(t)}{\phi(s)},1
\right\}
=\boxed{\min\left\{\frac{f(x,s)}{f(x,t)},1\right\}}.
$$

This is the [Preconditioned Crank–Nicolson algorithm](../../../statistical-inference.md#preconditioned-crank-nicolson-algorithm) with proposal scale $\beta=\sqrt{2\delta}$.

A real-valued process $(B_t)_{t\geq0}$ is a standard [Brownian motion](../../../brownian-motion.md) if:

- $B_0=0$ almost surely;
- it has [independent increments](../../../stochastic-process.md#independent-increments);
- $B_t-B_s$ has the [normal distribution](../../../probability-theory.md#normal-distribution) $N(0,t-s)$ whenever $0\leq s<t$; and
- its sample paths are almost surely [continuous](../../../calculus.md#continuous-function).

Let $M_t=\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}B_s$ and let $a>0$. The [Brownian reflection principle](../../../brownian-motion.md#reflection-principle-wiener-process) says that reflecting a Brownian path about the level $a$ after its first hitting time of $a$ preserves its law. In particular, for $b\leq a$,

$$
\mathbb P(M_t\geq a,\ B_t\leq b)
=\mathbb P(B_t\geq2a-b).

Taking $b=a$ gives

$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(M_t\geq a)=2\mathbb P(B_t\geq a).}
$$

For $x\leq y$, the [Brownian reflection principle](../../../brownian-motion.md#reflection-principle-wiener-process) gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb P(B_t\leq x,M_t\leq y)
&=\mathbb P(B_t\leq x)
-\mathbb P(B_t\leq x,M_t>y)\\
&=\Phi\!\left(\frac x{\sqrt t}\right)

&=\Phi\!\left(\frac x{\sqrt t}\right)

\end{aligned}

The [joint distribution function](../../../probability-theory.md#joint-distribution-function) is therefore

$$
\boxed{
F_{B_t,M_t}(x,y)=
\begin{cases}
0,&y<0,\\
\Phi(x/\sqrt t)+\Phi((2y-x)/\sqrt t)-1,&y\geq0,\ x\leq y,\\

\end{cases}}

## 31E

<h3 id="31e/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [31E](#31e)

<h4 id="31e/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#31e/a)

Let $\phi_t(x_0)$ denote the [flow map](../../../dynamical-systems.md#flow-map) generated by $\dot x=f(x)$.


- The fixed point $0$ is [asymptotically stable](../../../dynamical-systems.md#asymptotic-stability) if it is [Lyapunov stable](../../../dynamical-systems.md#lyapunov-stability) and there is a neighbourhood $U$ of $0$ such that $\phi_t(x_0)\to0$ as $t\to\infty$ for every $x_0\in U$.
- Its [domain of stability](../../../dynamical-systems.md#basin-of-attraction), or [basin of attraction](../../../dynamical-systems.md#basin-of-attraction), is $\{x_0:\phi_t(x_0)\to0\text{ as }t\to\infty\}$, with forward existence understood.
- A [Lyapunov function](../../../dynamical-systems.md#lyapunov-function) on a neighbourhood $E$ of $0$ is a continuous function $V$, differentiable away from the origin, such that $V(0)=0$, $V(x)>0$ for $x\ne0$, and its [orbital derivative](../../../dynamical-systems.md#orbital-derivative)


$$
\dot V(x)=\nabla V(x)\mathbin{\cdot}f(x)
$$

is nonpositive on $E$.

The [First Lyapunov theorem](../../../dynamical-systems.md#first-lyapunov-theorem) says that the existence of such a function makes the origin Lyapunov stable. To prove it, choose a sufficiently small closed ball $\overline B_\varepsilon\subset E$. By [compactness](../../../topology.md#compact-space) and positive definiteness,

$$
\alpha=\min_{\lVert x\rVert=\varepsilon}V(x)>0.
$$

By [continuity](../../../calculus.md#continuous-function), some $\delta>0$ satisfies $V(x)<\alpha$ whenever $\lVert x\rVert<\delta$. Along a trajectory beginning in $B_\delta$, $V$ cannot increase. Such a trajectory therefore cannot reach the sphere $\lVert x\rVert=\varepsilon$, where $V\geq\alpha$. Hence it remains in $B_\varepsilon$ for every $t\geq0$, which is Lyapunov stability.

The [LaSalle invariance principle](../../../dynamical-systems.md#lasalle-s-invariance-principle) says that if a trajectory remains in a compact [positively invariant set](../../../dynamical-systems.md#positively-invariant-set) on which $\dot V\leq0$, then it approaches the largest invariant subset of $\{x:\dot V(x)=0\}$.

On the two vertical sides of the square, the $x$ component of the [vector field](../../../calculus.md#vector-field) satisfies

$$
\dot x\big|_{x=1}=-1+\sin(2y)\leq0,
\qquad
\dot x\big|_{x=-1}=1+\sin(2y)\geq0.
$$

On the horizontal sides,

$$
\dot y\big|_{y=1}=-x-1\leq0,
\qquad
\dot y\big|_{y=-1}=1-x\geq0.
$$

Thus the vector field never points outward through the boundary, so the closed square is a [positively invariant set](../../../dynamical-systems.md#positively-invariant-set) and trajectories cannot leave $S$.

At a fixed point, $\dot y=0$ gives $x=-y^3$. Substitution into $\dot x=0$ gives

$$
2y^3-y^9+\sin(2y)=0.
$$

For $0<y<1$, both $y^3(2-y^6)$ and $\sin(2y)$ are positive; for $-1<y<0$, both are negative. Hence $y=0$, and then $x=0$, is the unique [equilibrium point](../../../dynamical-systems.md#equilibrium-point-of-a-dynamical-system) in $S$.

This alone does not prove that $S$ lies in the [domain of stability](../../../dynamical-systems.md#basin-of-attraction). For

$$

$$

the [orbital derivative](../../../dynamical-systems.md#orbital-derivative) is

$$
\dot V=x(-2x+x^3+\sin2y)+g'(y)(-x-y^3).

Choosing $g'(y)=\sin2y$ cancels the mixed term. Taking $g(0)=0$ gives

$$
g(y)=\int_0^y\sin(2s)\,ds
=\frac{1-\cos2y}{2}=\sin^2y,
$$

so

$$
\boxed{V(x,y)=\frac{x^2}{2}+\sin^2y}.
$$

This function has [positive definiteness](../../../dynamical-systems.md#positive-definiteness-of-a-lyapunov-function) near the origin, and

$$
\dot V
=-2x^2+x^4-y^3\sin2y
=-x^2(2-x^2)-y^3\sin2y.
$$

For $|x|<1$ and $|y|<1$, both terms are nonpositive and equality holds only at $(0,0)$. Thus $V$ is a [strict Lyapunov function](../../../dynamical-systems.md#strict-lyapunov-function) near the origin, and the [Second Lyapunov theorem](../../../dynamical-systems.md#second-lyapunov-theorem) proves that the origin is asymptotically stable.

\boxed{|x|<x_0=\sqrt2,
\qquad |y|<y_0=\frac\pi2}.

Neither side length can be enlarged while retaining a strict Lyapunov function on the whole rectangle: at $(x,0)$ with $|x|>\sqrt2$, the first term makes $\dot V>0$, while at $(0,y)$ immediately beyond $|y|=\pi/2$, the second term makes $\dot V>0$.

Strict decrease on this rectangle does not by itself put the whole rectangle in the [domain of stability](../../../dynamical-systems.md#basin-of-attraction), because the rectangle has not been shown to be [positively invariant](../../../dynamical-systems.md#positively-invariant-set); a trajectory may leave it before convergence.

On its boundary $|x|=\sqrt2$ or $|y|=\pi/2$, the minimum value of $V$ is $1$. Hence the maximal open estimate is

$$

\left\{(x,y): |y|<\frac\pi2,

Every smaller closed sublevel set $V\leq c<1$ is compact and positively invariant, and $\dot V<0$ there except at the origin. Taking the union over $c<1$ shows that $\mathcal D_V$ lies in the domain of stability. No larger sublevel component can be certified from this $V$ alone, because every level $c>1$ contains points $(0,y)$ just beyond $|y|=\pi/2$ where $\dot V>0$.

$$

In the coordinates $(x,u)$, the given action of the [special orthogonal group](../../../linear-algebra.md#special-orthogonal-group) is

$$
\widetilde x=x\cos s+u\sin s,
\qquad
\widetilde u=u\cos s-x\sin s.
$$

Differentiating at $s=0$ gives the [infinitesimal generator of a Lie point symmetry](../../../partial-differential-equation.md#infinitesimal-generator-of-a-lie-point-symmetry)

$$
V=u\partial_x-x\partial_u,
$$

so $\xi=u$ and $\eta=-x$. The recursive formula gives

$$
\eta^{(1)}=D_x(-x)-u_xD_xu=-1-u_x^2
$$

and

$$
\eta^{(2)}=D_x(-1-u_x^2)-u_{xx}D_xu
=-3u_xu_{xx}.
$$

Therefore the [second prolongation of the rotation generator for plane graphs](../../../differential-equation.md#second-prolongation-of-the-rotation-generator-for-plane-graphs) is

$$
\boxed{
\operatorname{pr}^{(2)}V
=u\partial_x-x\partial_u
-(1+u_x^2)\partial_{u_x}
-3u_xu_{xx}\partial_{u_{xx}}}.
$$

For $\Delta_0=u_{xx}$,

$$
\operatorname{pr}^{(2)}V(\Delta_0)=-3u_xu_{xx},
$$

which vanishes when $\Delta_0=0$. For

$$
\Delta_1=u_{xx}-(1+u_x^2)^{3/2},
$$

we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{pr}^{(2)}V(\Delta_1)
&=-3u_xu_{xx}
-3u_x(1+u_x^2)^{1/2}[-(1+u_x^2)]\\
&=-3u_x\Delta_1,
\end{aligned}
$$

which likewise vanishes on the equation. This is the infinitesimal invariance criterion for both equations.

Geometrically, $u_{xx}=0$ describes straight lines. The [signed curvature of a plane graph](../../../differential-geometry.md#signed-curvature-of-a-plane-graph) is

$$
\kappa=\frac{u_{xx}}{(1+u_x^2)^{3/2}},
$$

so the second equation says $\kappa=1$ and describes consistently oriented arcs of unit circles. [Rotation](../../../linear-algebra.md#rotation-matrix) preserves straight lines, circles, and signed curvature, explaining both invariances.

<h3 id="32c/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [32C](#32c)

<h4 id="32c/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#32c/b)

The transformations

$$
g^s:(X,T,u)\longmapsto(e^sX,e^{-s}T,u)
$$

form a [one-parameter group](../../../lie-theory.md#one-parameter-subgroup) with infinitesimal generator

$$
V=X\partial_X-T\partial_T.
$$

Under this scaling, $\partial_X$ has weight $-1$ and $\partial_T$ has weight $1$, so $u_{XT}$ has weight zero, as does $\sin u$. Thus $u_{XT}=\sin u$ is invariant and $g^s$ is a [Scaling symmetry of the sine-Gordon equation](../../../integrable-systems.md#scaling-symmetry-of-the-sine-gordon-equation).

The product

$$
z=XT
$$

is invariant under the group. A [group-invariant solution](../../../partial-differential-equation.md#group-invariant-solution) therefore has the form $u(X,T)=F(z)$. Since

$$
u_X=TF'(z),
\qquad
u_{XT}=F'(z)+zF''(z),
$$

the [Sine-Gordon equation](../../../integrable-systems.md#sine-gordon-equation) reduces to

$$
zF''+F'=\sin F.
$$

Now put $w=e^{iF}$. Then

$$
F'=-i\frac{w'}w,
\qquad
F''=-i\left(\frac{w''}w-\frac{(w')^2}{w^2}\right),
\qquad
\sin F=\frac{w-w^{-1}}{2i}.
$$

Substitution and multiplication by $iw$ give

$$
zw''-z\frac{(w')^2}{w}+w'
=\frac{w^2-1}{2}.
$$

Hence

$$
\boxed{
w''=\frac{(w')^2}{w}-\frac{w'}z+\frac{w^2-1}{2z}}.
$$

This is the [Painlevé III equation](../../../integrable-systems.md#painleve-iii-equation) with parameters $(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta)=(1/2,-1/2,0,0)$.

## 33A

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="33a/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [33A](#33a)

A [boson](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#boson) is an identical particle whose total [multiparticle quantum state](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#multiparticle-quantum-state) is symmetric under exchange of any two particles, whereas a [fermion](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#fermion) has a total state that is antisymmetric under every exchange. The [Spin-statistics theorem](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#spin-statistics-theorem) associates integer spin with bosons and half-integer spin with fermions.

For three distinguishable spin-one particles, the spin [Hilbert space](../../../hilbert-space.md) has dimension

$$
3^3=27.
$$

Let

$$
\mathbf S=\mathbf S_1+\mathbf S_2+\mathbf S_3
$$

be the [total angular momentum operator](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#total-angular-momentum-operator). Since each particle has

$$
\mathbf S_i^2=1(1+1)\hbar^2=2\hbar^2,
$$

we have

$$
\mathbf S^2=6\hbar^2
+2(\mathbf S_1\mathbin\cdot\mathbf S_2
+\mathbf S_2\mathbin\cdot\mathbf S_3
+\mathbf S_3\mathbin\cdot\mathbf S_1).
$$

The [Hamiltonian operator](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#hamiltonian-quantum-mechanics) is consequently

$$
H=\frac\lambda{\hbar^2}(\mathbf S^2-6\hbar^2).
$$

On the [total-spin sector](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#total-spin-sector) with quantum number $J$, its [energy eigenvalue](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#energy-eigenvalue) is

$$
E_J=\lambda[J(J+1)-6].
$$

The [Clebsch-Gordan decomposition](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#clebsch-gordan-decomposition) may be performed by first coupling particles $1$ and $2$. Their intermediate spin is $j_{12}=0,1,2$, and coupling the third spin gives

$$
\begin{array}{c|c}
j_{12}&J\\ \hline
0&1\\
1&0,1,2\\
2&1,2,3.
\end{array}
$$

Thus the [three spin-one angular-momentum decomposition](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#three-spin-one-angular-momentum-decomposition) contains total spin $J=0,1,2,3$ with multiplicities $1,3,2,1$. Multiplying each multiplicity by the multiplet dimension $2J+1$ gives

$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
J&E_J&\text{degeneracy}\\ \hline
0&-6\lambda&1\\
1&-4\lambda&9\\
2&0&10\\
3&6\lambda&7.
\end{array}
$$

The degeneracies sum to $27$, as required.

When the particles are indistinguishable, their integer spin makes them bosons. Their common spatial wavefunction is symmetric, so their spin wavefunction must also lie in the [symmetric three-spin-one subspace](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#symmetric-three-spin-one-subspace). Its decomposition is

$$
\operatorname{Sym}^3(1)=3\oplus1.
$$

Hence only the $J=3$ and $J=1$ levels remain, now with one copy of each multiplet:

$$
\boxed{
E=-4\lambda\text{ has degeneracy }3,
\qquad
E=6\lambda\text{ has degeneracy }7.}
$$

## 34C

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="34c/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [34C](#34c)

<h4 id="34c/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#34c/a)

For a real short-range [spherically symmetric potential](../../../classical-mechanics.md#spherically-symmetric-potential), the stationary [wavefunction](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#wave-function) must solve the [Schrödinger equation](../../../physics.md#schrodinger-equation), be regular at the origin whenever the potential is finite there, and satisfy the scattering boundary condition

$$
\psi(\mathbf r)\sim e^{ikz}+f(\theta)\frac{e^{ikr}}r
\qquad(r\to\infty).
$$

Here $e^{ikz}$ is the incident [plane wave](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#plane-wave), the second term is an outgoing spherical wave, and its coefficient $f(\theta)$ is the [scattering amplitude](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#scattering-amplitude).

Comparing the stated asymptotic expression with the [partial-wave expansion of a scattering amplitude](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#partial-wave-expansion-of-a-scattering-amplitude) gives

$$
f(\theta)=\frac1k\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}(2l+1)f_lP_l(\cos\theta),
$$

where $P_l$ is a [Legendre polynomial](../../../differential-equation.md#legendre-polynomial). The outgoing-to-incoming coefficient in channel $l$ is

$$
S_l=1+2if_l.
$$

[Partial-wave unitarity](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#partial-wave-unitarity) requires

$$
|S_l|=1,
$$

or equivalently

$$
\operatorname{Im}f_l=|f_l|^2.
$$

The real [scattering phase shift](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#scattering-phase-shift) $\delta_l$ is defined modulo $\pi$ by

$$
\boxed{S_l=e^{2i\delta_l},
\qquad
f_l=\frac{e^{2i\delta_l}-1}{2i}
=e^{i\delta_l}\sin\delta_l.}
$$

<h3 id="34c/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [34C](#34c)

<h4 id="34c/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#34c/b)

<h5 id="34c/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#34c/b/i)

For [S-wave scattering](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#s-wave-quantum-scattering), write the radial wavefunction as $R_0(r)=u(r)/r$. In the attractive shell $a<r\leq2a$, the hard-core boundary condition $u(a)=0$ gives

$$
u_{\mathrm{in}}(r)=A\sin[\kappa(r-a)],
\qquad
\kappa^2=k^2+\frac{2mV_0}{\hbar^2}.
$$

Outside the potential, choose

$$
u_{\mathrm{out}}(r)=B\sin(kr+\delta_0).
$$

Continuity of the logarithmic derivative at $r=2a$ yields

$$
\kappa\cot(\kappa a)
=k\cot(2ka+\delta_0).
$$

Put $C=\kappa\cot(\kappa a)$. The [tangent addition formula](../../../geometry-and-topology.md#tangent-addition-formula) gives

$$
\tan(2ka+\delta_0)=\frac{k}{C},
$$

and solving for $\tan\delta_0$ gives

$$
\tan\delta_0
=\frac{k-C\tan(2ka)}{C+k\tan(2ka)}
=\boxed{
\frac{k\cos(2ka)-\kappa\cot(\kappa a)\sin(2ka)}
{k\sin(2ka)+\kappa\cot(\kappa a)\cos(2ka)}}.
$$

<h4 id="34c/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#34c/b)

<h5 id="34c/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#34c/b/ii)

Let

$$
\kappa_0=\lim_{k\to0}\kappa
=\frac{\sqrt{2mV_0}}\hbar.
$$

Using $\sin(2ka)=2ka+O(k^3)$ and $\cos(2ka)=1+O(k^2)$ in the phase-shift formula gives

$$
\frac{\tan\delta_0}{k}
\longrightarrow
\frac1{\kappa_0\cot(\kappa_0a)}-2a
=\frac{\tan(\kappa_0a)}{\kappa_0}-2a.
$$

By the definition of the [scattering length from a partial-wave S-matrix](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#scattering-length-from-a-partial-wave-s-matrix),

$$
\boxed{
a_s=-\lim_{k\to0}\frac{\tan\delta_0}{k}
=2a-\frac{\tan(\kappa_0a)}{\kappa_0}}.
$$

Thus $a_s$ diverges exactly when

$$
\boxed{\kappa_0a=\left(n+\frac12\right)\pi,
\qquad n=0,1,2,\ldots}.
$$

At each such value, an S-wave [bound state](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#bound-state) lies at the continuum threshold: the exterior zero-energy radial solution becomes constant, its logarithmic derivative vanishes, and the system has a [zero-energy scattering resonance](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#zero-energy-scattering-resonance). As the well deepens through the critical value, a new bound state appears and the scattering length passes through infinity and changes sign.

## 35A

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="35a/i">i</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [35A](#35a)

<h4 id="35a/i/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#35a/i)

In a box of volume $V$, the [electromagnetic mode density in a box](../../../statistical-physics.md#electromagnetic-mode-density-in-a-box), including the two transverse [polarizations](../../../electromagnetism.md#polarization-of-an-electromagnetic-wave), is

$$
dN=2\frac{V}{(2\pi)^3}d^3k
=\frac{V}{\pi^2}k^2\,dk.
$$

Each [photon](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#photon) has energy $\hbar\omega=\hbar ck$, and the given [mean occupation number](../../../statistical-physics.md#planck-photon-distribution) is $(e^{\hbar ck/T}-1)^{-1}$. Omitting the temperature-independent [zero-point energy](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#zero-point-energy), the total energy is therefore

$$
E=\frac{V\hbar c}{\pi^2}
\int_0^\infty\frac{k^3}{e^{\hbar ck/T}-1}\,dk.
$$

With the [change of variables](../../../calculus.md#change-of-variables-formula) $x=\hbar ck/T$,

$$
E=\frac{VT^4}{\pi^2\hbar^3c^3}
\int_0^\infty\frac{x^3}{e^x-1}\,dx.
$$

<h3 id="35a/ii">ii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [35A](#35a)

<h4 id="35a/ii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#35a/ii)

Expanding $(e^x-1)^{-1}=\sum_{n\geq1}e^{-nx}$ and using the [Gamma integral](../../../complex-analysis.md#gamma-integral) gives

$$
\int_0^\infty\frac{x^3}{e^x-1}\,dx
=\sum_{n=1}^\infty\int_0^\infty x^3e^{-nx}\,dx
=6\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac1{n^4}
=\frac{\pi^4}{15}.
$$

Thus the energy of [blackbody radiation](../../../statistical-physics.md#black-body-radiation) is

$$
\boxed{E(T,V)=\frac{\pi^2}{15\hbar^3c^3}VT^4}.
$$

<h3 id="35a/iii">iii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [35A](#35a)

<h4 id="35a/iii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#35a/iii)

The [first law of thermodynamics](../../../thermodynamics.md#first-law-of-thermodynamics) is

$$
dE=T\,dS-P\,dV,
$$

so, regarding $S$ as a function of $(T,V)$,

$$
\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial T}\right)_V
=\frac1T\left(\frac{\partial E}{\partial T}\right)_V,
\qquad
\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial V}\right)_T
=\frac1T\left[
\left(\frac{\partial E}{\partial V}\right)_T+P
\right].
$$

Equality of mixed [partial derivatives](../../../calculus.md#partial-derivative) gives the [thermodynamic derivation of blackbody radiation pressure](../../../statistical-physics.md#thermodynamic-derivation-of-blackbody-radiation-pressure)

$$
\boxed{
\left(\frac{\partial E}{\partial V}\right)_T
=T\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_V-P}.
$$

Writing $a=\pi^2/(15\hbar^3c^3)$, we have $E=aVT^4$, and hence

$$
T\frac{dP}{dT}-P=aT^4.
$$

Equivalently, $d(P/T)/dT=aT^2$, so

$$
P=\frac a3T^4+CT.
$$

The condition $P=o(T)$ as $T\to0$ forces $C=0$. Therefore the [radiation pressure](../../../thermodynamics.md#radiation-pressure) is

$$
\boxed{P=\frac{\pi^2T^4}{45\hbar^3c^3}
=\frac{E}{3V}}.
$$

## 36D

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="36d/i">i</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [36D](#36d)

<h4 id="36d/i/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#36d/i)

Let $\boldsymbol\sigma$ be the stated [Maxwell stress tensor](../../../electromagnetism.md#maxwell-stress-tensor). Taking its divergence and using

$$
(\mathbf A\mathbin\cdot\nabla)\mathbf A
-\frac12\nabla|\mathbf A|^2
=-\mathbf A\times(\nabla\times\mathbf A)
$$

gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\partial_j\sigma_{ij}
={}&\epsilon_0[\mathbf E\times(\nabla\times\mathbf E)]_i
-\epsilon_0E_i\nabla\mathbin\cdot\mathbf E\\
&+\frac1{\mu_0}[\mathbf B\times(\nabla\times\mathbf B)]_i
-\frac1{\mu_0}B_i\nabla\mathbin\cdot\mathbf B.
\end{aligned}
$$

The [Maxwell equations](../../../electromagnetism.md#maxwell-equations) in vacuum with sources are

$$
\nabla\mathbin\cdot\mathbf E=\frac\rho{\epsilon_0},
\qquad
\nabla\mathbin\cdot\mathbf B=0,
\qquad
\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\partial_t\mathbf B,
\qquad
\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf J+\mu_0\epsilon_0\partial_t\mathbf E.
$$

Substitution, together with antisymmetry of the [cross product](../../../vector-space.md#cross-product), yields

$$
\begin{aligned}
\partial_j\sigma_{ij}
&=-\rho E_i-(\mathbf J\times\mathbf B)_i
-\epsilon_0[\mathbf E\times\partial_t\mathbf B
+\partial_t\mathbf E\times\mathbf B]_i\\
&=-[\rho\mathbf E+\mathbf J\times\mathbf B]_i
-\partial_t[\epsilon_0\mathbf E\times\mathbf B]_i.
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore the required vector is the [electromagnetic momentum density](../../../electromagnetism.md#electromagnetic-momentum-density)

$$
\boxed{\mathbf g=\epsilon_0\mathbf E\times\mathbf B
=\frac{\mathbf S}{c^2}},
$$

and

$$
\boxed{\sum_{j=1}^3\frac{\partial\sigma_{ij}}{\partial x_j}
+\frac{\partial g_i}{\partial t}
=-(\rho\mathbf E+\mathbf J\times\mathbf B)_i}.
$$

This is [local conservation of electromagnetic momentum](../../../electromagnetism.md#local-conservation-of-electromagnetic-momentum). The tensor component $\sigma_{ij}$ is the flux of $i$-momentum in the $j$ direction in the convention of the question, $\mathbf g$ is field momentum per unit volume, and $\rho\mathbf E+\mathbf J\times\mathbf B$ is the [Lorentz force density](../../../electromagnetism.md#lorentz-force-density) transferred to matter.

<h3 id="36d/ii">ii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [36D](#36d)

<h4 id="36d/ii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#36d/ii)

In the [radiation zone](../../../electromagnetism.md#radiation-zone), both fields are transverse to $\widehat{\mathbf x}$ and obey

$$
\mathbf E_0=-c\widehat{\mathbf x}\times\mathbf B_0,
\qquad
|\mathbf E_0|=c|\mathbf B_0|.
$$

The outward [Poynting vector](../../../electromagnetism.md#poynting-vector) is

$$
\mathbf S
=\frac1{\mu_0}\mathbf E\times\mathbf B
=\frac c{\mu_0}|\mathbf B_0|^2
\sin^2(\omega t-\mathbf k\mathbin\cdot\mathbf x)
\widehat{\mathbf x},
$$

so its [time average](../../../continuum-mechanics.md#eulerian-time-average) is

$$
\langle S_r\rangle=\frac c{2\mu_0}|\mathbf B_0|^2.
$$

An absorbing shell receives the incident momentum rather than reversing it. Hence [radiation pressure on a perfectly absorbing surface](../../../electromagnetism.md#radiation-pressure-on-a-perfectly-absorbing-surface) gives

$$
p(\widehat{\mathbf x})
=\frac{\langle S_r\rangle}{c}
=\frac{|\mathbf B_0|^2}{2\mu_0}.
$$

At $r=R$,

$$
|\mathbf B_0|^2
=\frac{\mu_0^2\omega^4}{16\pi^2R^2c^2}
|\widehat{\mathbf x}\times\mathbf p_0|^2,
$$

and therefore

$$
\boxed{
p(\widehat{\mathbf x})
=\frac{\mu_0\omega^4}{32\pi^2R^2c^2}
|\widehat{\mathbf x}\times\mathbf p_0|^2}.
$$

If $\vartheta$ is the angle between $\widehat{\mathbf x}$ and $\mathbf p_0$, this is

$$
p(\vartheta)=
\frac{\mu_0\omega^4|\mathbf p_0|^2}
{32\pi^2R^2c^2}\sin^2\vartheta.
$$

Its spherical average is $\mu_0\omega^4|\mathbf p_0|^2/(48\pi^2R^2c^2)$; the vector force integrates to zero by the inversion symmetry of the [electric dipole radiation](../../../electromagnetism.md#electric-dipole-radiation) pattern.

## 37D

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="37d/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [37D](#37d)

<h4 id="37d/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#37d/a)

In coordinates $x^\mu$, write the type-$(1,1)$ tensor as

$$
T=T^\mu{}_{\nu}\,
\partial_\mu\otimes dx^\nu.
$$

For a covector $\eta=\eta_\mu dx^\mu$ and vector $V=V^\nu\partial_\nu$,

$$
T(\eta,V)=T^\mu{}_{\nu}\eta_\mu V^\nu.
$$

Since this must equal $\eta(V)=\eta_\nu V^\nu$ for every $\eta$ and $V$, the components are

$$
\boxed{T^\mu{}_{\nu}=\delta^\mu{}_{\nu}}.
$$

Thus $T$ is the [identity tensor](../../../general-relativity.md#identity-tensor).

The [tensor component transformation law](../../../general-relativity.md#tensor-component-transformation-law) gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
T^{\mu'}{}_{\nu'}
&=\frac{\partial x^{\mu'}}{\partial x^\alpha}
\frac{\partial x^\beta}{\partial x^{\nu'}}
T^\alpha{}_{\beta}\\
&=\frac{\partial x^{\mu'}}{\partial x^\alpha}
\frac{\partial x^\alpha}{\partial x^{\nu'}}\\
&=\delta^{\mu'}{}_{\nu'}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The components are therefore the same [Kronecker delta](../../../linear-algebra.md#kronecker-delta) in every coordinate system.

<h3 id="37d/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [37D](#37d)

<h4 id="37d/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#37d/b)

<h5 id="37d/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#37d/b/i)

Put

$$
\rho=\sqrt{r^2+a^2},
\qquad
\varpi=\rho\sin\theta,
\qquad
z=r\cos\theta.
$$

Since $x^2+y^2=\varpi^2$, the Euclidean spatial line element is

$$
dx^2+dy^2+dz^2=d\varpi^2+\varpi^2d\phi^2+dz^2.
$$

Now

$$
d\varpi=\frac r\rho\sin\theta\,dr
+\rho\cos\theta\,d\theta,
\qquad
dz=\cos\theta\,dr-r\sin\theta\,d\theta.
$$

The cross terms cancel. With

$$
\Sigma=r^2+a^2\cos^2\theta,
\qquad
\Delta=r^2+a^2,
$$

we obtain the [oblate spheroidal coordinates](../../../general-relativity.md#oblate-spheroidal-coordinates) line element

$$
dx^2+dy^2+dz^2
=\frac\Sigma\Delta dr^2+\Sigma d\theta^2
+\Delta\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2.
$$

Adding the time coordinate gives

$$
\boxed{
ds^2=-dt^2
+\frac{r^2+a^2\cos^2\theta}{r^2+a^2}dr^2
+(r^2+a^2\cos^2\theta)d\theta^2
+(r^2+a^2)\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2}.
$$

<h4 id="37d/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#37d/b)

<h5 id="37d/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#37d/b/ii)

Set $u=t-r$ and $R=r$, so

$$
dt=du+dR,
\qquad dr=dR.
$$

Writing now $\Sigma=R^2+a^2\cos^2\theta$ and $\Delta=R^2+a^2$, the [Minkowski metric](../../../special-relativity.md#minkowski-metric) becomes

$$
ds^2=-du^2-2\,du\,dR
-\frac{a^2\sin^2\theta}{\Delta}dR^2
+\Sigma d\theta^2
+\Delta\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2.
$$

The squared norm of the coordinate vector is its $RR$ metric component:

$$
g(\partial_R,\partial_R)=g_{RR}
=-\frac{a^2\sin^2\theta}{R^2+a^2}.
$$

For $a>0$ this is nonzero away from the symmetry axis, so $\partial_R$ is not a [null vector](../../../special-relativity.md#null-vector).

<h4 id="37d/b/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#37d/b)

<h5 id="37d/b/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#37d/b/iii)

Let $\varphi=\phi-F(R)$, so

$$
d\phi=d\varphi+F'(R)dR.
$$

The new coefficient of $dR^2$ is

$$
-\frac{a^2\sin^2\theta}{\Delta}
+\Delta\sin^2\theta\,[F'(R)]^2.
$$

It vanishes if

$$
F'(R)=\frac a{R^2+a^2}.
$$

One choice that tends to zero as $R\to\infty$ and also has a regular $a\to0$ limit is

$$
\boxed{F(R)=-\arctan\frac aR}.
$$

Substitution gives the [outgoing null oblate spheroidal coordinates](../../../general-relativity.md#outgoing-null-oblate-spheroidal-coordinates) form

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
ds^2={}&-du^2-2\,du\,dR
+2a\sin^2\theta\,dR\,d\varphi\\
&+(R^2+a^2\cos^2\theta)d\theta^2
+(R^2+a^2)\sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2.
\end{aligned}}
$$

There is no $dR^2$ term, so $g_{RR}=0$ and $\partial_R$ is null for every $a\geq0$.

## 38B

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="38b/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [38B](#38b)

<h4 id="38b/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#38b/a)

Choose $x$ tangential to the solid boundary and locally along the outer inviscid streamlines, and let $y$ be the inward normal coordinate. Write $u$ and $v$ for the corresponding tangential and normal [velocity field](../../../fluid-mechanics.md#velocity-field) components, $U(x)$ for the outer tangential velocity, and $\nu$ for the [kinematic viscosity](../../../fluid-mechanics.md#kinematic-viscosity).

For a layer of streamwise scale $L$ and thickness $\delta\ll L$, [incompressible flow](../../../fluid-mechanics.md#incompressible-flow) gives

$$
u_x+v_y=0,
$$

so $u=O(U)$ and $v=O(U\delta/L)$. The normal [Navier-Stokes equation](../../../viscous-fluid-flow.md#navier-stokes-equation) then gives $p_y=0$ to leading order, while the outer [Euler equations](../../../fluid-mechanics.md#euler-equations-for-an-inviscid-fluid) give

$$
-\frac1\rho p_x=U\frac{dU}{dx}.
$$

In the tangential Navier-Stokes equation, streamwise viscous diffusion is smaller than normal diffusion by $O((\delta/L)^2)$. Retaining the leading inertial and normal-diffusion terms produces the [Prandtl boundary-layer equation](../../../viscous-fluid-flow.md#prandtl-boundary-layer-equation)

$$
\boxed{u u_x+v u_y=U\frac{dU}{dx}+\nu u_{yy}}.
$$

It is supplemented by $u=v=0$ at a stationary wall and $u\to U(x)$ on matching to the outer flow.

<h3 id="38b/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [38B](#38b)

<h4 id="38b/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#38b/b)

<h5 id="38b/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#38b/b/i)

Conservation of volume flux through a circular arc of radius $r$ gives

$$
\alpha r(-u_r)=\alpha Q,
$$

so the outer flow is the [sink flow in a sector](../../../fluid-mechanics.md#sink-flow-in-a-sector)

$$
\boxed{u_r=-\frac Qr,
\qquad u_\theta=0,
\qquad \psi=-Q\theta}.
$$

This is a [potential flow](../../../fluid-mechanics.md#potential-flow) and already satisfies the [no-penetration boundary condition](../../../viscous-fluid-flow.md#no-penetration-boundary-condition) on the radial walls. It has nonzero tangential speed $Q/r$ there, however, and therefore violates the [no-slip boundary condition](../../../viscous-fluid-flow.md#no-slip-boundary-condition). Thin vorticity-bearing [boundary layers](../../../continuum-mechanics.md#boundary-layer) are needed to reduce that speed to zero at each wall.

<h4 id="38b/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#38b/b)

<h5 id="38b/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#38b/b/ii)

Along $y=0$, put $x=r$ and $U(x)=-Q/x$. If $\delta(x)$ is the layer thickness, then

$$
u u_x=O\!\left(\frac{Q^2}{x^3}\right),
\qquad
\nu u_{yy}=O\!\left(\frac{\nu Q}{x\delta^2}\right).
$$

The [boundary-layer thickness from an advection-diffusion balance](../../../viscous-fluid-flow.md#boundary-layer-thickness-from-an-advection-diffusion-balance) therefore gives

$$
\frac{Q^2}{x^3}\sim\frac{\nu Q}{x\delta^2},
$$

and hence

$$
\boxed{\delta=x\sqrt{\frac\nu Q}}
$$

up to a constant factor absorbed into the similarity coordinate.

<h4 id="38b/b/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#38b/b)

<h5 id="38b/b/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#38b/b/iii)

Let

$$
\epsilon=\sqrt{\frac\nu Q},
\qquad
\eta=\frac{y}{\epsilon x},
\qquad
\psi=\sqrt{\nu Q}\,f(\eta)=Q\epsilon f(\eta).
$$

Using the [Cartesian streamfunction](../../../fluid-mechanics.md#cartesian-streamfunction) convention $u=\psi_y$, $v=-\psi_x$ gives

$$
u=\frac Qx f'(\eta),
\qquad
v=\frac{Q\epsilon}{x}\eta f'(\eta).
$$

At fixed $y$,

$$
u_x=-\frac Q{x^2}[f'+\eta f''],
\qquad
u_y=\frac Q{\epsilon x^2}f'',
\qquad
u_{yy}=\frac Q{\epsilon^2x^3}f'''.
$$

The terms proportional to $\eta f'f''$ cancel, leaving

$$
u u_x+v u_y=-\frac{Q^2}{x^3}(f')^2.
$$

Since $U=-Q/x$, $UU'=-Q^2/x^3$, while $\nu u_{yy}=Q^2f'''/x^3$. The boundary-layer equation therefore becomes

$$
\boxed{f'''=1-(f')^2}.
$$

Choosing the wall value of the streamfunction as zero, imposing no slip, and matching to $U=-Q/x$ give

$$
\boxed{f(0)=0,
\qquad f'(0)=0,
\qquad f'(\eta)\to-1\quad(\eta\to\infty)}.
$$

<h4 id="38b/b/iv">iv</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#38b/b)

<h5 id="38b/b/iv/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#38b/b/iv)

Set

$$
F(\eta)=f'(\eta)
=\frac{5-\cosh z}{1+\cosh z},
\qquad
z=\sqrt2\eta+c.
$$

Writing $C=\cosh z$, direct differentiation gives

$$
F''(\eta)=\frac{12(C-2)}{(1+C)^2}.
$$

On the other hand,

$$
1-F^2
=\frac{(1+C)^2-(5-C)^2}{(1+C)^2}
=\frac{12(C-2)}{(1+C)^2}.
$$

Thus $F''=1-F^2$, which is precisely $f'''=1-(f')^2$. The far-field condition is automatic because $F\to-1$. No slip requires

$$
0=F(0)=\frac{5-\cosh c}{1+\cosh c},
$$

so

$$
\boxed{c=\pm\operatorname{arcosh}5}.
$$

For $c=+\operatorname{arcosh}5$, $F$ decreases directly from $0$ to $-1$, matching flow everywhere toward the sink. For the negative root, $F$ initially becomes positive and creates a reverse-flow region next to the wall. The physically likely choice is therefore

$$
\boxed{c=+\operatorname{arcosh}5}.
$$

## 39B

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="39b/i">i</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [39B](#39b)

<h4 id="39b/i/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#39b/i)

Use the [Fourier transform](../../../analysis.md#fourier-transform) convention

$$
\widehat\eta(k,t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\eta(x,t)e^{-ikx}\,dx,
\qquad
\eta(x,t)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\widehat\eta(k,t)e^{ikx}\,dk.
$$

The [deep-water capillary-wave dispersion relation](../../../fluid-mechanics.md#deep-water-capillary-wave-dispersion-relation) makes each Fourier mode satisfy

$$
\partial_t^2\widehat\eta+S^2|k|^3\widehat\eta=0.
$$

The transformed initial data are

$$
\widehat\eta(k,0)=0,
\qquad
\partial_t\widehat\eta(k,0)
=-W\int_{-\epsilon}^{\epsilon}e^{-ikx}\,dx
=-\frac{2W\sin(k\epsilon)}k.
$$

Therefore

$$
\widehat\eta(k,t)
=-\frac{2W\sin(k\epsilon)}{kS|k|^{3/2}}
\sin(S|k|^{3/2}t).
$$

The integrand is even, so Fourier inversion gives the [surface response to a localized impulsive velocity](../../../fluid-mechanics.md#surface-response-to-a-localized-impulsive-velocity)

$$
\boxed{
\eta(x,t)=-\frac{2W}{\pi S}
\int_0^\infty
\frac{\sin(k\epsilon)\sin(Sk^{3/2}t)\cos(kx)}{k^{5/2}}\,dk}.
$$

<h3 id="39b/ii">ii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [39B](#39b)

<h4 id="39b/ii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#39b/ii)

Put $x=Vt$ with fixed $V>0$. The relevant [stationary point](../../../calculus-of-variations.md#stationary-point) will have $k=O(V^2/S^2)$, so the assumption $\epsilon V^2/S^2\ll1$ allows the long-wave approximation

$$
\sin(k\epsilon)\sim k\epsilon.
$$

Using $\sin A\cos B=[\sin(A+B)+\sin(A-B)]/2$, the phase $Sk^{3/2}+Vk$ has no stationary point for $k>0$. The other phase is

$$
\phi(k)=Sk^{3/2}-Vk,
$$

with

$$
\phi'(k_0)=0,
\qquad
k_0=\frac{4V^2}{9S^2},
$$

and

$$
\phi(k_0)=-\frac{4V^3}{27S^2},
\qquad
\phi''(k_0)=\frac{9S^2}{8V}>0.
$$

The stationary part of the integral is therefore

$$
-\frac{W\epsilon}{\pi S}
\int_0^\infty k^{-3/2}\sin[t\phi(k)]\,dk.
$$

By the [one-dimensional stationary-phase formula](../../../analysis.md#one-dimensional-stationary-phase-formula), this is asymptotic to

$$
-\frac{W\epsilon}{\pi S}k_0^{-3/2}
\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{t\phi''(k_0)}}
\sin\left(t\phi(k_0)+\frac\pi4\right).
$$

Since

$$
k_0^{-3/2}
\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{t\phi''(k_0)}}
=\frac{9S^2\sqrt\pi}{2V^{5/2}\sqrt t},
$$

we obtain

$$
\eta(x,t)\sim
\frac{9W\epsilon S}{2\sqrt\pi V^{5/2}\sqrt t}
\sin\left(\frac{4V^3t}{27S^2}-\frac\pi4\right).
$$

Using $V=x/t$, this has the requested form

$$
\boxed{
\eta(x,t)\sim\frac{W\epsilon S t^2}{x^{5/2}}
F\!\left(\frac{x^3}{S^2t^2}\right)},
$$

where

$$
\boxed{F(q)=\frac9{2\sqrt\pi}
\sin\left(\frac{4q}{27}-\frac\pi4\right)}.
$$

<h3 id="39b/iii">iii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [39B](#39b)

<h4 id="39b/iii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#39b/iii)

The stationary contribution travels with [group velocity](../../../wave-equation.md#group-velocity)

$$
V=\omega'(k_0)=\frac{3S}{2}\sqrt{k_0},
$$

so $k_0=4V^2/(9S^2)$. The condition

$$
\frac{\epsilon V^2}{S^2}\ll1
$$

is therefore equivalent, up to a constant factor, to $k_0\epsilon\ll1$: the dominant [wavelength](../../../wave-equation.md#wavelength) is much longer than the impacted interval. Its Fourier transform can then be replaced by

$$
-\frac{2W\sin(k\epsilon)}k\sim-2W\epsilon,
$$

which is the total initial vertical velocity integrated over space. The far field cannot resolve $W$ and $\epsilon$ separately and depends only on their product $W\epsilon$.

## 40E

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="40e/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [40E](#40e)

<h4 id="40e/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#40e/a)

A complex square matrix $A$ is a [normal matrix](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#normal-matrix) if it commutes with its [conjugate transpose](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#conjugate-transpose):

$$
AA^*=A^*A.
$$

By [unitary diagonalization of a normal matrix](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#unitary-diagonalization-of-a-normal-matrix), there are a [unitary matrix](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#unitary-matrix) $U$ and a diagonal matrix $\Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_N)$ such that

$$
A=U\Lambda U^*.
$$

The [matrix 2-norm](../../../continuous-dual-space.md#matrix-2-norm) is invariant under multiplication by unitary matrices, so

$$
\lVert A\rVert_2=\lVert\Lambda\rVert_2.
$$

For a diagonal matrix,

$$
\lVert\Lambda x\rVert_2^2
=\sum_j|\lambda_j|^2|x_j|^2
\leq\left(\max_j|\lambda_j|^2\right)\lVert x\rVert_2^2,
$$

with equality on a coordinate vector belonging to an eigenvalue of greatest modulus. Hence

$$
\boxed{\lVert A\rVert_2=\max_j|\lambda_j|=\rho(A)},
$$

the [matrix 2-norm of a normal matrix](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#matrix-2-norm-of-a-normal-matrix).

<h3 id="40e/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [40E](#40e)

<h4 id="40e/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#40e/b)

Let $D\in\mathbb R^{M\times M}$ be the [Toeplitz antisymmetric tridiagonal matrix](../../../linear-algebra.md#toeplitz-antisymmetric-tridiagonal-matrix) with $D_{m,m+1}=1$, $D_{m,m-1}=-1$, and zero diagonal. Fixed homogeneous endpoint values give the homogeneous update

$$
\left(I-\frac\mu4D\right)u^{n+1}
=\left(I+\frac\mu4D\right)u^n.
$$

The hint gives the eigenvalues

$$
d_j=2i\cos\frac{j\pi}{M+1},
\qquad 1\leq j\leq M.
$$

Because each $d_j$ is purely imaginary, $1-(\mu/4)d_j\ne0$ for every real $\mu$, so the left matrix is invertible. The two matrices are polynomials in $D$ and therefore have the same orthonormal eigenvectors. The amplification matrix

$$
Q=\left(I-\frac\mu4D\right)^{-1}
\left(I+\frac\mu4D\right)
$$

has eigenvalues

$$
q_j=
\frac{1+i(\mu/2)\cos(j\pi/(M+1))}
{1-i(\mu/2)\cos(j\pi/(M+1))}.
$$

The numerator and denominator are complex conjugates, so $|q_j|=1$. Moreover, the common orthonormal eigenbasis makes $Q$ a [normal matrix](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#normal-matrix). By the [matrix 2-norm of a normal matrix](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#matrix-2-norm-of-a-normal-matrix),

$$
\lVert Q^n\rVert_2=\rho(Q^n)=1
$$

for every $n$. Thus the [Crank-Nicolson centered-advection scheme on a finite interval](../../../finite-difference.md#crank-nicolson-centered-advection-scheme-on-a-finite-interval) is unconditionally stable:

$$
\boxed{\text{every }\mu>0.}
$$

<h3 id="40e/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [40E](#40e)

<h4 id="40e/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#40e/c)

Insert the Fourier mode

$$
u_m^n=\zeta^n e^{im\theta}
$$

into the [leapfrog advection scheme](../../../finite-difference.md#leapfrog-advection-scheme). This gives

$$
\zeta^{n+1}
=\mu(e^{i\theta}-e^{-i\theta})\zeta^n+\zeta^{n-1},
$$

and hence the amplification polynomial

$$
\zeta^2-2i\mu\sin\theta\,\zeta-1=0.
$$

Its roots are

$$
\zeta_\pm=i\mu\sin\theta
\pm\sqrt{1-\mu^2\sin^2\theta}.
$$

If $0<\mu<1$, the square root is real for every $\theta$, and

$$
|\zeta_\pm|^2
=\mu^2\sin^2\theta+1-\mu^2\sin^2\theta=1.
$$

If $\mu>1$, choosing $|\sin\theta|>1/\mu$ makes the roots non-real multiples of $i$ with reciprocal moduli, one of which exceeds one, so the method is unstable.

The endpoint $\mu=1$ requires care. At $\theta=\pi/2$ the polynomial is

$$
(\zeta-i)^2,
$$

so a generic mode has the form $(c_1+c_2n)i^n$ and grows linearly. It satisfies the weaker test $|\zeta_\pm|\leq1$ often quoted in elementary Fourier analysis, but it violates the [root condition for a multistep method](../../../numerical-analysis.md#root-condition-for-a-multistep-method) because the unit-modulus root is repeated. Therefore the rigorous uniform stability range for arbitrary two-level initial data is

$$
\boxed{0<\mu<1}.
$$

If “stable” is being used only for the non-amplification condition on root moduli, the conventionally quoted range is $0<\mu\leq1$, with $\mu=1$ a defective marginal endpoint rather than a uniformly stable case.
