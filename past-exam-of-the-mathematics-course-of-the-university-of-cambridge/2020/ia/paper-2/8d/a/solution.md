<h1 id="8d/a/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [A](../a.md)

A set is [countable](../../../../../../countable-set.md) when it is finite or admits a bijection with a subset of $\mathbb N$; equivalently, its elements can be listed in a sequence. The integers are countable, for example in the order $0,1,-1,2,-2,\ldots$. Enumerating pairs by diagonals, or applying the [Cantor pairing function](../../../../../../cantor-pairing-function.md), then shows that $\mathbb N\times\mathbb Z$ is countable.

Suppose instead that the [power set](../../../../../../power-set.md) $\mathcal P(\mathbb N)$ had an enumeration $A_1,A_2,\ldots$. The [Cantor diagonal argument](../../../../../../cantor-diagonal-argument.md) forms

$$
D=\{n\in\mathbb N:n\notin A_n\}.
$$

For every $n$, the set $D$ differs from $A_n$ at the element $n$, so it is absent from the alleged enumeration. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\mathcal P(\mathbb N)\text{ is uncountable}}.
$$
