<h1 id="2/i/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [I](../i.md)

For the usual [scalar field theory](../../../../../../scalar-field-theory-split.md) with a nonnegative potential $U$, shifted to zero in the vacuum, write its static energy in $d$ dimensions as

$$
E[\phi]=T+P,\qquad T=\frac12\int|\nabla\phi|^2d^dx,\qquad P=\int U(\phi)d^dx.
$$

Apply [Derrick scaling](../../../../../../derrick-scaling.md) $\phi_\lambda(x)=\phi(\lambda x)$. The gradient acquires a factor $\lambda$ and the volume a factor $\lambda^{-d}$, giving

$$
E(\lambda)=\lambda^{2-d}T+\lambda^{-d}P.
$$

A smooth finite-energy static solution is a stationary point of this energy, so its [Derrick virial identity](../../../../../../derrick-virial-identity.md) is

$$
\boxed{(d-2)T+dP=0.}
$$

For $d\geq3$, both terms are nonnegative, forcing $T=P=0$. The field is a constant vacuum: **there is no nontrivial finite-energy static soliton in this scalar-gradient-plus-nonnegative-potential model**. The assumptions matter: extra derivative terms, gauge fields, time dependence or a potential not bounded below by its vacuum value can change the argument.

For $d=2$, the relation becomes $P=0$ and says nothing further about $T$. A gradient-only energy is scale invariant, so [Derrick theorem](../../../../../../derrick-s-theorem.md) alone does not rule out nonconstant solutions; [sigma-model lumps](../../../../../../sigma-model-lump.md) are examples. For a scalar field with isolated potential minima, $P=0$ constrains the continuous field to one vacuum and hence makes it constant. If the vacuum values form a nontrivial manifold, the scaling relation alone leaves open a nonconstant map into it. In neither case does the two-dimensional scaling argument itself prove existence.
