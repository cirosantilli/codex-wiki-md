<h1 id="5/iv/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](../iv.md)

For the mild-to-moderate [transition intensity](../../../../../../transition-intensity.md), an increment $\Delta$ in a single [covariate](../../../../../../covariate.md), with the others held fixed, gives the multiplicative ratio $\exp(b_{12,k}\Delta)$. Of the four printed effects on this transition, sex and HAQ have $95\%$ [confidence intervals](../../../../../../confidence-interval.md) excluding zero on the log-intensity scale.

With male coded $1$ and female coded $0$, the estimated [hazard ratio](../../../../../../hazard-ratio.md) for males relative to females is

$$
\boxed{e^{-0.7695}=0.4632,\qquad95\%\text{ confidence interval }(e^{-1.328},e^{-0.2108})=(0.2650,0.8099).}
$$

Thus, conditional on currently being mildly fatigued and on the other [covariates](../../../../../../covariate.md), males have an estimated mild-to-moderate transition intensity about $54\%$ lower than females. The conclusion concerns this particular instantaneous transition, rather than a general $54\%$ reduction in fatigue probability.

For a one-point increase in HAQ, indicating greater physical disability, the estimated intensity multiplier is

$$
\boxed{e^{0.9913}=2.6947,\qquad95\%\text{ confidence interval }(e^{0.5232},e^{1.459})=(1.6874,4.3017).}
$$

Greater disability is therefore associated with a substantially faster mild-to-moderate transition, after adjustment for sex, arthritis duration and haemoglobin. Both conclusions use the specified [log-linear transition intensity model](../../../../../../log-linear-transition-intensity-model.md) and the nominal individual $95\%$ intervals. Arthritis duration and haemoglobin have intervals including zero for this transition, so their effects here are not statistically significant at $5\%$; this says neither that their effects are zero nor that they are insignificant for all other transitions.
