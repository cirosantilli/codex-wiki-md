<h1 id="1f/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [1F](../1f.md)

The [polynomial ring](../../../../../polynomial-ring.md) $F[X]$ is an [integral domain](../../../../../integral-domain.md), since the product of two nonzero [polynomials](../../../../../polynomial-split.md) has nonzero leading coefficient. Let $I$ be an [ideal](../../../../../ideal.md). If $I=0$, it is generated by zero. Otherwise choose a nonzero [polynomial](../../../../../polynomial-split.md) $g\in I$ of minimum degree. For any $f\in I$, the [polynomial](../../../../../polynomial-split.md) division algorithm over the field gives $f=qg+r$ with $r=0$ or $\deg r<\deg g$. Since $r=f-qg\in I$, minimality forces $r=0$. Hence every element of $I$ is a multiple of $g$, and $I=(g)$. Thus **$F[X]$ is a [principal ideal domain](../../../../../principal-ideal-domain.md)**.

The [nonprincipal coordinate ideal in two variables](../../../../../nonprincipal-coordinate-ideal-in-two-variables.md) provides the contrast:

$$
\boxed{(X,Y)\subset F[X,Y]\text{ is not principal}.}
$$

If it were $(g)$, then $g$ would divide $X$ and $Y$. Total degrees add when nonzero [polynomials](../../../../../polynomial-split.md) over a field are multiplied. A nonconstant divisor of $X$ consequently has degree one, its cofactor is constant, and it is a nonzero scalar multiple of $X$. Such a [polynomial](../../../../../polynomial-split.md) cannot divide $Y$, as setting $X=0$ shows. Thus $g$ would be a nonzero constant and the [ideal](../../../../../ideal.md) would contain one. But all elements of $(X,Y)$ vanish at $(0,0)$, so this is impossible.
