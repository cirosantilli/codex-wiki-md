<h1 id="1/e/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [E](../e.md)

All three normalized sums converge in probability to $\mu$: the [weak law of large numbers](../../../../../../weak-law-of-large-numbers.md) controls $S_L/L$, and the two fixed [random variables](../../../../../../random-variable-split.md) vanish after division by $L$. Their rare fluctuations nevertheless differ. A fixed [normal random variable](../../../../../../gaussian-random-variable.md) would have to fluctuate by order $L$ to alter the mean by a fixed amount, and that costs $\exp(-\text{constant}\,L^2)$. A fixed [exponential random variable](../../../../../../exponential-distribution.md) can do the same for a cost of order $\exp(-\text{constant}\,L)$, exactly the [large-deviation speed](../../../../../../large-deviation-speed.md) of the empirical mean.

Below $\mu+\lambda\sigma^2$, the optimal exponential contribution is zero, and the [Gaussian](../../../../../../normal-distribution.md) empirical mean supplies the fluctuation. Above it, the optimal empirical mean remains at $\mu+\lambda\sigma^2$, while $B/L$ supplies $x-\mu-\lambda\sigma^2$. This explains the quadratic-to-linear change in the [rate function](../../../../../../rate-function.md) and why only the upper tail is altered. **Convergence to the same typical value does not imply the same large-deviation behavior.**
