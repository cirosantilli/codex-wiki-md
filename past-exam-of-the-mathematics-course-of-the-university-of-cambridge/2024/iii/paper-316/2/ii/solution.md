<h1 id="2/ii/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](../ii.md)

The half-target mass assigned to equal fragments contains

$$
N_f=\frac{\tfrac12(\pi\rho D_t^3/6)}{\pi\rho D_f^3/6}
=\frac{D_t^3}{2D_f^3}
$$

fragments. Their total [geometric cross-section](../../../../../../geometric-collision-cross-section.md) is consequently

$$
\boxed{\sigma_{{\rm tot},2}
=N_f\frac{\pi D_f^2}{4}
=\frac{\pi}{8}\frac{D_t^3}{D_f}}.
$$

Ignoring the negligible single largest fragment in $\sigma_{{\rm tot},1}$,

$$
\boxed{
\frac{\sigma_{{\rm tot},2}}{\sigma_{{\rm tot},1}}
\simeq
\frac{\alpha_f-3}{4-\alpha_f}
\frac{D_{\min,f}^{\alpha_f-3}D_{\max,f}^{4-\alpha_f}}{D_f}}.
$$

For example, if $D_f=D_{\min,f}$, this ratio is $(\alpha_f-3)(D_{\max,f}/D_{\min,f})^{4-\alpha_f}/(4-\alpha_f)$ and is large for a broad size range. This reflects the inverse-size [cross-section per unit mass](../../../../../../cross-section-per-unit-mass.md) of equal-density spherical fragments: concentrating the mass at small $D_f$ creates more area.
