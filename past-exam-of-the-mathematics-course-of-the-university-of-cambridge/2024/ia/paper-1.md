## 1A

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="1a/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1A](#1a)

<h4 id="1a/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1a/a)

<h5 id="1a/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#1a/a/i)

The fixed-point equation is

$$
\frac{az+b}{z+1}=z
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
z^2+(1-a)z-b=0.
$$

A unique solution at $z=i$ means that this [polynomial](../../../polynomial.md) is

$$
(z-i)^2=z^2-2iz-1.
$$

Comparing coefficients gives

$$
\boxed{a=1+2i,\qquad b=1}.
$$

This is the repeated-root case of [prescribed fixed points of a Möbius transformation](../../../group-theory.md#prescribed-fixed-points-of-a-mobius-transformation).

<h4 id="1a/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1a/a)

<h5 id="1a/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#1a/a/ii)

For roots $3i$ and $1+i$, the fixed-point [polynomial](../../../polynomial.md) must be

$$
(z-3i)(z-1-i)
=z^2-(1+4i)z+(3-3i).
$$

Comparing this with $z^2+(1-a)z-b$ gives

$$
\boxed{a=2+4i,\qquad b=-3+3i}.
$$

<h3 id="1a/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1A](#1a)

<h4 id="1a/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1a/b)

Write $z=x+iy$. Rationalizing gives

$$
\frac{z-2}{z}
=\frac{x^2-2x+y^2+2iy}{x^2+y^2}.
$$

An argument of $\pi/4$ requires equal positive real and imaginary parts. Hence

$$
x^2-2x+y^2=2y,
\qquad y>0.
$$

Completing squares yields

$$
\boxed{(x-1)^2+(y-1)^2=2,\qquad y>0}.
$$

Thus the [constant-argument locus of a Möbius transformation](../../../group-theory.md#constant-argument-locus-of-a-mobius-transformation) is the part above the real axis of the circle with centre $(1,1)$ and radius $\sqrt2$. Its endpoints $z=0$ and $z=2$ are excluded because the quotient is respectively undefined and zero.

The $j$th column is

$$
c_j=v+aj\mathbf1,
\qquad
v=(1+b,2+b,3+b)^T.
$$

All three columns therefore lie in the two-dimensional span of $v$ and $\mathbf1$, so

$$
\boxed{\det A=0}.
$$

<h4 id="2c/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#2c/a)

<h5 id="2c/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#2c/a/ii)

For $x=(x_1,x_2,x_3)^T$,

$$
Ax=v(x_1+x_2+x_3)
+a\mathbf1(x_1+2x_2+3x_3).
$$

The [vectors](../../../vector-space.md#vector) $v$ and $\mathbf1$ are linearly independent, and $a\ne0$. Thus $Ax=0$ exactly when

$$
x_1+x_2+x_3=0,
\qquad
x_1+2x_2+3x_3=0.
$$

Solving gives

$$
\boxed{\ker A=\operatorname{span}\{(1,-2,1)^T\}},
\qquad
\boxed{\operatorname{nullity}A=1}.
$$

<h4 id="2c/a/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#2c/a)

<h5 id="2c/a/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#2c/a/iii)

Every column lies in $\operatorname{span}\{v,\mathbf1\}$. Conversely, differences of two columns are nonzero multiples of $\mathbf1$, while any one column together with $\mathbf1$ recovers $v$. Hence

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{im}A
=\operatorname{span}\{(1+b,2+b,3+b)^T,(1,1,1)^T\}},
$$

and

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{rank}A=2}.
$$

This is an instance of the [rank of a matrix with affine columns](../../../linear-algebra.md#rank-of-a-matrix-with-affine-columns).

<h4 id="2c/a/iv">iv</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#2c/a)

<h5 id="2c/a/iv/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#2c/a/iv)

The [rank-nullity theorem](../../../linear-algebra.md#rank-nullity-theorem) states that for a [linear map](../../../vector-space.md#linear-map) with finite-dimensional domain,

$$
\dim(\text{domain})
=\operatorname{rank}+\operatorname{nullity}.
$$

Here the domain is $\mathbb R^3$, and the preceding parts give

$$
3=2+1,
$$

which verifies the theorem.

<h3 id="2c/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2C](#2c)

<h4 id="2c/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2c/b)

The $j$th column of $B$ is

$$
c_j=v+aj\mathbf1,
\qquad
v=(1^2+b,2^2+b,\ldots,n^2+b)^T.
$$

Thus the image is contained in $\operatorname{span}\{v,\mathbf1\}$. Since $n>1$, $v$ is not constant; since $m>1$ and $a\ne0$, two columns have a nonzero constant difference. The two spanning [vectors](../../../vector-space.md#vector) therefore both lie in the image, so $\operatorname{rank}B=2$. By [rank-nullity theorem](../../../linear-algebra.md#rank-nullity-theorem),

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{nullity}B=m-2}.
$$

## 3F

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="3f/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3F](#3f)

<h4 id="3f/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3f/a)

Convergence of $\sum a_n$ implies $a_n\to0$. Hence $0<a_n\leq1$ for all sufficiently large $n$, and then $a_n^2\leq a_n$. The comparison test, with the finitely many initial terms treated separately, proves that

$$
\boxed{\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_n^2<\infty}.
$$

This is the [squares of a summable positive sequence](../../../real-analysis.md#squares-of-a-summable-positive-sequence) lemma.

<h3 id="3f/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3F](#3f)

<h4 id="3f/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3f/b)

For every $N$, the [Cauchy-Schwarz inequality](../../../probability-and-statistics.md#cauchy-schwarz-inequality) gives

$$
\sum_{n=1}^N\sqrt{a_nb_n}
\leq
\left(\sum_{n=1}^Na_n\right)^{1/2}
\left(\sum_{n=1}^Nb_n\right)^{1/2}.
$$

The right-hand side is bounded independently of $N$. Since the summands are positive, the partial sums increase to a finite [limit](../../../calculus.md#limit-of-a-function), proving the [geometric means of two summable positive sequences](../../../real-analysis.md#geometric-means-of-two-summable-positive-sequences) result.

<h3 id="3f/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3F](#3f)

<h4 id="3f/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#3f/c)

Another application of the [Cauchy-Schwarz inequality](../../../probability-and-statistics.md#cauchy-schwarz-inequality) gives

$$
\sum_{n=1}^N\sqrt{a_n}\,n^{-p}
\leq
\left(\sum_{n=1}^Na_n\right)^{1/2}
\left(\sum_{n=1}^Nn^{-2p}\right)^{1/2}.
$$

When $p>1/2$, the second factor is bounded because $2p>1$, so the [series](../../../real-analysis.md#series-mathematics) converges.

At the endpoint, take $a_1=1$ and, for $n\geq2$,

$$
a_n=\frac1{n(\log n)^2}.
$$

Then $\sum a_n$ converges, while

$$
\sqrt{a_n}\,n^{-1/2}=\frac1{n\log n},
$$

whose [series](../../../real-analysis.md#series-mathematics) diverges. This proves both parts of [weighted square roots of a summable sequence](../../../real-analysis.md#weighted-square-roots-of-a-summable-sequence).

## 4D

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="4d/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4D](#4d)

<h4 id="4d/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#4d/a)

Suppose the [series](../../../real-analysis.md#series-mathematics) converges at $w\ne0$. Then its terms $a_nw^n$ are bounded, say by $M$. Whenever $|z|<|w|$,

$$
|a_nz^n|
\leq M\left|\frac zw\right|^n,
$$

so comparison with a geometric [series](../../../real-analysis.md#series-mathematics) proves absolute convergence at $z$.

Let

$$
R=\sup\{|w|:\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_nw^n
\text{ converges}\},
$$

with the natural values $0$ or $\infty$ allowed. If $|z|<R$, choose a convergent point $w$ with $|z|<|w|\leq R$; the preceding argument gives convergence at $z$. If $|z|>R$, convergence at $z$ would contradict the definition of $R$. Hence the [series](../../../real-analysis.md#series-mathematics) converges for $|z|<R$ and diverges for $|z|>R$. No universal assertion is possible on $|z|=R$.

<h3 id="4d/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4D](#4d)

<h4 id="4d/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#4d/b)

By the [Cauchy-Hadamard theorem](../../../real-analysis.md#cauchy-hadamard-theorem), put

$$
L=\limsup_{n\to\infty}a_n^{1/n},
\qquad R=\frac1L.
$$

The squared coefficients have root limsup $L^2$, so their radius is $R^2$. Equal finite nonzero radii therefore require $R=R^2$, hence $R=1$. This can happen: for $a_n=n+1$, both $\sum a_nz^n$ and $\sum a_n^2z^n$ have radius one.

For the coefficients $a_n^{a_n}$, the relevant root terms are

$$
\left(a_n^{a_n}\right)^{1/n}
=\exp\left(\frac{a_n\log a_n}{n}\right).
$$

Equality can again occur. For example, take

$$
a_n=\log(n+2).
$$

Then $a_n\to\infty$, $a_n^{1/n}\to1$, and

$$
\frac{a_n\log a_n}{n}\to0.
$$

**Thus both $\sum a_nz^n$ and $\sum a_n^{a_n}z^n$ have radius one. These examples illustrate the [radius of convergence after powering coefficients](../../../real-analysis.md#radius-of-convergence-after-powering-coefficients).**

## 5A

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="5a/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [5A](#5a)

<h4 id="5a/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#5a/a)

<h5 id="5a/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#5a/a/i)

Both sides are antisymmetric in $j,k$ and in $p,q$. If either pair has equal indices, both sides vanish. It therefore suffices to take $j\ne k$ and $p\ne q$. The sum over $i$ is nonzero exactly when $(p,q)=(j,k)$ or $(k,j)$; it is respectively $1$ or $-1$. This is exactly

$$
\boxed{\epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon_{ipq}
=\delta_{jp}\delta_{kq}-\delta_{jq}\delta_{kp}},
$$

the [contraction of two Levi-Civita symbols](../../../calculus.md#contraction-of-two-levi-civita-symbols).

<h4 id="5a/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#5a/a)

<h5 id="5a/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#5a/a/ii)

Expanding a sum of squares gives the $n$-dimensional [Lagrange identity for the cross product](../../../calculus.md#lagrange-identity-for-the-cross-product):

$$
\frac12\sum_{i,j}(x_iy_j-x_jy_i)^2
=\sum_{i,j}x_i^2y_j^2-\sum_{i,j}x_ix_jy_iy_j
=|x|^2|y|^2-(x\cdot y)^2.
$$

The left-hand side is nonnegative, so

$$
\boxed{|x|^2|y|^2\geq|x\cdot y|^2}.
$$

<h4 id="5a/a/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#5a/a)

<h5 id="5a/a/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#5a/a/iii)

In components, part (i) gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
[a\times(b\times c)]_i
&=\epsilon_{ijk}a_j\epsilon_{klm}b_lc_m\\
&=(\delta_{il}\delta_{jm}-\delta_{im}\delta_{jl})
a_jb_lc_m\\
&=b_i(a\cdot c)-c_i(a\cdot b).
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore the [vector triple product](../../../calculus.md#vector-triple-product) is

$$
\boxed{a\times(b\times c)
=b(a\cdot c)-c(a\cdot b)}.
$$

<h3 id="5a/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [5A](#5a)

<h4 id="5a/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#5a/b)

Put

$$
c=y\cdot z,\qquad p=x\cdot y,\qquad q=x\cdot z.
$$

Since $x,y,z$ are unit [vectors](../../../vector-space.md#vector),

$$
S=2-p^2-q^2+p+q
=\frac52-\left(p-\frac12\right)^2
-\left(q-\frac12\right)^2.
$$

The [feasible inner products with two unit vectors](../../../calculus.md#feasible-inner-products-with-two-unit-vectors) satisfy

$$
\frac{p^2-2cpq+q^2}{1-c^2}\leq1.
$$

The desired pair $p=q=1/2$ is feasible exactly when $c\geq-1/2$. In that case every maximizer has these [inner products](../../../linear-algebra.md#inner-product). If $c<-1/2$, symmetry and strict convexity force $p=q$, and the nearest feasible diagonal point is

$$
p=q=\sqrt{\frac{1+c}{2}}.
$$

Using the [vector triple product](../../../calculus.md#vector-triple-product),

$$
x\times(y\times z)=y(x\cdot z)-z(x\cdot y)=qy-pz.
$$

Hence

$$
\boxed{
F(y,z)=
\begin{cases}
\dfrac12(y-z),&y\cdot z\geq-\dfrac12,\\[6pt]
\sqrt{\dfrac{1+y\cdot z}{2}}\,(y-z),
&y\cdot z<-\dfrac12.
\end{cases}}
$$

Rotations preserve dot products and cross-product lengths, while $z'=2z$. Thus

$$
S'=5-p^2-4q^2+p+2q
=\frac{11}{2}
-\left(p-\frac12\right)^2
-4\left(q-\frac14\right)^2.
$$

Let

$$
t=\begin{pmatrix}1/2\\1/4\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
K=\begin{pmatrix}1&c\\c&1\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
W=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&4\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The target pair is feasible precisely when

$$
t^TK^{-1}t=\frac{5-4c}{16(1-c^2)}\leq1,
$$

or

$$
\frac{1-3\sqrt5}{8}\leq c\leq
\frac{1+3\sqrt5}{8}.
$$

On this interval,

$$
\boxed{G(y,z)=\frac14y-\frac12z}.
$$

Outside this interval, let $\lambda>0$ be the unique number for which

$$
r=(W+\lambda K^{-1})^{-1}Wt,
\qquad
r^TK^{-1}r=1,
$$

and write $r=(p,q)^T$. These equations give the unique weighted projection of $t$ onto the feasible ellipse, and therefore determine the maximizing [inner products](../../../linear-algebra.md#inner-product) solely from $c=y\cdot z$. In the remaining cases,

$$
\boxed{G(y,z)=qy-pz}.
$$

Neither answer depends on $\theta_1$ or $\theta_2$.

## 6C

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="6c/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [6C](#6c)

<h4 id="6c/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#6c/a)

Since $P=P'=0$, it suffices to map the two edge [vectors](../../../vector-space.md#vector). Let the columns of the required [matrix](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) be $u_1,u_2,u_3$. The condition $MQ=Q'$ gives

$$
u_2=\left(-\frac12,0,\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)^T.
$$

The condition $MR=R'$ then gives

$$
\frac{\sqrt3}{2}u_1+\frac12u_2=R',
$$

and hence

$$
u_1=\left(\frac{\sqrt3}{2},0,\frac12\right)^T.
$$

These are orthonormal. Taking $u_3=u_1\times u_2=(0,-1,0)^T$ produces the orthogonal [matrix](../../../vector-space.md#matrix)

$$
\boxed{
M=
\begin{pmatrix}
\sqrt3/2&-1/2&0\\
0&0&-1\\
1/2&\sqrt3/2&0
\end{pmatrix}}.
$$

Its columns are orthonormal, and direct substitution verifies $MP=P'$, $MQ=Q'$, and $MR=R'$.

<h3 id="6c/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [6C](#6c)

<h4 id="6c/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#6c/b)

Write $a=(a_1,a_2,a_3,a_4)^T$. The [reflection in a hyperplane](../../../linear-algebra.md#reflection-in-a-hyperplane) orthogonal to $a$ sends

$$
x\longmapsto x-2\frac{a^Tx}{a^Ta}a,
$$

so

$$
\boxed{M=I_4-\frac{2aa^T}{a^Ta}}.
$$

It acts as $-1$ on $\operatorname{span}\{a\}$ and as $1$ on the three-dimensional hyperplane $a^\perp$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\det M=-1}.
$$

<h3 id="6c/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [6C](#6c)

<h4 id="6c/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#6c/c)

For $a=(a_1,a_2,a_3)^T$, the [cross-product matrix](../../../linear-algebra.md#cross-product-matrix) is

$$
\boxed{
A=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&-a_3&a_2\\
a_3&0&-a_1\\
-a_2&a_1&0
\end{pmatrix}}.
$$

Its diagonal is zero, so $\operatorname{tr}A=0$. Also $Aa=a\times a=0$, and $a\ne0$, so $A$ has a nontrivial kernel. Hence

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{tr}A=0,\qquad\det A=0}.
$$

<h3 id="6c/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [6C](#6c)

<h4 id="6c/d/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#6c/d)

<h5 id="6c/d/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#6c/d/i)

Expanding $\det A^{(n)}$ along its last row gives the last diagonal contribution $a_nd_{n-1}$. The only other nonzero entry is $c_{n-1}$; expanding its minor along the last column contributes

$$
-b_{n-1}c_{n-1}d_{n-2}.
$$

Thus the [tridiagonal determinant recurrence](../../../linear-algebra.md#tridiagonal-determinant-recurrence) is

$$
\boxed{d_n=a_nd_{n-1}-b_{n-1}c_{n-1}d_{n-2}},
$$

so

$$
\boxed{X_n=a_n,\qquad Y_n=-b_{n-1}c_{n-1}}.
$$

<h4 id="6c/d/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#6c/d)

<h5 id="6c/d/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#6c/d/ii)

The recurrence becomes

$$
d_n=-2d_{n-1}-d_{n-2},
\qquad d_0=1,\quad d_1=-2.
$$

Its characteristic [polynomial](../../../polynomial.md) is

$$
r^2+2r+1=(r+1)^2.
$$

Therefore $d_n=(\alpha+\beta n)(-1)^n$. The initial conditions give $\alpha=1$ and $\beta=1$, so

$$
\boxed{d_n=(-1)^n(n+1)}.
$$

## 7B

<h3 id="7b/a">a</h3>

<h4 id="7b/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#7b/a)

The [matrix trace](../../../linear-algebra.md#matrix-trace) is

$$
\operatorname{Tr}(A)=\sum_{i=1}^nA_{ii}.
$$

Using [matrix](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) multiplication and interchanging dummy indices,

$$
\operatorname{Tr}(AB)
=\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}B_{ji}
=\sum_{j,i}B_{ji}A_{ij}
=\operatorname{Tr}(BA).
$$

The proposed noncyclic interchange is false. With [matrix](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) units,

$$
A=E_{11},\qquad B=E_{12},\qquad C=E_{21},
$$

one has

$$
\operatorname{Tr}(ABC)=1,
\qquad
\operatorname{Tr}(ACB)=0.
$$

Trace is invariant under cyclic permutations, but an arbitrary swap need not be cyclic.

<h3 id="7b/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [7B](#7b)

<h4 id="7b/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#7b/b)

The [characteristic polynomial](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#characteristic-polynomial) of $A$ is

$$
\chi_A(t)=\det(tI-A).
$$

A [scalar](../../../vector-space.md#scalar) $\lambda\in\mathbb C$ is an [eigenvalue](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) when there is a nonzero [vector](../../../vector-space.md#vector) $v$ such that

$$
Av=\lambda v.
$$

Such a $v$ is an [eigenvector](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvector) belonging to $\lambda$. Equivalently, $\lambda$ is a root of $\chi_A$ and the corresponding [eigenvectors](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvector) are the nonzero elements of $\ker(A-\lambda I)$.

<h3 id="7b/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [7B](#7b)

<h4 id="7b/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#7b/c)

Consider the real [polynomial](../../../polynomial.md)

$$
p(t)=\det(A+tB).
$$

The hypothesis says $p(i)\ne0$, so $p$ is not identically zero. A nonzero [polynomial](../../../polynomial.md) has only finitely many roots, whereas $\mathbb R$ is infinite. Hence there is a real $\lambda$ with

$$
p(\lambda)=\det(A+\lambda B)\ne0.
$$

**Thus $A+\lambda B$ is invertible, as summarized by the [real parameter avoiding a singular matrix pencil](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#real-parameter-avoiding-a-singular-matrix-pencil).**

<h3 id="7b/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [7B](#7b)

<h4 id="7b/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#7b/d)

Write the complex similarity [matrix](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) as

$$
S=X+iY,
$$

with $X,Y$ real. From $C=SDS^{-1}$ we obtain $CS=SD$, and equating real and imaginary parts gives

$$
CX=XD,\qquad CY=YD.
$$

Therefore

$$
C(X+tY)=(X+tY)D
$$

for every real $t$.

Now $p(t)=\det(X+tY)$ is a real [polynomial](../../../polynomial.md) and

$$
p(i)=\det(X+iY)=\det S\ne0.
$$

Thus some real $t$ has $p(t)\ne0$. Taking $T=X+tY$, we have a real invertible [matrix](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) satisfying $CT=TD$, or

$$
\boxed{C=TDT^{-1}}.
$$

This proves [real similarity from complex similarity](../../../linear-algebra.md#real-similarity-from-complex-similarity).

<h3 id="7b/e">e</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [7B](#7b)

<h4 id="7b/e/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [E](#7b/e)

[Eigenvectors](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvector) belonging to distinct [eigenvalues](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) are linearly independent. Indeed, suppose

$$
\sum_{j=1}^m c_jv_j=0
$$

is a shortest nontrivial relation, where $Av_j=\lambda_jv_j$ and the $\lambda_j$ are distinct. Applying $A-\lambda_mI$ gives

$$
\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}c_j(\lambda_j-\lambda_m)v_j=0,
$$

a shorter nontrivial relation, a contradiction.

Thus $n$ distinct [eigenvalues](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) provide a [basis](../../../vector-space.md#basis) of $n$ [eigenvectors](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvector), proving that $A$ is diagonalizable over $\mathbb C$. If $A$ is real and all its [eigenvalues](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) are real, each real singular [matrix](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) $A-\lambda I$ has a nonzero real null [vector](../../../vector-space.md#vector). Choosing one for each [eigenvalue](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) gives a real eigenbasis. Hence the real [matrix](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) is diagonalizable over $\mathbb R$. This is the [distinct eigenvalues imply diagonalizability](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#distinct-eigenvalues-imply-diagonalizability) criterion.

$$

**Thus $\lambda$ is an [eigenvalue](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) and $v$ is a corresponding [eigenvector](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvector).**

The [adjoint matrix](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#conjugate-transpose) of $A$ is its conjugate transpose:

\boxed{A^\dagger=\overline A^T},
\qquad
(A^\dagger)_{ij}=\overline{A_{ji}}.

<h4 id="8b/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#8b/b)

<h5 id="8b/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#8b/b/ii)

A complex [matrix](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) $U$ is [unitary matrix](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#unitary-matrix) when

$$
U^\dagger U=UU^\dagger=I.
$$

If $Uv=\lambda v$ with $v\ne0$, unitarity preserves norms, so

$$
\|v\|=\|Uv\|=\|\lambda v\|=|\lambda|\,\|v\|.
$$

Therefore every [eigenvalue](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) satisfies

$$
\boxed{|\lambda|=1}.
$$

<h4 id="8b/b/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#8b/b)

<h5 id="8b/b/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#8b/b/iii)

Suppose $A$ is diagonalized by a unitary [matrix](../../../vector-space.md#matrix):

$$
A=UDU^\dagger,
$$

where $D$ is diagonal. Then

$$
A^\dagger=UD^\dagger U^\dagger.
$$

Since the diagonal [matrices](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) $D$ and $D^\dagger$ commute,

$$
AA^\dagger=UDD^\dagger U^\dagger
=UD^\dagger DU^\dagger=A^\dagger A.
$$

**Thus $A$ is a [normal matrix](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#normal-matrix).**

<h3 id="8b/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [8B](#8b)

<h4 id="8b/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#8b/c)

First note that if $A$ is normal, then

$$
\|(A-\lambda I)v\|
=\|(A^\dagger-\overline\lambda I)v\|
$$

for every $v$. Hence an [eigenvector](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvector) $v$ with $Av=\lambda v$ also satisfies

$$
A^\dagger v=\overline\lambda v.
$$

Normalize $v$ and extend it to an orthonormal [basis](../../../vector-space.md#basis). Let $U$ have these [basis](../../../vector-space.md#basis) [vectors](../../../vector-space.md#vector) as columns. The first column of $U^\dagger AU$ is $(\lambda,0,\ldots,0)^T$. For every $w\perp v$,

$$
\langle v,Aw\rangle
=\langle A^\dagger v,w\rangle
=\langle\overline\lambda v,w\rangle=0,
$$

so the first row also has no off-diagonal entries. Therefore

$$
U^\dagger AU=
\begin{pmatrix}
\lambda&0\\
0&B
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Comparing the two block products in the normality identity shows that $BB^\dagger=B^\dagger B$, so $B$ is normal.

The result is trivial in dimension one. Applying the induction hypothesis to $B$ and adjoining the [eigenvector](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvector) $v$ gives an orthonormal eigenbasis for $A$. This proves the [unitary diagonalization of a normal matrix](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#unitary-diagonalization-of-a-normal-matrix).

↑ **Parent:** [8B](#8b)

## 9F

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="9f/i">i</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [9F](#9f)

<h4 id="9f/i/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#9f/i)

The [ratio test](../../../real-analysis.md#ratio-test) says that if

$$
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\left|\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\right|=L<1,
$$

then $\sum a_n$ converges absolutely. If the ratio has a [limit](../../../calculus.md#limit-of-a-function) $L>1$, the [series](../../../real-analysis.md#series-mathematics) diverges.

For the first assertion, choose $q$ with $L<q<1$. For all sufficiently large $n$,

$$
|a_{n+1}|\leq q|a_n|.
$$

Iteration bounds the tail by a constant multiple of the convergent geometric [series](../../../real-analysis.md#series-mathematics) $\sum q^n$. If $L>1$, choose $q$ with $1<q<L$. Eventually $|a_{n+1}|\geq q|a_n|$, so $a_n$ cannot tend to zero; the [series](../../../real-analysis.md#series-mathematics) therefore diverges.

<h3 id="9f/ii">ii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [9F](#9f)

<h4 id="9f/ii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#9f/ii)

First suppose $L>0$. Taking logarithms,

$$
\log a_n
=\log a_1+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}
\log\left(\frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k}\right).
$$

Divide by $n$. Since the summands tend to $\log L$, their Cesàro means also tend to $\log L$. Hence

$$
\frac1n\log a_n\to\log L,
$$

and exponentiation gives $a_n^{1/n}\to L$.

If $L=0$, then for every $\varepsilon>0$ the ratios are eventually at most $\varepsilon$, so $a_n\leq C\varepsilon^n$ for a suitable constant $C$. Thus

$$
\limsup a_n^{1/n}\leq\varepsilon.
$$

Letting $\varepsilon\downarrow0$ proves the [ratio limit implies root limit](../../../real-analysis.md#ratio-limit-implies-root-limit) in all cases.

<h3 id="9f/iii">iii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [9F](#9f)

<h4 id="9f/iii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#9f/iii)

Set

$$
a_n=\frac{n!}{n^n}.
$$

Then

$$
\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}
=\frac{(n+1)!}{(n+1)^{n+1}}
\frac{n^n}{n!}
=\left(\frac n{n+1}\right)^n
\longrightarrow e^{-1}<1.
$$

The [ratio test](../../../real-analysis.md#ratio-test) therefore proves

$$
\boxed{\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{n!}{n^n}<\infty}.
$$

<h3 id="9f/iv">iv</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [9F](#9f)

<h4 id="9f/iv/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#9f/iv)

For the [sequence](../../../real-analysis.md#sequence) in part (iii), part (ii) gives

$$
\left(\frac{n!}{n^n}\right)^{1/n}\longrightarrow e^{-1}.
$$

Taking reciprocals yields the [factorial-over-power series](../../../real-analysis.md#factorial-over-power-series) [limit](../../../calculus.md#limit-of-a-function)

$$
\boxed{\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n}{(n!)^{1/n}}=e}.
$$

The [function](../../../function.md) $f$ is [differentiable](../../../analysis.md#differentiable-function) at $a$ when there is a real number $L$ such that

$$
\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)-Lh}{h}=0.
$$

Equivalently, the difference quotient has a finite [limit](../../../calculus.md#limit-of-a-function), and

$$
\boxed{f'(a)=L
=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}h}.
$$

<h3 id="10e/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [10E](#10e)

<h4 id="10e/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#10e/b)

<h5 id="10e/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#10e/b/i)

Write

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(a+h)g(a+h)-f(a)g(a)
={}&[f(a+h)-f(a)]g(a+h)\\
&+f(a)[g(a+h)-g(a)].
\end{aligned}
$$

Divide by $h$ and let $h\to0$. Differentiability implies continuity, so $g(a+h)\to g(a)$. Therefore the [product rule](../../../calculus.md#product-rule) gives

$$
\boxed{(fg)'(a)=f'(a)g(a)+f(a)g'(a)}.
$$

<h4 id="10e/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#10e/b)

<h5 id="10e/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#10e/b/ii)

**No.** Take $a=0$ and

$$
f(x)=x,\qquad g(x)=1+|x|.
$$

Then $f$ is [differentiable](../../../analysis.md#differentiable-function) and

$$
(fg)(x)=x+x|x|.
$$

Since $x|x|/x=|x|\to0$, the product is [differentiable](../../../analysis.md#differentiable-function) at zero with

$$
(fg)'(0)=1\ne0.
$$

However, $g$ has one-sided [derivatives](../../../calculus.md#derivative) $-1$ and $1$ and is not [differentiable](../../../analysis.md#differentiable-function) at zero. This is a [nondifferentiable factor with a differentiable nondegenerate product](../../../calculus.md#nondifferentiable-factor-with-a-differentiable-nondegenerate-product).

<h3 id="10e/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [10E](#10e)

<h4 id="10e/c/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#10e/c)

<h5 id="10e/c/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#10e/c/i)

Differentiability lets us write

$$
f(a+h)=f(a)+f'(a)h+r(h),
\qquad \frac{r(h)}h\to0,
$$

and, with $k\to0$,

$$
g(f(a)+k)=g(f(a))+g'(f(a))k+s(k),
\qquad \frac{s(k)}k\to0.
$$

Substitute $k=f(a+h)-f(a)=f'(a)h+r(h)$. Since $k=O(h)$, the remainder $s(k)$ is $o(h)$. Thus

$$
g(f(a+h))-g(f(a))
=g'(f(a))f'(a)h+o(h),
$$

and the [chain rule](../../../calculus.md#chain-rule) is

$$
\boxed{(g\circ f)'(a)=g'(f(a))f'(a)}.
$$

$$

so the composite has [derivative](../../../calculus.md#derivative) one at zero. But

$$

$$

is unbounded, so $f$ is not [differentiable](../../../analysis.md#differentiable-function). This is a [nondifferentiable inner function with a differentiable nondegenerate composite](../../../calculus.md#nondifferentiable-inner-function-with-a-differentiable-nondegenerate-composite).

<h4 id="10e/c/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#10e/c)

<h5 id="10e/c/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#10e/c/iii)

To say that $f$ is twice [differentiable](../../../analysis.md#differentiable-function) at $a$ means that $f'$ is defined in a neighbourhood of $a$ and is [differentiable](../../../analysis.md#differentiable-function) at $a$; its [derivative](../../../calculus.md#derivative) is $f''(a)$.

By the [chain rule](../../../calculus.md#chain-rule), near $a$,

$$
(g\circ f)'=(g'\circ f)f'.
$$

Since $f$ is [differentiable](../../../analysis.md#differentiable-function) and $g'$ is [differentiable](../../../analysis.md#differentiable-function) at $f(a)$, another use of the chain rule shows that $g'\circ f$ is [differentiable](../../../analysis.md#differentiable-function) at $a$. The [product rule](../../../calculus.md#product-rule) then proves that the displayed product is [differentiable](../../../analysis.md#differentiable-function). Hence $g\circ f$ is twice [differentiable](../../../analysis.md#differentiable-function), with the [second derivative chain rule](../../../calculus.md#second-derivative-chain-rule)

$$
\boxed{(g\circ f)''(a)
=g''(f(a))[f'(a)]^2+g'(f(a))f''(a)}.
$$

## 11E

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="11e/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [11E](#11e)

The [intermediate value theorem](../../../calculus.md#intermediate-value-theorem) states that if $f:[\alpha,\beta]\to\mathbb R$ is continuous and $y$ lies between $f(\alpha)$ and $f(\beta)$, then some $c\in[\alpha,\beta]$ satisfies $f(c)=y$.

It is enough to prove the case

$$
f(\alpha)<y<f(\beta).
$$

Let

$$
E=\{x\in[\alpha,\beta]:f(x)<y\},
\qquad c=\sup E.
$$

Choose $x_n\in E$ with $x_n\to c$. Continuity gives $f(c)\leq y$. If $f(c)<y$, continuity would make $f(x)<y$ for some $x>c$, contradicting that $c$ is an upper bound of $E$. Thus $f(c)=y$. The case with reversed endpoint inequalities follows by replacing $f$ with $-f$.

<h3 id="11e/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [11E](#11e)

<h4 id="11e/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#11e/a)

Choose $z_+,z_-\in\mathbb C$ with

$$
f(z_+)>0,\qquad f(z_-)<0.
$$

Restrict $f$ to the [line segment](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#line-segment) between them:

$$
h(t)=f\bigl((1-t)z_-+tz_+\bigr),
\qquad 0\leq t\leq1.
$$

This is continuous, with $h(0)<0<h(1)$. The [intermediate value theorem](../../../calculus.md#intermediate-value-theorem) gives a $t_0$ with $h(t_0)=0$. Therefore

$$
z=(1-t_0)z_-+t_0z_+
$$

is the required [zero on a line segment](../../../calculus.md#zero-on-a-line-segment).

<h3 id="11e/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [11E](#11e)

<h4 id="11e/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#11e/b)

Define the [continuous function](../../../calculus.md#continuous-function)

$$
h(x)=g(x+a)-g(x)-a,
\qquad 0\leq x\leq(n-1)a.
$$

At the mesh points its values telescope:

$$
\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}h(ka)
=g(na)-g(0)-na=0.
$$

If one term is zero, the result follows immediately. Otherwise the terms cannot all have the same sign, so there are mesh points at which $h$ is positive and negative. The [intermediate value theorem](../../../calculus.md#intermediate-value-theorem) between those points gives an $x\in[0,(n-1)a]$ with $h(x)=0$. Thus the [telescoping increment lemma](../../../calculus.md#telescoping-increment-lemma) yields

$$
\boxed{g(x+a)=g(x)+a}.
$$

U(f,P)=\sum_{j=1}^mM_j(x_j-x_{j-1}),
\qquad
L(f,P)=\sum_{j=1}^mm_j(x_j-x_{j-1}).
$$

The [upper and lower Darboux integrals](../../../real-analysis.md#upper-and-lower-darboux-integrals) are

$$
\overline{\int_0^1}f=\inf_PU(f,P),
\qquad
\underline{\int_0^1}f=\sup_PL(f,P).
$$

The [bounded function](../../../function.md#bounded-function) is Riemann integrable when these two numbers agree; their common value is its [integral](../../../calculus.md#integral).

<h4 id="12d/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#12d/a)

<h5 id="12d/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#12d/a/ii)

Let $f$ be continuous on $[0,1]$. Compactness makes it uniformly continuous. Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose $\delta>0$ such that

$$
|x-y|<\delta\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
|f(x)-f(y)|<\varepsilon.
$$

Choose a partition with mesh smaller than $\delta$. The oscillation $M_j-m_j$ on every subinterval is then at most $\varepsilon$, so

$$
U(f,P)-L(f,P)
=\sum_j(M_j-m_j)(x_j-x_{j-1})
\leq\varepsilon.
$$

The [Riemann integrability criterion](../../../real-analysis.md#riemann-integrability-criterion) proves that every [continuous function](../../../calculus.md#continuous-function) on $[0,1]$ is Riemann integrable.

<h3 id="12d/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [12D](#12d)

<h4 id="12d/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#12d/b)

<h5 id="12d/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#12d/b/i)

**No.** Define

$$
f(0)=0,\qquad f(x)=\frac1x\quad(0<x\leq1).
$$

It is continuous at every point of $(0,1)$ and is unbounded. Its truncation is Riemann integrable, but

$$
\int_0^1\min\left(\frac1x,r\right)\,dx
=\int_0^{1/r}r\,dx+\int_{1/r}^1\frac{dx}{x}
=1+\log r\longrightarrow\infty.
$$

**Thus it is not improperly integrable with a finite [integral](../../../calculus.md#integral).**

<h4 id="12d/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#12d/b)

<h5 id="12d/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#12d/b/ii)

**No.** Enumerate the rationals in $[0,1]$ as

$$
A=\{q_1,q_2,\ldots\},
$$

and define

$$
f(q_n)=n,\qquad f(x)=0\quad(x\notin A).
$$

The [function](../../../function.md) is nonnegative and unbounded, while it is identically zero on $[0,1]\setminus A$. For any $r>0$, however, $f_r$ is positive on the dense set of rationals and zero on the dense set of irrationals. Every interval therefore has positive oscillation, and the upper and lower Darboux [integrals](../../../calculus.md#integral) differ. Thus even the truncations need not be Riemann integrable.

<h4 id="12d/b/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#12d/b)

<h5 id="12d/b/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#12d/b/iii)

For $k\geq1$, let $E_k$ be the set of numbers whose first digit $5$ occurs in position $k$. The first $k-1$ digits each have nine allowed values, while the $k$th is fixed, so $E_k$ is a union of decimal intervals of total length

$$
|E_k|=\frac1{10}\left(\frac9{10}\right)^{k-1}.
$$

The set $E_\infty$ of numbers having no digit $5$ can, after $N$ places, be covered by intervals of total length $(9/10)^N$; hence it has length zero.

For each fixed truncation level, the [function](../../../function.md) is constant on finitely many decimal cylinders apart from the remaining set, whose covering length can be made arbitrarily small. The [Riemann integrability criterion](../../../real-analysis.md#riemann-integrability-criterion) therefore shows that every truncation is Riemann integrable.

For integer $N$,

$$
\int_0^1\min(f,N)\,dx
=\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}
\frac{k}{10}\left(\frac9{10}\right)^{k-1}
+N\left(\frac9{10}\right)^{N-1}.
$$

The final term tends to zero, and monotonicity handles noninteger truncation levels. Therefore the [first occurrence of a decimal digit](../../../real-analysis.md#first-occurrence-of-a-decimal-digit) calculation gives

$$
\int_0^1f(x)\,dx
=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}
\frac{k}{10}\left(\frac9{10}\right)^{k-1}
=\frac{1/10}{(1-9/10)^2}
=\boxed{10}.
$$
