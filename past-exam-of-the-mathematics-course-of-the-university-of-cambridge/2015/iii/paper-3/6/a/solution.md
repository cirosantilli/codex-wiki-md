<h1 id="6/a/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [A](../a.md)

A nonzero finite-dimensional [quiver representation](../../../../../../representation-of-a-quiver.md) is a [brick module](../../../../../../brick-module.md) when $\operatorname{End}_Q(X)=k\,1_X$. If $X=U\oplus V$ with both summands nonzero, projection onto $U$ is an [idempotent](../../../../../../idempotent.md) endomorphism different from zero and the identity. That is impossible in $k\,1_X$. Hence **every brick is indecomposable**.

For the requested converse counterexample, take the [dual-number algebra](../../../../../../dual-number.md) $A=k[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2)$ and its left regular module $A$. The algebra is local with maximal ideal $(\varepsilon)$, and it is not simple because this is a nonzero proper ideal. The module is indecomposable: its [endomorphism ring](../../../../../../endomorphism-ring.md) is $A^{\mathrm{op}}\cong A$, which has no nontrivial [idempotents](../../../../../../idempotent.md). But this ring has dimension two over $k$, so **the regular module is not a brick**. Thus indecomposable need not mean brick without the positive-definiteness hypothesis used below.
