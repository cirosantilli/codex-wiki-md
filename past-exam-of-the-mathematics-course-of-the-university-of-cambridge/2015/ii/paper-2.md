## 1H

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 2](paper-2.md)

<h3 id="1h/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1H](#1h)

The [Euler totient function](../../../number-theory.md#euler-totient-function) counts integers $1\leq a\leq n$ with $\gcd(a,n)=1$. The [Möbius function](../../../number-theory.md#mobius-function) has $\mu(1)=1$, is zero when a prime square divides $n$, and equals $(-1)^r$ when $n$ is a product of $r$ distinct primes. Consequently

$$
\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)=\begin{cases}1&n=1,\\0&n>1,\end{cases}
$$

because for $n>1$ the sum over squarefree divisors is $(1-1)^r$.

The [Möbius inversion formula](../../../number-theory.md#mobius-inversion-formula) is **$g(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)f(n/d)$**. Substituting the divisor sum defining $f$ and collecting the coefficient of $g(e)$ gives

$$
\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)f(n/d)=\sum_{e\mid n}g(e)\sum_{d\mid n/e}\mu(d)=g(n).
$$

This proof uses only finite sums, so no convergence hypothesis is needed.

Classifying $1,\ldots,n$ by their greatest common divisor with $n$ gives $\sum_{d\mid n}\varphi(d)=n$: the class with greatest common divisor $n/d$ contains exactly $\varphi(d)$ integers. Applying the [Möbius inversion formula](../../../number-theory.md#mobius-inversion-formula) yields

$$
\boxed{\varphi(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)\frac nd=n\prod_{p\mid n}(1-p^{-1}).}
$$

The [Riemann zeta function](../../../analytic-number-theory.md#riemann-zeta-function) is $\zeta(s)=\sum_{n\geq1}n^{-s}$ for $\operatorname{Re}s>1$. [Absolute convergence](../../../real-analysis.md#absolute-convergence) justifies multiplication of [Dirichlet series](../../../analytic-number-theory.md#dirichlet-series). The divisor identity for the [Möbius function](../../../number-theory.md#mobius-function) gives $\sum\mu(n)n^{-s}=1/\zeta(s)$ there. For $\operatorname{Re}s>2$ both factors below converge absolutely, and their [Dirichlet convolution](../../../number-theory.md#dirichlet-convolution) is the [Euler totient function](../../../number-theory.md#euler-totient-function):

$$
\boxed{\frac{\zeta(s-1)}{\zeta(s)}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\varphi(n)}{n^s}.}
$$

## 2I

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 2](paper-2.md)

<h3 id="2i/i">i</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2I](#2i)

<h4 id="2i/i/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#2i/i)

A degree-$n$ [Legendre polynomial](../../../differential-equation.md#legendre-polynomial) $P_n$ is orthogonal, for integration on $[-1,1]$, to every [polynomial](../../../polynomial.md) of degree at most $n-1$, and has $n$ distinct roots in $(-1,1)$. Divide $p$ by $P_n$:

$$
p=P_nq+r,\qquad \deg q\leq n-1,\quad\deg r\leq n-1.
$$

The [Orthogonality of Legendre polynomials](../../../differential-equation.md#orthogonality-of-legendre-polynomials) gives $\int p=\int r$. At every quadrature node $x_j$, $p(x_j)=r(x_j)$. The assumed exactness for $r$ therefore proves **exactness up to degree $2n-1$**, the characteristic property of [Gaussian quadrature](../../../numerical-analysis.md#gaussian-quadrature).

<h3 id="2i/ii">ii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2I](#2i)

<h4 id="2i/ii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#2i/ii)

Use the [Lagrange interpolation polynomial](../../../numerical-analysis.md#lagrange-polynomial)

$$
\ell_j(t)=\prod_{i\ne j}\frac{t-x_i}{x_j-x_i},\qquad \ell_j(x_i)=\delta_{ij}.
$$

Its square has degree $2n-2$, so the exactness already proved gives

$$
\boxed{A_j=\int_{-1}^1\ell_j(t)^2\,dt>0.}
$$

The [positive weights of Gaussian quadrature](../../../numerical-analysis.md#positive-weights-of-gaussian-quadrature) follow because $\ell_j$ is a nonzero continuous [polynomial](../../../polynomial.md). Thus these [Gaussian quadrature](../../../numerical-analysis.md#gaussian-quadrature) weights are in fact positive, rather than merely nonnegative.

<h3 id="2i/iii">iii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2I](#2i)

<h4 id="2i/iii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#2i/iii)

Apply the [Gaussian quadrature](../../../numerical-analysis.md#gaussian-quadrature) rule to the constant [polynomial](../../../polynomial.md) $1$. Exactness gives **$\sum_{j=1}^n A_j=2$**.

<h3 id="2i/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2I](#2i)

To prove [convergence of positive quadrature on continuous functions](../../../numerical-analysis.md#convergence-of-positive-quadrature-on-continuous-functions), for a continuous $f$, the [Weierstrass approximation theorem](../../../functional-analysis.md#weierstrass-approximation-theorem) supplies a [polynomial](../../../polynomial.md) $p$ with $\|f-p\|_\infty<\varepsilon$. For every sufficiently large $n$, the [Gaussian quadrature](../../../numerical-analysis.md#gaussian-quadrature) rule is exact for this fixed [polynomial](../../../polynomial.md). Positivity of its weights and their sum $2$ then give

$$
\left|Q_n(f)-\int_{-1}^1f\right|\leq\sum_jA_j|f(x_j)-p(x_j)|+\int_{-1}^1|f-p|\leq4\varepsilon.
$$

Since $\varepsilon$ is arbitrary, **$Q_n(f)\to\int_{-1}^1f$**. Notice that no differentiability of $f$ is needed; uniform [polynomial](../../../polynomial.md) approximation and positivity provide the whole argument.

## 3G

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 2](paper-2.md)

<h3 id="3g/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3G](#3g)

The [Shannon entropy](../../../information-theory.md#information-entropy), in bits, is

$$
H(A)=-\sum_ap_a\log_2p_a=\log_2 5-\frac15\approx\boxed{2.121928}.
$$

For a prescribed alphabet distribution and code alphabet, an [optimal code](../../../coding-theory.md#optimal-source-code) is a [uniquely decodable code](../../../coding-theory.md#decipherable-code) minimizing expected codeword length. [Huffman coding](../../../information-theory.md#huffman-coding) produces an optimal binary [prefix code](../../../coding-theory.md#prefix-code), and its minimum is also the minimum among binary [uniquely decodable codes](../../../coding-theory.md#decipherable-code).

Two optimal binary [prefix codes](../../../coding-theory.md#prefix-code), listing words in the order $a,b,c,d,e$, are

$$
(00,01,10,110,111),\qquad(0,10,110,1110,1111).
$$

Their length vectors are $(2,2,2,3,3)$ and $(1,2,3,4,4)$, and both have **expected length $2.2$ bits**. To verify optimality rather than just equal length, run [Huffman coding](../../../information-theory.md#huffman-coding). First combine $d,e$ into a node of weight $0.2$. Among the resulting three weight-$0.2$ nodes, combining $b,c$ and then combining the remaining weight-$0.2$ node with one weight-$0.4$ node gives the first length vector. Instead combine $c$ with the $d,e$ node, then combine that weight-$0.4$ node with $b$, and finally with $a$; this gives the second. Each step combines two smallest current weights, so both constructions are [Huffman coding](../../../information-theory.md#huffman-coding) trees. Ties therefore allow **different optimal length profiles**.

## 4J

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 2](paper-2.md)

<h3 id="4j/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4J](#4j)

Writing $y_i$ for the observed counts, independence of the [Poisson distributions](../../../discrete-probability-distribution.md#poisson-distribution) gives the [log-likelihood](../../../statistical-modelling.md#log-likelihood)

$$
\ell(\beta)=\sum_{i=1}^n\left\{y_i\beta x_i-e^{\beta x_i}-\log(y_i!)\right\}.
$$

Its [score function](../../../statistical-modelling.md#informant-function) and negative second derivative are

$$
U(\beta)=\sum_ix_i(y_i-e^{\beta x_i}),\qquad J(\beta)=\sum_ix_i^2e^{\beta x_i}.
$$

Unless all $x_i$ vanish, $J(\beta)>0$, so the [log-likelihood](../../../statistical-modelling.md#log-likelihood) is strictly concave. Use [Newton method](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#newton-s-method-in-optimization), starting from a finite $\beta_0$, with

$$
\boxed{\beta_{r+1}=\beta_r+\frac{\sum_ix_i(y_i-e^{\beta_rx_i})}{\sum_ix_i^2e^{\beta_rx_i}}.}
$$

Stop when the [score function](../../../statistical-modelling.md#informant-function) or the change in $\beta$ is small; a reduced step can ensure that the [log-likelihood](../../../statistical-modelling.md#log-likelihood) increases. Any finite zero of the [score function](../../../statistical-modelling.md#informant-function) is the unique [maximum-likelihood estimator](../../../statistical-modelling.md#maximum-likelihood-estimator).

Existence needs a qualification. If the nonzero $x_i$ include both signs, $U$ decreases from $+\infty$ to $-\infty$, so a finite root exists. If all nonzero $x_i$ are positive, a finite root exists exactly when $\sum_ix_iy_i>0$; otherwise the supremum is approached as $\beta\to-\infty$. The corresponding condition for all negative $x_i$ is $\sum_ix_iy_i<0$, with a supremum at $+\infty$ otherwise. When all $x_i=0$, the parameter is unidentifiable. These cases explain when the iterative algorithm actually has a finite estimate to find.

## 5E

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 2](paper-2.md)

<h3 id="5e/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [5E](#5e)

At a positive spatially homogeneous [equilibrium point](../../../dynamical-systems.md#equilibrium-point-of-a-dynamical-system), $v=u^2$ and $2+u-v=0$. Hence **$(u,v)=(2,4)$**. The reaction [Jacobian matrix](../../../calculus.md#jacobian-matrix) there is

$$
J=\begin{pmatrix}2&-2\\4a&-a\end{pmatrix},\qquad\operatorname{tr}J=2-a,\quad\det J=6a.
$$

The [two-dimensional Routh-Hurwitz stability criterion](../../../dynamical-systems.md#two-dimensional-routh-hurwitz-stability-criterion) gives **strict linear asymptotic stability for $a>2$**, and instability for $0<a<2$.

For a spatial mode with $s=k^2$, the reaction-diffusion [linearization](../../../algebra.md#linearization) is $J_s=J-\operatorname{diag}(s,ds)$. When $a>2$, its trace is negative for every $s\geq0$, while

$$
D(s)=\det J_s=ds^2+(a-2d)s+6a.
$$

A growing mode exists precisely when this quadratic is negative at some $s>0$. Its minimum must occur at positive $s$, and must be strictly negative, giving

$$
2d>a,\qquad(2d-a)^2>24ad.
$$

With $z=d/a$, the latter inequality becomes $4z^2-28z+1>0$. Selecting the root compatible with $2d>a$ yields the [Turing instability](../../../diffusion-equation.md#turing-instability) threshold

$$
\boxed{d>\left(\frac72+2\sqrt3\right)a.}
$$

The unstable band is

$$
\frac{2d-a-\sqrt{(2d-a)^2-24ad}}{2d}<k^2<\frac{2d-a+\sqrt{(2d-a)^2-24ad}}{2d}.
$$

On a bounded domain a boundary condition must permit a mode in this band. Equality at the threshold gives a neutral mode, not positive growth.

At $a=2$, the homogeneous [linearization](../../../algebra.md#linearization) has purely imaginary [eigenvalues](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue), so it is not strictly linearly asymptotically stable. If “stable” is read as nonlinear stability, the boundary deserves attention. Put $x=u-2$, $y=(v-4-x)/\sqrt3$, and $x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$. The exact equations give $\dot r=r^2F(\theta)$, $\dot\theta=\omega+rG(\theta)$, where $\omega=2\sqrt3$,

$$
F=-\frac{\cos^2\theta\sin\theta}{\sqrt3}+\cos\theta\sin^2\theta,\qquad G=\frac{2\cos^3\theta}{\sqrt3}+\cos^2\theta\sin\theta+\sqrt3\cos\theta\sin^2\theta.
$$

Expanding $dr/d\theta$ through cubic order, $\int_0^{2\pi}F=0$ cancels the quadratic displacement. The correction from the quadratic change in $r$ also integrates to zero, because it is proportional to $(\int F)^2$. Since $\int_0^{2\pi}FG=\pi/(2\sqrt3)$, the [Poincaré return map](../../../dynamical-systems.md#poincare-map) is

$$
r\longmapsto r-\frac{\pi}{24\sqrt3}r^3+O(r^4).
$$

This [cubic return-map stability of a weak focus](../../../dynamical-systems.md#cubic-return-map-stability-of-a-weak-focus) shows that $a=2$ is a weak attracting focus. The strict linear range used in the [Turing instability](../../../diffusion-equation.md#turing-instability) calculation is $a>2$; the positive homogeneous equilibrium is also nonlinearly asymptotically stable at the boundary $a=2$. For that boundary, $\operatorname{tr}J_s=-(1+d)s<0$ for every nonzero spatial mode. The same strict [Turing instability](../../../diffusion-equation.md#turing-instability) threshold and unstable band therefore also apply there, with the homogeneous mode understood through nonlinear stability.

## 6B

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 2](paper-2.md)

<h3 id="6b/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [6B](#6b)

[Analytic continuation](../../../complex-analysis.md#analytic-continuation) extends a [holomorphic function](../../../complex-analysis.md#holomorphic-function) by holomorphic elements agreeing on overlaps; along a path, a chain of overlapping neighbourhoods transports the initial element. The [identity theorem](../../../complex-analysis.md#identity-theorem) makes compatible continuation unique on connected overlaps, although different paths can lead to different branches.

Choose the principal [complex logarithm](../../../analysis.md#complex-logarithm) and the slit domain $D=\mathbb C\setminus[1,\infty)$. On $D$, the function

$$
h(z)=-\frac{\operatorname{Log}(1-z)}z
$$

is holomorphic, with the removable value $h(0)=1$. The domain $D$ is simply connected, so $h$ has a primitive and its integral from $0$ to $z$ is independent of the path in $D$. For $|z|<1$, the uniformly convergent [power series](../../../real-analysis.md#power-series) on compact subdiscs gives

$$
f(z)=\int_0^zh(u)\,du=\int_0^z\sum_{n\geq1}\frac{u^{n-1}}n\,du=\sum_{n\geq1}\frac{z^n}{n^2}.
$$

Therefore **$f$ is a single-valued [analytic continuation](../../../complex-analysis.md#analytic-continuation) of the [dilogarithm](../../../complex-analysis.md#dilogarithm) to $\mathbb C\setminus[1,\infty)$**. The apparent problem at $0$ is removable; $1$ is a genuine branch point, even though the function has a finite one-sided limit there, because its derivative has a logarithmic singularity. Other cuts from $1$ to infinity give corresponding branches.

## 7D

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 2](paper-2.md)

<h3 id="7d/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [7D](#7d)

<h4 id="7d/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#7d/a)

The two cyclic [Euler angles](../../../classical-mechanics.md#euler-angles) give conserved [canonical momenta](../../../classical-mechanics.md#canonical-momentum)

$$
L_3=p_\psi=I_3(\dot\psi+\dot\phi\cos\theta),\qquad L_z=p_\phi=I_1\dot\phi\sin^2\theta+L_3\cos\theta.
$$

Here $L_3$ is the body-axis [angular momentum](../../../classical-mechanics.md#angular-momentum) and $L_z$ the vertical [angular momentum](../../../classical-mechanics.md#angular-momentum). Thus **$\omega_3=L_3/I_3$ is constant**. Since the [Lagrangian](../../../calculus-of-variations.md#lagrangian) has no explicit time dependence, the [energy](../../../classical-mechanics.md#energy) is also conserved:

$$
E=\frac{I_1}2\dot\theta^2+\frac{I_1}2\dot\phi^2\sin^2\theta+\frac{L_3^2}{2I_3}+Mgl\cos\theta.
$$

These are the three independent conserved quantities furnished by the continuous symmetries of the [heavy symmetric top](../../../classical-mechanics.md#heavy-symmetric-top).

<h3 id="7d/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [7D](#7d)

<h4 id="7d/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#7d/b)

Eliminate the cyclic velocities using $\dot\phi=(L_z-L_3\cos\theta)/(I_1\sin^2\theta)$. The conserved [energy](../../../classical-mechanics.md#energy) becomes

$$
E-\frac{L_3^2}{2I_3}=\frac{I_1}2\dot\theta^2+V_{\rm eff}(\theta),\qquad\boxed{V_{\rm eff}=\frac{(L_z-L_3\cos\theta)^2}{2I_1\sin^2\theta}+Mgl\cos\theta.}
$$

Differentiating the [effective potential](../../../physics.md#effective-potential) gives

$$
-\frac{dV_{\rm eff}}{d\theta}=I_1\dot\phi^2\sin\theta\cos\theta-L_3\dot\phi\sin\theta+Mgl\sin\theta,
$$

which is also the $\theta$ [Euler-Lagrange equation](../../../analysis.md#euler-lagrange-equation). Hence **$I_1\ddot\theta=-V_{\rm eff}'(\theta)$**. Deriving it directly from the [Euler-Lagrange equation](../../../analysis.md#euler-lagrange-equation) ensures it also holds at turning points, where division by $\dot\theta$ in an [energy](../../../classical-mechanics.md#energy) argument would be invalid.

<h3 id="7d/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [7D](#7d)

<h4 id="7d/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#7d/c)

For nonvertical [uniform precession of a heavy symmetric top](../../../classical-mechanics.md#uniform-precession-of-a-heavy-symmetric-top), write $\dot\phi=\Omega$ and fix $\theta$. The $\theta$ [Euler-Lagrange equation](../../../analysis.md#euler-lagrange-equation) reduces to

$$
\boxed{I_1\cos\theta\,\Omega^2-I_3\omega_3\Omega+Mgl=0.}
$$

For $\cos\theta\ne0$ its solutions are

$$
\Omega=\frac{I_3\omega_3\pm\sqrt{I_3^2\omega_3^2-4I_1Mgl\cos\theta}}{2I_1\cos\theta}.
$$

For $0<\theta<\pi/2$, uniform [precession](../../../classical-mechanics.md#precession) therefore requires **$|\omega_3|\geq2\sqrt{I_1Mgl\cos\theta}/I_3$**. For $\pi/2<\theta<\pi$, the discriminant is positive for every [spin](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#spin), including zero. At $\theta=\pi/2$, the condition is $\Omega=Mgl/(I_3\omega_3)$, requiring nonzero [spin](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#spin). Once this condition holds, choose $L_z=I_1\Omega\sin^2\theta+L_3\cos\theta$ and $\dot\psi=\omega_3-\Omega\cos\theta$; both cyclic equations are then satisfied. The exactly vertical configurations are also solutions for arbitrary [spin](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#spin), but the azimuthal member of the [Euler angles](../../../classical-mechanics.md#euler-angles) is degenerate there, so their existence is separate from this nonvertical discriminant condition.

## 9I

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 2](paper-2.md)

<h3 id="9i/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [9I](#9i)

[Sperner's lemma](../../../topological-analysis.md#sperner-s-lemma) says that a triangulated triangle whose vertices are labelled $1,2,3$, with each outer vertex having its own label and each boundary edge using only its endpoint labels, contains a small triangle with all three labels. In fact the number of such triangles is odd. Count incidences of small triangles with edges labelled $1,2$. Interior edges contribute twice. On the outer $1,2$ edge, the number of changes between these labels is odd; the other two outer edges contribute zero. A small triangle contributes one incidence if it has all three labels, two if it uses both $1,2$ but not $3$, and zero otherwise. The total parity proves the assertion.

The resulting [no-retraction theorem](../../../topological-analysis.md#no-retraction-theorem) is: **there is no continuous [retraction](../../../topology.md#retraction) of a closed disc onto its boundary**. Work first with a closed triangle $P$. If $r:P\to\partial P$ fixes the boundary, colour each vertex of increasingly fine triangulations by an index of a largest barycentric coordinate of $r(v)$. The largest coordinate is at least $1/3$; on the boundary the colouring satisfies [Sperner's lemma](../../../topological-analysis.md#sperner-s-lemma). Choose a tricoloured small triangle in each triangulation. By [compactness](../../../topology.md#compact-space) and shrinking mesh, a subsequence of their three vertices converges to one point $x$. Continuity forces all three barycentric coordinates of $r(x)$ to be at least $1/3$, so $r(x)$ is the triangle's centre, contradicting $r(x)\in\partial P$. A [homeomorphism](../../../topology.md#homeomorphism) of a disc with $P$ proves the disc assertion.

[Brouwer fixed-point theorem](../../../topological-analysis.md#brouwer-fixed-point-theorem) states that every continuous self-map $F$ of a closed disc has a fixed point. If it had none, the ray from $F(x)$ through $x$ would meet the boundary beyond $x$, continuously in $x$. That intersection defines a [retraction](../../../topology.md#retraction), since a boundary point is already the outgoing intersection. This contradicts the [no-retraction theorem](../../../topological-analysis.md#no-retraction-theorem).

Finally fix $y\in\mathbb R^2$ and define $F_y(x)=y+x-g(x)$. The displacement bound implies $\|F_y(x)-y\|\leq K$, so $F_y$ maps the closed radius-$K$ disc centred at $y$ into itself. [Brouwer fixed-point theorem](../../../topological-analysis.md#brouwer-fixed-point-theorem) gives $F_y(x)=x$, which is exactly $g(x)=y$. Since $y$ was arbitrary, **$g$ is surjective**.

## 10G

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 2](paper-2.md)

<h3 id="10g/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [10G](#10g)

In [RSA](../../../algebra.md#rsa-cryptosystem), choose $N=pq$ with distinct large primes, an exponent $e$ coprime to $\varphi(N)$, and $d$ satisfying $ed\equiv1\pmod{\varphi(N)}$. Publish $(N,e)$ and keep $d$ secret. Encrypt a [residue](../../../analysis.md#residue) $m$ as $c=m^e\bmod N$ and decrypt by $c^d\bmod N$. [Fermat's little theorem](../../../number-theory.md#fermat-little-theorem) on each prime and the [Chinese remainder theorem](../../../mathematics.md#chinese-remainder-theorem) prove decryption, including messages not coprime to $N$.

A customer knows a nonzero multiple $M=ed-1$ of $\varphi(N)$. Write $M=2^st$ with $t$ odd. Choose a base $a$ at random. A nontrivial $\gcd(a,N)$ already factors $N$. Otherwise compute $a^t,a^{2t},\ldots,a^{2^st}$ modulo $N$. The last entry is $1$. If the first occurrence of $1$ is preceded by $z\not\equiv\pm1\pmod N$, then

$$
\boxed{\gcd(z-1,N)\text{ is a nontrivial factor of }N.}
$$

Indeed, the two prime components of this square root of $1$ must have opposite signs. This procedure succeeds for some bases: by the [Chinese remainder theorem](../../../mathematics.md#chinese-remainder-theorem), choose a base whose component modulo $p$ has order $2$ and whose component modulo $q$ has order $1$. Its odd $t$-th power is already a nontrivial square root of $1$. Trying bases therefore gives an explicit factoring algorithm, and repeated random choices give the usual efficient attack. More precisely, if the two components' orders have different powers of $2$, the procedure succeeds. For a uniform element of a cyclic group with $2$-part $2^r$, those order valuations have probabilities $2^{-r}$ for $0$ and $2^{j-1-r}$ for $1\leq j\leq r$. Independence of the two prime components shows that equal valuations have probability at most $1/2$. Thus a random unit succeeds with probability at least $1/2$. Once $N$ is factored, every customer's private exponent is computable. The exceptional $ed=1$ is an identity cipher and is already insecure.

For the outsider's [RSA common-modulus attack](../../../algebra.md#common-modulus-rsa-attack), find integers $a,b$ with $ae_1+be_2=1$ by [Bézout's identity](../../../algebra.md#bezout-identity). For a unit message,

$$
\boxed{m\equiv c_1^a c_2^b\pmod N,}
$$

using modular inverses for negative powers. If a ciphertext is a nonzero nonunit, its greatest common divisor with $N$ factors $N$ instead; zero ciphertext corresponds to the zero message for a squarefree modulus. **The common message is recoverable without a private key.** For a generic unit message this alone does not supply the factorization or all private keys, so it does not demonstrate a complete break for unrelated messages. The nonunit case does give that stronger break.

## 11B

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 2](paper-2.md)

<h3 id="11b/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [11B](#11b)

For $\operatorname{Re}s>1$, expand $(e^t-1)^{-1}=\sum_{n\geq1}e^{-nt}$. [Absolute convergence](../../../real-analysis.md#absolute-convergence) of the integrated series follows from the same expression with $\operatorname{Re}s$ in place of $s$. Integrating termwise and substituting $u=nt$ gives

$$
\int_0^\infty\frac{t^{s-1}}{e^t-1}\,dt=\sum_{n\geq1}n^{-s}\Gamma(s)=\Gamma(s)\zeta(s).
$$

This proves the [Mellin transform](../../../analysis.md#mellin-transform) representation of the [Riemann zeta function](../../../analytic-number-theory.md#riemann-zeta-function).

Take the [Hankel contour](../../../complex-analysis.md#hankel-contour) along the lower side of the negative axis towards zero, around zero counterclockwise, and back along the upper side. For $\operatorname{Re}s>1$ the small-circle contribution tends to zero. On the lower and upper rays, respectively, $\arg t=-\pi,+\pi$, so the two integrals combine to

$$
\int_H\frac{t^{s-1}}{e^{-t}-1}\,dt=\left(e^{-i\pi(s-1)}-e^{i\pi(s-1)}\right)\int_0^\infty\frac{x^{s-1}}{e^x-1}\,dx=2i\sin(\pi s)\Gamma(s)\zeta(s).
$$

The [gamma reflection formula](../../../complex-analysis.md#gamma-reflection-formula) now proves agreement of the two representations. At an integer $s=m\geq2$, the contour integral has a zero while $\Gamma(1-s)$ has a pole: **their product is interpreted by its finite limit, equal to $\zeta(m)$**, not by multiplying literal zero and infinity.

For nonpositive integers the integrand is single-valued, and the contour closes around zero. Since

$$
\frac1{e^{-t}-1}=-\frac1t-\frac12+O(t),
$$

the [residue](../../../analysis.md#residue) of $t^{-1}/(e^{-t}-1)$ is $-1/2$, giving **$\zeta(0)=-1/2$**. Also

$$
\frac1{e^{-t}-1}+\frac12=-\frac12\coth(t/2)
$$

is odd. Its Laurent series therefore has only odd powers. Multiplication by $t^{-2n-1}$ cannot produce a $t^{-1}$ term, and the subtracted constant cannot do so when $n\geq1$. Hence the contour integral vanishes and **$\zeta(-2n)=0$ for every positive integer $n$**.

The recursive definition of [ordinal addition](../../../set-theory.md#ordinal-addition) is $\alpha+0=\alpha$, $\alpha+(\beta+1)=(\alpha+\beta)+1$, and $\alpha+\lambda=\sup_{\beta<\lambda}(\alpha+\beta)$ for a nonzero [limit ordinal](../../../set-theory.md#limit-ordinal) $\lambda$. The synthetic definition is the order type of the ordered disjoint union of a copy of $\alpha$ followed by a copy of $\beta$.

For the synthetic operation, adding the empty order does nothing, adding a successor appends one final point, and concatenation with a limit order is the increasing union of the initial concatenations with all its proper initial segments. Their order types have supremum $\sup_{\beta<\lambda}(\alpha+\beta)$. Thus the synthetic operation satisfies the recursive equations. [Transfinite induction](../../../set-theory.md#transfinite-induction) gives uniqueness of the recursively defined operation, proving equivalence.

The recursive definitions of [ordinal multiplication](../../../set-theory.md#ordinal-multiplication) and [ordinal exponentiation](../../../set-theory.md#ordinal-exponentiation) are

$$
\alpha\cdot0=0,\quad\alpha(\beta+1)=\alpha\beta+\alpha,\quad\alpha\lambda=\sup_{\beta<\lambda}\alpha\beta;
$$



$$
\alpha^0=1,\quad\alpha^{\beta+1}=\alpha^\beta\alpha,\quad\alpha^\lambda=\sup_{\beta<\lambda}\alpha^\beta.
$$

The last equation in each line is for nonzero limit $\lambda$. In particular the convention $0^0=1$ belongs to this recursive definition.

The [graph Ramsey number](../../../graph-theory.md#graph-ramsey-number) $R(s,t)$ is the least $N$ for which every [graph](../../../graph.md) on $N$ vertices has a [clique](../../../graph-theory.md#clique-graph-theory) of size $s$ or an [independent set](../../../graph-theory.md#independent-set-graph-theory) of size $t$; $R(s)=R(s,s)$ is the [diagonal Ramsey number](../../../graph-theory.md#diagonal-ramsey-number). The base cases are $R(2,t)=t$ and $R(s,2)=s$.

Assume the smaller parameters already have finite [graph Ramsey numbers](../../../graph-theory.md#graph-ramsey-number), and take $N=R(s-1,t)+R(s,t-1)$. For any vertex, either its neighbourhood has at least $R(s-1,t)$ vertices, or its nonneighbourhood has at least $R(s,t-1)$ vertices. In the first case it contains an independent $t$-set, or an $(s-1)$-clique which extends with the chosen vertex. In the second it contains an $s$-clique, or a $(t-1)$-independent set which extends with that vertex. Induction proves existence and

$$
\boxed{R(s,t)\leq R(s-1,t)+R(s,t-1).}
$$

<h3 id="14i/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [14I](#14i)

<h4 id="14i/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#14i/b)

Induction with Pascal's identity gives the [binomial upper bound for a Ramsey number](../../../graph-theory.md#binomial-upper-bound-for-a-ramsey-number) $R(s,t)\leq\binom{s+t-2}{s-1}$. Thus $R(s)\leq\binom{2s-2}{s-1}\leq4^{s-1}$, proving **$R(s)=O(4^s)$**.

For the lower bound, take a [binomial random graph](../../../graph-theory.md#binomial-random-graph) $G(n,1/2)$. The expected total number of $s$-cliques and independent $s$-sets is

$$
2\binom ns2^{-\binom s2}\leq2\frac{n^s}{s!}2^{-s(s-1)/2}.
$$

For $n=\lfloor2^{s/2}/2\rfloor$, this is at most $2^{1-s/2}/s!<1$ for all sufficiently large $s$. Some [graph](../../../graph.md) therefore has neither forbidden set, so $R(s)>n$. This proves **$R(s)=\Omega(2^{s/2})$** by the [probabilistic method](../../../probabilistic-combinatorics.md#probabilistic-method).

For the [triangle-free Ramsey lower bound by alteration](../../../graph-theory.md#triangle-free-ramsey-lower-bound-by-alteration), a [binomial random graph](../../../graph-theory.md#binomial-random-graph) $G(n,p)$ has expected triangle count $\binom n3p^3$ and expected independent $t$-set count $\binom nt(1-p)^{\binom t2}$. ## 15F

<h4 id="15f/i/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#15f/i)

The [symmetric power](../../../linear-algebra.md#symmetric-power) $S^nV$ is the quotient of $V^{\otimes n}$ identifying tensors which differ by permutation of the factors. The [exterior power](../../../linear-algebra.md#exterior-power) $\Lambda^nV$ is the alternating quotient, with a tensor having repeated equal factors set to zero. Both quotients inherit the given group action. For a [basis](../../../vector-space.md#basis) $e_1,\ldots,e_d$, bases consist respectively of monomials $e_1^{a_1}\cdots e_d^{a_d}$ with $\sum a_i=n$, and wedge products $e_{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge e_{i_n}$ with $i_1<\cdots<i_n$. Thus

$$
\boxed{\dim S^nV=\binom{d+n-1}{n},\qquad\dim\Lambda^nV=\binom dn,}
$$

with the second dimension zero for $n>d$.

A finite-order group element is diagonalizable over $\mathbb C$, because its minimal [polynomial](../../../polynomial.md) divides $X^m-1$, which has no repeated roots. Choose an [eigenbasis](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenbasis) with [eigenvalues](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_d$. The induced [eigenvalues](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue), including multiplicities, are

$$
\boxed{\lambda_1^{a_1}\cdots\lambda_d^{a_d}\ (a_i\geq0,\ \sum a_i=n)\text{ on }S^nV;\quad\lambda_{i_1}\cdots\lambda_{i_n}\ (i_1<\cdots<i_n)\text{ on }\Lambda^nV.}
$$

The zeroth powers are the one-dimensional trivial representation, with the empty product equal to $1$.

<h3 id="15f/ii">ii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [15F](#15f)

<h4 id="15f/ii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#15f/ii)

The [character of a representation](../../../representation-theory.md#character-of-a-representation) is its trace. The [exterior power](../../../linear-algebra.md#exterior-power) character is the elementary symmetric [polynomial](../../../polynomial.md) of degree $n$ in the [eigenvalues](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue), so the specified sign convention $Q(g,X)=\det(g-XI)$ gives

$$
\boxed{Q(g,X)=\sum_{n=0}^d(-1)^{d-n}\chi_{\Lambda^nV}(g)X^{d-n}.}
$$

To obtain the [character generating series of symmetric powers](../../../linear-algebra.md#character-generating-series-of-symmetric-powers), sum the monomial [eigenvalues](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) over all degrees. As an identity of [formal power series](../../../commutative-algebra.md#formal-power-series),

$$
\boxed{\sum_{n\geq0}\chi_{S^nV}(g)t^n=\prod_{j=1}^d(1-\lambda_jt)^{-1}=\frac1{(-t)^dQ(g,t^{-1})}.}
$$

The apparent negative powers cancel in the denominator, which has constant term $1$. Equivalently, this series is the reciprocal of $\sum_{j=0}^d(-1)^j\chi_{\Lambda^jV}(g)t^j$. Comparing coefficients gives $\sum_{j=0}^{\min(d,n)}(-1)^j\chi_{\Lambda^jV}(g)\chi_{S^{n-j}V}(g)=0$ for $n>0$, an explicit recurrence computing the symmetric-power characters from $Q$.

Use the customary squarefree convention for the quadratic radicand $d$. For $d\equiv2,3\pmod4$, the [ring of integers of a number field](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#ring-of-integers) is $\mathbb Z[\sqrt d]$, so

$$

The ideal $(p)$ is prime exactly when this quotient is an [integral domain](../../../commutative-algebra.md#integral-domain). A [polynomial](../../../polynomial.md) quotient of a field is an [integral domain](../../../commutative-algebra.md#integral-domain) exactly when the defining [polynomial](../../../polynomial.md) is irreducible. This proves the assertion, including the possibility of ramified primes where the reduction has a repeated root.

The prime $3$ is inert in $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)$ because $X^2-2$ is irreducible over $\mathbb F_3$, although $X^2-18=X^2$ is reducible. Thus **the result requires a squarefree quadratic radicand**.

<h3 id="16h/ii">ii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [16H](#16h)

<h4 id="16h/ii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#16h/ii)

Put $s=\sqrt{-14}$. Then $\mathcal O_K=\mathbb Z[s]$ and the [field discriminant](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#field-discriminant) is $-56$. Reduction of $X^2+14$ modulo $2$ and $3$ gives

$$
\boxed{(2)=\mathfrak p_2^2,\ \mathfrak p_2=(2,s);\qquad(3)=\mathfrak p_3\overline{\mathfrak p}_3,\ \mathfrak p_3=(3,s-1),\ \overline{\mathfrak p}_3=(3,s+1).}
$$

Their [ideal norms](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#ideal-norm) are $2,3,3$, respectively. The [Minkowski bound for ideal classes](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#minkowski-s-bound) is $(2/\pi)\sqrt{56}<5$, so every ideal class has an integral representative of [norm](../../../functional-analysis.md#norm) at most $4$. The only possible representatives are the unit ideal, $\mathfrak p_2$, $\mathfrak p_3$, $\overline{\mathfrak p}_3$, and the norm-$4$ ideal $\mathfrak p_2^2=(2)$. Thus there are at most four classes.

The class of $\mathfrak p_2$ is nontrivial, since a [principal ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal) of [norm](../../../functional-analysis.md#norm) $2$ would require $x^2+14y^2=2$. Also $\mathfrak p_3^2$ is not principal: [norm](../../../functional-analysis.md#norm) $9$ forces $x+ys=\pm3$, whose ideal is $\mathfrak p_3\overline{\mathfrak p}_3$, not $\mathfrak p_3^2$.

Now $N(2+s)=18$. It is divisible by $\mathfrak p_2$ and $\mathfrak p_3$, but not by $\overline{\mathfrak p}_3$, as substitution of $s=-1$ modulo $3$ gives $1$. Unique ideal factorization and [norms](../../../functional-analysis.md#norm) therefore give $(2+s)=\mathfrak p_2\mathfrak p_3^2$. Consequently $[\mathfrak p_3]^2=[\mathfrak p_2]^{-1}=[\mathfrak p_2]$, a nontrivial class of order $2$. The class of $\mathfrak p_3$ has order $4$, and the four-class bound is attained:

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Cl}(\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-14}))\cong C_4,\text{ generated by }[(3,\sqrt{-14}-1)].}
$$

The [Fundamental theorem of Galois theory](../../../galois-theory.md#fundamental-theorem-of-galois-theory) gives an inclusion-reversing bijection between [subgroups](../../../group.md#subgroup) $H\leq\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)$ and intermediate fields $M$, by $H\mapsto L^H$ and $M\mapsto\operatorname{Gal}(L/M)$, for a finite [Galois extension](../../../galois-theory.md#finite-galois-extension). One has $[L:L^H]=|H|$ and $[L^H:K]=[\operatorname{Gal}(L/K):H]$. The field $L^H$ is Galois over $K$ exactly when $H$ is normal; then its Galois group is the quotient by $H$.

Let $\alpha=\sqrt[3]2$ and $\zeta=e^{2\pi i/3}$. The [splitting field](../../../galois-theory.md#splitting-field) is $L=\mathbb Q(\alpha,\zeta)$. A [splitting field](../../../galois-theory.md#splitting-field) in [characteristic zero](../../../algebra.md#characteristic-zero) is normal and separable, hence Galois. [Eisenstein criterion](../../../commutative-algebra.md#eisenstein-criterion) at $2$ gives $[\mathbb Q(\alpha):\mathbb Q]=3$. This field is real, whereas $\zeta$ is not, and $\zeta$ satisfies a quadratic [polynomial](../../../polynomial.md). Thus $[L:\mathbb Q]=6$. The faithful permutation action on the three roots identifies the Galois group with $S_3$. The [subgroup](../../../group.md#subgroup) fixing $\alpha$ is generated by the transposition interchanging the other two roots. Its conjugates are different transposition [subgroups](../../../group.md#subgroup), so **it is not normal**.

The [cyclotomic polynomial](../../../galois-theory.md#cyclotomic-polynomial) is $\Phi_8(t)=t^4+1$. Over $\mathbb F_7$,

## 18H

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 2](paper-2.md)

<h3 id="18h/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [18H](#18h)

A [covering map](../../../algebraic-topology.md#covering-space) is a continuous surjection $p:\widetilde X\to X$ for which each point has an open neighbourhood $U$ with $p^{-1}(U)$ a disjoint union of open sets, each mapped homeomorphically onto $U$. A connected simply connected [covering space](../../../algebraic-topology.md#covering-space) is a [universal cover](../../../algebraic-topology.md#universal-cover).

There is a necessary connectedness qualification in the printed conclusion. Let $A_0$ be the path component of $a_0$ in $A$. Then $p(\widetilde A)=A_0$: one inclusion follows by projecting paths, and the other by lifting a path in $A_0$ from $a_0$. Thus the asserted surjection onto all of $A$ requires $A$ to be path connected. Without it, take $p$ to be the identity of $\mathbb R$ and $A$ to be two disjoint intervals; a selected component cannot cover both.

To prove the assertion about a [component of a pulled-back universal cover](../../../algebraic-topology.md#component-of-a-pulled-back-universal-cover), choose an evenly covered neighbourhood $U$ in $X$. Local path connectedness of $A$ supplies a path-connected relatively open neighbourhood $V\subseteq U\cap A_0$. Each inverse sheet over $V$ is path connected, so it lies wholly in $\widetilde A$ or is disjoint from it. These sheets evenly cover $V$ under $p|_{\widetilde A}$. Therefore **$p|_{\widetilde A}:\widetilde A\to A_0$ is a [covering map](../../../algebraic-topology.md#covering-space)**.

Under the [classification of connected covering spaces](../../../algebraic-topology.md#classification-of-connected-covering-spaces), its [subgroup](../../../group.md#subgroup) consists of based loops in $A_0$ whose lifts close at $\widetilde a_0$. A loop viewed in $X$ closes in the [universal cover](../../../algebraic-topology.md#universal-cover) exactly when its class in $\pi_1(X,a_0)$ is trivial. Hence the [subgroup](../../../group.md#subgroup) is

$$
\boxed{p_*\pi_1(\widetilde A,\widetilde a_0)=\ker\{\pi_1(A,a_0)\xrightarrow{i_*}\pi_1(X,a_0)\}.}
$$

Here $\pi_1(A,a_0)=\pi_1(A_0,a_0)$, so the [subgroup](../../../group.md#subgroup) formula itself needs no change. Injectivity of the covering's induced fundamental-group map is the usual homotopy-lifting property.

## 19G

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 2](paper-2.md)

<h3 id="19g/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [19G](#19g)

<h4 id="19g/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#19g/a)

A [linear map](../../../vector-space.md#linear-map) $T:X\to Y$ between normed spaces is a [bounded linear operator](../../../topological-vector-space.md#continuous-linear-operator) if $\|Tx\|\leq C\|x\|$ for one finite $C$ and every $x$. This implies $\|Tx-Ty\|\leq C\|x-y\|$, hence continuity. Conversely, continuity at zero supplies $\delta>0$ such that $\|x\|<\delta$ implies $\|Tx\|<1$. Apply this to $\delta x/(2\|x\|)$ to obtain $\|Tx\|\leq2\|x\|/\delta$ for nonzero $x$. Thus **boundedness and continuity are equivalent for [linear maps](../../../vector-space.md#linear-map)**.

The [operator norm](../../../continuous-dual-space.md#operator-norm) is $\|T\|=\sup_{\|x\|\leq1}\|Tx\|$, equivalently $\sup_{x\ne0}\|Tx\|/\|x\|$. The bounded operators form a [vector space](../../../vector-space.md), since $\|(S+T)x\|\leq(\|S\|+\|T\|)\|x\|$ and scalar multiples remain bounded. Taking suprema proves the [triangle inequality](../../../topological-analysis.md#triangle-inequality) and absolute homogeneity for the [operator norm](../../../continuous-dual-space.md#operator-norm); $\|T\|=0$ forces $Tx=0$ for every $x$. Thus $B(X,Y)$ is a normed space, whether or not $X,Y$ are complete.

<h3 id="19g/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [19G](#19g)

<h4 id="19g/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#19g/b)

<h5 id="19g/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#19g/b/i)

Take $f_n(t)=\sin(2\pi nt)$. Its domain [norm](../../../functional-analysis.md#norm) is $1$, whereas its image [norm](../../../functional-analysis.md#norm) is $1+2\pi n$. Hence **$T$ is unbounded**, so it has no finite [operator norm](../../../continuous-dual-space.md#operator-norm) and is not a [compact operator](../../../compact-operator.md).

<h4 id="19g/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#19g/b)

<h5 id="19g/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#19g/b/ii)

For the inclusion, $\|Tf\|_\infty\leq\|f\|_\infty+\|f'\|_\infty$, with equality for a nonzero constant. Thus **$\|T\|=1$**. The domain unit ball is uniformly bounded and has $\|f'\|_\infty\leq1$, so it is [equicontinuous](../../../topological-analysis.md#equicontinuity). The [Arzelà-Ascoli theorem](../../../topological-analysis.md#arzela-ascoli-theorem) makes its image relatively compact in $C[0,1]$. Therefore **$T$ is compact**.

<h4 id="19g/b/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#19g/b)

<h5 id="19g/b/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#19g/b/iii)

Differentiation satisfies $\|Tf\|_\infty\leq\|f\|$, so its [operator norm](../../../continuous-dual-space.md#operator-norm) is at most $1$. With $f_n(t)=\sin(2\pi nt)/(2\pi n)$, the ratio $\|Tf_n\|_\infty/\|f_n\|$ tends to $1$. Hence **$\|T\|=1$**.

Normalize $g_n=f_n/(1+(2\pi n)^{-1})$, so $\|g_n\|=1$. Their derivatives are $\cos(2\pi nt)/(1+(2\pi n)^{-1})$. Distinct cosine functions are orthogonal in $L^2(0,1)$, and these normalizing factors stay bounded away from zero. Consequently the derivative images have pairwise $L^2$ distances bounded away from zero. Since [uniform convergence](../../../real-analysis.md#uniform-convergence) would imply $L^2$ convergence, no subsequence converges uniformly. Thus **$T$ is not compact**.

<h4 id="19g/b/iv">iv</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#19g/b)

<h5 id="19g/b/iv/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#19g/b/iv)

The integration functional satisfies $|Tf|\leq\|f\|_\infty\int_0^1|h|$. If $h$ never vanishes, its sign is constant and $f=\operatorname{sgn}h$ attains equality. In general use the [continuous functions](../../../calculus.md#continuous-function) $f_\varepsilon=h/\sqrt{h^2+\varepsilon}$, of supremum [norm](../../../functional-analysis.md#norm) at most $1$. Then

$$
Tf_\varepsilon=\int_0^1\frac{h^2}{\sqrt{h^2+\varepsilon}}\,dt\longrightarrow\int_0^1|h(t)|\,dt
$$

by the [dominated convergence theorem](../../../measure-theory.md#dominated-convergence-theorem). Therefore **$\|T\|=\int_0^1|h|$**. Every bounded subset of its finite-dimensional target $\mathbb R$ is relatively compact, so **$T$ is compact**, including when $h=0$.

## 20F

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 2](paper-2.md)

<h3 id="20f/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [20F](#20f)

Two [holomorphic function](../../../complex-analysis.md#holomorphic-function) elements represent the same [germ of a holomorphic function](../../../complex-analysis.md#germ-of-a-holomorphic-function) at $z$ if they agree on some neighbourhood of $z$. For a holomorphic $f$ on an open $D\subseteq G$, set $U(f,D)=\{[f]_z:z\in D\}$. These sets form a [basis of a topology](../../../topology.md#basis-of-a-topology) on the [space of germs of holomorphic functions](../../../complex-analysis.md#space-of-germs-of-holomorphic-functions): every germ lies in one, and if a germ lies in two, equality of the representatives on a smaller neighbourhood supplies a [basis of a topology](../../../topology.md#basis-of-a-topology) set in their intersection. Declaring arbitrary unions of [basis of a topology](../../../topology.md#basis-of-a-topology) sets open therefore gives a topology.

Different base points are separated by disjoint small discs in $G$. If two different germs have the same base point, choose representatives on one connected small disc. Their sheets are disjoint: any common germ elsewhere on that disc would force equality throughout the disc by the [identity theorem](../../../complex-analysis.md#identity-theorem). This proves the Hausdorff property.

The projection $\pi([f]_z)=z$ maps each sheet $U(f,D)$ homeomorphically onto $D$. Use these maps as complex charts. On overlaps their transition functions are the identity, hence holomorphic; thus each connected component is a [Riemann surface](../../../complex-analysis.md#riemann-surfaces), and $\pi$ is a local [biholomorphism](../../../complex-analysis.md#biholomorphism), an [analytic germ projection](../../../complex-analysis.md#analytic-germ-projection). For completeness, second countability of a component follows by following continuation chains through a countable [basis of a topology](../../../topology.md#basis-of-a-topology) of rational discs: starting at one germ, finite chains of such discs give only countably many continuation sheets, by uniqueness on overlaps, and they cover every germ in that component.

A [complete analytic function](../../../complex-analysis.md#complete-analytic-function) consists of all germs reachable from one initial germ by [analytic continuation](../../../complex-analysis.md#analytic-continuation). The supplied path-lifting equivalence identifies these with its path component. Since the germ space is locally path connected, connected components and path components agree. Thus its continuation surface is exactly one component $\mathcal G_{\mathcal F}$.

A local [biholomorphism](../../../complex-analysis.md#biholomorphism) need not evenly cover the whole base; the germ projection is an analytic covering in this local sense. The examples below distinguish a genuine [regular covering map](../../../complex-analysis.md#regular-covering-map) from a surjective nonregular projection, with the meaning of regular stated explicitly.

<h3 id="20f/i">i</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [20F](#20f)

<h4 id="20f/i/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#20f/i)

Take $G=\mathbb C^*$ and the [complete analytic function](../../../complex-analysis.md#complete-analytic-function) generated by a logarithm germ. Its continuation surface identifies with $\mathbb C$ via $w\mapsto[\log]_z$ with $z=e^w$ and logarithm value $w$. Thus $\pi(w)=e^w$. It is a [universal cover](../../../algebraic-topology.md#universal-cover); the deck transformations $w\mapsto w+2\pi in$ act transitively on each fibre, so it is also a [regular covering map](../../../complex-analysis.md#regular-covering-map) in the deck-transitive sense. **It is not bijective**, since every nonzero $z$ has infinitely many logarithm values. It also meets the alternative convention in which “regular” merely requires an evenly covered neighbourhood at each base point.

<h3 id="20f/ii">ii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [20F](#20f)

<h4 id="20f/ii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#20f/ii)

A [surjective inverse-polynomial germ projection without even covering](../../../complex-analysis.md#surjective-inverse-polynomial-germ-projection-without-even-covering) comes from the inverse of $P(w)=w^3-3w$ over $G=\mathbb C$. Consider $R=\mathbb C\setminus\{-1,1\}$, with $\pi(w)=P(w)$. Since $P'(w)=3(w^2-1)\ne0$ on $R$, each $w$ defines a holomorphic inverse germ of $P$ at $P(w)$. This identifies $R$ with a component of the [space of germs of holomorphic functions](../../../complex-analysis.md#space-of-germs-of-holomorphic-functions): inverse germs continue along paths in $R$, and the holomorphic identity $P(f(z))=z$ persists under continuation. A germ with value $\pm1$ cannot exist, because differentiating that identity would give $P'(\pm1)f'(z)=1$. Since $R$ is connected, all its inverse germs belong to one [complete analytic function](../../../complex-analysis.md#complete-analytic-function).

The projection is surjective. A generic value has three distinct regular preimages; the critical values $2,-2$ each retain a simple regular preimage, respectively $2,-2$, even after their double critical preimages $-1,1$ are removed. Thus fibre size drops from $3$ to $1$ at these values, making an evenly covered neighbourhood impossible. This is **surjective but not a regular topological covering**.

If “regular” is specifically used for deck transitivity among genuine coverings, there is also a genuine nonnormal example. Remove the critical values from the base and all their preimages from the source:

$$
G=\mathbb C\setminus\{-2,2\},\qquad R=\mathbb C\setminus\{-2,-1,1,2\},\qquad\pi(w)=w^3-3w.
$$

This is a connected three-sheeted analytic covering. A small loop about each critical value swaps the two roots meeting there. The two swaps are distinct and generate $S_3$ (connectedness also forces transitive monodromy). The stabilizer of one sheet is an order-$2$ [subgroup](../../../group.md#subgroup), which is not normal in $S_3$. The [classification of connected covering spaces](../../../algebraic-topology.md#classification-of-connected-covering-spaces) therefore shows **this covering is not regular in the deck-transitive sense**. Either convention has a fully explicit example.

## 21F

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 2](paper-2.md)

<h3 id="21f/i">i</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [21F](#21f)

<h4 id="21f/i/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#21f/i)

The [radical of an ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#radical-of-an-ideal) $I$ in a commutative ring $A$ is $\sqrt I=\{a\in A:a^m\in I\text{ for some }m\geq1\}$. It is an ideal: if $a^m,b^n\in I$, every term of $(a+b)^{m+n}$ has either at least $m$ factors $a$ or at least $n$ factors $b$, so the sum belongs to $I$. Also $(ca)^m\in I$ for every $c\in A$, and additive inverses preserve the defining condition.

<h3 id="21f/ii">ii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [21F](#21f)

<h4 id="21f/ii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#21f/ii)

If $f\in\sqrt I$, a power $f^m$ vanishes at every point of $Z(I)$, so $f$ itself vanishes there. This gives $\sqrt I\subseteq I(Z(I))$.

For the reverse inclusion, take $f$ vanishing on $Z(I)$ and use the [Rabinowitsch trick](../../../algebraic-geometry.md#rabinowitsch-trick). In $k[x_1,\ldots,x_n,t]$ form $J=(I,1-tf)$. A common zero would have $x\in Z(I)$, whence $f(x)=0$ and $1-tf(x)=1$, a contradiction. The assumed [Weak Hilbert Nullstellensatz](../../../algebraic-geometry.md#weak-hilbert-nullstellensatz) gives $J=(1)$. Thus there is a finite expression

$$
1=\sum_ja_j(x,t)i_j(x)+b(x,t)(1-tf(x)),\qquad i_j\in I.
$$

If $f=0$ the conclusion is immediate. Otherwise substitute $t=f^{-1}$ in the localized [polynomial](../../../polynomial.md) ring. The last term vanishes, and clearing a sufficiently high power of $f$ gives $f^N\in I$. Hence $f\in\sqrt I$, proving the [Strong Hilbert Nullstellensatz](../../../algebraic-geometry.md#strong-hilbert-nullstellensatz):

$$
\boxed{I(Z(I))=\sqrt I.}
$$

<h3 id="21f/iii">iii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [21F](#21f)

<h4 id="21f/iii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#21f/iii)

If a power of $a$ lies in $I\cap J$, then $a\in\sqrt I\cap\sqrt J$. Conversely, if $a^m\in I$ and $a^n\in J$, the power $a^{\max(m,n)}$ lies in both ideals. Therefore **$\sqrt{I\cap J}=\sqrt I\cap\sqrt J$** for any commutative ring.

<h3 id="21f/iv">iv</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [21F](#21f)

<h4 id="21f/iv/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#21f/iv)

**The equality is false.** In $A=k[x,y]$, take $I=(y)$ and $J=(y-x^2)$. Both are [prime ideals](../../../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal), since their quotient rings are isomorphic to $k[x]$, so both are radical. But

$$
\sqrt I+\sqrt J=(y,x^2),\qquad\sqrt{I+J}=(x,y).
$$

The element $x$ belongs to the latter ideal and not the former. This also shows why taking the [radical of an ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#radical-of-an-ideal) can enlarge a sum of already radical ideals.

## 22G

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 2](paper-2.md)

<h3 id="22g/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [22G](#22g)

An [immersion](../../../differential-geometry.md#immersion) $\phi:U\to\mathbb R^3$ has differential of rank $2$, equivalently [linearly independent](../../../vector-space.md#linear-independence) $\phi_u,\phi_v$. It has [isothermal coordinates](../../../differential-geometry.md#isothermal-coordinates) when its [first fundamental form](../../../differential-geometry.md#first-fundamental-form) has $E=G>0$, $F=0$. A [minimal surface](../../../second-fundamental-form.md#minimal-surface) has zero [mean curvature](../../../second-fundamental-form.md#mean-curvature).

For an isothermal [immersion](../../../differential-geometry.md#immersion), differentiation of $E=G$ and $F=0$ gives

$$
(\phi_{uu}+\phi_{vv})\cdot\phi_u=\tfrac12E_u+F_v-\tfrac12G_u=0,
$$

and similarly its scalar product with $\phi_v$ is zero. Its normal component is $e+g$. The given [mean curvature](../../../second-fundamental-form.md#mean-curvature) formula reduces to $H=(e+g)/(2E)$, so

$$
\boxed{\phi_{uu}+\phi_{vv}=2EH\mathbf n.}
$$

Thus an isothermal [immersion](../../../differential-geometry.md#immersion) is minimal exactly when each coordinate is a [harmonic function](../../../partial-differential-equation.md#harmonic-function).

An explicit example is the [Enneper surface](../../../second-fundamental-form.md#enneper-surface), parametrized on $\mathbb R^2$ by

$$
\phi(u,v)=(u-u^3/3+uv^2,\ v-v^3/3+u^2v,\ u^2-v^2).
$$

Every coordinate has zero Laplacian. Direct differentiation gives $\phi_u\cdot\phi_v=0$ and $|\phi_u|^2=|\phi_v|^2=(1+u^2+v^2)^2>0$. Thus it is an isothermal [immersion](../../../differential-geometry.md#immersion) and is minimal everywhere.

It is not an open subset of any of the three listed surfaces. The parameter points $(\sqrt3,0)$ and $(-\sqrt3,0)$ both map to $(0,0,3)$, but their tangent planes differ: $\phi_u=(-2,0,\pm2\sqrt3)$ while $\phi_v=(0,4,0)$. A plane, [catenoid](../../../differential-geometry.md#catenoid), or [helicoid](../../../differential-geometry.md#helicoid) is an embedded smooth surface with a unique tangent plane at each point. If our whole image lay in any one of them, both rank-$2$ tangent planes at this point would have to equal its tangent plane, a contradiction.

For $1\leq p<\infty$, $L^p(E,\mathcal E,\mu)$ consists of [measurable functions](../../../measure-theory.md#measurable-function) with $\int_E|f|^p\,d\mu<\infty$, identifying functions equal [almost everywhere](../../../measure-theory.md#almost-everywhere). <h3 id="23j/b">b</h3>

[Hölder's inequality](../../../real-analysis.md#holder-s-inequality) states that for conjugate exponents $1/p+1/q=1$, $f\in L^p$ and $g\in L^q$ imply $fg\in L^1$ and

<h3 id="23j/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [23J](#23j)

<h4 id="23j/c/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#23j/c)

<h5 id="23j/c/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#23j/c/i)

By the [Cauchy-Schwarz inequality](../../../probability-and-statistics.md#cauchy-schwarz-inequality), the integral is bounded by $\|f\|_2\|k_x\|_2$, where $k_x(y)=|x-y|^{-\alpha}$. For $0\leq x\leq1$,

$$
\int_0^1|x-y|^{-2\alpha}\,dy=\frac{x^{1-2\alpha}+(1-x)^{1-2\alpha}}{1-2\alpha}<\infty.
$$

For $x$ outside the interval, the integral is bounded by the value at the nearest endpoint; the endpoint value is finite as well. Since $2\alpha<1$, the kernel is square-integrable for every real $x$, proving **[absolute convergence](../../../real-analysis.md#absolute-convergence) for every $x$**.

<h4 id="23j/c/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#23j/c)

<h5 id="23j/c/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#23j/c/ii)

Subtract the two absolutely convergent integrals and apply the [Cauchy-Schwarz inequality](../../../probability-and-statistics.md#cauchy-schwarz-inequality) to $f$ and the difference of the kernels. This gives precisely

$$
\boxed{|g(x+h)-g(x)|\leq\|f\|_2\left(\int_0^1\bigl(|x+h-y|^{-\alpha}-|x-y|^{-\alpha}\bigr)^2\,dy\right)^{1/2}.}
$$

<h4 id="23j/c/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#23j/c)

<h5 id="23j/c/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#23j/c/iii)

For $h\ne0$, enlarge the nonnegative integral $I(h)$ to all of $\mathbb R$, and substitute $y=x+|h|t$. Reflection if necessary gives

$$
I(h)\leq |h|^{1-2\alpha}C_\alpha,\qquad C_\alpha=\int_{\mathbb R}\bigl(|t-1|^{-\alpha}-|t|^{-\alpha}\bigr)^2\,dt.
$$

Near $0$ and $1$, its integrand is bounded by a constant times $1+|t|^{-2\alpha}$ or $1+|t-1|^{-2\alpha}$, which is integrable. For large $|t|$, the [mean value theorem](../../../calculus.md#mean-value-theorem) bounds the difference by a constant times $|t|^{-\alpha-1}$, whose square is integrable. Thus $C_\alpha<\infty$. The preceding estimate yields

$$
\boxed{|g(x+h)-g(x)|\leq\|f\|_2\sqrt{C_\alpha}\,|h|^{1/2-\alpha}.}
$$

In particular **$g$ is continuous on all of $\mathbb R$**; the proof establishes [Hölder continuity of a one-dimensional fractional kernel integral](../../../sobolev-space.md#holder-continuity-of-a-one-dimensional-fractional-kernel-integral) of exponent $1/2-\alpha$ uniformly in $x$.

## 24K

<h3 id="24k/i">i</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [24K](#24k)

<h4 id="24k/i/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#24k/i)

A rate-$\lambda$ [Poisson process](../../../probability-theory.md#poisson-process) is a counting process starting at zero, with right-continuous nondecreasing integer-valued paths, independent stationary increments, and $N_t-N_s\sim\operatorname{Pois}(\lambda(t-s))$ for $0\leq s<t$. Its jumps have size one almost surely.

For independent [Poisson processes](../../../probability-theory.md#poisson-process) $N,M$, increments of $N+M$ on disjoint intervals are independent, because all the corresponding increments of the two processes are jointly independent. On an interval of length $t$, multiplying their [probability generating functions](../../../probability-theory.md#probability-generating-function) gives

$$
\exp(\lambda t(z-1))\exp(\mu t(z-1))=\exp((\lambda+\mu)t(z-1)).
$$

Thus the increment is Poisson with mean $(\lambda+\mu)t$. The path requirements hold, and independent processes almost surely have no simultaneous jumps. Therefore **$N+M$ is a [Poisson process](../../../probability-theory.md#poisson-process) of rate $\lambda+\mu$**.

<h3 id="24k/ii">ii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [24K](#24k)

<h4 id="24k/ii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#24k/ii)

The random clock is understood to be independent of the discrete-time [Markov chain](../../../markov-process.md#markov-chain); without this hypothesis its law need not be determined by $K$ alone. Conditional on $N_t=n$, the transition matrix is $K^n$, so

$$
P_t=e^{-\lambda t}\sum_{n\geq0}\frac{(\lambda t)^n}{n!}K^n=e^{\lambda t(K-I)},\qquad\boxed{Q=\lambda(K-I).}
$$

Hence $q_{xy}=\lambda K_{xy}$ for $x\ne y$ and $q_{xx}=-\lambda(1-K_{xx})$. If $\pi K=\pi$, then $\pi P_t=\pi$ by the series, proving that the [invariant distribution](../../../markov-process.md#stationary-distribution) is unchanged.

Let $\xi_i$ be the independent exponential interarrival times of the [Poisson process](../../../probability-theory.md#poisson-process). The absorption time is $T_a=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau_a}\xi_i$. By independence of the chain and clock and [Tonelli theorem](../../../measure-theory.md#tonelli-theorem),

$$
\mathbb E_xT_a=\sum_{i\geq1}\mathbb E[\xi_i]\mathbb P_x(\tau_a\geq i)=\boxed{\frac1\lambda\mathbb E_x\tau_a.}
$$

This includes infinite expectations and absorption at time zero. The same argument proves the hinted random-sum identity for an independent nonnegative integer-valued count $M$.

For $n\geq1$, the binomial [log-likelihood](../../../statistical-modelling.md#log-likelihood) is $X\log\theta+(n-X)\log(1-\theta)$ plus a constant. Its maximizer, including endpoints, is **$\widehat\theta_{\rm MLE}=X/n$**. For $0<\theta<1$, the [Fisher information](../../../statistical-modelling.md#fisher-information-matrix) is

$$
\boxed{I(\theta)=\frac{n}{\theta(1-\theta)}.}
$$

This follows either from the expected negative second derivative or the [variance](../../../variance.md) of $(X-n\theta)/(\theta(1-\theta))$. At $0,1$ the usual regular score formula does not apply; the interior information diverges as an endpoint is approached.

For a symmetric [Beta distribution](../../../probability-theory.md#beta-distribution) prior $\operatorname{Beta}(s,s)$, conjugacy gives $\theta\mid X\sim\operatorname{Beta}(X+s,n-X+s)$, with [posterior mean](../../../statistical-inference.md#posterior-mean) $(X+s)/(n+2s)$. All three priors below are instances of this calculation.

<h3 id="25j/i">i</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [25J](#25j)

<h4 id="25j/i/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#25j/i)

The [uniform prior](../../../statistical-inference.md#uniform-prior) has $s=1$, so the posterior is $\operatorname{Beta}(X+1,n-X+1)$. Its mean and mode are

$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[\theta\mid X]=\frac{X+1}{n+2},\qquad m_{\theta\mid X}=\frac Xn.}
$$

Thus **the uniform-prior [posterior mode](../../../statistical-inference.md#maximum-a-posteriori-estimate) is the [maximum-likelihood estimator](../../../statistical-modelling.md#maximum-likelihood-estimator)**, including $X=0,n$. The mean shrinks toward $1/2$ and equals the [maximum-likelihood estimator](../../../statistical-modelling.md#maximum-likelihood-estimator) only when $X=n/2$.

<h3 id="25j/ii">ii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [25J](#25j)

<h4 id="25j/ii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#25j/ii)

The [Jeffreys prior](../../../statistical-modelling.md#jeffreys-prior) is proportional to $[\theta(1-\theta)]^{-1/2}$, hence is $\operatorname{Beta}(1/2,1/2)$. Its posterior is $\operatorname{Beta}(X+1/2,n-X+1/2)$, giving

$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[\theta\mid X]=\frac{X+1/2}{n+1},\qquad m_{\theta\mid X}=\begin{cases}0&X=0,\\(X-1/2)/(n-1)&0<X<n,\\1&X=n.\end{cases}}
$$

The interior case is used only when $n\geq2$. Generally neither rule equals $X/n$: the mean agrees only at $X=n/2$; the mode agrees at the endpoints and, in the interior, at $X=n/2$. For the small experiments $n=1,2$, all possible observations happen to be among these cases, so the whole mode rule then coincides with the [maximum-likelihood estimator](../../../statistical-modelling.md#maximum-likelihood-estimator).

<h3 id="25j/iii">iii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [25J](#25j)

<h4 id="25j/iii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#25j/iii)

Put $s=\sqrt n/2$. The posterior is $\operatorname{Beta}(X+s,n-X+s)$, and

$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[\theta\mid X]=\frac{X+\sqrt n/2}{n+\sqrt n},\qquad m_{\theta\mid X}=\frac{\max(X+s-1,0)}{\max(X+s,1)+\max(n-X+s,1)-2}.}
$$

The denominator is positive for $n\geq1$. For $n>4$, both parameters exceed one and the mode simplifies to $(X+s-1)/(n+\sqrt n-2)$. For $n=4$ this is the uniform-prior mode, exactly $X/n$; for $n=1,2$ the endpoint or symmetric cases also make the mode rule coincide with $X/n$. Otherwise it is generally a different rule. The [posterior mean](../../../statistical-inference.md#posterior-mean) agrees with $X/n$ only at $X=n/2$.

To find the [minimax estimator](../../../statistical-modelling.md#minimax-estimator) under [quadratic loss](../../../statistical-inference.md#squared-error-loss), consider the [posterior mean](../../../statistical-inference.md#posterior-mean) $\delta_s=(X+s)/(n+2s)$. Its [risk function](../../../statistical-modelling.md#risk-function) is

$$
R(\theta,\delta_s)=\frac{n\theta(1-\theta)+s^2(1-2\theta)^2}{(n+2s)^2}.
$$

For $s^2=n/4$, the numerator is identically $n/4$, so

$$
\boxed{R(\theta,\delta_s)=\frac1{4(\sqrt n+1)^2}\quad\text{for all }\theta.}
$$

This [constant-risk binomial proportion estimator](../../../statistical-modelling.md#constant-risk-binomial-proportion-estimator) is the unique [Bayes estimator](../../../statistical-inference.md#bayes-estimator) for its proper beta prior under [quadratic loss](../../../statistical-inference.md#squared-error-loss). A competing rule's maximum risk is at least its risk averaged over that prior, which is at least this Bayes risk. The constant-risk rule attains the bound, so **the [posterior mean](../../../statistical-inference.md#posterior-mean) in (iii) is the unique minimax rule**. Consequently any coincident rule is minimax too: the mean in (i) is the same rule when $n=4$, and the mean in (ii) is the same rule when $n=1$. No [posterior mode](../../../statistical-inference.md#maximum-a-posteriori-estimate) listed here equals that mean rule for every observation, since at $X=0$ its value is positive whereas their endpoint modes differ from it. This accounts for the small-$n$ coincidences as well as the general answer.

Write $V(x)=F(\pi,x)$. The [Bellman equation](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#bellman-equation) implies $V(x)\geq r(x,u)+\beta\mathbb E[V(x_1)\mid x,u]$ for every available action. Under any competing policy, repeated conditioning gives

\boxed{V_x=x\,2^{(x-100)/2}.}

The matrix $\beta Q$ is diagonally similar, with diagonal factors $2^{x/2}$, to the symmetric tridiagonal matrix with off-diagonal entries $1/2$. The linearized perturbation obeys $\dot\eta=D\mathbf f(\mathbf X(t))\eta$, so its [fundamental matrix](../../../differential-equation.md#fundamental-matrix-of-a-linear-differential-equation) satisfies

\boxed{\dot\Phi=D\mathbf f(\mathbf X(t))\Phi,\qquad\Phi(0)=I.}

The [Floquet multipliers](../../../dynamical-systems.md#floquet-multiplier) are the [eigenvalues](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) of the [monodromy matrix](../../../integrable-systems.md#monodromy-matrix) $\Phi(T)$. For a nonconstant [periodic orbit](../../../dynamical-systems.md#periodic-orbit) this tangent solution is nonzero and periodic, giving one multiplier equal to $1$.

<h3 id="28b/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [28B](#28b)

<h4 id="28b/b/solution">Solution</h4>

Use the nearly conserved oscillator [energy](../../../classical-mechanics.md#energy) $E=(\dot x^2+x^2)/2$. The [differential equation](../../../differential-equation.md) gives

$$
\dot E=-\varepsilon(2\dot x^4+2x^3\dot x-4x^4\dot x^2-\dot x^2).
$$

For a leading-order orbit $x=A\cos\theta$, $\dot x=-A\sin\theta$, the cycle averages are $\langle\dot x^4\rangle=3A^4/8$, $\langle x^3\dot x\rangle=0$, $\langle x^4\dot x^2\rangle=A^6/16$, and $\langle\dot x^2\rangle=A^2/2$. $$
\langle\dot E\rangle=\frac\varepsilon4A^2(A^2-1)(A^2-2),\qquad\dot A=\frac\varepsilon4A(A^2-1)(A^2-2).
$$

The two nonzero simple roots give the leading [limit cycles](../../../dynamical-systems.md#limit-cycle) **$x\simeq\cos(t+\theta_0)$ and $x\simeq\sqrt2\cos(t+\theta_0)$**. Below amplitude $1$ the [energy](../../../classical-mechanics.md#energy) increases, between $1$ and $\sqrt2$ it decreases, and above $\sqrt2$ it increases. Hence **the amplitude-$1$ cycle is stable and the amplitude-$\sqrt2$ cycle unstable** for positive small $\varepsilon$.

The linearized averaged radial exponents are $-\varepsilon/2$ and $\varepsilon$, respectively, consistent with nontrivial [Floquet multipliers](../../../dynamical-systems.md#floquet-multiplier) $e^{-\pi\varepsilon+O(\varepsilon^2)}$ and $e^{2\pi\varepsilon+O(\varepsilon^2)}$. <h3 id="30e/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [30E](#30e)

Write $(\phi_\varepsilon*f)(x)=\int\phi(y)f(x-\varepsilon y)\,dy$. Since $\int\phi=1$,

$$
|(\phi_\varepsilon*f)(x)-f(x)|\leq\int|\phi(y)|\,|f(x-\varepsilon y)-f(x)|\,dy.
$$

A [Schwartz function](../../../fourier-analysis.md#schwartz-function) is bounded and [uniformly continuous](../../../topological-analysis.md#uniform-continuity). Given a tolerance, first choose $R$ so the tail integral of $|\phi|$ is small. On $|y|\leq R$, [uniform continuity](../../../topological-analysis.md#uniform-continuity) makes the difference uniformly small for every $x$ when $\varepsilon R$ is small. On the tail it is bounded by $2\|f\|_\infty$. This proves **[uniform convergence](../../../real-analysis.md#uniform-convergence)**, without assuming $\phi\geq0$, and is the [approximate identity](../../../fourier-analysis.md#approximate-identity) argument.

For the radial regularization of the three-dimensional [fundamental solution of the Laplace equation](../../../partial-differential-equation.md#fundamental-solution-of-the-laplace-equation), direct differentiation gives

$$
-\Delta N_\varepsilon(x)=\frac{3\varepsilon^2}{4\pi(|x|^2+\varepsilon^2)^{5/2}}=\varepsilon^{-3}\phi(x/\varepsilon),\qquad\phi(x)=\frac3{4\pi}(1+|x|^2)^{-5/2}.
$$

The radial integral is $\int\phi=3\int_0^\infty r^2(1+r^2)^{-5/2}\,dr=1$. This even [approximate identity](../../../fourier-analysis.md#approximate-identity) therefore gives $\int(-\Delta N_\varepsilon)f\to f(0)$ for $f\in\mathcal S(\mathbb R^3)$.

The function $N=1/(4\pi|x|)$ is locally integrable and determines a [tempered distribution](../../../fourier-analysis.md#tempered-distribution). We have $0<N_\varepsilon\leq N$, and $N|\Delta f|$ is integrable near zero and infinity for a Schwartz test function. Integration by parts and the [dominated convergence theorem](../../../measure-theory.md#dominated-convergence-theorem) give

$$
\langle-\Delta N,f\rangle=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow0}\int N_\varepsilon(-\Delta f)=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow0}\int(-\Delta N_\varepsilon)f=f(0).
$$

Thus **$-\Delta N=\delta_0$**, proving that $N$ is a [fundamental solution of a linear differential operator](../../../distribution-theory.md#fundamental-solution-of-a-linear-differential-operator). In this last part the test-function space is $\mathcal S(\mathbb R^3)$; the printed $\mathbb R^n$ in the three-dimensional integral is understood with $n=3$.

## 31A

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 2](paper-2.md)

<h3 id="31a/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [31A](#31a)

For [spin](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#spin) $1/2$, **$\mathbf S=\hbar\boldsymbol\sigma/2$**. The [Pauli matrices](../../../algebra.md#pauli-matrices) satisfy $\sigma_i\sigma_j=\delta_{ij}I+i\epsilon_{ijk}\sigma_k$. The antisymmetric term vanishes on contraction with $n_in_j$, and $|\mathbf n|=1$, so $(\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)^2=I$. Separating the even and odd terms of the exponential series gives

$$
\boxed{e^{-i\theta\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf S/\hbar}=I\cos(\theta/2)-i(\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)\sin(\theta/2).}
$$

For the single-particle [Hamiltonian](../../../classical-mechanics.md#hamiltonian), [unitary time evolution](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#unitary-time-evolution) consequently acts as

$$
|\uparrow\rangle\mapsto\cos(\alpha t/2)|\uparrow\rangle-i\sin(\alpha t/2)|\downarrow\rangle,\qquad|\downarrow\rangle\mapsto\cos(\alpha t/2)|\downarrow\rangle-i\sin(\alpha t/2)|\uparrow\rangle.
$$

For two [spins](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#spin) the joint eigenstates of total [angular momentum](../../../classical-mechanics.md#angular-momentum) are the [spin-one-half triplet state](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#spin-one-half-triplet-state)

$$
|1,1\rangle=|\uparrow\uparrow\rangle,\quad |1,0\rangle=\frac{|\uparrow\downarrow\rangle+|\downarrow\uparrow\rangle}{\sqrt2},\quad |1,-1\rangle=|\downarrow\downarrow\rangle,
$$

with $J^2=2\hbar^2$ and $J_3=m\hbar$, and the [spin-one-half singlet state](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#spin-one-half-singlet-state)

$$
|0,0\rangle=\frac{|\uparrow\downarrow\rangle-|\downarrow\uparrow\rangle}{\sqrt2},\qquad J^2=J_3=0.
$$

These follow by adding the two spin-$1/2$ angular momenta, or by direct action of the Pauli matrices.

The two terms of the specified [Hamiltonian](../../../classical-mechanics.md#hamiltonian) commute, so its evolution factorizes into rotations with opposite angles on the two tensor factors. The unique initial $J_3=\hbar$ state is $|\uparrow\uparrow\rangle$. With $c=\cos(\lambda t/2)$, $s=\sin(\lambda t/2)$, its evolved state is

$$
c^2|\uparrow\uparrow\rangle+s^2|\downarrow\downarrow\rangle+ics(|\uparrow\downarrow\rangle-|\downarrow\uparrow\rangle).
$$

The singlet amplitude is $i\sqrt2cs$, giving the requested probability

$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(J^2=0)=2c^2s^2=\tfrac12\sin^2(\lambda t).}
$$

## 32A

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 2](paper-2.md)

<h3 id="32a/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [32A](#32a)

Normalize the incident plane wave to unit amplitude. The scattering asymptotics are $\psi(\mathbf r)\sim e^{ikz}+f(\theta)e^{ikr}/r$; the outgoing coefficient $f$ is the [scattering amplitude](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#scattering-amplitude). The general axisymmetric free solution for $r>0$ is

$$
\psi(r,\theta)=\sum_{\ell\geq0}\{a_\ell j_\ell(kr)+b_\ell n_\ell(kr)\}P_\ell(\cos\theta),
$$

using [Spherical Bessel functions](../../../analysis.md#spherical-bessel-function), [spherical Neumann functions](../../../analysis.md#spherical-bessel-function-of-the-second-kind) and [Legendre polynomials](../../../differential-equation.md#legendre-polynomial).

For a real short-range central potential, the exterior radial solution in each [partial wave](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#partial-wave) is proportional to $\cos\delta_\ell\,j_\ell-\sin\delta_\ell\,n_\ell$, whose large-$r$ form is $\sin(kr-\ell\pi/2+\delta_\ell)/(kr)$. This defines the [scattering phase shift](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#scattering-phase-shift). Multiplying this combination by $(2\ell+1)i^\ell e^{i\delta_\ell}$ matches the incoming spherical component of the incident plane wave: the factor $e^{i\delta_\ell}$ cancels the shifted incoming phase. The outgoing coefficient instead acquires $e^{2i\delta_\ell}$. Subtracting the plane wave's outgoing component therefore gives

$$
\boxed{f(\theta)=\frac1{2ik}\sum_{\ell\geq0}(2\ell+1)(e^{2i\delta_\ell}-1)P_\ell(\cos\theta)=\frac1k\sum_{\ell\geq0}(2\ell+1)e^{i\delta_\ell}\sin\delta_\ell\,P_\ell(\cos\theta).}
$$

For the barrier's $s$-wave, the regular reduced radial function inside is $u(r)=A\sinh(\kappa r)$, with $\kappa=\sqrt{\gamma^2-k^2}$. Outside take $u(r)=B\sin(kr+\delta_0)$. Continuity of $u$ and its derivative at $a$ equates logarithmic derivatives:

$$
\kappa\coth(\kappa a)=k\cot(ka+\delta_0),\qquad\boxed{\frac{\tanh(\kappa a)}{\kappa a}=\frac{\tan(ka+\delta_0)}{ka}.}
$$

Use the branch with $\delta_0\to0$ as $k\to0$. The matching equation gives $\delta_0=k(\tanh(\gamma a)/\gamma-a)+o(k)$, so the $s$-wave [scattering amplitude](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#scattering-amplitude) tends to

$$
\boxed{f\longrightarrow\frac{\tanh(\gamma a)-\gamma a}{\gamma}.}
$$

Its negative is the [repulsive spherical barrier scattering length](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#repulsive-spherical-barrier-scattering-length), positive for this repulsive barrier.

## 33C

<h3 id="33c/a">a</h3>

<h4 id="33c/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#33c/a)

The [Bose-Einstein distribution](../../../statistical-physics.md#bose-einstein-distribution) gives $n_i=[\exp((E_i-\mu)/(k_BT))-1]^{-1}$, with [chemical potential](../../../thermodynamics.md#chemical-potential) below the lowest [energy](../../../classical-mechanics.md#energy) when its discrete occupation is finite. In the continuum approximation,

$$
\boxed{N=\int_0^\infty\frac{g(E)}{e^{(E-\mu)/(k_BT)}-1}\,dE,\qquad U=\int_0^\infty\frac{Eg(E)}{e^{(E-\mu)/(k_BT)}-1}\,dE.}
$$

If the zero-energy [ground state](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#ground-state) is macroscopically occupied, its discrete occupation $N_0$ must be added to the first integral; it contributes no [energy](../../../classical-mechanics.md#energy) to the second. This distinction is essential when applying the formulas to [Bose-Einstein condensation](../../../statistical-physics.md#bose-einstein-condensation).

<h3 id="33c/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [33C](#33c)

<h4 id="33c/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#33c/b)

The [photon](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#photon) number in thermal [blackbody radiation](../../../statistical-physics.md#black-body-radiation) is not fixed, so **$\mu=0$**. With $x=E/(k_BT)$,

$$
U=AV(k_BT)^4\int_0^\infty\frac{x^3}{e^x-1}\,dx=AV(k_BT)^4\frac{\pi^4}{15}.
$$

The integral is a temperature-independent finite constant (its explicit value follows from the gamma-zeta integral). Differentiating at fixed volume gives

$$
\boxed{C_V=\frac{4\pi^4}{15}AVk_B^4T^3,\qquad\alpha=3.}
$$

<h3 id="33c/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [33C](#33c)

<h4 id="33c/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#33c/c)

For fixed particle number, [Bose-Einstein condensation](../../../statistical-physics.md#bose-einstein-condensation) means that a macroscopic fraction occupies the single-particle [ground state](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#ground-state). As $\mu\uparrow0$, the excited-state population reaches its maximum

$$
N_{\rm ex}=B(k_BT)^3\int_0^\infty\frac{x^2}{e^x-1}\,dx=2B\zeta(3)(k_BT)^3.
$$

At the transition this equals $N$, so

$$
\boxed{T_c=\frac1{k_B}\left(\frac{N}{2B\zeta(3)}\right)^{1/3}\propto N^{1/3}.}
$$

Below $T_c$, the [chemical potential](../../../thermodynamics.md#chemical-potential) is approximately zero in the large-$N$ continuum description. Additional particles occupy the [ground state](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#ground-state), giving

$$
\boxed{\frac{N_0}{N}\simeq1-\left(\frac T{T_c}\right)^3,\qquad\gamma=3.}
$$

For finite $N$, the actual [chemical potential](../../../thermodynamics.md#chemical-potential) remains slightly negative; the saturation statement is the macroscopic approximation used here.

↑ **Parent:** [33C](#33c)

<h4 id="33c/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#33c/d)

## 34D

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 2](paper-2.md)

<h3 id="34d/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [34D](#34d)

<h4 id="34d/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#34d/a)

Let $f(r)=1-r_s/r$ and parametrize the [timelike geodesic](../../../general-relativity.md#timelike-geodesic) by [proper time](../../../special-relativity.md#proper-time) $\tau$. Time-translation and rotational symmetry conserve $E=f\dot t$ and $h=r^2\dot\phi$ in the equatorial plane. The normalized [four-velocity](../../../special-relativity.md#four-velocity) obeys

$$
-1=-f\dot t^2+f^{-1}\dot r^2+r^2\dot\phi^2.
$$

Substitution and multiplication by $f$ give $\dot r^2+f(1+h^2/r^2)=E^2$, or

$$
\boxed{\tfrac12\dot r^2+V(r)=\tfrac12E^2,\qquad2V=(1-r_s/r)(1+h^2/r^2).}
$$

The dot denotes a proper-time derivative, and $E,h$ are [energy](../../../classical-mechanics.md#energy) and [angular momentum](../../../classical-mechanics.md#angular-momentum) per unit rest mass.

<h3 id="34d/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [34D](#34d)

<h4 id="34d/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#34d/b)

At a circular orbit, $V'=0$. Differentiating the Schwarzschild [effective potential](../../../physics.md#effective-potential) at fixed $h$ gives

$$
V'=\frac{r_s}{2r^2}-\frac{h^2}{r^3}+\frac{3r_sh^2}{2r^4},\qquad\boxed{h^2=\frac{r^2r_s}{2r-3r_s}.}
$$

A timelike circular orbit requires $r>3r_s/2$. Evaluating the second derivative after this substitution gives

$$
V''=\frac{r_s(r-3r_s)}{r^3(2r-3r_s)}.
$$

A stable nondegenerate minimum therefore requires **$r>3r_s$**. The boundary $r=3r_s$ is the marginal [innermost stable circular orbit](../../../astrophysics.md#innermost-stable-circular-orbit), not a strict stable minimum.

<h3 id="34d/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [34D](#34d)

<h4 id="34d/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#34d/c)

For Alice's circular geodesic, the conserved quantities or radial geodesic equation give $\Omega=d\phi/dt=\sqrt{r_s/(2r^3)}$. Consequently

$$
\left(\frac{d\tau_A}{dt}\right)^2=f-r^2\Omega^2=1-\frac{3r_s}{2r}.
$$

Bob at rest far away has $d\tau_B/dt\simeq1$. Thus the ratio of ageing rates is

$$
\boxed{k^2=\frac1{1-3r_s/(2r)}=\frac{2r}{2r-3r_s}.}
$$

For Alice's stable orbit $r>3r_s$, the denominator factor exceeds $1/2$, proving **$k<\sqrt2$**. Both gravitational time dilation and Alice's orbital motion enter this proper-time ratio.

## 35E

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 2](paper-2.md)

<h3 id="35e/i">i</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [35E](#35e)

<h4 id="35e/i/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#35e/i)

Near the closest point, in $X=x-Ut$, the gap is $h(X)\simeq h_0+X^2/(2a)$. The horizontal length scale is **$\ell=\sqrt{2ah_0}$**, up to an inessential constant. Thus the aspect ratio $h_0/\ell\ll1$ follows from $h_0\ll a$. The [lubrication approximation](../../../viscous-fluid-flow.md#lubrication-theory) assumes an incompressible [Newtonian fluid](../../../viscous-fluid-flow.md#newtonian-fluid), no slip on the surfaces, slow variation of the gap, and negligible inertia relative to transverse viscous stress:

$$
\boxed{\frac{\rho U h_0^2}{\mu\ell}\ll1.}
$$

This is the reduced Reynolds-number condition; it is weaker than requiring a [Reynolds number](../../../fluid-mechanics.md#reynolds-number) based on $\ell$ to be small. In the cylinder frame the geometry and flow are steady. [Pressure](../../../thermodynamics.md#pressure) is uniform across the narrow gap to leading order, and equal ambient [pressures](../../../thermodynamics.md#pressure) far to either side close the model. The calculation assumes a full liquid film; [cavitation](../../../fluid-mechanics.md#cavitation) would change that [pressure](../../../thermodynamics.md#pressure) condition.

<h3 id="35e/ii">ii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [35E](#35e)

<h4 id="35e/ii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#35e/ii)

Reflection about the closest point leaves the gap geometry unchanged and reverses the direction of translation. The linear Stokes equations make the excess [pressure](../../../thermodynamics.md#pressure) change sign when the imposed [velocity](../../../classical-mechanics.md#velocity) changes sign. Combining reflection with this linearity shows that [pressure](../../../thermodynamics.md#pressure) is odd in $X$. Its leading normal force on the stationary plane therefore cancels between the two sides, giving **$\mathbf e_y\cdot\mathbf F=0$ in the lubrication limit**. Normal viscous stresses are of smaller order in the aspect ratio. This uses symmetry only, rather than a [pressure](../../../thermodynamics.md#pressure) calculation.

<h3 id="35e/iii">iii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [35E](#35e)

<h4 id="35e/iii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#35e/iii)

In the laboratory frame the lower wall has [velocity](../../../classical-mechanics.md#velocity) zero and the cylinder surface has [velocity](../../../classical-mechanics.md#velocity) $U$. Solving $\mu u_{yy}=p_X$ with these no-slip conditions gives

$$
\boxed{u(X,y)=\frac{Uy}{h}+\frac{p_X}{2\mu}y(y-h).}
$$

The relative [velocity](../../../classical-mechanics.md#velocity) in the cylinder frame is $u-U$. Its flux is constant by incompressibility:

$$
q=-\frac{Uh}{2}-\frac{h^3p_X}{12\mu}.
$$

Equal [pressures](../../../thermodynamics.md#pressure) at the two infinities require $\int p_X\,dX=0$. Put $I_j=\int h^{-j}\,dX$; then $q=-(U/2)I_2/I_3$. With $X=\ell s$ and $h=h_0(1+s^2)$, the given integrals yield $I_2/I_3=4h_0/3$. Hence $q=-2Uh_0/3$ and

$$
\boxed{p_X=\frac{2\mu U(4h_0-3h)}{h^3}.}
$$

Indeed $p=2\mu UX/h^2$ has this derivative and tends to ambient [pressure](../../../thermodynamics.md#pressure) at both ends. The distinction between the constant comoving flux and the varying laboratory flux is necessary because the gap translates.

<h3 id="35e/iv">iv</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [35E](#35e)

<h4 id="35e/iv/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#35e/iv)

The fluid's tangential traction on the lower stationary surface is $\tau=\mu u_y|_{y=0}$ in the positive $x$ direction. Using the [pressure](../../../thermodynamics.md#pressure) gradient found above gives

$$
\tau=\frac{\mu U}{h}-\frac h2p_X=4\mu U\left(\frac1h-\frac{h_0}{h^2}\right).
$$

The [drag on a plane beneath a translating cylinder](../../../viscous-fluid-flow.md#drag-on-a-plane-beneath-a-translating-cylinder) follows by integrating along the wall:

$$
\boxed{\mathbf e_x\cdot\mathbf F=4\mu U\frac\ell{h_0}\left(\pi-\frac\pi2\right)=2\pi\mu U\sqrt{\frac{2a}{h_0}}}
$$

per unit cylinder-axis length. This is the force on the lower surface, so its direction follows the cylinder's translation.

## 36B

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 2](paper-2.md)

<h3 id="36b/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [36B](#36b)

The linear elastic wave equation is $\rho\mathbf u_{tt}=(\lambda+\mu)\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf u)+\mu\Delta\mathbf u$. For a [shear-horizontal wave](../../../continuum-mechanics.md#shear-horizontal-wave), take $\mathbf u=(0,v(x,z,t),0)$ with no $y$ dependence. Its divergence vanishes, and

$$
v_{tt}=c_s^2(v_{xx}+v_{zz}),\qquad c_s=\sqrt{\mu/\rho}.
$$

Rigid fixed boundaries require $v=0$ at $z=0,h$. The modes are $v=A\sin(q_nz)\cos(kx-\omega_nt)$ with $q_n=n\pi/h$, $n=1,2,\ldots$, and

$$
\boxed{\omega_n(k)=c_s\sqrt{k^2+q_n^2},\qquad\omega_{n,\mathrm{cut}}=c_sq_n.}
$$

There is no $n=0$ displacement mode with these clamped boundary conditions. For $k>0$, the [phase velocity](../../../wave-equation.md#phase-velocity) and [group velocity](../../../wave-equation.md#group-velocity) are

$$
\boxed{c(k)=c_s\frac{\sqrt{k^2+q_n^2}}k,\qquad c_g(k)=c_s\frac{k}{\sqrt{k^2+q_n^2}}.}
$$

The [phase velocity](../../../wave-equation.md#phase-velocity) decreases from infinity to $c_s$, while the [group velocity](../../../wave-equation.md#group-velocity) increases from zero to $c_s$; their product is $c_s^2$.

<a id="36b/image-dispersion-phase-velocity-and-group-velocity-of-a-clamped-shear-horizontal-waveguide-mode"></a>
![](../../../past-exam-of-the-mathematics-course-of-the-university-of-cambridge/2015/ii/paper-2-sh-waveguide.png)

**[Figure 1](#36b/image-dispersion-phase-velocity-and-group-velocity-of-a-clamped-shear-horizontal-waveguide-mode). Dispersion, phase velocity and group velocity of a clamped shear-horizontal waveguide mode**.

For a mode of frequency $\omega$, the appropriate time and cross-sectional average is $\langle A\rangle=\omega/(2\pi h)\int_0^{2\pi/\omega}\int_0^h A\,dz\,dt$, per unit transverse width. Its average [kinetic energy density](../../../classical-mechanics.md#kinetic-energy-density) is $\rho A^2\omega^2/8$. The only nonzero strains are $e_{xy}=e_{yx}=v_x/2$ and $e_{yz}=e_{zy}=v_z/2$, while $e_{kk}=0$. Thus the elastic [energy](../../../classical-mechanics.md#energy) density is $\mu(v_x^2+v_z^2)/2$, whose average is $\mu A^2(k^2+q_n^2)/8$. The [dispersion relation](../../../wave-equation.md#dispersion-relation) proves **equality of average kinetic and elastic energies**.

For the [ray asymptotics of a clamped elastic waveguide mode](../../../wave-equation.md#ray-asymptotics-of-a-clamped-elastic-waveguide-mode), expand the localized initial displacement in transverse sine modes and [Fourier transform](../../../analysis.md#fourier-transform) in $x$. For a displacement released from rest, each mode evolves with $\cos(\omega_n(k)t)$. At $x=Vt$ the oscillatory phases are $t(kV\pm\omega_n(k))$. If $0<|V|<c_s$, one phase has a [stationary point](../../../calculus-of-variations.md#stationary-point) with

$$
|k_*|=\frac{q_n|V|}{\sqrt{c_s^2-V^2}},\qquad\omega_n''(k_*)>0.
$$

The [stationary phase method](../../../analysis.md#stationary-phase-method) gives a **generic oscillatory amplitude proportional to $t^{-1/2}$** for each contributing mode. If the initial [Fourier coefficient](../../../fourier-series.md#fourier-coefficient) vanishes at the [stationary point](../../../calculus-of-variations.md#stationary-point), that mode can decay faster; smooth localized data make the modal sum well behaved. If $|V|>c_s$, there is no [stationary point](../../../calculus-of-variations.md#stationary-point). Repeated integration by parts gives rapid decay for smooth rapidly decaying data; for initially compactly supported displacement, [finite propagation speed](../../../wave-equation.md#finite-propagation-speed) makes the displacement on that ray **exactly zero after a sufficiently long time**. These are the requested subsonic and super-shear-speed regimes.

## 37E

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 2](paper-2.md)

<h3 id="37e/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [37E](#37e)

<h4 id="37e/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#37e/a)

Order the interior grid values into a vector. The coefficient matrix has diagonal $4+ch^2$ and off-diagonal entries $-1$ at horizontal and vertical neighbours, with boundary values already zero. Every row is strictly diagonally dominant because $c>0$. The standard convergence theorem for [strict diagonal dominance](../../../vector-space.md#strictly-diagonally-dominant-matrix) gives **convergence of both the [Jacobi method](../../../numerical-analysis.md#jacobi-method) and the [Gauss-Seidel method](../../../numerical-analysis.md#gauss-seidel-method)**. The matrix is also symmetric positive definite.

For $D=(4+ch^2)I$, the [weighted Jacobi method](../../../numerical-analysis.md#weighted-jacobi-method) is $u^{(r+1)}=u^{(r)}+\omega D^{-1}(b-Au^{(r)})$, with iteration matrix $H_\omega=I-\omega D^{-1}A$. The sine-product grid [eigenvectors](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvector) give

$$
\boxed{\lambda_{k,l}=1-\omega\frac{4\{\sin^2(\pi kh/2)+\sin^2(\pi lh/2)\}+ch^2}{4+ch^2},\qquad1\leq k,l\leq m.}
$$

These [weighted Jacobi eigenvalues for a square Dirichlet grid](../../../numerical-analysis.md#weighted-jacobi-eigenvalues-for-a-square-dirichlet-grid) determine convergence. All [eigenvalues](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) of $A$ are positive. Thus for fixed $h$ convergence requires $0<\omega<2(4+ch^2)/a_{\max}$, where $a_{\max}=8\cos^2(\pi h/2)+ch^2$. As $h\downarrow0$, this upper bound tends to $1$ from above. Any fixed $\omega>1$ therefore fails on sufficiently fine grids; $\omega\leq0$ also fails. Consequently **$0<\omega\leq1$ is necessary for all mesh sizes**, and it is sufficient, since $a_{\max}<2(4+ch^2)$ for every finite grid.

<h3 id="37e/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [37E](#37e)

<h4 id="37e/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#37e/b)

A [multigrid method](../../../numerical-analysis.md#multigrid-method) combines a fine-grid smoother with a [coarse-grid correction](../../../numerical-analysis.md#coarse-grid-correction). Starting with $u_h$, apply a few smoothing iterations, compute $r_h=b_h-A_hu_h$, restrict this residual to a coarser grid, and approximately solve $A_He_H=Rr_h$. Prolong the coarse error and update $u_h\leftarrow u_h+Pe_H$, then post-smooth. The coarse solve is itself performed recursively, with a direct solve at the coarsest level; visiting each coarser level once gives a V-cycle. The smoother damps rapidly varying error, while error smooth on the fine grid is efficiently represented and corrected on the coarse grid.

For ordinary Jacobi, $\omega=1$, the highest-frequency sine-product error has [eigenvalue](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) approaching $-1$, not zero. In particular for the discrete Poisson equation it is $-\cos(\pi h)$, whose magnitude approaches one. This nearly alternating error changes sign between sweeps but is barely damped, and ordinary coarse-grid restriction does not represent it well. Thus **with the standard point-Jacobi smoother and coarse-grid transfers one chooses $\omega\ne1$, usually $0<\omega<1$, for fast multigrid convergence**. For example $\omega=2/3$ makes the extreme high-frequency [eigenvalue](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) approach $-1/3$. Convergence of Jacobi as a standalone iteration is therefore insufficient to guarantee that it is an effective smoother. This conclusion concerns that standard multigrid choice; other smoothers or specially designed transfers can change it.
