First prove the [primitive three-cycle criterion](../../../group-theory.md#primitive-three-cycle-criterion) directly. The useful generation fact is that adding a [three-cycle](../../../finite-group-theory.md#three-cycle) whose support meets a set $S$, with $|S|\geq3$, to the [alternating group](../../../finite-group-theory.md#alternating-group) $A_S$ generates the [alternating group](../../../finite-group-theory.md#alternating-group) on the enlarged set. If the support is $\{a,b,x\}$ with $a,b\in S$ and $x\notin S$, [conjugation](../../../group-theory.md#conjugation) by $A_S$ and taking inverses produces every [three-cycle](../../../finite-group-theory.md#three-cycle) with $x$ and two points of $S$. For $|S|\geq4$ this uses [two-transitivity](../../../group-theory.md#two-transitive-group-action) of $A_S$; for $|S|=3$, its cyclic action on pairs together with inversion gives both pair orientations. These cycles, together with the cycles supported inside $S$, are all the [three-cycles](../../../finite-group-theory.md#three-cycle) on $S\cup\{x\}$ and generate $A_{S\cup\{x\}}$. Every even [permutation](../../../combinatorics.md#permutation) is a product of [three-cycles](../../../finite-group-theory.md#three-cycle): pair its transpositions, and use $(a\,b)(b\,c)=(a\,b\,c)$ and $(a\,b)(c\,d)=(a\,c\,b)(a\,c\,d)$, with rightmost factors acting first.

If instead the new support is $\{a,x,y\}$ with just $a\in S$, choose $b\in S\setminus\{a\}$ and conjugate $(a\,x\,y)$ by an element of $A_S$ carrying $a$ to $b$. This gives $(b\,x\,y)$, and

$$
(a\,x\,y)(b\,x\,y)^{-1}=(a\,x\,b).
$$

The two-point-overlap case first adjoins $x$; the original cycle then adjoins $y$. This proves [connected triple supports generate an alternating group](../../../finite-group-theory.md#connected-triple-supports-generate-an-alternating-group) by successively adjoining intersecting supports.

Now take the supports of every conjugate in $G$ of a given [three-cycle](../../../finite-group-theory.md#three-cycle), and make these triples the edges of a [hypergraph](../../../hypergraph.md) on the [permutation](../../../combinatorics.md#permutation) domain. Its connected components are permuted by $G$, so they form a [block system](../../../group-theory.md#block-system). Since at least one component contains three points, [primitivity](../../../group-theory.md#primitive-group-action) forces the single component to be the entire domain. Start with one triple and adjoin intersecting triples until all points have been reached. The generation argument shows that the [subgroup](../../../group.md#subgroup) generated by the conjugate cycles is $A_n$. Hence

$$
\boxed{A_n\leq G.}
$$

To classify the proper [maximal subgroups](../../../group.md#maximal-subgroup) containing a [three-cycle](../../../finite-group-theory.md#three-cycle), distinguish the three possible types of action. A [primitive](../../../group-theory.md#primitive-group-action) [maximal subgroup](../../../group.md#maximal-subgroup) contains $A_n$, and thus equals $A_n$, since $[S_n:A_n]=2$. An intransitive [maximal subgroup](../../../group.md#maximal-subgroup) lies in the full stabilizer $S_k\times S_{n-k}$ of one of its orbits, and maximality makes it that full stabilizer. When $k=n-k$, this stabilizer lies properly inside the [subgroup](../../../group.md#subgroup) that can interchange the two parts, so it is not maximal. Take the smaller part to obtain $1\leq k<n/2$. Such an unequal-part stabilizer is maximal: its transpositions connect all points within each part, and any element outside it carries at least one within-part transposition to a cross-part transposition. The resulting connected graph of transpositions generates $S_n$. To see that last assertion, transpositions along a path generate the transposition of its endpoints by successive conjugations. The stabilizer contains a [three-cycle](../../../finite-group-theory.md#three-cycle) exactly when $n-k\geq3$.

A transitive imprimitive [maximal subgroup](../../../group.md#maximal-subgroup) preserves a nontrivial partition into $b$ blocks of size $a$, with $n=ab$. It equals the full [permutation wreath product](../../../group-theory.md#permutation-wreath-product) $S_a\wr S_b$. This full block stabilizer is maximal: any [permutation](../../../combinatorics.md#permutation) not preserving the partition sends some same-block pair to a different-block pair, giving a cross-block transposition by [conjugation](../../../group-theory.md#conjugation). The block stabilizer is transitive on cross-block pairs, so all cross-block transpositions follow; together with its within-block transpositions they generate $S_n$. A [three-cycle](../../../finite-group-theory.md#three-cycle) in this [group](../../../group.md) must act trivially on the blocks, since any nontrivial block [permutation](../../../combinatorics.md#permutation) moves at least two entire blocks, and a block three-cycle moves $3a$ points. Thus a single [three-cycle](../../../finite-group-theory.md#three-cycle) exists exactly when $a\geq3$. The full classification, up to [conjugation](../../../group-theory.md#conjugation), is therefore

$$
\boxed{A_n;\quad S_k\times S_{n-k}\ (1\leq k<n/2,\ n-k\geq3);\quad S_a\wr S_b\ (n=ab,\ a\geq3,\ b\geq2).}
$$

This is the [maximal symmetric-group subgroups containing a three-cycle](../../../group.md#maximal-symmetric-group-subgroups-containing-a-three-cycle) classification. For $n<3$ there are no such [subgroups](../../../group.md#subgroup).

For the first family of counterexamples to extending the criterion to longer cycles, let $G=PSL_2(p)$ act on the [projective line](../../../finite-group-theory.md#projective-line) $\mathbb F_p\cup\{\infty\}$. The determinant-one [matrices](../../../vector-space.md#matrix)

$$
\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}
$$

act respectively as $x\mapsto x+1$ and $x\mapsto-1/x$. The first fixes infinity and is one $p$-cycle on the finite points. Its translations make the infinity stabilizer transitive on the other points; the second moves infinity, so the full action is [two-transitive](../../../group-theory.md#two-transitive-group-action), hence [primitive](../../../group-theory.md#primitive-group-action). The action is faithful after quotienting by scalar [matrices](../../../vector-space.md#matrix). Counting a nonzero first column and the $p$ second columns with determinant one gives $|SL_2(p)|=p(p^2-1)$, and the scalar [group center](../../../group-theory.md#center-of-a-group) has order two. Thus

$$
|PSL_2(p)|=\frac{p(p^2-1)}2<\frac{(p+1)!}2=|A_{p+1}|\qquad(p>3),
$$

since the ratio on the right to the left is $(p-2)!>1$. Consequently **$PSL_2(p)$ is the required example for every prime $p>3$**.

For the final example take $p=59$, and let the [direct product of groups](../../../group-theory.md#direct-product-of-groups) $A_5\times A_5$ act on the sixty elements of $A_5$ by

$$
(x,y):t\longmapsto xty^{-1}.
$$

The action is transitive and the stabilizer of the identity is the diagonal [subgroup](../../../group.md#subgroup) $\Delta=\{(z,z):z\in A_5\}$. To prove that the diagonal is a [maximal subgroup](../../../group.md#maximal-subgroup), no classification theorem is needed. If $\Delta<K\leq A_5\times A_5$, choose $(x,y)\in K\setminus\Delta$. Multiplying by $(y^{-1},y^{-1})$ gives $(xy^{-1},1)\in K$ with $xy^{-1}\ne1$. The [subgroup](../../../group.md#subgroup) $L=\{z:(z,1)\in K\}$ is normal in $A_5$, by diagonal [conjugation](../../../group-theory.md#conjugation), and is nontrivial. Simplicity of $A_5$ gives $L=A_5$, and then $K=A_5\times A_5$. Thus the [coset](../../../group-theory.md#coset) action is [primitive](../../../group-theory.md#primitive-group-action). It is also faithful: if $xty^{-1}=t$ for every $t$, then $x=y$ by taking $t=1$, and $x$ is central in the centerless [simple group](../../../finite-group-theory.md#simple-group) $A_5$, so $x=y=1$. Finally,

$$
\boxed{n=60=59+1,\qquad |G|=60^2=3600,\qquad59\nmid |G|.}
$$

The prime $59$ is odd, and its primality follows by testing the primes $2,3,5,7$, the only ones below its square root. This is a faithful [diagonal primitive permutation group](../../../group-theory.md#diagonal-primitive-permutation-group) whose order is not divisible by the degree minus one.

A [subnormal series](../../../group-theory.md#subnormal-series) is a finite chain $1=G_r\trianglelefteq G_{r-1}\trianglelefteq\cdots\trianglelefteq G_0=G$, in which each term is normal in the preceding term. Its factors are the [quotient groups](../../../group-theory.md#quotient-group) $G_i/G_{i+1}$. A [normal series of a group](../../../group-theory.md#normal-series-of-a-group) imposes the stronger condition that every term be normal in $G$. These conditions should not be conflated: inserting a [subgroup](../../../group.md#subgroup) normal in an intermediate term need not preserve normality in the whole [group](../../../group.md). A refinement inserts additional terms, and two series are equivalent when their nontrivial factors can be paired by [group isomorphisms](../../../algebra.md#group-isomorphism).

The basic refinement mechanism is the [Zassenhaus butterfly lemma](../../../group-theory.md#zassenhaus-butterfly-lemma). For $A'\trianglelefteq A$ and $B'\trianglelefteq B$, let $C=A\cap B$ and $D=(A'\cap B)(A\cap B')$. Both factors of $D$ are normal in $C$, so $D\trianglelefteq C$. The map from $C$ onto $A'C/A'(A\cap B')$ has kernel $D$: if an element of $C$ equals $a'b'$ with $a'\in A'$ and $b'\in A\cap B'$, then $a'=cb'^{-1}\in A'\cap B$. Conversely each product in $D$ is in the kernel. The [first isomorphism theorem](../../../group-theory.md#first-isomorphism-theorem) therefore gives

$$
\frac{A'(A\cap B)}{A'(A\cap B')}\cong C/D
\cong\frac{B'(A\cap B)}{B'(B\cap A')}.
$$

The denominators are normal in their numerators: [conjugation](../../../group-theory.md#conjugation) by $C$ preserves them, while [conjugation](../../../group-theory.md#conjugation) by $A'$ or $B'$ changes their elements only by a factor already in that [normal subgroup](../../../group-theory.md#normal-subgroup).

Given two [subnormal series](../../../group-theory.md#subnormal-series) $(G_i)$ and $(H_j)$, refine the first by $G_{i+1}(G_i\cap H_j)$, and the second by $H_{j+1}(H_j\cap G_i)$. The endpoints are $G_i,G_{i+1}$ and $H_j,H_{j+1}$ respectively, and the [Zassenhaus butterfly lemma](../../../group-theory.md#zassenhaus-butterfly-lemma) pairs the successive factors indexed by $(i,j)$ and $(j,i)$. This proves the [Schreier refinement theorem](../../../group-theory.md#schreier-refinement-theorem).

A [composition series](../../../finite-group-theory.md#composition-series) is a strict [subnormal series](../../../group-theory.md#subnormal-series) all of whose factors are [simple groups](../../../finite-group-theory.md#simple-group). Every [finite group](../../../group.md#finite-group) has one: choose a maximal proper [normal subgroup](../../../group-theory.md#normal-subgroup), repeat inside it, and use decreasing orders to terminate. A simple factor cannot admit a nontrivial refinement. Applying the preceding refinement theorem to two [composition series](../../../finite-group-theory.md#composition-series) proves the [Jordan–Hölder theorem](../../../finite-group-theory.md#jordan-holder-theorem): their lengths and multisets of simple factors agree, up to [group isomorphism](../../../algebra.md#group-isomorphism). The factors are the [Jordan–Hölder factors](../../../finite-group-theory.md#jordan-holder-factor), and their number is the [composition length](../../../finite-group-theory.md#composition-length). If $N\trianglelefteq G$, lifting a [composition series](../../../finite-group-theory.md#composition-series) of $G/N$ and adjoining one for $N$ gives

$$
\ell(G)=\ell(N)+\ell(G/N).
$$

These factors do not determine the whole [group](../../../group.md), because they do not determine how its [group extensions](../../../group-theory.md#group-extension) are assembled. For example, $C_4$ and the [Klein four-group](../../../finite-group-theory.md#klein-four-group) both have two factors $C_2$, but one contains an element of order four and the other does not. The [symmetric group](../../../finite-group-theory.md#symmetric-group) $S_3$ has a [composition series](../../../finite-group-theory.md#composition-series) $1<C_3<S_3$, with factors $C_3,C_2$, whereas the [simple group](../../../finite-group-theory.md#simple-group) $A_5$ has the single factor $A_5$.

A [chief series](../../../group-theory.md#chief-series) is a maximal strict [normal series of a group](../../../group-theory.md#normal-series-of-a-group); each [chief factor](../../../group-theory.md#chief-factor) is a minimal nontrivial [normal subgroup](../../../group-theory.md#normal-subgroup) in the appropriate quotient of $G$. Finite [chief factors](../../../group-theory.md#chief-factor) are [characteristically simple](../../../algebra.md#characteristically-simple-group). To justify their structure, let $N$ be any nontrivial finite characteristically [simple group](../../../finite-group-theory.md#simple-group) and choose a minimal nontrivial [normal subgroup](../../../group-theory.md#normal-subgroup) $M$ of $N$. Its distinct images under automorphisms of $N$ are minimal [normal subgroups](../../../group-theory.md#normal-subgroup), so distinct ones intersect trivially and commute. Choose a maximal family giving an internal [direct product of groups](../../../group-theory.md#direct-product-of-groups). If any other image were not contained in that product, its intersection with the product would be trivial by minimality, and it could be added, a contradiction. Hence this product is characteristic and equals $N$. Each factor $M$ is simple: a proper nontrivial [normal subgroup](../../../group-theory.md#normal-subgroup) of $M$ would be normal in the whole direct product $N$, contrary to the choice of $M$. Thus $N$ is a [direct product of groups](../../../group-theory.md#direct-product-of-groups) of isomorphic finite [simple groups](../../../finite-group-theory.md#simple-group). This applies to [chief factors](../../../group-theory.md#chief-factor) by [conjugation](../../../group-theory.md#conjugation) from $G$.

Chief and composition factors need not coincide. In $A_4$, the chain $1<V_4<A_4$ is a [chief series](../../../group-theory.md#chief-series), since [conjugation](../../../group-theory.md#conjugation) cycles the three order-two [subgroups](../../../group.md#subgroup) of $V_4$. A [composition series](../../../finite-group-theory.md#composition-series) instead inserts any one of those [subgroups](../../../group.md#subgroup), giving factors $C_2,C_2,C_3$. Its inserted order-two [subgroup](../../../group.md#subgroup) is normal in $V_4$ but not in $A_4$. [Chief series](../../../group-theory.md#chief-series) retain the ambient [group](../../../group.md)'s [conjugation](../../../group-theory.md#conjugation) information that [composition series](../../../finite-group-theory.md#composition-series) discard.

The [derived series](../../../group-theory.md#derived-series) gives a canonical criterion for a [group](../../../group.md) to be [soluble](../../../group-theory.md#solvable-group): put $G^{(0)}=G$ and $G^{(i+1)}=[G^{(i)},G^{(i)}]$. Its terms are characteristic, and each factor is [Abelian](../../../group.md#abelian-group). A [finite group](../../../group.md#finite-group) is [soluble](../../../group-theory.md#solvable-group) exactly when this series reaches the identity, equivalently when all its composition factors are cyclic of prime order. For one direction, the [derived series](../../../group-theory.md#derived-series) supplies [Abelian](../../../group.md#abelian-group) factors, whose simple refinements are cyclic of prime order. Conversely an extension of two soluble [groups](../../../group.md) is soluble: if $(G/N)^{(r)}=1$, then $G^{(r)}\subseteq N$; if $N^{(s)}=1$, then $G^{(r+s)}=1$. Induction up a [composition series](../../../finite-group-theory.md#composition-series) with cyclic prime factors proves the other direction. In a finite soluble [group](../../../group.md), each [minimal normal subgroup](../../../group-theory.md#minimal-normal-subgroup) is elementary [Abelian](../../../group.md#abelian-group): its characteristic [commutator subgroup](../../../group-theory.md#commutator-subgroup) must be trivial, its primary components force a single prime, and its characteristic [subgroup](../../../group.md#subgroup) of $p$th powers must be trivial. Thus every [chief factor](../../../group-theory.md#chief-factor) is a [vector](../../../vector-space.md#vector) space over some $\mathbb F_p$. In a general [finite group](../../../group.md#finite-group) the [Jordan–Hölder factors](../../../finite-group-theory.md#jordan-holder-factor) can instead be nonabelian [simple groups](../../../finite-group-theory.md#simple-group).

The [central series](../../../group-theory.md#central-series) demand more than [Abelian](../../../group.md#abelian-group) factors: each factor must be central in the corresponding quotient of the whole [group](../../../group.md). Define the [upper central series](../../../group-theory.md#upper-central-series) by $Z_0=1$ and $Z_{i+1}/Z_i=Z(G/Z_i)$, and the [lower central series](../../../group-theory.md#lower-central-series) by $\gamma_1=G$ and $\gamma_{i+1}=[\gamma_i,G]$. A [group](../../../group.md) is [nilpotent](../../../commutative-algebra.md#nilpotent) exactly when the upper series reaches $G$, equivalently when the lower series reaches the identity. Indeed, for any ascending [central series](../../../group-theory.md#central-series) $1=C_0\leq\cdots\leq C_c=G$, induction gives $C_i\subseteq Z_i$ and $\gamma_{c+1-i}\subseteq C_i$; reversing the lower series gives an ascending [central series](../../../group-theory.md#central-series) when it terminates. The least such $c$ is the [nilpotency class](../../../group-theory.md#nilpotency-class). A [central series](../../../group-theory.md#central-series) has [Abelian](../../../group.md#abelian-group) factors, so every [nilpotent group](../../../group-theory.md#nilpotent-group) is [soluble](../../../group-theory.md#solvable-group); the converse fails, as $S_3$ is soluble but has trivial [group center](../../../group-theory.md#center-of-a-group).

For a finite [p-group](../../../finite-group-theory.md#p-group), the [class equation](../../../group-theory.md#class-equation) shows that the [group center](../../../group-theory.md#center-of-a-group) is nontrivial, since every noncentral conjugacy class has size divisible by $p$. Applying the same argument to successive central quotients makes the [upper central series](../../../group-theory.md#upper-central-series) reach the [group](../../../group.md). More generally a [finite group](../../../group.md#finite-group) is nilpotent exactly when it is the [direct product of groups](../../../group-theory.md#direct-product-of-groups) of its [Sylow subgroups](../../../finite-group-theory.md#sylow-subgroup). Here is the nontrivial direction. A [central series](../../../group-theory.md#central-series) gives the [normalizer condition for nilpotent groups](../../../group-theory.md#normalizer-condition-for-nilpotent-groups): for a proper [subgroup](../../../group.md#subgroup) $H$, choose the first $Z_i$ not contained in $H$. Then $[Z_i,H]\subseteq Z_{i-1}\subseteq H$, so $Z_i\subseteq N_G(H)$ and $N_G(H)>H$. If a [Sylow subgroup](../../../finite-group-theory.md#sylow-subgroup) $P$ had proper [normalizer](../../../group-theory.md#normalizer) $N$, then $N_G(N)>N$; but $P$ is the unique [Sylow subgroup](../../../finite-group-theory.md#sylow-subgroup) of $N$, hence characteristic there, and $N_G(N)\subseteq N_G(P)=N$, a contradiction. Thus all [Sylow subgroups](../../../finite-group-theory.md#sylow-subgroup) are normal, different ones commute because their intersection is trivial, and they form an internal direct product. Conversely each finite [Sylow subgroup](../../../finite-group-theory.md#sylow-subgroup) is nilpotent, and products of their [central series](../../../group-theory.md#central-series) give a [central series](../../../group-theory.md#central-series) for their direct product.

**[Composition series](../../../finite-group-theory.md#composition-series) record simple building blocks, [chief series](../../../group-theory.md#chief-series) record ambient normal structure, [derived series](../../../group-theory.md#derived-series) detect solubility, and [central series](../../../group-theory.md#central-series) detect nilpotence.** Their associated factors and extension data explain why the same [group](../../../group.md) can have very different useful series.

A [Hall subgroup](../../../group.md#hall-subgroup) for a set of primes $\pi$ is a [subgroup](../../../group.md#subgroup) $H\leq G$ such that every prime divisor of $|H|$ belongs to $\pi$, while no prime divisor of $[G:H]$ belongs to $\pi$. Equivalently $|H|=|G|_\pi$, the product of the full prime powers in $|G|$ belonging to $\pi$.

The [Hall conjugacy and embedding in finite soluble groups](../../../group.md#hall-conjugacy-and-embedding-in-finite-soluble-groups) theorem says that, for every $\pi$, a finite [soluble group](../../../group-theory.md#solvable-group) has a Hall $\pi$-subgroup, any two such [subgroups](../../../group.md#subgroup) are conjugate, and every $\pi$-subgroup is contained in one. We prove all three assertions together by induction, including the needed complement argument rather than assuming it.

First establish [coprime splitting over an elementary abelian normal subgroup](../../../group-theory.md#coprime-splitting-over-an-elementary-abelian-normal-subgroup). Suppose $V\trianglelefteq E$ is an elementary [Abelian](../../../group.md#abelian-group) $r$-group and $D=E/V$ has order $m$ prime to $r$. Write $V$ additively. [Conjugation](../../../group-theory.md#conjugation) defines a well-defined action of $D$ on $V$ because $V$ is [Abelian](../../../group.md#abelian-group). Choose a section $s:D\to E$, with $s(1)=1$, and let its [extension cocycle](../../../group-theory.md#extension-cocycle) be defined by $s(x)s(y)=f(x,y)s(xy)$. Associativity gives

$$
f(x,y)+f(xy,z)=x f(y,z)+f(x,yz).
$$

Sum over $z\in D$. With $S(x)=\sum_z f(x,z)$ this becomes

$$
mf(x,y)+S(xy)=xS(y)+S(x).
$$

Multiplication by $m$ is invertible on $V$, so setting $b(x)=m^{-1}S(x)$ gives $f(x,y)=b(x)+xb(y)-b(xy)$. The new section $s'(x)=(-b(x))s(x)$ has zero cocycle and is a homomorphism. Its image is a complement $K$ to $V$, proving $E=V\rtimes K$.

Every other complement has the form $\{h(x)s'(x):x\in D\}$, with $h(xy)=h(x)+xh(y)$. Summing this identity over $y$ and putting $T=\sum_y h(y)$ gives $mh(x)=T-xT$. Hence $h(x)=v-xv$, where $v=m^{-1}T$, and the other complement is $vKv^{-1}$. The same argument applies on any [subgroup](../../../group.md#subgroup) $D_0\leq D$: a [subgroup](../../../group.md#subgroup) intersecting $V$ trivially is a graph of such a one-cocycle over its image $D_0$, so [conjugation](../../../group-theory.md#conjugation) by an element of $V$ carries it into $K$. This proves the complement-conjugacy and embedding assertions needed below.

Now let $G\ne1$ be finite soluble, and choose a [minimal normal subgroup](../../../group-theory.md#minimal-normal-subgroup) $V$. It is elementary [Abelian](../../../group.md#abelian-group) of some prime characteristic $r$, by the minimal-normal argument in the series discussion. Induction gives Hall [subgroups](../../../group.md#subgroup) and the embedding and conjugacy assertions in $G/V$. Let $\overline H$ be a Hall $\pi$-subgroup there and $M$ its full preimage.

If $r\in\pi$, then $M$ itself is a Hall $\pi$-subgroup of $G$. Every Hall $\pi$-subgroup $H$ contains $V$, since $HV$ is a $\pi$-subgroup and $H$ already has the largest possible $\pi$-order. Thus $H$ is the full preimage of its image in $G/V$, and quotient conjugacy lifts to conjugacy in $G$. Given any $\pi$-subgroup $U$, induction puts its quotient image into some $\overline H$, whose preimage $M$ contains $U$.

If $r\notin\pi$, then $M/V=\overline H$ has order prime to $r$. The complement construction produces $K\leq M$ of order $|\overline H|=|G|_\pi$, hence a Hall [subgroup](../../../group.md#subgroup) of $G$. Any Hall [subgroup](../../../group.md#subgroup) has trivial intersection with $V$ and a Hall image in $G/V$. Conjugating two Hall [subgroups](../../../group.md#subgroup) first aligns these images, after which both are complements to $V$ in the same $M$; the complement-conjugacy argument then conjugates them by an element of $V$. For an arbitrary $\pi$-subgroup $U$, quotient induction puts its image into some $\overline H$. Then $U\subseteq M$ and $U\cap V=1$, so the embedding argument conjugates $U$ into the chosen complement $K$. Equivalently $U$ lies in a conjugate of $K$. This completes the induction and all three parts of the [Hall theorem for soluble groups](../../../group.md#hall-conjugacy-and-embedding-in-finite-soluble-groups).

For the specific [general linear group over a finite field](../../../finite-group-theory.md#general-linear-group-over-a-finite-field), counting ordered bases gives

$$
|GL_3(2)|=(8-1)(8-2)(8-4)=168.
$$

There are seven lines and seven planes in $\mathbb F_2^3$. The [group](../../../group.md) is transitive on each family, so line and plane stabilizers both have order $24$ and index seven. To show that these exhaust the index-seven [subgroups](../../../group.md#subgroup), let $H$ have order $24$. It contains a Sylow $2$-subgroup of order eight, which can be conjugated to

$$
U=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}1&a&b\\0&1&d\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}:a,b,d\in\mathbb F_2\right\}.
$$

The [group orbits](../../../group-theory.md#orbit-of-a-group-action) of $U$ on the seven nonzero [vectors](../../../vector-space.md#vector) are

$$
\{e_1\},\quad\{e_2,e_1+e_2\},\quad
\{e_3,e_1+e_3,e_2+e_3,e_1+e_2+e_3\},
$$

of sizes $1,2,4$. An $H$-orbit is a union of these. The [group](../../../group.md) $H$ cannot be transitive on seven points, since seven does not divide $24$. Therefore it has an orbit of size at most three. A size-one orbit fixes $e_1$. A size-two orbit must be $\{e_2,e_1+e_2\}$, whose [vector](../../../vector-space.md#vector) sum $e_1$ is consequently fixed. A size-three orbit must be the union of the first two $U$-orbits, the nonzero part of the plane $\langle e_1,e_2\rangle$, so that plane is invariant. In every case $H$ lies in a line or plane stabilizer, and equality follows from their equal orders.

The two stabilizer families are not conjugate. A line stabilizer fixes its nonzero [vector](../../../vector-space.md#vector) and is transitive on the other six nonzero [vectors](../../../vector-space.md#vector): extend that fixed [vector](../../../vector-space.md#vector) and either chosen other [vector](../../../vector-space.md#vector) to a basis, and map one such basis to the other. Thus it has no invariant three-element subset of nonzero [vectors](../../../vector-space.md#vector) and fixes no plane. In contrast a plane stabilizer fixes its defining plane. [Conjugation](../../../group-theory.md#conjugation) sends line stabilizers to line stabilizers, so cannot send one to a plane stabilizer. We obtain

$$
\boxed{\text{Exactly two conjugacy classes of index-seven subgroups: line stabilizers and plane stabilizers.}}
$$

They are [Hall subgroups](../../../group.md#hall-subgroup) for $\pi=\{2,3\}$ but are not conjugate. This explicitly shows why the solubility hypothesis of the [Hall theorem for soluble groups](../../../group.md#hall-conjugacy-and-embedding-in-finite-soluble-groups) matters.

Finally, an index-three [subgroup](../../../group.md#subgroup) would give a nontrivial transitive [coset](../../../group-theory.md#coset) homomorphism $GL_3(2)\to S_3$. Its kernel is normal; the permitted simplicity of $GL_3(2)$ forces that kernel to be trivial, because the action is nontrivial. But an injective map from a [group](../../../group.md) of order $168$ into one of order six is impossible. Hence **$GL_3(2)$ has no [subgroup](../../../group.md#subgroup) of index three**.

## 4

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="4/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#4)

For the [transfer homomorphism](../../../group-theory.md#transfer-group-theory), choose representatives $T$ of the right [cosets](../../../group-theory.md#coset) $H\backslash G$. For $t\in T$ and $g\in G$, write $tg=h(t,g)t_g$, where $h(t,g)\in H$ and $t_g\in T$. The [permutation](../../../combinatorics.md#permutation) $t\mapsto t_g$ represents right multiplication on the [cosets](../../../group-theory.md#coset). Define

$$
\boxed{V_{G,H}(g)=\prod_{t\in T}h(t,g)\pmod{H'}.}
$$

The product has no order ambiguity in the [abelianization](../../../group-theory.md#abelianization) $H/H'$. Replace each representative $t$ by $a_tt$, with $a_t\in H$. The new factor is $a_t h(t,g)a_{t_g}^{-1}$. In the [Abelian](../../../group.md#abelian-group) quotient, the extra products cancel because $t\mapsto t_g$ permutes $T$. Thus the transfer is independent of the transversal. Moreover,

$$
h(t,g_1g_2)=h(t,g_1)h(t_{g_1},g_2).
$$

Multiplying and using the same [permutation](../../../combinatorics.md#permutation) of representatives gives $V(g_1g_2)=V(g_1)V(g_2)$. This proves that [group transfer](../../../group-theory.md#transfer-group-theory) is a well-defined [group homomorphism](../../../group-theory.md#group-homomorphism).

For the [Burnside transfer theorem](../../../group-theory.md#burnside-transfer-theorem), let $P\subseteq Z(N_G(P))$ be a Sylow $p$-subgroup. In particular $P$ is [Abelian](../../../group.md#abelian-group). We first prove the fusion fact that two elements of $P$ which are conjugate in $G$ must be equal. Suppose $u\in P$ and $v=g^{-1}ug\in P$. Both $P$ and $g^{-1}Pg$ centralize $v$, and they are [Sylow subgroups](../../../finite-group-theory.md#sylow-subgroup) of $C_G(v)$: they have the maximal $p$-order possible even in $G$. By [Sylow theorems](../../../finite-group-theory.md#sylow-theorems) inside this [centralizer](../../../group-theory.md#centralizer), choose $h\in C_G(v)$ with $h^{-1}g^{-1}Pgh=P$. Thus $gh\in N_G(P)$ and

$$
(gh)^{-1}u(gh)=h^{-1}vh=v.
$$

But $gh$ centralizes $P$ by hypothesis, so $v=u$.

Evaluate transfer to $P$ on $u\in P$. Partition the right [cosets](../../../group-theory.md#coset) of $P$ into cycles under multiplication by $u$. For a cycle of length $r$ beginning at $Pt$, the product of its transfer factors telescopes to $tu^rt^{-1}\in P$. It is conjugate to $u^r\in P$, so the fusion fact makes it equal to $u^r$. The cycle lengths sum to $m=[G:P]$. Since $P$ is [Abelian](../../../group.md#abelian-group), all factors commute and

$$
V_{G,P}(u)=u^m.
$$

The integer $m$ is prime to $p$, and this power map is an automorphism of the finite [Abelian](../../../group.md#abelian-group) $p$-group $P$: choose an inverse exponent modulo the exponent of $P$. Therefore $V_{G,P}:G\to P$ is surjective. Its kernel $K$ is normal and the [first isomorphism theorem](../../../group-theory.md#first-isomorphism-theorem) gives

$$
\boxed{[G:K]=|P|,\qquad |K|=[G:P].}
$$

Thus $K$ is the required [normal p-complement](../../../group-theory.md#normal-p-complement); it also has trivial intersection with $P$.

Now suppose $P$ is cyclic of order $p^a$, where $p$ is the smallest prime divisor of $|G|$. [Conjugation](../../../group-theory.md#conjugation) embeds $N_G(P)/C_G(P)$ into $\operatorname{Aut}(P)$, whose order is $p^{a-1}(p-1)$. Since $P\subseteq C_G(P)$ and $P$ is Sylow in $N_G(P)$, the quotient has order prime to $p$. Any remaining prime factor would divide $p-1$ and hence be smaller than $p$, but would also divide $|G|$, an impossibility. Thus $N_G(P)=C_G(P)$ and $P\subseteq Z(N_G(P))$. The [Burnside transfer theorem](../../../group-theory.md#burnside-transfer-theorem) proves that **a cyclic [Sylow subgroup](../../../finite-group-theory.md#sylow-subgroup) at the least prime divisor gives a normal complement**.

Apply this to a finite nonabelian [simple group](../../../finite-group-theory.md#simple-group). If $p^3\nmid |G|$, its Sylow $p$-subgroup has order $p$ or $p^2$. A cyclic [Sylow subgroup](../../../finite-group-theory.md#sylow-subgroup) would give a normal complement; simplicity would then force either a trivial complement and a $p$-group, or an improper complement, neither possible for a nonabelian [simple group](../../../finite-group-theory.md#simple-group). [Groups](../../../group.md) of order $p^2$ are [Abelian](../../../group.md#abelian-group): their nontrivial [group center](../../../group-theory.md#center-of-a-group), supplied by the [class equation](../../../group-theory.md#class-equation), is either the whole [group](../../../group.md) or has cyclic quotient, which also forces the [group](../../../group.md) [Abelian](../../../group.md#abelian-group). Thus the only remaining Sylow possibility is $P\cong C_p^2$.

The [conjugation](../../../group-theory.md#conjugation) quotient $N_G(P)/C_G(P)$ now embeds in $GL_2(p)$ and is still prime to $p$. Its possible prime divisors come from

$$
|GL_2(p)|=(p^2-1)(p^2-p)=p(p-1)^2(p+1).
$$

If $p$ is odd, every prime divisor of $p-1$ is smaller than $p$, and every prime divisor of the even number $p+1$ is at most $(p+1)/2<p$. Since $p$ was the least prime divisor of $|G|$, the quotient must be trivial. Then the [Burnside transfer theorem](../../../group-theory.md#burnside-transfer-theorem) again contradicts simplicity. If $p=2$, the only nontrivial odd divisor of $|GL_2(2)|=6$ is three. Trivial [conjugation](../../../group-theory.md#conjugation) would again give a normal complement, so three must divide $|G|$; the [Sylow subgroup](../../../finite-group-theory.md#sylow-subgroup) has order four. We have proved the [least-prime divisibility constraint for a finite simple group](../../../finite-group-theory.md#least-prime-divisibility-constraint-for-a-finite-simple-group):

$$
\boxed{p^3\mid |G|\quad\text{or}\quad p=2\text{ and }12\mid |G|.}
$$

For infinitely many instances of the latter alternative only, take

$$
G_m=PSL_2(5^{2m+1}),\qquad m\geq0.
$$

These are simple, as permitted in the question (and also covered by the next solution). For $q=5^{2m+1}$, the [group](../../../group.md) order is $q(q^2-1)/2$. Since $q\equiv5\pmod8$, $q-1$ is divisible by four but not eight and $q+1$ by two but not four. Hence the order is divisible by four but not eight. Also $q\not\equiv0\pmod3$, so $3\mid q^2-1$. Their smallest prime divisor is two and

$$
\boxed{12\mid |G_m|,\qquad8\nmid |G_m|.}
$$

Their orders strictly increase with $m$, so they give infinitely many distinct examples.

## 5

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="5/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#5)

Write $x_{ij}(t)=I+tE_{ij}$ for $i\ne j$ and $t\in\mathbb F_q$. When $t\ne0$ these are [transvections](../../../vector-space.md#transvection), and their inverses are $x_{ij}(-t)$. Row addition by such an [elementary transvection matrix](../../../vector-space.md#elementary-transvection-matrix) preserves the [determinant](../../../linear-algebra.md#determinant). It also realizes signed interchanges and compensating diagonal scalings: in any two coordinate positions,

$$
w(a)=\begin{pmatrix}1&a\\0&1\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\-a^{-1}&1\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}1&a\\0&1\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}0&a\\-a^{-1}&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
w(a)w(-1)=\begin{pmatrix}a&0\\0&a^{-1}\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Thus elimination can reduce any invertible [matrix](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) to diagonal form using only row additions and signed interchanges, all products of [transvections](../../../vector-space.md#transvection). For a diagonal [matrix](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) with determinant one, $\operatorname{diag}(d_1,\ldots,d_n)$, use the displayed two-coordinate diagonal [matrices](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) in positions $(i,n)$ with parameter $d_i$, for $1\leq i<n$. Their product has diagonal entries $d_1,\ldots,d_{n-1},\prod_{i<n}d_i^{-1}=d_n$. Hence the residual diagonal [matrix](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) is also a product of [transvections](../../../vector-space.md#transvection), and reversing elimination proves

$$
\boxed{SL_n(q)=\langle x_{ij}(t):i\ne j,\ t\in\mathbb F_q\rangle.}
$$

This proves [transvections generate the special linear group](../../../vector-space.md#transvections-generate-the-special-linear-group), including the small fields.

For perfectness, use the convention $[x,y]=xyx^{-1}y^{-1}$ in the following calculations. If $n\geq3$ and $i,j,k$ are distinct, direct [matrix](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) multiplication gives

$$
[x_{ik}(a),x_{kj}(b)]=x_{ij}(ab).
$$

Taking $b=1$ puts every elementary generator in the [commutator subgroup](../../../group-theory.md#commutator-subgroup). Thus $SL_n(q)$ is [perfect](../../../group-theory.md#perfect-group). When $n=2$ and $q>3$, choose $s\in\mathbb F_q^\times$ with $s^2\ne1$; such an element exists because a degree-two polynomial has at most two roots and $q-1>2$. For $D=\operatorname{diag}(s,s^{-1})$,

$$
[D,x_{12}(t)]=x_{12}((s^2-1)t),\qquad
[D,x_{21}(t)]=x_{21}((s^{-2}-1)t).
$$

Both coefficients are nonzero, so varying $t$ supplies every upper and lower elementary generator as a commutator. Therefore

$$
\boxed{SL_n(q)\text{ is perfect for }n\geq3\text{ or }(n=2,q>3).}
$$

The exceptions really fail: $SL_2(2)\cong S_3$ has a nontrivial sign quotient, and $SL_2(3)$ has quotient $PSL_2(3)\cong A_4$, which has the nontrivial [Abelian](../../../group.md#abelian-group) quotient $A_4/V_4\cong C_3$. For the latter identification, the faithful projective action embeds the order-twelve [group](../../../group.md) $PSL_2(3)$ as an index-two [subgroup](../../../group.md#subgroup) of $S_4$, necessarily $A_4$.

The [Iwasawa simplicity lemma](../../../finite-group-theory.md#iwasawa-simplicity-lemma) states that, in a faithful [primitive group action](../../../group-theory.md#primitive-group-action), if a point stabilizer has an [Abelian](../../../group.md#abelian-group) [normal subgroup](../../../group-theory.md#normal-subgroup) whose conjugates generate the whole [group](../../../group.md), then every nontrivial [normal subgroup](../../../group-theory.md#normal-subgroup) contains the [commutator subgroup](../../../group-theory.md#commutator-subgroup). In particular a nontrivial perfect [group](../../../group.md) with this property is simple. The lemma is applied here, as allowed, without assuming the desired simplicity theorem.

Let $SL_n(q)$ act on the one-dimensional subspaces of $\mathbb F_q^n$. This action is [two-transitive](../../../group-theory.md#two-transitive-group-action): a linear map taking two specified distinct lines to two others can be made determinant one by rescaling one of the target basis [vectors](../../../vector-space.md#vector), without changing either target line. Its kernel consists exactly of scalar [matrices](../../../vector-space.md#matrix). Indeed fixing all lines makes a [matrix](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) diagonal in any fixed basis, and fixing the lines through $e_i+e_j$ makes all its diagonal entries equal. The scalar [matrices](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) in $SL_n(q)$ are $aI$ with $a^n=1$. They also form its [group center](../../../group-theory.md#center-of-a-group), since a central [matrix](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) commutes with every elementary generator, forcing all off-diagonal entries to vanish and all diagonal entries to agree. Their number is $\gcd(n,q-1)$. Consequently the quotient $PSL_n(q)$ acts faithfully and primitively.

Fix a line $L=\langle v\rangle$ and consider

$$
T_L=\{I+vf:f\in(\mathbb F_q^n)^*,\ f(v)=0\}.
$$

Here $vf$ is the rank-at-most-one map $z\mapsto f(z)v$. These [matrices](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) have determinant one, and $(vf)(vh)=0$, so $T_L$ is [Abelian](../../../group.md#abelian-group) under addition of the functionals. If $gL=L$, then $gv=av$ and $g(I+vf)g^{-1}=I+av(fg^{-1})\in T_L$. Thus $T_L$ is normal in the line stabilizer. Its image in $PSL_n(q)$ is a nontrivial [Abelian](../../../group.md#abelian-group) [normal subgroup](../../../group-theory.md#normal-subgroup) of the point stabilizer. Conjugates of these [groups](../../../group.md) include every elementary [transvection](../../../vector-space.md#transvection), hence generate $PSL_n(q)$. Perfectness passes to a quotient. The [Iwasawa simplicity lemma](../../../finite-group-theory.md#iwasawa-simplicity-lemma) therefore gives

$$
\boxed{PSL_n(q)\text{ is simple unless }(n,q)=(2,2),(2,3).}
$$

In those exceptions the projective [groups](../../../group.md) are $S_3$ and $A_4$, as above.

For $q=4$, counting columns gives $|SL_2(4)|=4(4^2-1)=60$. Its scalar [group center](../../../group-theory.md#center-of-a-group) is trivial, so $PSL_2(4)$ has order sixty. Its faithful action on the five points of the [projective line](../../../finite-group-theory.md#projective-line) embeds it in $S_5$. Because it is perfect, its sign homomorphism is trivial and its image lies in $A_5$. Equal orders then give

$$
\boxed{PSL_2(4)\cong A_5.}
$$

For $q=5$, use an explicit [subgroup](../../../group.md#subgroup) to obtain a five-point action. In $SL_2(5)$ set

$$
i=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\4&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
j=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&3\end{pmatrix},\qquad
r=\begin{pmatrix}3&2\\1&1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

All three have determinant one. [Matrix](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) multiplication gives $i^2=j^2=-I$, $ij=-ji$, and $r^3=I$. The eight distinct [matrices](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) $\{\pm I,\pm i,\pm j,\pm ij\}$ form a [quaternion group](../../../finite-group-theory.md#quaternion-group). Moreover,

$$
r i r^{-1}=ij,\qquad r(ij)r^{-1}=j,\qquad r j r^{-1}=i.
$$

Thus $r$ normalizes that quaternion [group](../../../group.md) and has order three. Their generated [subgroup](../../../group.md#subgroup) has order $24$ and contains the scalar [group center](../../../group-theory.md#center-of-a-group) $\{\pm I\}$. Its image in $PSL_2(5)$ has order twelve, hence index five since $|PSL_2(5)|=5(25-1)/2=60$. The resulting [coset](../../../group-theory.md#coset) action is nontrivial and, by the simplicity already proved, faithful. Perfectness again puts its image in $A_5$, and equal orders imply

$$
\boxed{PSL_2(5)\cong A_5.}
$$

This is the [quaternion construction of an index-five subgroup of PSL2 over F5](../../../finite-group-theory.md#quaternion-construction-of-an-index-five-subgroup-of-psl2-over-f5); it avoids assuming a classification of [groups](../../../group.md) of order sixty.

For the last comparison, the [order of a general linear group over a finite field](../../../finite-group-theory.md#order-of-a-general-linear-group-over-a-finite-field) comes from choosing independent columns:

$$
|GL_n(q)|=\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}(q^n-q^i).
$$

The determinant map onto $\mathbb F_q^\times$ has kernel $SL_n(q)$, and the scalar quotient divides by $\gcd(n,q-1)$. Hence

$$
|PSL_4(2)|=(16-1)(16-2)(16-4)(16-8)=20160,
$$



$$
|PSL_3(4)|=\frac{(64-1)(64-4)(64-16)}{(4-1)\gcd(3,3)}=20160.
$$

The highest power of two dividing either order is $64$. Their Sylow $2$-subgroups are respectively the [upper unitriangular groups](../../../finite-group-theory.md#upper-unitriangular-group) $U_4(2)$ of order $2^6$ and the image of $U_3(4)$ of order $4^3$. The latter image is isomorphic to $U_3(4)$ because its intersection with the odd-order scalar [group center](../../../group-theory.md#center-of-a-group) is trivial.

The [center of an upper unitriangular group](../../../finite-group-theory.md#center-of-an-upper-unitriangular-group) can be computed directly here. Commuting $X\in U_4(2)$ with $I+E_{12}$ forces $X_{23}=X_{24}=0$; commuting with $I+E_{23}$ forces $X_{12}=X_{34}=0$; commuting with $I+E_{34}$ forces $X_{13}=0$. Only $X_{14}$ can remain, and those [matrices](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) commute with the whole [group](../../../group.md). Thus $Z(U_4(2))=\{I+tE_{14}:t\in\mathbb F_2\}$ has order two. In $U_3(4)$, commutation with $I+E_{12}$ and $I+E_{23}$ similarly forces $X_{23}=X_{12}=0$, leaving the arbitrary entry $X_{13}\in\mathbb F_4$. Its [group center](../../../group-theory.md#center-of-a-group) has order four. An isomorphism of the whole [groups](../../../group.md) would send a [Sylow subgroup](../../../finite-group-theory.md#sylow-subgroup) to a [Sylow subgroup](../../../finite-group-theory.md#sylow-subgroup) and preserve its [group center](../../../group-theory.md#center-of-a-group) order, which is impossible. Therefore

$$
\boxed{|PSL_4(2)|=|PSL_3(4)|=20160,\qquad PSL_4(2)\not\cong PSL_3(4).}
$$
