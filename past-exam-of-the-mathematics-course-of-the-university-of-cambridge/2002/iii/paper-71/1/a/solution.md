<h1 id="1/a/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [A](../a.md)

A [photon](../../../../../../photon.md) loses [energy](../../../../../../energy.md) by $1+z=a_0/a_e$ between emission and reception. The interval between successive [photon](../../../../../../photon.md) arrivals is also longer by $1+z$, so the received [energy](../../../../../../energy.md) per unit time is reduced by $(1+z)^2$. An isotropic wavefront at reception has area $4\pi(a_0r)^2$: the astronomical radial coordinate is an areal coordinate, even in curved space. Hence

$$
F=\frac{L}{4\pi(a_0r)^2(1+z)^2},\qquad\boxed{d_L\equiv\sqrt{\frac{L}{4\pi F}}=a_0r(1+z).}
$$

This defines the [luminosity distance](../../../../../../luminosity-distance.md). [Photon](../../../../../../photon.md) absorption or conversion would require a further transmission factor; the relation assumes freely propagating conserved [photons](../../../../../../photon.md) and bolometric [luminosity](../../../../../../luminosity.md). The radial [cosmological proper distance](../../../../../../proper-distance-in-cosmology.md) is generally different from the areal distance $a_0r$.
