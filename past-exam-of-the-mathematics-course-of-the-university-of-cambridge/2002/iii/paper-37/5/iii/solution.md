<h1 id="5/iii/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](../iii.md)

Let the initial [genotype](../../../../../../genotype.md) frequencies be arbitrary $u,v,w$ for $a/a,a/b,b/b$, with $u+v+w=1$. [Mendelian segregation](../../../../../../mendelian-segregation.md) makes the [allele](../../../../../../allele.md) frequency among gametes

$$
p=u+\frac v2,\qquad 1-p=w+\frac v2.
$$

In random mating, the two parents are selected independently. If $g(G)$ is a parent's transmission probability for allele $a$, then independence gives $\mathbb E[g(G_1)g(G_2)]=\mathbb E g(G_1)\mathbb E g(G_2)=p^2$. Thus the two independently united gametes have [allele](../../../../../../allele.md) probabilities $p,1-p$, even though the two alleles within an original parent need not have been independent. The offspring frequencies are therefore

$$
\boxed{p^2,\quad2p(1-p),\quad(1-p)^2.}
$$

No initial condition $v^2=4uw$ was used. This proves [one-generation random-mating equilibrium](../../../../../../one-generation-random-mating-equilibrium.md). The usual assumptions are a common allele pool in the two sexes, random union of gametes, ordinary [Mendelian segregation](../../../../../../mendelian-segregation.md), and no intervening mutation or genotype-dependent survival selection. Unequal male and female allele frequencies, for example, would instead give $p_m p_f$ for $a/a$, not generally the square of a single common frequency.
