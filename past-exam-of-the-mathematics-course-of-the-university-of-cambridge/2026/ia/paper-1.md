The principal value is $\log z=\log|z|+i\operatorname{Arg}z$, where $-\pi\lt \operatorname{Arg}z\leq\pi$ and $z\ne0$.

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1a/b)

Since $1+i=\sqrt2e^{i\pi/4}$, the principal value is $\tfrac12\log2+i\pi/4$. All values are $\tfrac12\log2+i(\pi/4+2\pi k)$, $k\in\mathbb Z$.

<h3 id="1a/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1A](#1a)

<h4 id="1a/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#1a/c)

Write $2=2e^{2\pi i k}$. Then $z=i+2^{1/5}e^{2\pi i k/5}$ for $k=0,\ldots,4$: the vertices of a regular pentagon of radius $2^{1/5}$ centred at $i$.

## 3E

The radius is $R=\sup\{r\geq0:\sum a_nz^n\text{ converges whenever }|z|\lt r\}$; equivalently $R^{-1}=\limsup|a_n|^{1/n}$. The same definition applies over $\mathbb R$ and $\mathbb C$.

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3e/b)

Here $a_{2n}=(-1)^n$ and $a_{2n+1}=0$, so $\sum a_nz^n=\sum(-z^2)^n=(1+z^2)^{-1}=((z-i)(z+i))^{-1}$ for $|z|\lt 1$.

<h4 id="3e/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3e/b)

Along real $z=x\uparrow1$, the [modulus](../../../complex-analysis.md#modulus) is $1/(1+x^2)$ and stays bounded, but along $z=iy$ with $y\uparrow1$ it is $1/(1-y^2)\to\infty$. Since $\limsup|a_n|^{1/n}=1$, $R=1$.

## 4E

<h3 id="4e/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4E](#4e)

<h4 id="4e/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#4e/b)

$a_n\to a$ means that for every $\varepsilon\gt 0$ there is $N$ such that $n\ge N$ implies $|a_n-a|\lt \varepsilon$.

<h3 id="4e/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4E](#4e)

<h4 id="4e/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#4e/c)

If $(a_n)$ is bounded, apply Bolzano–Weierstrass to $(\Re a_n,\Im a_n)$ (or successively to both coordinates). If it is unbounded, recursively choose $n_k\gt n_{k-1}$ with $|a_{n_k}|\ge k^2$. The alternatives are not exclusive: $0,1,0,4,0,9,\ldots$ has both kinds of subsequence.

$x\cdot y=\sum_i x_iy_i$ and $\|x\|=(x\cdot x)^{1/2}$.

<h3 id="5a/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [5A](#5a)

<h4 id="5a/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#5a/b)

[Cauchy-Schwarz inequality](../../../probability-and-statistics.md#cauchy-schwarz-inequality) states $|x\cdot y|\le\|x\|\|y\|$. For $y\ne0$, positivity of $\|x-ty\|^2$ at $t=(x\cdot y)/\|y\|^2$ gives $\|x\|^2-(x\cdot y)^2/\|y\|^2\ge0$; $y=0$ is immediate.

<h4 id="5a/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#5a/c)

$(b-a)\times(c-a)=(-1,0,0)\times(-1,-1,1)=(0,1,1)$. Thus one choice is $\hat n=(0,1,1)/\sqrt2$ and $\beta=\sqrt2$.

<h4 id="5a/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#5a/d)

The distance is $|d\cdot\hat n-\beta|=|3/\sqrt2-\sqrt2|=1/\sqrt2$.

<h3 id="5a/e">e</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [5A](#5a)

<h4 id="5a/e/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [E](#5a/e)

The centre-to-plane distance is $|g-2|/\sqrt2$. Intersection occurs iff this is at most $2\sqrt2$, i.e. $-2\le g\le6$. Equality gives one tangent point; strict inequality gives a circle of radius $\sqrt{8-(g-2)^2/2}$.

## 7B

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="7b/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [7B](#7b)

<h4 id="7b/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#7b/a)

From $Hv=\mu v$, $v^\dagger Hv=\mu\|v\|^2$ is real, so $\mu\in\mathbb R$. For distinct [eigenvalues](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue), $\mu_i v_i^\dagger v_j=v_i^\dagger Hv_j=\mu_jv_i^\dagger v_j$, hence orthogonality. Normalising the [eigenvectors](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvector) and taking them as the rows of $U$ gives $H=U^\dagger\Lambda U$.

<h3 id="7b/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [7B](#7b)

<h4 id="7b/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#7b/b)

$\chi_H(\lambda)=\det(H-\lambda I)=\prod_i(\mu_i-\lambda)$ for this sign convention.

<h3 id="7b/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [7B](#7b)

<h4 id="7b/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#7b/c)

The subspace $y^\perp$ is fixed by $M=I-xy^\dagger$, while the remaining [determinant](../../../linear-algebra.md#determinant) factor is $1-y^\dagger x$. Thus $\det M=1-y^*\cdot x$.

<h3 id="7b/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [7B](#7b)

<h4 id="7b/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#7b/d)

Apply the [matrix determinant lemma](../../../linear-algebra.md#matrix-determinant-lemma) to $HP-\lambda I=(H-\lambda I)-Hu,u^T$: its [determinant](../../../linear-algebra.md#determinant) is $\chi_H(\lambda)[1-u^T(H-\lambda I)^{-1}Hu]$.

<h3 id="7b/e">e</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [7B](#7b)

<h4 id="7b/e/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [E](#7b/e)

$Pu=0$, hence $HPu=0$. In the diagonal [basis](../../../vector-space.md#basis), $f(\lambda)=1-\sum_j u_j^2\mu_j/(\mu_j-\lambda)=-\lambda\sum_j u_j^2/(\mu_j-\lambda)$. Between consecutive $\mu_j$ its nonzero factor is strictly monotone from one infinite sign to the other, giving one root; zero weights give equality at endpoints. Hence $\mu_1\le\sigma_1\le\mu_2\le\cdots\le\sigma_{n-1}\le\mu_n$.

## 8B

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="8b/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [8B](#8b)

<h4 id="8b/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#8b/a)

All three Pauli [matrices](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) are Hermitian. Direct multiplication gives $\sigma_a^2=I$ and, for $a\ne b$, $\sigma_a\sigma_b=i\varepsilon_{abc}\sigma_c$, which combines as the displayed identity.

<h3 id="8b/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [8B](#8b)

<h4 id="8b/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#8b/b)

Because $(n\cdot\sigma)^2=I$, even and odd powers sum separately: $R=I\cos\theta+i(n\cdot\sigma)\sin\theta$. Then $RR^\dagger=I$.

<h3 id="8b/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [8B](#8b)

<h4 id="8b/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#8b/c)

$A=\begin{pmatrix}x_3&x_1-ix_2\\x_1+ix_2&-x_3\end{pmatrix}$, so $\operatorname{tr}A=0$ and $\det A=-|x|^2$. Unitary conjugation preserves Hermiticity, trace, and [determinant](../../../linear-algebra.md#determinant); every traceless Hermitian $2\times2$ [matrix](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) is $x\prime\cdot\sigma$ with real $x\prime$, and $|x\prime|=|x|$.

<h3 id="8b/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [8B](#8b)

<h4 id="8b/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#8b/d)

Using $(a\cdot\sigma)(b\cdot\sigma)=(a\cdot b)I+i(a\times b)\cdot\sigma$ gives

$$
x\prime=x\cos(2\theta)-(n\times x)\sin(2\theta)+n(n\cdot x)(1-\cos(2\theta)).
$$

The sign follows from the convention $R=e^{i\theta n\cdot\sigma}$.

## 9E

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="9e/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [9E](#9e)

<h4 id="9e/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#9e/a)

For a [polynomial](../../../polynomial.md) of degree $d$, Taylor expansion about $x_0$ terminates: $P(x_0+h)=\sum_{n=0}^dP^{(n)}(x_0)h^n/n!$.

<h3 id="9e/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [9E](#9e)

<h4 id="9e/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#9e/b)

If $f$ has $n+1$ [derivatives](../../../calculus.md#derivative) near $x_0$, then $f(x)=\sum_{k=0}^nf^{(k)}(x_0)(x-x_0)^k/k!+f^{(n+1)}(\xi)(x-x_0)^{n+1}/(n+1)!$ for some intermediate $\xi$.

<h3 id="9e/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [9E](#9e)

<h4 id="9e/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#9e/c)

Choose a compact interval around $x_0$ and its constant $C$. For $|h|\lt 1/C$, the Lagrange remainder after order $n$ is at most $(C|h|)^{n+1}\to0$. Thus the [taylor series](../../../calculus.md#taylor-series) with $a_n=f^{(n)}(x_0)/n!$ equals $f(x_0+h)$.

<h3 id="9e/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [9E](#9e)

<h4 id="9e/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#9e/d)

$e^x$ is real analytic because $e^{x_0+h}=e^{x_0}\sum_{n\ge0}h^n/n!$; it is not a [polynomial](../../../polynomial.md) since all its [derivatives](../../../calculus.md#derivative) are nonzero.

## 10E

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="10e/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [10E](#10e)

<h4 id="10e/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#10e/a)

If $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$, [differentiable](../../../analysis.md#differentiable-function) on $(a,b)$, and $f(a)=f(b)$, then some $c\in(a,b)$ has $f\prime(c)=0$. A maximum or minimum is attained; unless $f$ is constant, one extremum lies inside, where the [derivative](../../../calculus.md#derivative) vanishes.

<h3 id="10e/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [10E](#10e)

<h4 id="10e/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#10e/b)

The [mean value theorem](../../../calculus.md#mean-value-theorem) gives $f(b)-f(a)=(b-a)f\prime(c)\gt 0$.

<h3 id="10e/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [10E](#10e)

<h4 id="10e/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#10e/c)

Since $f\prime\prime\prime\lt 0$, $f\prime\prime$ decreases from $0$, so $f\prime$ strictly decreases from $1$ and $f(x)\lt x$. If $f\prime$ never vanished, its decreasing positive [limit](../../../calculus.md#limit-of-a-function) would make $f$ stay positive, but $f\prime\prime\lt 0$ bounded away from zero after any fixed point forces $f\prime$ negative eventually. Thus $f$ rises to one maximum and then decreases strictly to $-\infty$, crossing zero exactly once; it is positive before that crossing.

<h3 id="10e/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [10E](#10e)

<h4 id="10e/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#10e/d)

$\sin x=\sum_{n\ge0}(-1)^nx^{2n+1}/(2n+1)!$ and $\cos x=\sum_{n\ge0}(-1)^nx^{2n}/(2n)!$. Here $\sin\prime\prime\prime=-\cos$ is negative while $\cos\gt 0$ near zero; applying the preceding shape argument until the first zero of $\cos$ and then the addition identities establishes a first positive zero $x_0$ with positivity before it.

<h3 id="10e/e">e</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [10E](#10e)

<h4 id="10e/e/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [E](#10e/e)

From the addition formula and $\sin x_0=0$, $\cos x_0=-1$, so $2x_0$ is a period. Repeatedly subtract $2x_0$ from a positive period $2x_1$; the remainder in $[0,2x_0)$ is also a period, and positivity plus the addition identities force it to be $0$. Thus $x_1=nx_0$; the converse follows from periodicity and oddness.

## 11E

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="11e/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [11E](#11e)

<h4 id="11e/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#11e/a)

For continuous $f$, $F(x)=\int_a^xf(t)dt$ is [differentiable](../../../analysis.md#differentiable-function) with $F\prime=f$. Conversely, if $F\prime$ is continuous, $\int_a^bF\prime=F(b)-F(a)$.

<h3 id="11e/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [11E](#11e)

<h4 id="11e/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#11e/b)

Put $h(x)=|f(0)|+\int_0^xg(t)|f(t)|dt$. Then $|f|\le h$ and $h\prime\le gh$. If $h\gt 0$, $(\log h)\prime\le g$; integration gives $h(x)\le h(0)e^{\int_0^xg}$. For $f(0)=0$, apply the same argument after adding $\varepsilon$ and let $\varepsilon\downarrow0$.

<h3 id="11e/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [11E](#11e)

<h4 id="11e/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#11e/c)

For $h=f_1-f_2$, $h\prime=\sqrt{1+x}(f_1+f_2)h$ and $h(0)=0$. On every compact subinterval the coefficient is continuous, so [Gronwall inequality](../../../probability-and-statistics.md#gronwall-inequality) gives $h=0$; hence equality holds throughout $[0,a)$.

<h3 id="12e/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [12E](#12e)

**False.** [Continuous functions](../../../calculus.md#continuous-function) can converge pointwise to the non-Riemann-integrable Dirichlet [function](../../../function.md); for example enumerate the rationals and use continuous narrow bumps whose first $n$ peaks cover the first $n$ rationals while widths shrink suitably pointwise off $\mathbb Q$.

<h3 id="12e/f">f</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [12E](#12e)

<h4 id="12e/f/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [F](#12e/f)

**True.** If $|f(x_0)|\gt C$, continuity gives an interval of positive length on which $|f|\gt C+\delta$. Its $L^p$ norm exceeds $(C+\delta)\ell^{1/p}$, which tends to $C+\delta\gt C$ as $p\to\infty$, a contradiction.
