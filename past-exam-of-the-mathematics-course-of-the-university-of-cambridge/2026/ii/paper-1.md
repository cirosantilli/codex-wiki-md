## 1G

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="1g/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1G](#1g)

A [primitive root](../../../number-theory.md#primitive-root-modulo-n) modulo $N$ is a unit of order $\varphi(N)$; a nonzero [quadratic residue](../../../number-theory.md#quadratic-residue) modulo an odd prime $p$ is a square.

If a primitive root $g$ modulo $p$ were $x^2$, then its order would divide $(p-1)/2$, the order of the [subgroup](../../../group.md#subgroup) of squares, contradicting $\operatorname{ord}_p(g)=p-1$.

Modulo $11$, $2$ has order $10$, so the primitive roots are $2^k$ for $\gcd(k,10)=1$:

$$
2,\quad 8,\quad 7,\quad 6.
$$

Finally $2^{10}=1024\equiv56\not\equiv1\pmod{121}$, so the lifting criterion makes $2$ primitive modulo $121$. It is plainly the smallest possible positive primitive root.

## 2I

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="2i/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2I](#2i)

The planar [Brouwer fixed-point theorem](../../../topological-analysis.md#brouwer-fixed-point-theorem) says every continuous $F:D^2\to D^2$ has a fixed point. The equivalent [no-retraction theorem](../../../topological-analysis.md#no-retraction-theorem) says no continuous $r:D^2\to S^1$ restricts to the identity on $S^1$. A fixed-point-free map gives a retraction by extending the ray from $F(x)$ through $x$ to the boundary; a retraction followed by the antipodal map gives a fixed-point-free self-map.

Suppose the stated sphere map is not onto and omits $p$. Compactness gives $\varepsilon>0$ such that its image lies in $S^2\setminus B_\varepsilon(p)$. That set is homeomorphic to a closed disc. Restricting $f$ to it gives a continuous self-map of a disc, so Brouwer supplies a fixed point, contrary to the hypothesis. Hence $f$ is surjective.

## 4J

The basic [functions](../../../function.md) are zero $Z(\mathbf x)=0$, successor $S(n)=n+1$, and projections $P_i^k(\mathbf x)=x_i$. Close these under composition and primitive recursion:

$$
\boxed{f(0,\mathbf x)=g(\mathbf x),\qquad
f(n+1,\mathbf x)=h(n,f(n,\mathbf x),\mathbf x).}
$$

<h3 id="4j/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4J](#4j)

<h4 id="4j/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#4j/b)

Define $A(0,m)=m$ and $A(n+1,m)=S(A(n,m))$. This is primitive recursion from a projection and successor, so $A(n,m)=n+m$ is [primitive recursive function](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#primitive-recursive-function).

<h3 id="4j/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4J](#4j)

<h4 id="4j/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#4j/c)

If $f$ is primitive recursive, then $d(n)=f(P_1^1(n),P_1^1(n))$ is obtained by composition with projections. Hence the diagonal is primitive recursive.

<h3 id="4j/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4J](#4j)

<h4 id="4j/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#4j/d)

Assume a complete list $f$ were primitive recursive. Closure under diagonal composition and successor makes

$$
d(k)=f(k,k)+1
$$

primitive recursive. Completeness gives a row $n$ with $f(n,k)=d(k)$ for every $k$, but at $k=n$ this says $f(n,n)=f(n,n)+1$. This is the [diagonal argument](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#diagonal-argument).

## 5K

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="5k/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [5K](#5k)

The [normal linear model](../../../statistical-modelling.md#normal-linear-model) is $Y=X\beta+\varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon\sim N(0,\sigma^2I_n)$. Full column rank gives

$$
\widehat\beta=(X^TX)^{-1}X^TY.
$$

With hat [matrix](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) $H=X(X^TX)^{-1}X^T$, the $i$th [regression leverage](../../../statistical-modelling.md#regression-leverage) is $h_{ii}$. Since $e=(I-H)Y=(I-H)\varepsilon$ and $I-H$ is an orthogonal projection,

$$
\operatorname{Var}(e_i)=\sigma^2(1-h_{ii}).
$$

High-leverage observations can move their own fitted values strongly and may exert disproportionate influence; residual size alone can therefore conceal them.

## 6C

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="6c/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [6C](#6c)

<h4 id="6c/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#6c/a)

The cross-infection terms transfer susceptible individuals into the infected class: $\beta S\widehat I$ is male incidence caused by infected females and $\widehat\beta\widehat S I$ is female incidence caused by infected males. The terms $\nu I$ and $\widehat\nu\widehat I$ are recovery flows back to susceptibility. Adding each susceptible-infected pair shows that $N$ and $\widehat N$ are conserved.

<h3 id="6c/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [6C](#6c)

<h4 id="6c/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#6c/b)

Substitute $S=N-I$ and $\widehat S=\widehat N-\widehat I$:

$$
\dot I=\beta(N-I)\widehat I-\nu I,\qquad
\dot{\widehat I}=\widehat\beta(\widehat N-\widehat I)I-\widehat\nu\widehat I.
$$

Besides the origin, solve both nullcline equations. Eliminating one infected population gives

$$
I_*=\frac{\widehat NN-\widehat\nu\nu/(\widehat\beta\beta)}
{\nu/\beta+\widehat N},\qquad
\widehat I_*=\frac{\widehat NN-\widehat\nu\nu/(\widehat\beta\beta)}
{\widehat\nu/\widehat\beta+N}.
$$

The assumed [epidemic threshold](../../../mathematical-biology.md#epidemic-threshold) inequality makes both positive.

<h3 id="6c/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [6C](#6c)

<h4 id="6c/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#6c/c)

At the origin the Jacobian is

$$
J_0=\begin{pmatrix}-\nu&\beta N\\ \widehat\beta\widehat N&-\widehat\nu\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\det J_0=\nu\widehat\nu-\beta\widehat\beta N\widehat N<0.
$$

Thus it is a saddle and unstable. At the positive equilibrium the trace is negative. The product of the off-diagonal entries is $\nu\widehat\nu$ by the nullcline identities, while the product of the magnitudes of the diagonal entries is strictly larger, so the [determinant](../../../linear-algebra.md#determinant) is positive. Both [eigenvalues](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) therefore have negative real part and the endemic equilibrium is stable.

## 7D

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="7d/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [7D](#7d)

The ordinary [integral](../../../calculus.md#integral) fails because the simple real poles at $-2$ and $3$ produce nonintegrable logarithmic singularities. Partial fractions give

$$
\frac1{x^2-x-6}=\frac15\left(\frac1{x-3}-\frac1{x+2}\right).
$$

Closing above for $\omega>0$ and below for $\omega<0$, with symmetric semicircular indentations, yields

$$
I(\omega)=\frac{i\pi}{5}\operatorname{sgn}(\omega)
\left(e^{3i\omega}-e^{-2i\omega}\right).
$$

Taking real and imaginary parts,

$$
\operatorname{PV}\int\frac{\cos\omega x}{x^2-x-6}\,dx
=-\frac{\pi}{5}\operatorname{sgn}(\omega)(\sin3\omega+\sin2\omega),
$$



$$
\operatorname{PV}\int\frac{\sin\omega x}{x^2-x-6}\,dx
=\frac{\pi}{5}\operatorname{sgn}(\omega)(\cos3\omega-\cos2\omega).
$$

All [integrals](../../../calculus.md#integral) here run over $\mathbb R$.

## 8A

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="8a/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [8A](#8a)

<h4 id="8a/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#8a/a)

Stationarity of the action under variations with fixed endpoints gives the Euler-Lagrange equation. The [generalized momentum](../../../classical-mechanics.md#generalized-momentum) is $p=\partial L/\partial\dot q$. The [Legendre transform in mechanics](../../../classical-mechanics.md#legendre-transform-in-mechanics) is

$$
H(q,p,t)=p\dot q-L(q,\dot q,t),
$$

after solving $p(q,\dot q,t)$ for $\dot q$. This requires at least local invertibility, $\partial^2L/\partial\dot q^2\ne0$; global single-valuedness requires global invertibility.

<h3 id="8a/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [8A](#8a)

<h4 id="8a/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#8a/b)

<h5 id="8a/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#8a/b/i)

By [Hamiltonian of a charged particle](../../../classical-mechanics.md#hamiltonian-of-a-charged-particle),

$$
\mathbf p=m\dot{\mathbf r}+e\mathbf A,\qquad
H=\frac{|\mathbf p-e\mathbf A|^2}{2m}+e\phi.
$$

<h4 id="8a/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#8a/b)

<h5 id="8a/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#8a/b/ii)

Here

$$
H=\frac{p_x^2+p_y^2+(p_z+eBx)^2}{2m}.
$$

Hence

$$
\dot x=p_x/m,\quad\dot y=p_y/m,\quad\dot z=(p_z+eBx)/m,
$$



$$
\dot p_x=-\frac{eB}{m}(p_z+eBx)=-eB\dot z,\qquad
\dot p_y=\dot p_z=0.
$$

## 9D

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="9d/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [9D](#9d)

<h4 id="9d/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#9d/a)

Differentiate $\mathcal G=\sum_i\mathbf p_i\cdot\mathbf r_i$:

$$
\dot{\mathcal G}=\sum_i(\mathbf F_i\cdot\mathbf r_i+\mathbf p_i\cdot\dot{\mathbf r}_i)
=\sum_i\mathbf F_i\cdot\mathbf r_i+2T.
$$

<h3 id="9d/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [9D](#9d)

<h4 id="9d/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#9d/b)

Pair the $ij$ and $ji$ terms and use $\mathbf f_{ji}=-\mathbf f_{ij}$:

$$
\sum_i\mathbf F_i\cdot\mathbf r_i
=\sum_i\sum_{j<i}\mathbf f_{ij}\cdot(\mathbf r_i-\mathbf r_j).
$$

For gravity, $\mathbf f_{ij}=-Gm^2(\mathbf r_i-\mathbf r_j)/|\mathbf r_i-\mathbf r_j|^3$, so each summand is $-Gm^2/|\mathbf r_i-\mathbf r_j|$. Their sum is $V$.

<h3 id="9d/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [9D](#9d)

<h4 id="9d/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#9d/c)

For a confined system, $\mathcal G(t)$ stays bounded, so its long-time averaged [derivative](../../../calculus.md#derivative) is zero. The preceding identities give the [virial theorem](../../../classical-mechanics.md#virial-theorem)

$$
\boxed{2\langle T\rangle=-\langle V\rangle.}
$$

<h3 id="9d/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [9D](#9d)

<h4 id="17j/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#17j/a)

A matching from $X$ to $Y$ consists of disjoint edges saturating every vertex of $X$. The criterion is [Hall marriage theorem](../../../graph-theory.md#hall-s-marriage-theorem).

For sufficiency, induct on $|X|$. If a nonempty proper $S\subset X$ is tight, $|N(S)|=|S|$, apply induction to $S,N(S)$ and to the graph left after deleting them. If no proper set is tight, choose any edge $xy$, delete its endpoints, and Hall still holds because every nonempty proper subset formerly had at least one spare neighbour. Induction completes the matching. Necessity is immediate.

↑ **Parent:** [17J](#17j)

<h4 id="17j/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#17j/b)

For $S\subseteq X$, count with reciprocal degrees. On every edge $xy$, $d(x)\ge d(y)$, so

$$
|S|=\sum_{x\in S}\sum_{y\sim x}\frac1{d(x)}
\le\sum_{y\in N(S)}\sum_{\substack{x\in S\\x\sim y}}\frac1{d(y)}
\le|N(S)|.
$$

Hall's condition holds, so [Hall marriage theorem](../../../graph-theory.md#hall-s-marriage-theorem) supplies the matching.

## 22I

<h4 id="22i/a/solution">Solution</h4>

The open mapping lemma says: if a bounded [linear map](../../../vector-space.md#linear-map) between Banach spaces satisfies $\overline{T(B_X)}\supset rB_Y$, then $T(B_X)$ contains a (possibly smaller) ball. Given $y$, repeatedly choose corrections $x_n$ whose residuals shrink geometrically; completeness makes $\sum x_n$ converge and maps it to $y$. Baire category applied to $Y=\bigcup_nT(nB_X)$ supplies the closure hypothesis for a surjective map. This proves the [Open mapping theorem](../../../functional-analysis.md#open-mapping-theorem-functional-analysis).

If $\|Tx\|\ge\delta\|x\|$ and $Tx_n\to y$, then $x_n$ is Cauchy, so $x_n\to x$ and $y=Tx$; the range is closed. Conversely a closed range is Banach, and $T:X\to T(X)$ has bounded inverse by the [Open mapping theorem](../../../functional-analysis.md#open-mapping-theorem-functional-analysis), giving the lower bound. This is the [closed-range criterion for an injection](../../../functional-analysis.md#closed-range-criterion-for-an-injection).

For Schwartz $f,g$, Fourier inversion and Fubini give

## 25F

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="25f/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [25F](#25f)

In the affine chart $z=1$, the equation is $y^2=x^2(x+1)$, irreducible because $x+1$ is not a square in $k(x)$. The [partial derivatives](../../../calculus.md#partial-derivative) show the unique singular point is $P=[0:0:1]$; the point at infinity is smooth.

A line $y=tx$ through $P$ meets the curve again at

$$
x=t^2-1,\qquad y=t(t^2-1).
$$

Thus $t\mapsto[t^2-1:t(t^2-1):1]$ is birational, with inverse $t=y/x$ away from $P$. The incidence closure has one point over every smooth $Q$ and two points over $P$, corresponding to $t=\pm1$. This is the [normalization of a nodal curve](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#normalization-of-a-nodal-curve) and separates the two branches.

Dominated by projective space does not imply being projective space. A smooth quadric surface $Y\simeq\mathbb P^1\times\mathbb P^1$ is rational and hence dominated by $\mathbb P^2$, but is not any $\mathbb P^k$; for example its Picard rank is $2$, not $1$.

## 27L

↑ **Parent:** [27L](#27l)

<h4 id="27l/a/solution">Solution</h4>

The statements and proof are in [Borel-Cantelli lemmas](../../../probability-theory.md#borel-cantelli-lemmas). For the first,  
$\mathbb P(\bigcup_{n\ge m}A_n)\le\sum_{n\ge m}\mathbb P(A_n)\to0$. For independent events in the divergent case,

\mathbb P(\text{none of }A_m,\ldots,A_n)
=\prod_{j=m}^n(1-p_j)\le e^{-\sum_{j=m}^np_j}\to0,
$$

<h3 id="27l/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [27L](#27l)

<h4 id="27l/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#27l/b)

The characteristic [function](../../../function.md) of $S_n/n$ is

$$
\Phi(\xi/n)^n=e^{-n|\xi|/n}=e^{-|\xi|}.
$$

**Thus every $S_n/n$ already has the standard Cauchy law, and convergence in law follows from [characteristic-function convergence theorem](../../../probability-theory.md#levy-s-continuity-theorem).**

<h3 id="27l/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [27L](#27l)

<h4 id="27l/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#27l/c)

If $S_n/n$ converged almost surely to a finite [limit](../../../calculus.md#limit-of-a-function), then

$$
\frac{X_n}{n}=\frac{S_n}{n}-\frac{n-1}{n}\frac{S_{n-1}}{n-1}\longrightarrow0.
$$

But $\mathbb P(X_n/n>1)=\mathbb P(X_1>n)\sim1/(\pi n)$. These independent-event probabilities have divergent sum, so the second [Borel-Cantelli lemmas](../../../probability-theory.md#borel-cantelli-lemmas) says $X_n/n>1$ infinitely often almost surely, a contradiction.

<h3 id="28k/b">b</h3>

<h4 id="28k/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#28k/b)

Conditionally on $T=t$, $N_T$ is Poisson with mean $\lambda t$. Mixing over the exponential density gives

$$
P(N_T=n)=\int_0^\infty e^{-\lambda t}\frac{(\lambda t)^n}{n!}\nu e^{-\nu t}\,dt
=\frac{\nu\lambda^n}{(\lambda+\nu)^{n+1}}.
$$

**Thus it is geometric on $\{0,1,\ldots\}$ with success probability $\nu/(\lambda+\nu)$.**

$$

The required properties are those of a [strict Lyapunov function](../../../dynamical-systems.md#strict-lyapunov-function); the domain of stability is the set of initial states whose forward trajectories converge to the fixed point.

For $V=(x^2+y^2)/2$,

$$
\dot V=(x^2+y^2)(-2+x^2+4y^2).
$$

Thus $\dot V<0$ when $V<1/4$, proving that disc lies in the basin. On $V=1$, $\dot V=6y^2\ge0$ (with the tangency points checked from the [vector](../../../vector-space.md#vector) field), so trajectories outside cannot enter the basin through that boundary. Hence the basin is contained in $V\le1$.

$$
\lambda^2+\lambda+2\mu^2.
$$

They are stable nodes for $0<|\mu|<1/(2\sqrt2)$, degenerate nodes at equality, and stable spirals above it.

$$
a=\frac1{1+2\mu^2},\qquad

Thus $W^u$ has

↑ **Parent:** [33B](#33b)

<h4 id="33b/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#33b/c)

Rotational invariance gives $\{m_y,H\}=\{m_z,H\}=0$. Direct calculation gives

$$
m_x=\{m_y,m_z\}=yp_z-zp_y.
$$

Take $G=m_x^2+m_y^2+m_z^2$; angular-momentum brackets imply $\{G,m_x\}=0$, and central symmetry gives $\{G,H\}=0$. Hence $(H,G,m_x)$ are in involution.

At $\mathbf x=(1,0,0)$, $m_y=-p_z$, $m_z=p_y$. The differentials of $G$ and $m_x$ have independent components in the $p_y,p_z$ and transverse position directions when $p_y,p_z\ne0$; $dH$ supplies an independent radial/[momentum](../../../classical-mechanics.md#momentum) component. Thus they are locally independent and the system is Liouville integrable.

## 35E

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="35e/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [35E](#35e)

<h4 id="35e/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#35e/a)

Insert the incident plane wave for $\psi$ in the [integral](../../../calculus.md#integral). At large $r$, $|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|\simeq r-\widehat{\mathbf r}\cdot\mathbf r'$, so the outgoing amplitude is

$$
f(\mathbf k',\mathbf k)=-\frac{m}{2\pi\hbar^2}\widetilde V(\mathbf q),
\quad
\widetilde V(\mathbf q)=\int e^{-i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf r}V(\mathbf r)\,d^3r,
\quad\mathbf q=\mathbf k'-\mathbf k.
$$

**Thus [Born approximation](../../../quantum-theory.md#born-approximation) gives the stated differential cross section.**

<h3 id="35e/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [35E](#35e)

<h4 id="35e/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#35e/b)

For $V(r)\sim r^{-n}$, the small-$q$ transform scales as $q^{n-3}$. Since $d\Omega$ contributes $q\,dq$ near forward scattering, the total Born cross section behaves as $\int_0 q^{2n-5}\,dq$, finite exactly for $n>2$. Coulomb decay $n=1$ gives the familiar divergent forward Rutherford cross section and requires long-range treatment.

<h3 id="35e/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [35E](#35e)

<h4 id="35e/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#35e/c)

The Gaussian transform is

$$
\widetilde V(\mathbf q)=(2\pi)^{3/2}V_0r_0^3e^{-q^2r_0^2/2}.
$$

Therefore

$$
\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}
=\frac{2\pi m^2V_0^2r_0^6}{\hbar^4}e^{-q^2r_0^2},
\qquad q=2k\sin(\vartheta/2).
$$

A sufficient weak-scattering regime is [kinetic energy](../../../classical-mechanics.md#kinetic-energy) much larger than $|V_0|$ (more intrinsically $m|V_0|r_0^2/\hbar^2\ll1$).

## 36E

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="36e/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [36E](#36e)

<h4 id="36e/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#36e/a)

<h5 id="36e/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#36e/a/i)

At equal [pressure](../../../thermodynamics.md#pressure) and [temperature](../../../thermodynamics.md#temperature), [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) gases have equal molecular number density. The mean air molecular mass is $.8(28)+.2(32)=28.8$, while helium has mass $4$, so

$$
\boxed{\rho_{\rm He}/\rho_{\rm air}=4/28.8=5/36.}
$$

<h4 id="36e/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#36e/a)

<h5 id="36e/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#36e/a/ii)

Monatomic helium has molar $C_p=5R/2$ and diatomic air has $7R/2$. Dividing by molar masses,

$$
\frac{c_p^{\rm He}}{c_p^{\rm air}}
=\frac{(5/2)R/4}{(7/2)R/28.8}=\frac{36}{7}.
$$

<h3 id="36e/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [36E](#36e)

<h4 id="36e/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#36e/b)

<h5 id="36e/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#36e/b/i)

The [ideal gas free energy](../../../statistical-physics.md#ideal-gas-free-energy) contributes $-Nk_BT\log V$. With $V=4\pi r^3/3$ and surface energy $4\pi tr^2$, the radius-dependent free energy is therefore

$$
F(r)=-Nk_BT\log V+4\pi tr^2+\text{constant}.
$$

For equilibrium against external [pressure](../../../thermodynamics.md#pressure) $P_0$, minimize $F+P_0V$.

<h4 id="36e/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#36e/b)

<h5 id="36e/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#36e/b/ii)

[Differentiation](../../../calculus.md#differentiation) gives

$$
-\frac{3Nk_BT}{r}+8\pi tr+4\pi P_0r^2=0,
$$

equivalently the Laplace-pressure condition

$$
P_{\rm in}=\frac{Nk_BT}{V}=P_0+\frac{2t}{r}.
$$

<h4 id="36e/b/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#36e/b)

<h5 id="36e/b/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#36e/b/iii)

Let $r_0=(3Nk_BT/(4\pi P_0))^{1/3}$ be the zero-tension radius. From  
$P_0r^3+2tr^2=P_0r_0^3$, put $r=r_0+\delta$ and retain first order:

$$
3P_0r_0^2\delta+2tr_0^2=0,\qquad
r=r_0-\frac{2t}{3P_0}+O(t^2).
$$

## 37D

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="37d/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [37D](#37d)

<h4 id="37d/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#37d/a)

With $u^\mu=\gamma(c,\mathbf v)$ and $d/d\tau=\gamma d/dt$, the spatial components of the [relativistic Lorentz force](../../../electromagnetism.md#relativistic-lorentz-force) give

$$
\frac{d(\gamma m\mathbf v)}{dt}=q(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B).
$$

The time component gives $mc^2\,d\gamma/dt=q\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf v$. It says [magnetic fields](../../../electromagnetism.md#magnetic-field) do no work and electric power changes relativistic energy.

<h3 id="37d/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [37D](#37d)

<h4 id="37d/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#37d/b)

For a wave travelling in $+x$,

$$
\mathbf B=\frac1c\widehat{\mathbf x}\times\mathbf E
=\frac{E_0}{c}(0,-\sin\omega\xi,\cos\omega\xi).
$$

At $x=0$, choose

$$
\mathbf v=\frac{qE_0}{\gamma m\omega}(0,\sin\omega t,-\cos\omega t).
$$

It is parallel to $-\mathbf B$, so the magnetic force vanishes, while its [derivative](../../../calculus.md#derivative) supplies $q\mathbf E/(\gamma m)$. Constant speed then gives

$$
\gamma^2=1+\left(\frac{qE_0}{m\omega c}\right)^2.
$$

The circular radius is

$$
\boxed{R=\frac{|q|E_0}{\gamma m\omega^2}.}
$$

## 38A

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="38a/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [38A](#38a)

<h4 id="38a/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#38a/a)

Spherical symmetry puts any geodesic in a plane through the origin, chosen as $\theta=\pi/2$. The cyclic coordinates give

$$
E=(1-r_s/r)\dot t,\qquad L=r^2\dot\phi.
$$

The null condition gives

$$
\dot r^2=F(r)=E^2-(1-r_s/r)\frac{L^2}{r^2}.
$$

For $u=1/r$, $(du/d\phi)^2=\dot r^2/L^2$, hence

$$
\boxed{\left(\frac{du}{d\phi}\right)^2=\frac1{b^2}-u^2+r_su^3,\qquad b=L/E.}
$$

<h3 id="38a/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [38A](#38a)

<h4 id="38a/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#38a/b)

[Differentiation](../../../calculus.md#differentiation) gives $u''+u=\frac32r_su^2$. With $u=b^{-1}(v_0+\varepsilon v_1+\cdots)$,

$$
v_0''+v_0=0,\qquad v_1''+v_1=\frac32v_0^2.
$$

The symmetry selects $v_0=\sin\phi$, the straight line at closest distance $b$. Since  
$\frac32\sin^2\phi=\frac34(1-\cos2\phi)$,

$$
v_1=\frac34+\frac14\cos2\phi.
$$

The two asymptotic zeros shift from $0,\pi$ to $-\varepsilon,\pi+\varepsilon$, so the deflection is $2\varepsilon=2r_s/b=4GM/(bc^2)$, the [Schwarzschild light deflection](../../../general-relativity.md#schwarzschild-light-deflection). It is tested by stellar deflection near the Sun and gravitational lensing.

## 39C

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="39c/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [39C](#39c)

Boundary-layer theory assumes steady incompressible high-Reynolds-number flow, $\delta/x\ll1$, weak streamwise viscous diffusion, nearly constant [pressure](../../../thermodynamics.md#pressure) across the layer, and outer [pressure](../../../thermodynamics.md#pressure) [gradient](../../../calculus.md#gradient) $-\rho^{-1}p_x=U\,U'$.

For $\phi=-A r^k\cos(k\theta)/k$,

$$
u_r=-Ar^{k-1}\cos k\theta,\qquad u_\theta=Ar^{k-1}\sin k\theta.
$$

At $\theta=\pm\pi/k$ the normal [velocity](../../../classical-mechanics.md#velocity) vanishes; along the upper wall $U(x)=Ax^{k-1}$. [Boundary-layer scaling](../../../continuum-mechanics.md#boundary-layer-scaling) gives

$$
\delta=\sqrt{\frac{\nu x}{U}}=(\nu x^{2-k}/A)^{1/2}.
$$

Set $m=k-1$. The [Falkner-Skan equation](../../../continuum-mechanics.md#falkner-skan-equation) becomes

$$
f^{(3)}=(k-1)f'^2-\frac{k}{2}ff''+(1-k),
$$

so $\alpha=k-1$, $\beta=-k/2$, $\gamma=1-k$, with  
$f(0)=f'(0)=0$ and $f'(\infty)=1$. Finally

$$
\frac{\delta}{x}=(\nu/A)^{1/2}x^{-k/2}\ll1
$$

requires $x\gg(\nu/A)^{1/k}$; thus $a=1/k$ and the tip region violates the approximation.

## 40C

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="40c/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [40C](#40c)

<h4 id="40c/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#40c/a)

Use a [velocity potential](../../../fluid-mechanics.md#velocity-potential) proportional to $e^{ik(x-Vt)+ik\beta_1y}$, where  
$\beta_1=\sqrt{(V/c_1)^2-1}$. The wall condition gives amplitude $-V\epsilon/\beta_1$. With $p'=-\rho_1\partial_t\phi$, [acoustic energy flux](../../../continuum-mechanics.md#acoustic-energy-flux) gives

$$
\langle I_y\rangle=\frac{\rho_1k^2V^3\epsilon^2}{2\beta_1}.
$$

For $V<c_1$ the vertical wave number is imaginary: the disturbance is evanescent and carries no mean vertical energy flux.

<h3 id="40c/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [40C](#40c)

<h4 id="40c/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#40c/b)

Let $H=k\beta_0h$, $\beta_0=\sqrt{1-V^2/c_0^2}$, and  
$\beta_1=\sqrt{V^2/c_1^2-1}$. Match [pressure](../../../thermodynamics.md#pressure) and normal [velocity](../../../classical-mechanics.md#velocity) between an evanescent lower potential and an outgoing upper wave. The transmitted amplitude has

$$
|A_1|^2=\frac{V^2\epsilon^2}
{\beta_1^2\cosh^2H+(\rho_1/\rho_0)^2\beta_0^2\sinh^2H}.
$$

Therefore

$$
\langle I_y\rangle=
\frac{\rho_1k^2V^3\epsilon^2\beta_1}
{2[\beta_1^2\cosh^2H+(\rho_1/\rho_0)^2\beta_0^2\sinh^2H]}.
$$

For $H\gg1$ this is

$$
\frac{2\rho_1k^2V^3\epsilon^2\beta_1e^{-2H}}
{\beta_1^2+(\rho_1/\rho_0)^2\beta_0^2},
$$

showing tunnelling suppression. For $H\ll1$ it tends to  
$\rho_1k^2V^3\epsilon^2/(2\beta_1)$, as if the upper medium met the wall.

## 41B

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="41b/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [41B](#41b)

<h4 id="41b/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#41b/a)

The [power method](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#power-method) repeatedly normalizes $Aq^{(k)}$ and estimates the [eigenvalue](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) with the Rayleigh quotient $(q^{(k)})^TAq^{(k)}$. It converges when the [dominant eigenvalue](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#dominant-eigenvalue) is unique in [modulus](../../../complex-analysis.md#modulus) and the initial [vector](../../../vector-space.md#vector) has a nonzero component in its eigendirection.

<h3 id="41b/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [41B](#41b)

<h4 id="41b/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#41b/b)

The [real spectral theorem](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#real-spectral-theorem) gives real [eigenvalues](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) and orthogonal distinct eigenspaces. Constructively, a [Householder reflection](../../../linear-algebra.md#householder-transformation) sends an [eigenvector](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvector) to $e_1$; symmetry makes the remaining block symmetric. Induction diagonalizes that block, producing orthogonal $Q$ with $Q^TAQ=\Lambda$. Multiplying by $Q$ gives $AQe_k=\lambda_kQe_k$.

<h3 id="41b/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [41B](#41b)

<h4 id="41b/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#41b/c)

Write $q^{(0)}=\sum_i c_iq_i$ with $c_1\ne0$. After $k$ multiplications, the tangent of the angle to $q_1$ is at most

$$
\frac{\sqrt{\sum_{i\ge2}c_i^2|\lambda_i|^{2k}}}
{|c_1||\lambda_1|^k}
\le\left|\frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1}\right|^k\tan\theta_0.
$$

Since $|\sin\theta_k|\le\tan\theta_k$, the first bound follows. Also

$$
\lambda^{(k)}-\lambda_1
=\sum_{i\ge2}(\lambda_i-\lambda_1)|q_i^Tq^{(k)}|^2.
$$

Bound the coefficient by $\max_{i\ge2}|\lambda_i-\lambda_1|$ and the sum by $\sin^2\theta_k$, then use the squared first estimate to obtain the second bound.
