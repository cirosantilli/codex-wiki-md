## 1G

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="1g/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1G](#1g)

For $n=\prod p_i^{e_i}$ odd, define $(a/n)=\prod(a/p_i)^{e_i}$. Multiplying [quadratic reciprocity](../../../number-theory.md#quadratic-reciprocity) for all prime-factor pairs gives

$$
\left(\frac an\right)\left(\frac na\right)=(-1)^{((a-1)/2)((n-1)/2)}
$$

for positive odd coprime $a,n$; the sign exponents add modulo two, proving Jacobi reciprocity from Legendre reciprocity. Since $959=7\cdot137$, $(5/7)=-1$ and $(5/137)=(2/5)=-1$, so $(5/959)=1$. Nevertheless $5$ is a nonresidue modulo both prime factors, so $x^2\equiv5\pmod{959}$ has no solution.

## 2I

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="2i/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2I](#2i)

A subset is dense when every nonempty [open set](../../../topology.md#open-set) meets it, equivalently its closure is the whole space. [Baire category theorem](../../../topological-analysis.md#baire-category-theorem) says a complete metric space has dense intersection for every countable family of open dense sets. Here

$$
S=\bigcap_{N\ge1}\bigcup_{j\ge N}A_j,
$$

so Baire makes $S$ dense. It need not be open: enumerate the rationals $q_j$ and take $A_j=[0,1]\setminus\{q_1,\ldots,q_j\}$, giving the irrationals. Nor need $\cap_jA_j$ be nonempty: take $A_1=\varnothing$ and $A_j=[0,1]$ for $j\ge2$.

## 3H

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="3h/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3H](#3h)

A cyclic code is a linear subspace invariant under cyclic coordinate shift. Identifying words with $\mathbb F_2[X]/(X^n-1)$ makes such codes exactly [ideals](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal). Since this quotient is a principal [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) [ring](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ring), every code is generated uniquely by a monic divisor of $X^n-1$. For $n=7$,

$$
X^7-1=(X+1)(X^3+X+1)(X^3+X^2+1),
$$

so its three distinct irreducible factors have $2^3=8$ monic divisors and there are eight cyclic codes.

↑ **Parent:** [4I](#4i)

<h4 id="4i/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#4i/a)

A DFA consists of finite $Q$, alphabet $\Sigma$, transition $\delta$, initial $q_0$, and accepting set $F$. Extend by $\hat\delta(q,\epsilon)=q$ and $\hat\delta(q,wa)=\delta(\hat\delta(q,w),a)$. It accepts $\{w:\hat\delta(q_0,w)\in F\}$; a language is regular if some DFA accepts it.

<h3 id="4i/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4I](#4i)

<h4 id="4i/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#4i/b)

The [pumping lemma for regular languages](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#pumping-lemma-for-regular-languages) says that for some $N$, every accepted word $w$ of length at least $N$ factors $w=xyz$ with $|xy|\le N$, $|y|>0$, and $xy^kz$ accepted for every $k\ge0$.

The residual-versus-fitted [smooth curve](../../../differential-geometry.md#smooth-curve) bends downward, indicating nonlinearity, and the spread increases at high fitted values. ## 6C

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="6c/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [6C](#6c)

<h4 id="6c/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#6c/a)

The reaction is $F(n)=-n(n-A)(n-B)$. Its homogeneous equilibria are $0,A,B$; $F'(0)<0$ and $F'(B)<0$, while $F'(A)>0$. Thus the stable equilibria are $0$ and $B$.

<h3 id="6c/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [6C](#6c)

<h4 id="6c/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#6c/b)

Substitution $n=f(\xi)$, $\xi=x-ct$, gives $f''+cf'+F(f)=0$ with $f(-\infty)=0$, $f(\infty)=B$. Multiplying by $f'$ gives $-c\int(f')^2d\xi=\int_0^BF(s)ds=B^4(1-2A/B)/12$. The populated state invades the empty state when $c<0$, equivalently $B>2A$; this is the condition preventing eventual extinction.

## 7D

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="7d/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [7D](#7d)

A point $z_0$ is regular singular when $(z-z_0)p$ and $(z-z_0)^2q$ are analytic there. The Papperitz symbol records the three singular points and their two local exponents. Here the finite points are $0,1$ and the third is $\infty$. The indicial equations give exponents $(0,-1)$ at $0$ and $(0,0)$ at $1$. Comparing with the hypergeometric equation gives $c=2$, $a+b=2$, $ab=1$, hence $a=b=1$ and exponents $(1,1)$ at infinity. Thus

$$
P\left\{\begin{matrix}0&1&\infty\\0&0&1\\-1&0&1\end{matrix};z\right\}.
$$

## 8A

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="8a/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [8A](#8a)

For the standard symplectic form, $D_f=\sum_a(f_{p_a}\partial_{q^a}-f_{q^a}\partial_{p_a})$; a transformation is canonical when it preserves the symplectic form, equivalently Poisson brackets. The Jacobi identity gives $[D_f,D_g]=D_{\{f,g\}}$ with the stated convention. For $Z^A=e^{-sD_f}z^A$, differentiate $\{Z^A,Z^B\}$ in $s$; Jacobi makes the [derivative](../../../calculus.md#derivative) the Lie transport of the bracket, preserving the canonical constant brackets at $s=0$. Hence every map in the one-parameter flow is canonical.

## 9D

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="9d/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [9D](#9d)

<h4 id="9d/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#9d/a)

Substitution gives $\dot\phi=2M_{\rm Pl}/t$, $\ddot\phi=-2M_{\rm Pl}/t^2$ and $V=V_*e^{-\phi_*/M_{\rm Pl}}(t_*/t)^2=10M_{\rm Pl}^2/t^2$, exactly satisfying both equations when $\phi_*=M_{\rm Pl}\log(V_*t_*^2/(10M_{\rm Pl}^2))$. Since $a\propto t^2$, $\ddot a>0$: it inflates. $N=2\log(t_*/t)$ and $\Delta\phi=2M_{\rm Pl}\log(t_*/t)=NM_{\rm Pl}$, so 60 e-folds require a field excursion about $60M_{\rm Pl}$.

<h3 id="9d/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [9D](#9d)

<h4 id="9d/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#9d/b)

In a decelerating universe the comoving Hubble radius grows, so regions now in causal contact extrapolate to many disconnected early Hubble patches. Inflation shrinks the comoving Hubble radius and stretches one causal patch beyond the present horizon. Radiation scaling gives a present-horizon physical size at $10^{-35}$ s of roughly $ct_0(a/a_0)\sim ct_0(t/t_0)^{1/2}\sim1$ m. Comparing this with the inflationary Hubble length (about $ct\sim10^{-27}$ m at that epoch) requires $e^N\sim10^{27}$, or $N\simeq62$ (order 60).

## 10E

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="10e/i">i</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [10E](#10e)

<h4 id="10e/i/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#10e/i)

The all-zero amplitude is $2^{-n}\sum_x(-1)^{f(x)}=1-2k/2^n$, so its probability is $(1-2k/2^n)^2$. For a general nontrivial $k$ it lies strictly between zero and one.

<h3 id="10e/ii">ii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [10E](#10e)

<h4 id="10e/ii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#10e/ii)

For constant $f$, $k=0$ or $2^n$, so the all-zero result occurs with probability one.

<h3 id="10e/iii">iii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [10E](#10e)

<h4 id="10e/iii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#10e/iii)

For perfect balance, $k=2^{n-1}$ and the all-zero probability is zero. For other balances the one-shot outcomes overlap the constant case. Repeat [Deutsch-Jozsa algorithm](../../../quantum-theory.md#deutsch-jozsa-algorithm) and declare nonconstant upon any nonzero result. Constants are never misclassified; a $k$-balanced [function](../../../function.md) fails after $R$ trials with probability $q^R$, $q=(1-2k/2^n)^2$. It suffices that $R\ge\log\epsilon/\log q$, using that many oracle calls.

## 11G

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="11g/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [11G](#11g)

<h4 id="11g/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#11g/a)

With $p_{-1}=1,p_0=a_0$, $q_{-1}=0,q_0=1$ and $p_n=a_np_{n-1}+p_{n-2}$, $q_n=a_nq_{n-1}+q_{n-2}$, induction gives $p_nq_{n-1}-p_{n-1}q_n=(-1)^{n+1}$. [Matrix](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) multiplication gives $[a_0,\ldots,a_n,\beta]=(p_n\beta+p_{n-1})/(q_n\beta+q_{n-1})$; monotonicity in $\beta>0$ places it strictly between the adjacent convergents.

<h3 id="11g/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [11G](#11g)

<h4 id="11g/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#11g/b)

Apply the formula to the complete quotient and use the [determinant](../../../linear-algebra.md#determinant) identity to express $b_n$. Eliminating the complete quotient from the consecutive expressions yields the stated quadratic for $q_{n+1}/q_n$. Taking the compatible signs for adjacent indices and using $c_n\le b_j$ gives

$$
\boxed{2c_na_{n+1}\le2b_na_{n+1}\le\sqrt{1-4b_nb_{n+1}}+\sqrt{1-4b_{n-1}b_n}\le2\sqrt{1-4c_n^2}.}
$$

<h3 id="11g/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [11G](#11g)

<h4 id="11g/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#11g/c)

If all but finitely many convergents had $b_n>1/\sqrt5$, then eventually $c_n>1/\sqrt5$. Part (b) would imply $c_na_{n+1}\le\sqrt{1-4c_n^2}<c_n$, impossible since $a_{n+1}\ge1$. Thus infinitely many $b_n\le1/\sqrt5$, which is exactly $|\theta-p_n/q_n|\le1/(\sqrt5q_n^2)$.

## 12J

<h4 id="12j/a/solution">Solution</h4>

A register machine has finitely many natural-number registers and labelled increment, conditional decrement/jump, and halt instructions. Starting with the input in designated registers, instructions determine a partial computation. Encode the finite instruction list by a standard effective Gödel coding, giving each machine an index in $\mathbb N$.

<h3 id="12j/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [12J](#12j)

## 13E

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="13e/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [13E](#13e)

<h4 id="13e/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#13e/a)

For jump rate $W(n,r)$ from $n$ to $n+r$, probability enters state $n$ from $n-r$ and leaves it from $n$. The resulting [kramers-Moyal master equation](../../../mathematical-biology.md#kramers-moyal-master-equation) is

$$
\boxed{\partial_tP(n,t)=\sum_r
[P(n-r,t)W(n-r,r)-P(n,t)W(n,r)].}
$$

<h3 id="13e/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [13E](#13e)

<h4 id="13e/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#13e/b)

When jumps are small relative to a large population and moments exist, the [Kramers-Moyal expansion](../../../mathematical-biology.md#kramers-moyal-expansion) through second order gives

$$
P_t=-\partial_x(uP)+\partial_x^2(DP),
\qquad
u=\sum_r rW(x,r),
\qquad
D=\frac12\sum_r r^2W(x,r).
$$

This [Fokker-Planck equation](../../../probability-theory.md#fokker-planck-equation) is a conservation law with vanishing boundary flux; $u$ is the drift and $D$ the diffusivity. Equal constant rates for jumps $r=\mathord\pm1$ give pure diffusion.

↑ **Parent:** [13E](#13e)

<h4 id="13e/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#13e/c)

Integration by parts gives $d\langle x\rangle/dt=\langle u\rangle$ and $d\operatorname{var}x/dt=2\langle D\rangle+2\operatorname{Cov}(x,u)$.

<h3 id="13e/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [13E](#13e)

<h4 id="13e/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#13e/d)

Here $u=-2\sinh(\lambda x)$ and $D=\cosh(\lambda x)$. Zero stationary flux gives $(DP)'=uP$, so $P\propto D^{-1}\exp\int u/D,dx\propto\cosh(\lambda x)^{-(1+2/\lambda)}$, a symmetric unimodal distribution.

## 23G

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="23g/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [23G](#23g)

An elliptic [function](../../../function.md) is meromorphic and $\Lambda$-periodic. The argument principle on opposite sides of a period parallelogram cancels, so zeros and poles have equal total multiplicity. The [Weierstrass elliptic function](../../../complex-analysis.md#weierstrass-elliptic-function)

$$
\wp(z)=z^{-2}+\sum_{0\ne\omega\in\Lambda}[(z-\omega)^{-2}-\omega^{-2}]
$$

is elliptic. Its half-period values $e_i$ are distinct, and comparing poles and leading coefficients proves $\wp'^2=4\prod_i(\wp-e_i)$. The half-period addition formula gives

$$
\wp(z+\omega_1/2)-e_1=\frac{(e_1-e_2)(e_1-e_3)}{\wp(z)-e_1}.
$$

Differentiate and use the same formula at $z=\omega_1/4$ to identify $(e_1-e_2)(e_1-e_3)=(\wp(\omega_1/4)-e_1)^2$, yielding the required [derivative](../../../calculus.md#derivative) ratio.

\boxed{\eta(x,t)\sim\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi t}}\sum_j\frac{F(k_j)}{\sqrt{|\omega''(k_j)|}}\exp\{i[k_jx-\omega(k_j)t-i\tfrac\pi4\operatorname{sgn}\omega''(k_j)]\}.}
$$

<h3 id="39c/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [39C](#39c)

<h4 id="39c/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#39c/b)

Fourier modes of the harmonic potential decay as $e^{|k|y}$; the two surface conditions give $\eta_{tt}+|k|^3\eta=0$, hence $\omega^2=|k|^3$. The initial transform is $F(k)=-i\sqrt\pi,k e^{-k^2/4}/2$. At $x=Vt$, the contributing stationary wavenumber is $k_0=4V^2/9$, giving

$$
\eta(Vt,t)\sim\frac{4V^{5/2}}{27\sqrt t}e^{-4V^4/81}\sin\left(\frac{4V^3t}{27}-\frac\pi4\right).
$$

Different observer speeds select different group-velocity wavenumbers; fast observers see exponentially weaker, higher-frequency ripples.
