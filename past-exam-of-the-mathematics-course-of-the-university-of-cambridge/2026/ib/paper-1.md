## 2F

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="2f/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2F](#2f)

A neighbourhood of $x$ contains an [open set](../../../topology.md#open-set) containing $x$; $x_n\to x$ when every neighbourhood eventually contains every $x_n$. If two metrics have the same topology they plainly have the same convergent [sequences](../../../real-analysis.md#sequence). Conversely, if a $d$-open set $U$ were not $d\prime$-open, some $x\in U$ would have points $x_n\notin U$ with $d\prime(x_n,x)\lt 1/n$; the common-sequence assumption would imply $d(x_n,x)\to0$, contradicting $d$-openness. Symmetry finishes the proof. This fails generally: on an uncountable set the discrete and cocountable topologies differ, but in both every convergent [sequence](../../../real-analysis.md#sequence) is eventually constant.

## 3

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="3/3-1g">3.1G</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/3-1g/a">a</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [3.1G](#3/3-1g)

<h5 id="3/3-1g/a/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/3-1g/a)

The [residue](../../../analysis.md#residue) is the coefficient $a_{-1}$ in the Laurent expansion. At a double pole, $h(z)=(z-z_0)^2f(z)$ is holomorphic and $h(z)=h(z_0)+h\prime(z_0)(z-z_0)+\cdots$, so $\operatorname{Res}(f,z_0)=h\prime(z_0)$.

<h4 id="3/3-1g/b">b</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [3.1G](#3/3-1g)

<h5 id="3/3-1g/b/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/3-1g/b)

Partial fractions give

$$
f=\frac1{2z^2}-\frac3{4z}+\frac1{z+1}-\frac1{4(z+2)}.
$$

For $1\lt |z|\lt 2$ this becomes

$$
\boxed{\frac1{2z^2}-\frac3{4z}+\sum_{n\ge0}(-1)^nz^{-n-1}-\frac18\sum_{n\ge0}(-z/2)^n.}
$$

<h3 id="3/3-2c">3.2C</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/3-2c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [3.2C](#3/3-2c)

At zero: $\sin z$ is holomorphic with a zero; $1/\sin z$ has a simple pole of residue $1$; $z/\sin z$ has a removable singularity with value $1$; $\sin(1/z)$ is essential with residue $1$; $z\sin(1/z)$ is essential with residue $0$. Finally $1/\sin(1/z)$ has poles accumulating at zero, so zero is a non-isolated singularity and no residue there is defined.

## 4B

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="4b/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4B](#4b)

Fermat’s principle makes $\int ds/c$ stationary. Writing the ray as $x=x(y)$ gives a [Lagrangian](../../../calculus-of-variations.md#lagrangian) independent of $y$, and its first [integral](../../../calculus.md#integral) is $\sin\theta/c=K$. At the origin $K=(1/\sqrt2)/(1/\sqrt3)=\sqrt{3/2}$. At $x=1$, $c=1/\sqrt2$, so $\sin\theta=\sqrt3/2$ and the forward angle is $\theta=\pi/3$.

## 5C

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="5c/i">i</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [5C](#5c)

<h4 id="5c/i/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#5c/i)

Successive substitution shows every stage is a [polynomial](../../../polynomial.md) in $z=h\lambda$ times $y_n$, of degree at most its stage number. Hence $y_{n+1}=r(z)y_n$ with $r$ a degree-$\nu$ [polynomial](../../../polynomial.md).

<h3 id="5c/ii">ii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [5C](#5c)

<h4 id="5c/ii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#5c/ii)

Order $\nu$ requires $r(z)-e^z=O(z^{\nu+1})$ on the test equation, fixing all $\nu+1$ coefficients: $r(z)=\sum_{l=0}^{\nu}z^l/l!$.

<h3 id="5c/iii">iii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [5C](#5c)

<h4 id="5c/iii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#5c/iii)

For $\nu=2$, $|r(iy)|^2=1+y^4/4$, so only $y=0$ is stable. For $\nu=3$, $|r(iy)|^2=1-y^4/12+y^6/36$, so the stable imaginary segment is $|y|\le\sqrt3$.

Factorisation of the likelihood $(1-\theta)^{-n}1_{\{\theta\le X_{(1)}\}}$ shows that $T=X_{(1)}$ is sufficient: conditional on it, the sample law is independent of $\theta$.

<h4 id="6h/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#6h/b)

The Rao–Blackwell theorem says that for convex loss, conditioning any estimator on a sufficient statistic cannot increase risk; for squared error it preserves the mean and weakly reduces variance.

<h3 id="6h/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [6H](#6h)

<h4 id="6h/c/solution">Solution</h4>

Since $E X_{(1)}=\theta+(1-\theta)/(n+1)$, take $\hat\theta=((n+1)X_{(1)}-1)/n$. It is unbiased, and the supplied variance formula gives $\operatorname{var}\hat\theta=(1-\theta)^2/[n(n+2)]$, which is its MSE.

## 7H

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="7h/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [7H](#7h)

Replace the quadratic constraint by the two linear inequalities $-2\le x_1-x_2+x_3\le2$. Simplex pivots lead to the active constraints $x_1=0$, $-x_2+x_3=2$, and $3x_2+x_3=4$. Thus $x^*=(0,1/2,5/2)$ and the value is $3$. With right-hand side $4+\varepsilon$, the same [basis](../../../vector-space.md#basis) remains optimal for small nonzero $\varepsilon$, giving $x^*=(0,1/2+\varepsilon/4,5/2+\varepsilon/4)$.

## 8E

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="8e/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [8E](#8e)

<h4 id="8e/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#8e/a)

A Jordan form is block diagonal with blocks $J_k(\lambda)$ having $\lambda$ on the diagonal and ones on the superdiagonal. Over $\mathbb C$ every square [matrix](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) is similar to one, uniquely up to permutation of blocks.

<h3 id="8e/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [8E](#8e)

<h4 id="8e/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#8e/b)

<h5 id="8e/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#8e/b/i)

$D$ lowers total degree, so it is nilpotent and has only [eigenvalue](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) zero. Solving $(\partial_z+\partial_w)f=0$ gives $f=h(z-w)$; the degree restrictions give the eigenspace $\operatorname{span}\{1,z-w,(z-w)^2\}$.

<h4 id="8e/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#8e/b)

<h5 id="8e/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#8e/b/ii)

Direct degree counting gives $\dim\ker D^k=3,5,7,8,9$ for $k=1,\ldots,5$. Hence the Jordan block sizes are $5,3,1$.

<h3 id="8e/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [8E](#8e)

<h4 id="8e/c/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#8e/c)

<h5 id="8e/c/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#8e/c/i)

On each Jordan block of $A$, $g(A)$ is triangular with diagonal entry $g(\lambda)$; hence the [eigenvalues](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue), with multiplicity, are $g(\lambda_i)$.

<h4 id="8e/c/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#8e/c)

<h5 id="8e/c/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#8e/c/ii)

The shift [matrix](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) obeys $A^4=I$ and $B=aI+bA+cA^2+dA^3$. Evaluating $g(t)=a+bt+ct^2+dt^3$ at $1,i,-1,-i$ gives

$$
\boxed{a+b+c+d,quad a-c+i(b-d),quad a-b+c-d,quad a-c-i(b-d).}
$$

## 9E

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="9e/i">i</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [9E](#9e)

<h4 id="9e/i/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#9e/i)

If $a^m=b^n=0$, then every term in $(a+b)^{m+n-1}$ vanishes; also $(ra)^m=0$. Thus the nilpotents form an [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) $N$. The quotient $R/N$ has no nonzero nilpotents.

<h3 id="9e/ii">ii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [9E](#9e)

<h4 id="9e/ii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#9e/ii)

One direction follows because finitely many nilpotent coefficients generate a nilpotent [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal). Conversely, reducing a [nilpotent polynomial](../../../algebra.md#nilpotent-polynomial) modulo $N$ gives a nilpotent [polynomial](../../../polynomial.md) over the reduced [ring](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ring) $R/N$; comparing its highest nonzero coefficient shows it must be zero, so every coefficient lay in $N$.

<h3 id="9e/iii">iii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [9E](#9e)

<h4 id="9e/iii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#9e/iii)

If $b^k=0$, then $(u+b)^{-1}=u^{-1}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}(-bu^{-1})^j$. For the [polynomial](../../../polynomial.md), its positive-degree part is nilpotent by (ii), so adding it to the unit $a_0$ gives a unit.

<h3 id="9e/iv">iv</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [9E](#9e)

<h4 id="9e/iv/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#9e/iv)

Choose nonzero $g$ of least degree with $fg=0$. If $b$ is its leading coefficient, then the top coefficient equation gives $a_nb=0$; minimality applied after removing the top term forces $bf=0$. In $(\mathbb Z/m\mathbb Z)[X]$, a nonzero annihilator $r$ therefore satisfies $ra_i=0$ for every $i$; a prime divisor of $m/\gcd(r,m)$ divides all $a_i$ and $m$, so the displayed gcd exceeds one.

## 10G

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="10g/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [10G](#10g)

<h4 id="10g/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#10g/a)

[Uniform convergence](../../../real-analysis.md#uniform-convergence) means: for every $\varepsilon\gt 0$ there is $N$ such that $n\ge N$ implies $|f_n(x)-f(x)|\lt \varepsilon$ for every $x\in E$.

<h3 id="10g/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [10G](#10g)

<h4 id="10g/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#10g/b)

If $|f_n(x)|\le M_n$ and $\sum M_n\lt \infty$, then every tail is bounded uniformly by the corresponding numerical tail. The uniform Cauchy criterion therefore proves [uniform convergence](../../../real-analysis.md#uniform-convergence).

<h3 id="10g/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [10G](#10g)

<h4 id="10g/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#10g/c)

For each $a\gt 1$, $n^{-x}\le n^{-a}$ on $[a,\infty)$ and the p-series converges. The M-test gives locally [uniform convergence](../../../real-analysis.md#uniform-convergence) there, so the sum $\zeta$ is continuous on $(1,\infty)$.

<h3 id="10g/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [10G](#10g)

<h4 id="10g/d/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#10g/d)

<h5 id="10g/d/i/solution">Solution</h5>

The [series](../../../real-analysis.md#series-mathematics) for $S$ converges uniformly by the M-test. Therefore [termwise integration](../../../mathematics.md#termwise-integration) is valid and every term integrates to $\sin(n^3\pi)/n^7=0$, so the [integral](../../../calculus.md#integral) is zero.

<h4 id="10g/d/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#10g/d)

<h5 id="10g/d/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#10g/d/ii)

It is not uniform. For a small fixed $c\gt 0$, set $x_N=c/N^3$. On a block $N\le n\le(1+\delta)N$, all $n^3x_N$ lie in a compact subinterval of $(0,\pi)$, so the corresponding tail has a positive lower bound independent of $N$; this violates the uniform Cauchy criterion.

## 11

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="11/11-1g">11.1G</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [11](#11)

<h4 id="11/11-1g/a">a</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [11.1G](#11/11-1g)

<h5 id="11/11-1g/a/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#11/11-1g/a)

Apply the maximum [modulus](../../../complex-analysis.md#modulus) principle to $f(z)/z$, with its removable value $f\prime(0)$, to obtain $|f(z)|\le|z|$. Equality at a nonzero interior point makes $f(z)/z$ constant, so $f(z)=az$ with $|a|=1$.

<h4 id="11/11-1g/b">b</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [11.1G](#11/11-1g)

<h5 id="11/11-1g/b/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#11/11-1g/b)

Let $a=e^{i\pi/3}$ and $b=e^{-i\pi/3}$. The Möbius map $T(z)=(z-a)/(z-b)$ sends the two circular arcs through $a,b$ to rays bounding a wedge of angle $2\pi/3$. After choosing the branch and a unimodular constant so the interior has positive argument, $f(z)=e^{i\phi}T(z)^{3/2}$ maps that wedge bijectively onto the upper half-plane.

<h3 id="11/11-2c">11.2C</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [11](#11)

<h4 id="11/11-2c/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [11.2C](#11/11-2c)

<h5 id="11/11-2c/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#11/11-2c/i)

Off the slit choose the stated analytic square-root branch. [Differentiation](../../../calculus.md#differentiation) gives $f\prime(z)=c^{-1}(1+z/\sqrt{z^2-c^2})$, which does not vanish there, so the map is conformal.

<h4 id="11/11-2c/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [11.2C](#11/11-2c)

<h5 id="11/11-2c/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#11/11-2c/ii)

Multiplying $(z+\sqrt{z^2-c^2})(z-\sqrt{z^2-c^2})=c^2$ gives the reciprocal identity and hence $z=c(\zeta+\zeta^{-1})/2$.

<h4 id="11/11-2c/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [11.2C](#11/11-2c)

<h5 id="11/11-2c/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#11/11-2c/iii)

For $\zeta=Re^{i\theta}$,  
$x=\tfrac c2(R+R^{-1})\cos\theta$ and $y=\tfrac c2(R-R^{-1})\sin\theta$. Thus $a=c(R+R^{-1})/2$, $b=c(R-R^{-1})/2$. Nested level curves and the behaviour at infinity show that exteriors correspond.

<h4 id="11/11-2c/iv">iv</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [11.2C](#11/11-2c)

<h5 id="11/11-2c/iv/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#11/11-2c/iv)

The Möbius map sends $|\zeta|=R$ to the imaginary axis and $\infty$ to $1$; testing one exterior point shows that $|\zeta|\gt R$ maps to $\Re w\gt 0$.

<h4 id="11/11-2c/v">v</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [11.2C](#11/11-2c)

<h5 id="11/11-2c/v/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [V](#11/11-2c/v)

The required harmonic [function](../../../function.md) is

$$
u(z)=\frac2\pi\operatorname{Im}\log\!\left(\frac{f(z)-R}{f(z)+R}\right).
$$

On the upper and lower boundary its argument is respectively $\pi/2$ and $-\pi/2$, while at infinity the logarithm tends to $0$.

## 12D

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="12d/i">i</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [12D](#12d)

<h4 id="12d/i/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#12d/i)

For the odd two-periodic extensions, $\bar f=\sum b_n\sin(n\pi x)$ with $b_n=2[1+(-1)^n]/(n\pi)$, while $\bar F$ has $b_n=4[1-(-1)^n]/(n^3\pi^3)$. Thus the first has $O(n^{-1})$ convergence because its periodic extension jumps, while the smoother second extension has $O(n^{-3})$ coefficients.

<h3 id="12d/ii">ii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [12D](#12d)

<h4 id="12d/ii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#12d/ii)

Writing $y=e^{-x}v$ gives $-v\prime\prime+v=\lambda v$, hence $\lambda_n=n^2\pi^2+1$ and $y_n=e^{-x}\sin(n\pi x)$. The Sturm–Liouville form is $-(e^{2x}y\prime)\prime=\lambda e^{2x}y$, so $\int_0^1e^{2x}y_ny_m=0$ for $n\ne m$. Expanding the source gives coefficients $c_n=2\int_0^1f\sin(n\pi x)dx$, hence

$$
\boxed{a_n=\frac{2[1+(-1)^n]}{n\pi(n^2\pi^2+1)}.}
$$

## 13B

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="13b/i">i</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [13B](#13b)

<h4 id="13b/i/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#13b/i)

The nonrelativistic Schrödinger [Hamiltonian](../../../classical-mechanics.md#hamiltonian) uses $p^2/(2m)$ and therefore has no massless [limit](../../../calculus.md#limit-of-a-function); massless particles require a relativistic wave equation or quantum field theory.

<h3 id="13b/ii">ii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [13B](#13b)

<h4 id="13b/ii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#13b/ii)

The walls give $\omega=n\pi/(2a)$; normalisation gives $A=1/\sqrt a$, and $E_n=\hbar^2\pi^2n^2/(8ma^2)$. Product-to-sum integration over $(-a,a)$ gives $\langle\psi_n,\psi_m\rangle=\delta_{nm}$.

<h3 id="13b/iii">iii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [13B](#13b)

<h4 id="13b/iii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#13b/iii)

Using the quadratic form of the [Hamiltonian](../../../classical-mechanics.md#hamiltonian),  
$E_{av}=(\hbar^2/2m)\int|\psi\prime|^2dx=2\pi^2\hbar^2/(ma^2)$.

<h3 id="13b/iv">iv</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [13B](#13b)

<h4 id="13b/iv/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#13b/iv)

Only odd [stationary states](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#stationary-state) contribute, and for $j=2n+1$ their squared coefficients are $128/[\pi^2(j^2-16)^2]$. Since $E_{av}/E_1=16$, substitution gives

$$
\pi^2=8\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{(2n+1)^2}{[(2n+1)^2-16]^2},
$$

so $B=8$.

## 14D

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="14d/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [14D](#14d)

<h4 id="14d/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#14d/a)

Using $\nabla|x-x\prime|^{-1}=-\nabla\prime|x-x\prime|^{-1}$, the divergence is a line [integral](../../../calculus.md#integral) of a total [derivative](../../../calculus.md#derivative), zero for a closed wire or suitable vanishing endpoints. Taking the curl and moving it under the [integral](../../../calculus.md#integral) gives the stated Biot–Savart formula.

<h3 id="14d/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [14D](#14d)

<h4 id="14d/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#14d/b)

The negative-$z$ segment integrates to the stated azimuthal field. The negative-$y$ segment contributes

$$
\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi}\frac{x\hat z-z\hat x}{x^2+z^2}\left(1-\frac y{\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}}\right).
$$

Adding this to the first field is the complete answer. On $x=0,y=1$, the field tends to $0$ as $z\to+\infty$ and to $-\mu_0I\hat x/(2\pi)$ as $z\to-\infty$.

For fixed $x$ add $K\Pi(t)$ to $p(t)-f(t)$ so the resulting [function](../../../function.md) also vanishes at $t=x$. Repeated Rolle gives a point $\xi$ where its $(n+1)$st [derivative](../../../calculus.md#derivative) vanishes, yielding the formula.

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#16c/ii)

The recurrence $T_{k+1}=2xT_k-T_{k-1}$ shows that $T_{n+1}$ has leading coefficient $2^n$. Since its roots are the interpolation nodes, $T_{n+1}=2^n\Pi$, and $|T_{n+1}|\le1$ on $[-1,1]$.

Since $|f^{(n+1)}|\le k^{n+1}$, the error is at most $k^{n+1}/[2^n(n+1)!]\to0$ uniformly.

At $x=1$, direct substitution gives a product of odd half-integers. Taking logarithms and applying the hinted lower Riemann-sum estimate yields $\log\Pi(1)\gt n\log(2/e)$, hence $\Pi(1)\gt (2/e)^n$.

## 17H

With $P_0=X_0(X_0^TX_0)^{-1}X_0^T$, $\hat\beta_0=(X_0^TX_0)^{-1}X_0^TY$ and $\hat\sigma^2=\|(I-P_0)Y\|^2/n$.

The statistic is $F=[(RSS_0-RSS_1)/p_1]/[RSS_1/(n-p)]$, with an $F_{p_1,n-p}$ null distribution.

↑ **Parent:** [17H](#17h)

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#17h/iv)

The displayed linear form is centred normal with variance $n^{-1}X_{1j}^T(I-P_0)X_{1j}\le n^{-1}\|X_{1j}\|^2=1$. The normal tail bound proves the claim.

<h3 id="17h/v">v</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [17H](#17h)

<h4 id="17h/v/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [V](#17h/v)

## 18H

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="18h/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [18H](#18h)

<h4 id="18h/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#18h/a)

A stopping time $T$ has $\{T\le n\}$ determined by $X_0,\ldots,X_n$. The [Strong Markov property](../../../markov-process.md#strong-markov-property) says that, conditional on $T\lt \infty$ and $X_T=i$, the post-$T$ chain is a fresh chain started at $i$, independent of the pre-$T$ history.

↑ **Parent:** [18H](#18h)

<h4 id="18h/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#18h/b)

<h5 id="18h/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

First-step equations $h_B=1+h_W/2$ and $h_W=1+2h_B/3$ give $h_B=9/4$.

<h3 id="18h/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [18H](#18h)

<h4 id="18h/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#18h/c)

Augment the state by the current sound and $D=N_b-N_w$, and solve the translation-invariant first-step equations. Equivalently, solve the Poisson equation for the additive reward $+1,0,-1$. The stationary probabilities are $(2/5,3/10,3/10)$, so the drift is $1/10$ per sound. The correction term has the same value at the initial and terminal bark states; optional stopping at $D=m$ therefore gives the expected produced length $10m$.
