<h1 id="24f/d/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [D](../d.md)

Since $\mathbb C$ is [simply connected](../../../../../../simply-connected-domain.md), write

$$
u=\operatorname{Re}f
$$

for an [entire function](../../../../../../entire-function.md) $f$. If $u$ were bounded above, then

$$
|e^f|=e^u
$$

would be bounded, so the [Liouville theorem](../../../../../../liouville-theorem.md) would make $e^f$ constant. Its derivative $f'e^f$ would then vanish, whence $f$ and $u$ would be constant. Applying the same argument to $e^{-f}$ rules out a lower bound. Thus a nonconstant $u$ is unbounded both above and below.

Given any $c\in\mathbb R$, choose $z_-,z_+\in\mathbb C$ with

$$
u(z_-)<c<u(z_+).
$$

The restriction of $u$ to the line segment from $z_-$ to $z_+$ is [continuous](../../../../../../continuous-function.md), so the [intermediate value theorem](../../../../../../intermediate-value-theorem.md) supplies a point on that segment where $u=c$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{u(\mathbb C)=\mathbb R}.
$$

This proves the [surjectivity of a nonconstant entire harmonic function](../../../../../../surjectivity-of-a-nonconstant-entire-harmonic-function.md).
