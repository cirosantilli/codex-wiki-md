↑ **Parent:** [Paper 123](paper-123.md)

<h3 id="1/1">1</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/1/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1/1)

A [modulus of a number field](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#modulus-of-a-number-field) is a formal product

$$
\mathfrak m=\mathfrak m_0\mathfrak m_\infty,
$$

where $\mathfrak m_0=\prod_{\mathfrak p}\mathfrak p^{n_{\mathfrak p}}$ is a nonzero integral ideal and $\mathfrak m_\infty$ is a product of distinct real places. Only finitely many exponents $n_{\mathfrak p}$ are nonzero.

<h4 id="1/1/2">2</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1/1)

<h5 id="1/1/2/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#1/1/2)

Let $L/K$ be a finite abelian extension and let $\mathfrak m$ be an admissible modulus, so every ramified prime divides $\mathfrak m_0$ and the [Artin reciprocity map](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#artin-reciprocity-law)

$$
\theta_{L/K}:I_K(\mathfrak m)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)
$$

is defined. If $\mathfrak p\nmid\mathfrak m_0$, then the [ideal-theoretic decomposition law](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#ideal-theoretic-decomposition-law) says that its residue degree is

$$
f(\mathfrak P/\mathfrak p)
=\operatorname{ord}\theta_{L/K}(\mathfrak p),
$$

and, since $\mathfrak p$ is unramified, it has

$$
g(\mathfrak p)=\frac{[L:K]}{\operatorname{ord}\theta_{L/K}(\mathfrak p)}
$$

prime factors in $L$.

Indeed, $\theta_{L/K}(\mathfrak p)$ is the [Artin symbol](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#artin-symbol), whose restriction to the residue field is $x\mapsto x^{N\mathfrak p}$. The Galois group of the finite residue-field extension is generated by this Frobenius automorphism and has order $f(\mathfrak P/\mathfrak p)$. The fundamental identity $efg=[L:K]$, with $e=1$, gives the formula for $g$.

<h4 id="1/1/3">3</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1/1)

<h5 id="1/1/3/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#1/1/3)

The [Hilbert class field](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#hilbert-class-field) $H_K$ is the maximal unramified abelian extension of $K$, including the condition that real places do not become complex when infinite ramification is included. The [Artin reciprocity law](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#artin-reciprocity-law) induces a canonical isomorphism

$$
\operatorname{Cl}(K)\xrightarrow{\sim}\operatorname{Gal}(H_K/K),
\qquad
[\mathfrak a]\longmapsto\left(\frac{H_K/K}{\mathfrak a}\right).
$$

**Consequently $[H_K:K]=h_K$.**

<h4 id="1/1/4">4</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1/1)

<h5 id="1/1/4/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#1/1/4)

For an unramified prime $\mathfrak p$, the [ideal-theoretic decomposition law](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#ideal-theoretic-decomposition-law) gives

$$
\mathfrak p\text{ splits completely in }H_K
\iff
\left(\frac{H_K/K}{\mathfrak p}\right)=1.
$$

Under the [Hilbert class field](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#hilbert-class-field) isomorphism, this Artin symbol is the image of $[\mathfrak p]\in\operatorname{Cl}(K)$. It is trivial exactly when $[\mathfrak p]=1$, which means exactly that $\mathfrak p$ is a [principal ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal).

<h4 id="1/1/5">5</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1/1)

<h5 id="1/1/5/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#1/1/5)

Let $K=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-6})$, whose discriminant is $-24$, and put

$$
H=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-6},\sqrt2)
=\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,\sqrt{-3}).
$$

This is a biquadratic extension of $\mathbb Q$ whose three quadratic subfields have discriminants $-24$, $8$, and $-3$. The [discriminant of a biquadratic field](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#discriminant-of-a-biquadratic-field) is therefore

$$
d_H=(-24)\cdot8\cdot(-3)=576=d_K^2.
$$

The relative discriminant formula gives

$$
N_{K/\mathbb Q}(\mathfrak d_{H/K})
=\frac{|d_H|}{|d_K|^{[H:K]}}=1,
$$

so $H/K$ is unramified at every finite prime. Both fields are totally imaginary, so no infinite place ramifies. Since $[H:K]=2=h_K$, the extension has the full degree of the [Hilbert class field](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#hilbert-class-field), and hence

$$
\boxed{H_K=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-6},\sqrt2).}
$$

<h2 id="2">2</h2>

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 123](paper-123.md)

<h3 id="2/1">1</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/1/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#2/1)

For $\operatorname{Re}s>1$, the [Dedekind zeta function](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#dedekind-zeta-function) of $K$ is

$$
\zeta_K(s)=\sum_{0\ne\mathfrak a\subseteq\mathcal O_K}(N\mathfrak a)^{-s}.
$$

Unique factorization of ideals gives its [Euler product](../../../analytic-number-theory.md#euler-product)

$$
\zeta_K(s)=\prod_{\mathfrak p}\left(1-(N\mathfrak p)^{-s}\right)^{-1},
$$

over the nonzero prime ideals of $\mathcal O_K$.

<h3 id="2/2">2</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/2/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2/2)

The [analytic class number formula](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#analytic-class-number-formula) states that $\zeta_K(s)$ has a simple pole at $s=1$ with

$$
\operatorname*{res}_{s=1}\zeta_K(s)
=\frac{2^{r_1}(2\pi)^{r_2}h_KR_K}
{w_K\sqrt{|d_K|}},
$$

where $(r_1,r_2)$ is the signature, $h_K$ the class number, $R_K$ the regulator, $w_K$ the number of roots of unity in $K$, and $d_K$ the field discriminant.

<h4 id="2/2/3">3</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2/2)

<h5 id="2/2/3/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#2/2/3)

A [Dirichlet character](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#dirichlet-character) modulo $m$ is a group homomorphism

$$
\chi:(\mathbb Z/m\mathbb Z)^\times\longrightarrow\mathbb C^\times,
$$

extended periodically to $\mathbb Z$ by setting $\chi(n)=0$ when $(n,m)>1$. Its [Dirichlet L-function](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#dirichlet-l-function) is

$$
\boxed{L(s,\chi)=\sum_{n\geq1}\frac{\chi(n)}{n^s}
=\prod_p(1-\chi(p)p^{-s})^{-1}
\qquad(\operatorname{Re}s>1).}
$$

<h4 id="2/2/4">4</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2/2)

<h5 id="2/2/4/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#2/2/4)

Let $K/\mathbb Q$ be quadratic with discriminant $d_K$, and let

$$
\chi_{d_K}(n)=\left(\frac{d_K}{n}\right)
$$

be its primitive quadratic [Dirichlet character](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#kronecker-symbol). For an unramified prime $p$, the Euler factor of $\zeta_K$ is

$$
\begin{cases}
(1-p^{-s})^{-2},&p\text{ splits},\\
(1-p^{-2s})^{-1},&p\text{ is inert}.
\end{cases}
$$

Since $\chi_{d_K}(p)$ is respectively $1$ or $-1$, this equals the product of the Euler factors of $\zeta(s)$ and $L(s,\chi_{d_K})$. At a ramified prime $\chi_{d_K}(p)=0$, and the same equality holds. Thus

$$
\boxed{\zeta_K(s)=\zeta(s)L(s,\chi_{d_K}).}
$$

<h4 id="2/2/5">5</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2/2)

<h5 id="2/2/5/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#2/2/5)

Suppose $d_K<-4$. Then $K$ has signature $(0,1)$, regulator $1$, and exactly $w_K=2$ roots of unity. The [analytic class number formula](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#analytic-class-number-formula) becomes

$$
\operatorname*{res}_{s=1}\zeta_K(s)
=\frac{\pi h_K}{\sqrt{|d_K|}}.
$$

Because $\zeta_K(s)=\zeta(s)L(s,\chi_{d_K})$ and $\operatorname*{res}_{s=1}\zeta(s)=1$,

$$
h_K=\frac{\sqrt{|d_K|}}{\pi}|L(1,\chi_{d_K})|.
$$

Putting $m=|d_K|$ into the supplied odd-character identity yields the [quadratic class number formula](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#quadratic-class-number-formula)

$$
h_K
=\frac1{|2-\chi_{d_K}(2)|}
\left|
\sum_{\substack{a\in(\mathbb Z/m\mathbb Z)^\times\\a<m/2}}
\chi_{d_K}(a)
\right|.
$$

<h4 id="2/2/6">6</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2/2)

<h5 id="2/2/6/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#2/2/6)

For $K=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-11})$, the fundamental discriminant is $-11$ and $\chi_{-11}(2)=-1$ because $-11\equiv5\pmod8$. The quadratic residues modulo $11$ are

$$
1,3,4,5,9.
$$

Therefore

$$
\sum_{1\leq a<11/2}\chi_{-11}(a)
=\chi(1)+\cdots+\chi(5)
=1-1+1+1+1=3.
$$

The [quadratic class number formula](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#quadratic-class-number-formula) gives

$$
\boxed{h_K=\frac{|3|}{|2-(-1)|}=1.}
$$

<h2 id="3">3</h2>

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 123](paper-123.md)

<h3 id="3/1">1</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/1/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#3/1)

For a set $S$ of nonzero prime ideals of $K$, its [Dirichlet density](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#dirichlet-density) is

$$
\delta(S)=\lim_{s\to1^+}
\frac{\sum_{\mathfrak p\in S}(N\mathfrak p)^{-s}}
{\log(1/(s-1))},
$$

provided the limit exists.

<h3 id="3/2">2</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/2/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#3/2)

The [Chebotarev density theorem](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#chebotarev-density-theorem) says that if $L/K$ is a finite Galois extension with group $G$ and $C\subseteq G$ is a conjugacy class, then the unramified primes $\mathfrak p$ whose Frobenius conjugacy class equals $C$ have [Dirichlet density](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#dirichlet-density)

$$
\boxed{\frac{|C|}{|G|}.}
$$

<h3 id="3/3">3</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/3/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3/3)

Let $\mathfrak f_{L/K}$ be the [conductor of an abelian extension](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#conductor-of-an-abelian-extension) and let $\mathfrak m$ be divisible by it. For any $\sigma\in\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)$, the [Chebotarev density theorem](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#chebotarev-density-theorem) supplies infinitely many unramified prime ideals $\mathfrak p$ with

$$
\left(\frac{L/K}{\mathfrak p}\right)=\sigma.
$$

Only finitely many primes divide $\mathfrak m_0$, so $\mathfrak p$ may be chosen prime to $\mathfrak m_0$. It then belongs to $I_K(\mathfrak m)$ and its image under the [Artin reciprocity map](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#artin-reciprocity-law) is $\sigma$. Hence the Artin map is surjective.

<h4 id="3/3/4">4</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3/3)

<h5 id="3/3/4/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#3/3/4)

A prime splits completely in $L$ exactly when its Frobenius conjugacy class is $\{1\}$. Applying the [Chebotarev density theorem](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#chebotarev-density-theorem) to the identity class gives

$$
\boxed{\delta(\mathcal S_{L/K})
=\frac1{|\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)|}
=\frac1{[L:K]}.}
$$

<h4 id="3/3/5">5</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3/3)

<h5 id="3/3/5/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#3/3/5)

By Question 1, a prime ideal of $K$ is principal exactly when it splits completely in the [Hilbert class field](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#hilbert-class-field) $H_K$. Question 3(4) therefore gives

$$
\boxed{\delta\{\mathfrak p:\mathfrak p\text{ is principal}\}
=\frac1{[H_K:K]}
=\frac1{h_K}.}
$$

<h2 id="4">4</h2>

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 123](paper-123.md)

<h3 id="4/1">1</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#4)

<h4 id="4/1/a">a</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#4/1)

<h5 id="4/1/a/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#4/1/a)

The [adele ring](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#adele-ring) is the restricted product

$$
\mathbb A_K=\prod_v'K_v
=\left\{(x_v)_v:x_v\in\mathcal O_v\text{ for all but finitely many finite }v\right\}.
$$

Addition and multiplication are componentwise. If $x_v,y_v\in\mathcal O_v$ outside finite sets, then $x_v+y_v,x_vy_v\in\mathcal O_v$ outside their union, so these operations are closed on $\mathbb A_K$. The componentwise ring axioms make it a ring. Its [restricted product topology](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#restricted-product-topology) has basic open sets $\prod_vU_v$ with each $U_v\subseteq K_v$ open and $U_v=\mathcal O_v$ at all but finitely many finite places.

<h4 id="4/1/b">b</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#4/1)

<h5 id="4/1/b/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#4/1/b)

The [idele group](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#idele-group) is

$$
\mathbb I_K=\prod_v'K_v^\times
=\left\{(x_v)_v:x_v\in\mathcal O_v^\times
\text{ for all but finitely many finite }v\right\},
$$

the restricted product with respect to the local unit groups.

<h4 id="4/1/c">c</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#4/1)

<h5 id="4/1/c/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#4/1/c)

Under

$$
\phi:\mathbb A_K^\times\longrightarrow\mathbb A_K\times\mathbb A_K,
\qquad
x\longmapsto(x,x^{-1}),
$$

the preimage of a basic product neighbourhood $U\times V$ is

$$
U\cap V^{-1}.
$$

At almost all finite places, $U_v=V_v=\mathcal O_v$, and the condition $x_v,x_v^{-1}\in\mathcal O_v$ is exactly $x_v\in\mathcal O_v^\times$. Thus these preimages form the usual restricted-product basis for $\mathbb I_K$. Conversely, every basic idele neighbourhood is obtained by choosing suitable $U_v$ and $V_v$ locally. Hence the [restricted product topology on the idele group](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#restricted-product-topology-on-the-idele-group) equals the subspace topology induced by $\phi$.

<h3 id="4/2">2</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#4)

<h4 id="4/2/a">a</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#4/2)

<h5 id="4/2/a/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#4/2/a)

The diagonal map

$$
K^\times\longrightarrow\mathbb I_K,
\qquad
\alpha\longmapsto(\alpha)_v,
$$

is an injective homomorphism because every completion map $K\to K_v$ is injective. The diagonal copy of $K$ is discrete in the [adele ring](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#adele-ring). Choose an adelic neighbourhood $W$ of $1$ with $W\cap K=\{1\}$. By the subspace description of the idele topology, $W\cap\mathbb I_K$ is an idele neighbourhood of $1$ meeting diagonal $K^\times$ only at $1$. Translation proves that $K^\times$ is a discrete subgroup of $\mathbb I_K$.

<h4 id="4/2/b">b</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#4/2)

<h5 id="4/2/b/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#4/2/b)

For each place $v$ of $K$ and each $w\mid v$ of $L$, the inclusion $K_v\hookrightarrow L_w$ defines

$$
i_{L/K}:\mathbb I_K\longrightarrow\mathbb I_L,
\qquad
(x_v)_v\longmapsto(x_v)_{w\mid v}.
$$

At all but finitely many finite $v$, the component $x_v$ is a unit, and its image is a unit at every $w\mid v$, so the image is an idele. The map is a homomorphism and is injective because every local inclusion is injective.

<h4 id="4/2/c">c</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#4/2)

<h5 id="4/2/c/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#4/2/c)

The [idele norm](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#idele-norm) has $v$-component

$$
\bigl(N_{L/K}x\bigr)_v
=\prod_{w\mid v}N_{L_w/K_v}(x_w).
$$

For the principal idele associated with $\alpha\in L^\times$, compatibility of local and global field norms gives

$$
N_{L/K}((\alpha)_w)
=\bigl(N_{L/K}(\alpha)\bigr)_v.
$$

The right side is the principal idele of the element $N_{L/K}(\alpha)\in K^\times$.

<h4 id="4/2/d">d</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#4/2)

<h5 id="4/2/d/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#4/2/d)

Let $G=\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)$ and let

$$
M=L^{[G,G]}
$$

be the fixed field of the commutator subgroup. Then $M/K$ is the maximal abelian subextension of $L/K$, with

$$
\operatorname{Gal}(M/K)\cong G^{\mathrm{ab}}.
$$

The [nonabelian norm-residue kernel theorem](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#nonabelian-norm-residue-kernel-theorem) identifies the kernel of the global reciprocity map

$$
C_K\longrightarrow G^{\mathrm{ab}}
$$

with $N_{L/K}C_L$. Applied to the abelian extension $M/K$, the [Artin reciprocity law](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#artin-reciprocity-law) identifies the kernel of the same map with $N_{M/K}C_M$. Therefore

$$
N_{L/K}C_L=N_{M/K}C_M,
$$

so the norm group of the Galois extension $L/K$ is the norm group of the abelian extension $M/K$.
