<h1 id="16h/a/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [A](../a.md)

Assume the [axiom of choice](../../../../../../axiom-of-choice.md). By the [well-ordering theorem](../../../../../../well-ordering-theorem.md), every set is bijective with an [ordinal](../../../../../../ordinal.md), and hence with an [initial ordinal](../../../../../../initial-ordinal.md). Any two ordinals are comparable, so for any sets $K,L$ either $K$ injects into $L$ or $L$ injects into $K$. Transitivity is immediate, and antisymmetry on cardinalities follows from the [Cantor-Schröder-Bernstein theorem](../../../../../../cantor-schroder-bernstein-theorem.md). Thus cardinal comparison is a total order.

Conversely, assume the [cardinal comparability principle](../../../../../../cardinal-comparability-principle.md). Given any set $X$, let $h(X)$ be its Hartogs ordinal. By [Hartogs theorem](../../../../../../hartogs-theorem.md), there is no injection $h(X)\to X$. Comparability therefore supplies an injection $X\to h(X)$. Pulling the ordinal order back to $X$ gives a well-order of $X$. Hence every set can be well-ordered, and the [well-ordering theorem](../../../../../../well-ordering-theorem.md) implies the axiom of choice.
