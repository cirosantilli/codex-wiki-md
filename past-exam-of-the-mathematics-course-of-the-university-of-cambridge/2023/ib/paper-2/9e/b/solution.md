<h1 id="9e/b/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [B](../b.md)

Let $N\mathrel{\trianglelefteq}G$ be proper. If $7$ divides $|N|$, then $N$ contains a Sylow $7$-subgroup of $G$, and normality makes it contain all eight of them. Thus $n_7(N)=8$, so the Sylow divisibility theorem gives

$$
56=7\cdot8\mid |N|.
$$

Since $|N|$ divides 168 and $N$ is proper, this forces $|N|=56$.

Every nontrivial normal [subgroup](../../../../../../subgroup.md) is transitive by [normal subgroup orbits in a faithful prime-degree action](../../../../../../normal-subgroup-orbits-in-a-faithful-prime-degree-action.md). Hence

$$
|N\cap G_x|=|N_x|=56/7=8.
$$

But $N\cap G_x$ is normal in $G_x\cong S_4$, whereas $S_4$ has three, rather than one, Sylow $2$-subgroups. This contradiction proves that no proper normal [subgroup](../../../../../../subgroup.md) has order divisible by seven.

If instead $3$ divides $|N|$, normality makes $N$ contain all 28 Sylow $3$-subgroups of $G$. Hence $n_3(N)=28$, so $28$ divides $|N|/3$ and therefore $84$ divides $|N|$. A proper such [subgroup](../../../../../../subgroup.md) would have order 84, which is divisible by seven and has just been ruled out.

Finally, if $N$ is any nontrivial normal [subgroup](../../../../../../subgroup.md), the prime-degree orbit argument makes $N$ transitive, so seven divides $|N|$. This is impossible for a proper normal [subgroup](../../../../../../subgroup.md). Therefore $G$ is simple.
