↑ **Parent:** [C](../c.md)

The [Cayley-Hamilton theorem](../../../../../../cayley-hamilton-theorem.md) states that a square [matrix](../../../../../../matrix.md) satisfies its characteristic [polynomial](../../../../../../polynomial-split.md). If a $2\times2$ [matrix](../../../../../../matrix.md) is diagonalisable, $A=S\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\lambda_2)S^{-1}$; applying $p(t)=(t-\lambda_1)(t-\lambda_2)$ to the diagonal [matrix](../../../../../../matrix.md) gives zero and hence $p(A)=0$.

If $B^k=0$, every [eigenvalue](../../../../../../eigenvalue.md) $\lambda$ satisfies $\lambda^k=0$, so all [eigenvalues](../../../../../../eigenvalue.md) are zero and the characteristic [polynomial](../../../../../../polynomial-split.md) is $t^n$. Cayley--Hamilton then gives $B^n=0$.
