## 1

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 327](paper-327.md)

<h3 id="1/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

The [Schwartz space](../../../fourier-analysis.md#schwartz-space) consists of the [smooth functions](../../../analysis.md#smooth-function) $\varphi$ for which

$$
p_{\alpha,\beta}(\varphi)=\sup_{x\in\mathbb R^n}|x^\alpha\partial^\beta\varphi(x)|<\infty
$$

for all [multi-indices](../../../distribution-theory.md#multi-index-notation) $\alpha,\beta$. Its topology is generated by these [seminorms](../../../topological-vector-space.md#seminorm); $\varphi_j\to\varphi$ means convergence in every seminorm. A [tempered distribution](../../../fourier-analysis.md#tempered-distribution) is a continuous linear functional on this space. Convergence in $\mathcal S'$ here means weak convergence: $\langle u_j,\varphi\rangle\to\langle u,\varphi\rangle$ for every Schwartz function.

Use the unnormalized angular-frequency [Fourier transform](../../../analysis.md#fourier-transform)

$$
\widehat\varphi(\lambda)=\int_{\mathbb R^n}e^{-ix\cdot\lambda}\varphi(x)\,dx,
\qquad \langle\widehat u,\varphi\rangle=\langle u,\widehat\varphi\rangle.
$$

The transform is a continuous automorphism of the [Schwartz space](../../../fourier-analysis.md#schwartz-space), so this duality defines a [tempered distribution](../../../fourier-analysis.md#tempered-distribution). Put $D_j=-i\partial_j$, as required by the stated identities. [Integration by parts](../../../calculus.md#integration-by-parts) and differentiation under the integral give

$$
D_x^\alpha\widehat\varphi(x)=(-1)^{|\alpha|}\widehat{\lambda^\alpha\varphi}(x),
\qquad \widehat{D_\lambda^\beta\varphi}(x)=x^\beta\widehat\varphi(x).
$$

Thus, keeping the transpose factor of $D=-i\partial$ explicit,

$$
\langle\widehat{D_j u},\varphi\rangle
=i\langle u,\partial_{x_j}\widehat\varphi\rangle
=\langle\widehat u,\lambda_j\varphi\rangle,
$$

and

$$
\langle\widehat{x_j u},\varphi\rangle
=\langle u,x_j\widehat\varphi\rangle
=\langle\widehat u,D_j\varphi\rangle
=\langle-D_j\widehat u,\varphi\rangle.
$$

Iterating gives the [Fourier derivative identities for distributions](../../../fourier-analysis.md#fourier-derivative-identities-for-distributions)

$$
\boxed{\widehat{D^\alpha u}=\lambda^\alpha\widehat u,
\qquad \widehat{x^\beta u}=(-1)^{|\beta|}D^\beta\widehat u.}
$$

The logarithm defines a [tempered distribution](../../../fourier-analysis.md#tempered-distribution) because it is locally integrable and grows more slowly than a polynomial. Its derivative is $u'(x)=x/(1+x^2)$. The standard transform pair $\widehat{(1+x^2)^{-1}}=\pi e^{-|\lambda|}$, obtainable by the [residue theorem](../../../analysis.md#residue-theorem), gives

$$
\widehat{u'}(\lambda)=i\partial_\lambda(\pi e^{-|\lambda|})
=-i\pi\operatorname{sgn}(\lambda)e^{-|\lambda|}.
$$

As $\widehat{u'}=i\lambda\widehat u$, we have $\lambda\widehat u=-\pi\operatorname{sgn}(\lambda)e^{-|\lambda|}$. Define

$$
\langle T,\varphi\rangle
=-\pi\int_{\mathbb R}\frac{\varphi(\lambda)-\varphi(0)}{|\lambda|}e^{-|\lambda|}\,d\lambda.
$$

This integral is absolutely convergent: the numerator is $O(|\lambda|)$ near zero, and the exponential controls the tails. It defines a tempered distribution satisfying the same multiplication equation. The [kernel of multiplication by a coordinate](../../../distribution-theory.md#kernel-of-multiplication-by-a-coordinate) therefore gives $\widehat u=T+c\delta_0$.

To determine $c$, use the supplied [Gaussian Fourier transform](../../../fourier-analysis.md#fourier-transform-of-a-gaussian) pair with $\varphi_n(0)=1/(2\sqrt\pi)$. Then

$$
\langle\widehat u,\varphi_n\rangle
=\sqrt n\int_{\mathbb R}\tfrac12\log(1+x^2)e^{-nx^2}\,dx
=\int_{\mathbb R}\tfrac12\log(1+y^2/n)e^{-y^2}\,dy\longrightarrow0.
$$

Also $\langle T,\varphi_n\rangle\to0$ by the [dominated convergence theorem](../../../measure-theory.md#dominated-convergence-theorem): the relevant integrand is bounded by a constant times $|\lambda|e^{-|\lambda|}$ for $n\geq1$. Consequently

$$
\boxed{c=0,\qquad
\langle\widehat u,\varphi\rangle
=-\pi\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow0}\int_{|\lambda|>\varepsilon}
\frac{\varphi(\lambda)-\varphi(0)}{|\lambda|}e^{-|\lambda|}\,d\lambda.}
$$

The cutoff limit agrees with the absolutely convergent integral; this particular subtraction convention fixes the otherwise ambiguous delta term. The reusable identity is [Fourier transform of the logarithm of one plus x squared](../../../fourier-analysis.md#fourier-transform-of-the-logarithm-of-one-plus-x-squared).

<h3 id="2/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

The [space of test functions](../../../distribution-theory.md#space-of-test-functions) is $\mathcal D(\mathbb R)=C_c^\infty(\mathbb R)$. A sequence $\varphi_j$ converges to $\varphi$ when its supports eventually lie in a single compact set and every derivative converges uniformly. The [distribution](../../../distribution-theory.md#distribution-mathematical-analysis) space $\mathcal D'(\mathbb R)$ is its continuous linear dual; weak convergence means $\langle u_j,\varphi\rangle\to\langle u,\varphi\rangle$ for every test function. We use $\langle u',\varphi\rangle=-\langle u,\varphi'\rangle$ and $\langle au,\varphi\rangle=\langle u,a\varphi\rangle$ for [multiplication of a distribution by a smooth function](../../../distribution-theory.md#multiplication-of-a-distribution-by-a-smooth-function).

<h3 id="2/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#2/a)

If a [test function](../../../distribution-theory.md#test-function) $\varphi$ has integral zero, then

$$
\psi(x)=\int_{-\infty}^x\varphi(t)\,dt
$$

is another compactly supported smooth test function with $\psi'=\varphi$. Hence $v'=0$ implies $\langle v,\varphi\rangle=-\langle v',\psi\rangle=0$ for every such $\varphi$.

Choose a test function $\eta$ with integral one. For arbitrary $\varphi$, the function $\varphi-\eta\int\varphi$ has zero integral, so

$$
\boxed{\langle v,\varphi\rangle=\langle v,\eta\rangle\int_{\mathbb R}\varphi(x)\,dx.}
$$

Thus $v$ is the constant distribution $\langle v,\eta\rangle$. This proves [a distribution with zero derivative is constant](../../../distribution-theory.md#a-distribution-with-zero-derivative-is-constant).

<h3 id="2/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

The [principal-value reciprocal distribution](../../../distribution-theory.md#principal-value-reciprocal-distribution) is

$$
\left\langle\operatorname{pv}\frac1x,\varphi\right\rangle
=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow0}\int_{|x|>\varepsilon}\frac{\varphi(x)}x\,dx.
$$

The limit exists because the constant part of the numerator cancels symmetrically at zero. Multiplying by $x$ gives $x\operatorname{pv}(1/x)=1$.

If $v$ is any other solution, $w=v-\operatorname{pv}(1/x)$ obeys $xw=0$. To identify this [kernel of multiplication by a coordinate](../../../distribution-theory.md#kernel-of-multiplication-by-a-coordinate), choose a [cutoff function](../../../distribution-theory.md#cutoff-function) $\eta$ equal to one near zero. Every test function has the form $\varphi=\varphi(0)\eta+x\psi$ with $\psi\in\mathcal D$. Thus $\langle w,\varphi\rangle=\varphi(0)\langle w,\eta\rangle$, proving $w=c\delta_0$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{v=\operatorname{pv}\frac1x+c\delta_0,\qquad c\in\mathbb C.}
$$

For real distributions the constants are real.

<h3 id="2/i">i</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/i/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#2/i)

The [Leibniz rule](../../../calculus.md#leibniz-rule) rewrites the left side as $(xu)'$. By [a distribution with zero derivative is constant](../../../distribution-theory.md#a-distribution-with-zero-derivative-is-constant),

$$
(xu)'=\delta'_0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad xu=\delta_0+c.
$$

The [Dirac delta multiplication identity](../../../distribution-theory.md#dirac-delta-multiplication-identity) $x\delta'_0=-\delta_0$ supplies a particular solution, and the preceding division result supplies all solutions of $xu=c$. Hence

$$
\boxed{u=-\delta'_0+c\operatorname{pv}\frac1x+d\delta_0,\qquad c,d\in\mathbb C.}
$$

Multiplying by $x$ and then differentiating verifies the equation. The coordinate-multiplication kernel and the constant-derivative kernel show that no additional solutions are missing.

<h3 id="2/ii">ii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/ii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#2/ii)

The [distributional derivative of the Heaviside step function](../../../distribution-theory.md#distributional-derivative-of-the-heaviside-step-function) gives $H'=\delta_0$, while $((x-1)H(x-1))''=\delta_1$. A particular solution is therefore $H(x)-2(x-1)H(x-1)$.

The difference from any other solution has second derivative zero. Applying [a distribution with zero derivative is constant](../../../distribution-theory.md#a-distribution-with-zero-derivative-is-constant) twice shows that difference is affine. Thus

$$
\boxed{u=H(x)-2(x-1)H(x-1)+ax+b,\qquad a,b\in\mathbb C.}
$$

The ramp function is continuous at $x=1$, and its first derivative jumps there, producing the indicated delta source in its second derivative.

<h3 id="2/iii">iii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/iii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#2/iii)

Since $(x^2-1)(-\delta_0)=\delta_0$, write $u'=-\delta_0+w$, where $(x^2-1)w=0$. The polynomial has two simple zeros. The [distribution annihilated by a function with simple zeros](../../../distribution-theory.md#distribution-annihilated-by-a-function-with-simple-zeros) lemma gives

$$
w=a\delta_{-1}+b\delta_1.
$$

Integrating with the [Heaviside step function](../../../analysis.md#heaviside-step-function) yields

$$
\boxed{u=-H(x)+aH(x+1)+bH(x-1)+c,\qquad a,b,c\in\mathbb C.}
$$

This includes every solution: its derivative has exactly the required delta terms, and any two primitives differ only by a constant distribution.

## 3

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 327](paper-327.md)

<h3 id="3/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

The [Paley–Wiener–Schwartz theorem](../../../distribution-theory.md#paley-wiener-schwartz-theorem) in its closed-ball form says that a [tempered distribution](../../../fourier-analysis.md#tempered-distribution) $u$ is supported in $\overline B_R(0)$ if and only if its [Fourier transform](../../../analysis.md#fourier-transform) is the real restriction of an [entire function](../../../complex-analysis.md#entire-function) $F$ on $\mathbb C^n$ satisfying, for some $C>0$ and integer $N\geq0$,

$$
\boxed{|F(\zeta)|\leq C(1+|\zeta|)^N e^{R|\operatorname{Im}\zeta|}.}
$$

The distribution is unique by [Fourier inversion](../../../fourier-analysis.md#fourier-inversion-theorem). More generally a compact convex support set $K$ replaces $R|\operatorname{Im}\zeta|$ by its [support function](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#support-function) $H_K(\operatorname{Im}\zeta)$.

For the forward implication, a [compactly supported distribution](../../../distribution-theory.md#compactly-supported-distribution) has finite [order of a distribution](../../../distribution-theory.md#order-of-a-distribution), say $m$, on a fixed neighborhood of its support. Define

$$
F(\zeta)=\langle u,e^{-ix\cdot\zeta}\rangle,
$$

using a cutoff equal to one near the support. Differentiation with respect to each complex coordinate is allowed in this pairing and gives $\partial_{\zeta_j}F=\langle u,-ix_je^{-ix\cdot\zeta}\rangle$, so $F$ is entire.

To obtain the exact radius $R$, rather than an enlarged radius, choose a [cutoff function](../../../distribution-theory.md#cutoff-function) $\chi_\varepsilon$ equal to one near $\overline B_R$ and supported in $B_{R+\varepsilon}$, with derivatives through order $m$ bounded by $C_\gamma\varepsilon^{-|\gamma|}$. Set $\varepsilon=(1+|\zeta|)^{-1}$. The finite-order bound and the [Leibniz rule](../../../calculus.md#leibniz-rule) give

$$
|F(\zeta)|\leq C(1+|\zeta|)^m e^{(R+\varepsilon)|\operatorname{Im}\zeta|}
\leq Ce(1+|\zeta|)^m e^{R|\operatorname{Im}\zeta|}.
$$

This proves the required growth estimate.

For the converse, $F$ has at most [polynomial growth](../../../analysis.md#polynomial-growth) on real frequencies, so define its inverse [tempered distribution](../../../fourier-analysis.md#tempered-distribution) by

$$
\langle u,\varphi\rangle=(2\pi)^{-n}\int_{\mathbb R^n}F(\xi)\widehat\varphi(-\xi)\,d\xi.
$$

Let a compactly supported test function lie in a half-space $x\cdot\omega\geq R+\eta$, with $|\omega|=1$ and $\eta>0$. By [contour shifting](../../../complex-analysis.md#contour-shifting),

$$
\langle u,\varphi\rangle=(2\pi)^{-n}\int_{\mathbb R^n}
F(\xi+it\omega)\widehat\varphi(-\xi-it\omega)\,d\xi,\qquad t\geq0.
$$

Here is the decay needed to justify the shift and then let $t$ grow. Repeated [integration by parts](../../../calculus.md#integration-by-parts), applying $1-\Delta_x$ to $e^{-t x\cdot\omega}\varphi(x)$, gives for every integer $M$,

$$
|\widehat\varphi(-\xi-it\omega)|
\leq C_M(1+t)^{2M}e^{-(R+\eta)t}(1+|\xi|^2)^{-M}.
$$

Choose $2M>N+n$. For each fixed $t$, this decay makes the vertical sides of a large rectangle vanish in the one complex coordinate parallel to $\omega$; [Cauchy integral theorem](../../../complex-analysis.md#cauchy-s-integral-theorem) then supplies the contour identity. Combining the two bounds yields

$$
|\langle u,\varphi\rangle|
\leq C'(1+t)^{N+2M}e^{-\eta t}\longrightarrow0.
$$

Thus $u$ vanishes on such test functions. Every point outside $\overline B_R$ has a neighborhood of this form. A [partition of unity](../../../differential-geometry.md#partition-of-unity) therefore proves $\operatorname{supp}u\subseteq\overline B_R$, completing the converse. The same argument with separating half-spaces proves the compact-convex-set form. This is the [contour-shift proof of the Paley–Wiener–Schwartz theorem](../../../distribution-theory.md#contour-shift-proof-of-the-paley-wiener-schwartz-theorem).

For a regular function $u$, the [change of variables](../../../calculus.md#change-of-variables-formula) $y=tx$ gives

$$
\int u(tx)\varphi(x)\,dx=t^{-n}\int u(y)\varphi(y/t)\,dy.
$$

The map $\varphi\mapsto\varphi(\mathord\cdot/t)$ is continuous on the [Schwartz space](../../../fourier-analysis.md#schwartz-space), so duality extends the [dilation of a distribution](../../../distribution-theory.md#dilation-of-a-distribution) to

$$
\boxed{\langle\delta_tu,\varphi\rangle=t^{-n}\langle u,\delta_{1/t}\varphi\rangle.}
$$

Its Fourier transform obeys $\widehat{\delta_tu}(\zeta)=t^{-n}F(\zeta/t)$. If the original support is in the unit ball, the theorem bounds $F$ by $C(1+|\zeta|)^Ne^{|\operatorname{Im}\zeta|}$. Hence

$$
|\widehat{\delta_tu}(\zeta)|\leq C_t(1+|\zeta|)^N e^{|\operatorname{Im}\zeta|/t}.
$$

The converse theorem gives the [support law for distributional dilation](../../../distribution-theory.md#support-law-for-distributional-dilation)

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{supp}(\delta_tu)\subseteq\{x:|x|\leq1/t\}=\{x:|tx|\leq1\}.}
$$
