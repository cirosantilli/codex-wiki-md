<h1 id="12e/b/ii/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](../ii.md)

Let $(x_n)$ be any [sequence](../../../../../../../sequence.md) in a complete, totally bounded metric space $X$. Cover $X$ by finitely many balls of radius $2^{-1}$; one contains infinitely many terms. From those terms choose an infinite subsequence lying in one ball of radius $2^{-2}$, and continue. The [diagonal argument](../../../../../../../diagonal-argument.md) produces a subsequence $(x_{n_j})$ whose tail from its $j$th term onward lies in a ball of radius $2^{-j}$. Hence

$$
d(x_{n_k},x_{n_\ell})<2^{1-j}
$$

whenever $k,\ell\geq j$, so the subsequence is Cauchy. Completeness makes it converge in $X$.

Every sequence in $X$ therefore has a convergent subsequence, so $X$ is [sequentially compact](../../../../../../../sequentially-compact-space.md). Sequential compactness is equivalent to compactness for metric spaces, and thus $X$ is compact. This proves that a [complete totally bounded metric space is compact](../../../../../../../complete-totally-bounded-metric-space-is-compact.md).
