## 1I

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="1i/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1I](#1i)

A [reduced positive definite binary quadratic form](../../../number-theory.md#reduced-positive-definite-binary-quadratic-form) $f=[a,b,c]=ax^2+bxy+cy^2$ satisfies

$$
|b|\leq a\leq c,
$$

with $b\geq0$ when $|b|=a$ or $a=c$. Its [discriminant of a binary quadratic form](../../../number-theory.md#discriminant-of-a-binary-quadratic-form) is $d=b^2-4ac<0$. The first displayed inequality already gives $|b|\leq a$, while $c\geq a$ gives

$$
|d|=4ac-b^2\geq4a^2-a^2=3a^2.
$$

Taking the positive [square root](../../../algebra.md#square-root) yields

$$
\boxed{|b|\leq a\leq\sqrt{|d|/3}.}
$$

Moreover, $d=b^2-4ac\equiv b^2\equiv b\pmod2$, so $b\equiv d\pmod2$. For fixed negative $d$, the displayed bounds leave only [finitely many](../../../set.md#finite-set) possible [integers](../../../number-theory.md#integer) $a$ and $b$, and then $c=(b^2-d)/(4a)$ is uniquely determined. Hence only finitely many reduced forms have discriminant $d$.

For $d=-15$, the bound gives $a\leq\sqrt5$, so $a\in\{1,2\}$. The [modular congruence](../../../number-theory.md#modular-congruence) condition makes $b$ [odd](../../../number-theory.md#odd-number). If $a=1$, then $b=\pm1$ and $c=4$; the boundary convention $|b|=a\Rightarrow b\geq0$ retains $[1,1,4]$. If $a=2$, then $b=\pm1$ and $c=2$; the boundary convention $a=c\Rightarrow b\geq0$ retains $[2,1,2]$. Thus the complete list is

$$
\boxed{[1,1,4],\qquad [2,1,2].}
$$

## 2H

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="2h/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2H](#2h)

[Nash's theorem](../../../game-theory.md#nash-s-theorem) says that every finite game, including a two-player game with two pure choices each, has at least one [Nash equilibrium](../../../game-theory.md#nash-equilibrium) in [mixed strategies](../../../game-theory.md#mixed-strategy).

For player $1$, holding $p_2$ fixed, the [expected value](../../../probability-theory.md#expected-value) of the payoff is

$$
E_1=-Up_1p_2+2p_1(1-p_2)-2(1-p_1)p_2+(1-p_1)(1-p_2)
=1-3p_2+p_1\bigl[1-(U-1)p_2\bigr].
$$

It is an [affine function](../../../vector-space.md#affine-function) of $p_1$. Therefore player $1$ chooses $p_1=1$ when $1-(U-1)p_2>0$, chooses $p_1=0$ when it is negative, and is indifferent when it vanishes. By [symmetry](../../../physics.md#symmetry-physics), player $2$ has the analogous [best response](../../../game-theory.md#best-response).

If $U<2$, then $1-(U-1)p>0$ for every $p\in[0,1]$, so becoming a Dark Lord with probability one is a [strictly dominant strategy](../../../game-theory.md#strictly-dominant-strategy). The unique equilibrium is

$$
\boxed{(p_1,p_2)=(1,1).}
$$

If $U>2$, each player is indifferent when the other chooses $p=1/(U-1)$. This gives one interior mixed equilibrium, while the two asymmetric pure equilibria arise because a player facing a certain peasant chooses Lord and a player facing a certain Lord chooses peasant. The three equilibria are

$$
\boxed{(1,0),\qquad(0,1),\qquad
\left(\frac1{U-1},\frac1{U-1}\right).}
$$

Consequently $\boxed{U_0=2}$. At the threshold itself there is a continuum of equilibria with $p_1=1$ or $p_2=1$, consistent with the question's strict inequalities.

## 3G

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="3g/i">i</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3G](#3g)

<h4 id="3g/i/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#3g/i)

A [reliable transmission rate](../../../coding-theory.md#reliable-transmission-rate) $R$ means that length-$n$ codes can carry $M_n$ messages with $n^{-1}\log_2M_n$ tending to at least $R$ while the [probability measure](../../../probability-theory.md#probability-measure) of the decoding-error event tends to zero. By the [Shannon second coding theorem](../../../coding-theory.md#noisy-channel-coding-theorem), the supremum of reliable rates is the [binary symmetric channel capacity](../../../information-theory.md#binary-symmetric-channel-capacity)

$$
C(p)=1-h_2(p),
\qquad
h_2(p)=-p\log_2p-(1-p)\log_2(1-p).
$$

For small positive $p$, the [binary entropy function](../../../combinatorics.md#binary-entropy-function) has the [asymptotic expansion](../../../analysis.md#asymptotic-expansion)

$$
h_2(p)=p\log_2\frac1p+\frac{p}{\ln2}+O(p^2),
$$

so $C(p)$ starts at one bit per channel use and decreases rapidly from one as errors are introduced.

<h3 id="3g/ii">ii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3G](#3g)

<h4 id="3g/ii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#3g/ii)

At $p=1/2$, the output of the [binary symmetric channel](../../../coding-theory.md#binary-symmetric-channel) is [independent](../../../random-variable.md#independent-random-variables) of its input. It conveys no [mutual information](../../../information-theory.md#mutual-information), and therefore

$$
\boxed{C(1/2)=0.}
$$

<h3 id="3g/iii">iii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3G](#3g)

<h4 id="3g/iii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#3g/iii)

If $p>1/2$, complementing every received bit converts the channel into a binary symmetric channel with crossover probability $1-p<1/2$. This deterministic and invertible output relabelling does not change the [channel capacity](../../../information-theory.md#channel-capacity). Since $h_2(p)=h_2(1-p)$,

$$
\boxed{C(p)=1-h_2(p)=C(1-p).}
$$

**Thus the capacity rises again from zero as $p$ increases beyond $1/2$, reaching one at $p=1$, where every bit is flipped deterministically and can be recovered by complementation.**

## 4H

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="4h/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4H](#4h)

<h4 id="4h/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#4h/a)

A [context-free grammar](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#context-free-grammar) is in [Chomsky normal form](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#chomsky-normal-form) when every production is of one of the forms

$$
A\to BC,
\qquad A\to a,
$$

where $A,B,C$ are [nonterminal symbols](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#nonterminal-symbol) and $a$ is a [terminal symbol](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#terminal-symbol). Under this strict convention there is no [epsilon production](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#epsilon-production), so such a grammar cannot generate the [empty word](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#empty-word). Conversion of an arbitrary grammar therefore gives

$$
\boxed{\mathcal L(G_{\rm Chom})=\mathcal L(G)\setminus\{\epsilon\}.}
$$

If $\epsilon\notin\mathcal L(G)$, the two languages are equal. An alternative extended convention permits the exceptional start production $S_0\to\epsilon$; under that convention conversion preserves the whole language.

<h3 id="4h/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4H](#4h)

<h4 id="4h/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#4h/b)

Use the [CYK algorithm](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#cyk-algorithm). For a nonempty input word $v=v_1\cdots v_n$, let $T_{i,j}$ be the set of nonterminals that derive the substring $v_i\cdots v_j$. Initialize

$$
T_{i,i}=\{A:A\to v_i\text{ is a production}\}.
$$

For increasing substring lengths, compute

$$
T_{i,j}=\bigcup_{k=i}^{j-1}
\{A:A\to BC,\ B\in T_{i,k},\ C\in T_{k+1,j}\}.
$$

Accept exactly when the start symbol belongs to $T_{1,n}$. The proof is a [mathematical induction](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#mathematical-induction) on substring length: a one-letter derivation uses a terminal production, while every longer [parse tree](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#parse-tree) has a root production $A\to BC$ whose two subtrees yield two adjacent nonempty substrings. The recurrence considers every possible split point and therefore finds exactly all possible roots. For a fixed grammar the algorithm uses $O(n^3)$ time. Under the strict form in part (a), it rejects $\epsilon$ immediately.

<h3 id="4h/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4H](#4h)

<h4 id="4h/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#4h/c)

Introduce nonterminals for the terminals and one for the pair $bb$:

$$
A\to a,
\qquad B\to b,
\qquad C\to c,
\qquad U\to BB.
$$

Because the original start symbol occurs on a right-hand side, take a fresh start symbol $S_0$. After eliminating the [unit production](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#unit-production) $S\to T$, one grammar in [Chomsky normal form](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#chomsky-normal-form) is

$$
\begin{aligned}
S_0&\to SU\mid AS\mid CC,\\
S&\to SU\mid AS\mid CC,\\
U&\to BB,\\
A&\to a,\qquad B\to b,\qquad C\to c.
\end{aligned}
$$

The original grammar generates exactly

$$
\{a^i cc b^{2j}:i,j\geq0\}.
$$

The displayed grammar generates the same set: $AS$ adds an $a$ on the left, $SU$ adds one $bb$ pair on the right, and $CC$ terminates the derivation with $cc$. Since the original grammar does not generate $\epsilon$,

$$
\boxed{\mathcal L(G)=\mathcal L(G_{\rm Chom}).}
$$

## 5J

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="5j/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [5J](#5j)

<h4 id="5j/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#5j/a)

Writing the [log-likelihood](../../../statistical-modelling.md#log-likelihood) as $\ell(\beta)=\log L(\beta)$, the [Fisher information matrix](../../../statistical-modelling.md#fisher-information-matrix) is the expected negative [Hessian matrix](../../../calculus.md#hessian-matrix)

$$
\boxed{\mathcal I(\beta)=-\mathbb E_\beta\!\left[\nabla_\beta^2\ell(\beta)\right].}
$$

Under the usual regularity conditions it is equivalently the expected outer product of the [score function](../../../statistical-modelling.md#informant-function).

<h3 id="5j/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [5J](#5j)

<h4 id="5j/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#5j/b)

For independent observations from an [exponential dispersion family](../../../exponential-family.md#exponential-dispersion-model) with common known [dispersion parameter](../../../exponential-family.md#dispersion-parameter) $\sigma^2$, the [generalized linear model](../../../statistical-modelling.md#generalized-linear-model) log-likelihood is

$$
\boxed{
\ell(\beta)
=\sum_{i=1}^n
\left[
\frac{y_i\theta_i-b(\theta_i)}{\sigma^2}
+c(y_i,\sigma^2)
\right],
}
$$

where $\mu_i=b'(\theta_i)$ and the [link function](../../../statistical-modelling.md#link-function) satisfies $g(\mu_i)=\eta_i=x_i^T\beta$.

<h3 id="5j/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [5J](#5j)

<h4 id="5j/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#5j/c)

For the [canonical link function](../../../statistical-modelling.md#canonical-link-function), $\theta_i=\eta_i=x_i^T\beta$. Differentiating the log-likelihood gives the [score function](../../../statistical-modelling.md#informant-function)

$$
\nabla_\beta\ell(\beta)=\sigma^{-2}X^T(y-\mu).
$$

Since

$$
\frac{d\mu_i}{d\eta_i}=b''(\theta_i)=V(\mu_i),
$$

where $V$ is the [variance function](../../../exponential-family.md#variance-function), a second differentiation gives

$$
\nabla_\beta^2\ell(\beta)
=-\sigma^{-2}X^T
\operatorname{diag}\bigl(V(\mu_1),\ldots,V(\mu_n)\bigr)X.
$$

This Hessian does not depend on the observed $y$, so taking its negative expectation changes nothing. Hence

$$
\boxed{\mathcal I(\beta)=\sigma^{-2}X^TWX,
\qquad W=\operatorname{diag}\bigl(V(\mu_1),\ldots,V(\mu_n)\bigr).}
$$

## 6C

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="6c/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [6C](#6c)

With the [travelling wave](../../../analysis.md#travelling-wave) coordinate $\xi=x-ct$, the [reaction–diffusion system](../../../diffusion-equation.md#reaction-diffusion-system) becomes

$$
D f''+cf'+rf(1-f)=0,
\qquad f(-\infty)=1,quad f(+\infty)=0.
$$

At the advancing leading edge the healthy-tissue density is small. Put $f(\xi)\sim e^{-\lambda\xi}$ with $\lambda>0$. The [linearization](../../../algebra.md#linearization) at $f=0$ gives

$$
D\lambda^2-c\lambda+r=0,
$$

so a nonoscillatory decaying front requires real positive roots,

$$
\lambda=\frac{c\pm\sqrt{c^2-4Dr}}{2D},
\qquad c\geq2\sqrt{Dr}.
$$

The [pulled travelling front](../../../analysis.md#pulled-travelling-front) selects the [Fisher-KPP minimum wave speed](../../../analysis.md#fisher-kpp-minimum-wave-speed), and therefore

$$
\boxed{c_{\min}=2\sqrt{Dr}.}
$$

For the initial wound $0<x<W$, two mirror-image fronts enter from its endpoints. The left front moves right from $x=0$ and the right front moves left from $x=W$, each with speed approximately $c_{\min}$. A sequence of sketches would show the initial rectangular depression becoming two smooth monotone transition layers, the zero-density plateau shrinking, and the two layers meeting near $x=W/2$ before the profile relaxes to $S=1$. Their meeting time is

$$
\boxed{t_{\rm heal}\approx\frac{W/2}{c_{\min}}
=\frac{W}{4\sqrt{Dr}}.}
$$

There is an additional local relaxation time of order $1/r$ if “close to one” is assigned a fixed numerical tolerance, so the displayed estimate is the dominant healing time when $W$ is large compared with the front width $\sqrt{D/r}$.

<h4 id="7a/a/solution">Solution</h4>

Differentiating under the [contour integration](../../../complex-analysis.md#contour-integration) sign gives

$$
zw''+w=\int_\gamma e^{zt}\bigl(zt^2f(t)+f(t)\bigr)\,dt.
$$

Since $\partial_t e^{zt}=ze^{zt}$, [integration by parts](../../../calculus.md#integration-by-parts) turns this into

$$
\left[e^{zt}t^2f(t)\right]_{\partial\gamma}
+\int_\gamma e^{zt}\left[f(t)-\frac{d}{dt}\bigl(t^2f(t)\bigr)\right]dt.
$$

The integral vanishes when

$$
(t^2f)'=f,
\qquad
\frac{f'}f=\frac1{t^2}-\frac2t.
$$

Hence, up to an irrelevant multiplicative constant,

$$
\boxed{f(t)=t^{-2}e^{-1/t}.}
$$

The remaining endpoint condition is

$$
\boxed{\left[e^{zt-1/t}\right]_{\partial\gamma}=0.}
$$

**Thus each open end of $\gamma$ must lie where $\operatorname{Re}(zt-1/t)\to-\infty$; a closed contour also makes the boundary term vanish when the integrand is single-valued along it.**

<h3 id="7a/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [7A](#7a)

<h4 id="7a/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#7a/b)

One possible open contour begins at $t=0$ along a ray with $\operatorname{Re}(1/t)>0$, avoids the [essential singularity](../../../isolated-singularity.md#essential-singularity) at the origin, and ends at infinity along a ray with $\operatorname{Re}(zt)<0$. For example, when $z>0$ is real, it may leave the origin along the positive real axis, bend through the upper half-plane, and approach the negative real axis as $|t|\to\infty$. At its initial end, $e^{-1/t}\to0$; at its final end, $e^{zt}\to0$. More generally, the terminal ray may be chosen with

$$
\arg t=\pi-\arg z,
$$

so that $zt$ is negative real. This contour satisfies the endpoint condition from part (a) and therefore produces a solution of the differential equation.

Let

\qquad
H(x,p)=\frac{p^2}{2m}+\frac12m\omega^2x^2.

An orbit of energy $E>0$ is an ellipse in [phase space](../../../classical-mechanics.md#phase-space), with intercepts $x=\pm\sqrt{2E/(m\omega^2)}$ and $p=\pm\sqrt{2mE}$. Its enclosed area is $2\pi E/\omega$, so the [action variable](../../../classical-mechanics.md#action-variable) is

$$
I=\frac1{2\pi}\oint p\,dx=\frac E\omega.
$$

The transformation

$$
\boxed{x=\sqrt{\frac{2I}{m\omega}}\sin\theta,
\qquad
p=\sqrt{2m\omega I}\cos\theta}
$$

is canonical and gives the [action-angle variables](../../../classical-mechanics.md#action-angle-variables). Substitution yields

$$
\boxed{H(I)=\omega I,\qquad \dot I=0,\qquad\dot\theta=\omega.}

<h3 id="8e/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [8E](#8e)

<h4 id="8e/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#8e/b)

The [adiabatic invariance of the action](../../../classical-mechanics.md#adiabatic-invariance-of-the-action) applies when the frequency changes by only a small fraction during one oscillation and no [separatrix](../../../dynamical-systems.md#separatrix) is encountered. A convenient condition is

$$
\left|\frac{\dot\omega}{\omega^2}\right|\ll1,
\qquad \omega(t)=\sqrt{\frac{k(t)}m}.
$$

Then

$$
\boxed{I=\frac E\omega\simeq\text{constant}.}
$$

Consequently

$$
E=I\omega\propto\sqrt{k}.
$$

If $A$ is the displacement amplitude, $E=\tfrac12kA^2$, and hence

$$
A=\sqrt{\frac{2I}{m\omega}}
\propto\omega^{-1/2}
\propto k^{-1/4}.
$$

**Thus a slowly stiffened spring raises the energy as $k^{1/2}$ while reducing the amplitude as $k^{-1/4}$.**

## 9B

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="9b/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [9B](#9b)

Let $M(r)$ be the [enclosed mass](../../../stellar-structure.md#enclosed-mass). A spherical shell of thickness $dr$ has inward gravitational acceleration $GM(r)/r^2$, so [hydrostatic equilibrium](../../../stellar-structure.md#hydrostatic-pressure-support-equation) requires

$$
\boxed{\frac{dP}{dr}=-\frac{GM(r)\rho(r)}{r^2},
\qquad
\frac{dM}{dr}=4\pi r^2\rho(r).}
$$

With $\rho=Ar^2P$, these equations reduce to

$$
P'=-GAMP,
\qquad
M'=4\pi Ar^4P.
$$

For a regular central value $P(0)=P_c>0$, the first equation integrates to

$$
P(r)=P_c\exp\left(-GA\int_0^rM(s)\,ds\right).
$$

The [exponential function](../../../calculus.md#exponential-function) is strictly positive at every finite $r$, so the pressure and density cannot reach zero at a finite stellar surface. The equations may describe an atmosphere whose density decreases at large radius, but they admit no nontrivial hydrostatic star of finite radius. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\text{there is no stable finite-radius stellar solution with this relation.}}
$$

Because $H_n|0^n\rangle=|\psi_0\rangle$,

$$
-H_nI_0H_n=2|\psi_0\rangle\langle\psi_0|-I,
$$

which is the [reflection operator](../../../quantum-theory.md#reflection-operator) in the line spanned by $|\psi_0\rangle$. Meanwhile $I_G$ changes the sign of every good basis state and fixes every bad basis state; on

$$
\mathcal P=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb R}\{|\psi_G\rangle,|\psi_B\rangle\}
$$

it is the reflection in the $|\psi_B\rangle$ axis. Both reflections preserve $\mathcal P$, so their product $Q$ does too.

Write

$$
|\psi_0\rangle
=\sqrt{\frac{k}{N}}|\psi_G\rangle
+\sqrt{\frac{N-k}{N}}|\psi_B\rangle
=\sin\theta|\psi_G\rangle+\cos\theta|\psi_B\rangle,
\qquad
\sin\theta=\sqrt{\frac{k}{N}}.
$$

In [Euclidean geometry](../../../geometry-and-topology.md#euclidean-geometry), the composition of reflections in two lines through the origin has a [rotation matrix](../../../linear-algebra.md#rotation-matrix) through twice the oriented angle between the lines. Hence $Q$ rotates the good-bad plane by $2\theta$ from $|\psi_B\rangle$ toward $|\psi_G\rangle$. In particular, the [Grover rotation angle](../../../quantum-theory.md#grover-rotation-angle) formula is

$$
\boxed{Q^r|\psi_0\rangle
=\sin((2r+1)\theta)|\psi_G\rangle
+\cos((2r+1)\theta)|\psi_B\rangle.}
$$

<h3 id="10d/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [10D](#10d)

<h4 id="10d/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#10d/b)

When $k=N/4$,

$$
\sin\theta=\frac12,
\qquad \theta=\frac\pi6.
$$

One [Grover search algorithm](../../../quantum-theory.md#grover-s-algorithm) iteration therefore gives

$$
Q|\psi_0\rangle
=\sin(3\theta)|\psi_G\rangle+\cos(3\theta)|\psi_B\rangle
=|\psi_G\rangle.
$$

The selective sign change $I_G$ is implemented with one application of the [Boolean quantum oracle](../../../quantum-theory.md#boolean-quantum-oracle): prepare its answer qubit in $|-\rangle=(|0\rangle-|1\rangle)/\sqrt2$ and use [quantum phase kickback](../../../quantum-theory.md#phase-kickback), obtaining $(-1)^{f(x)}$ on each $|x\rangle$. The remaining operations $H_n$, $I_0$, and the final computational-basis measurement are independent of $f$. Measurement of $|\psi_G\rangle$ returns some $x\in G$ with probability one, so exactly one query to $U_f$ suffices.

## 11I

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="11i/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [11I](#11i)

<h4 id="11i/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#11i/a)

A [primitive root](../../../number-theory.md#primitive-root-modulo-n) modulo $p$ is an integer $g$ whose residue class generates the [multiplicative group of a finite field](../../../algebra.md#multiplicative-group-of-a-finite-field) $\mathbb F_p^\times$. Equivalently, its [multiplicative order](../../../number-theory.md#multiplicative-order) modulo $p$ is $p-1$, so

$$
\boxed{\{1,g,g^2,\ldots,g^{p-2}\}=\mathbb F_p^\times.}
$$

<h3 id="11i/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [11I](#11i)

<h4 id="11i/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#11i/b)

For $k=0$, the convention $0^0=1$ gives $S_0=p\equiv0\pmod p$. Now suppose $1\leq k<p-1$ and choose a [primitive root](../../../number-theory.md#primitive-root-modulo-n) $g$ modulo $p$. The nonzero residues are $g^j$ for $0\leq j\leq p-2$, while the $x=0$ term vanishes. Hence

$$
S_k\equiv\sum_{j=0}^{p-2}g^{jk}\pmod p.
$$

This is a [finite geometric series](../../../real-analysis.md#finite-geometric-series). Since $g^k\not\equiv1\pmod p$ but $g^{k(p-1)}\equiv1\pmod p$ by [Fermat's little theorem](../../../number-theory.md#fermat-little-theorem), multiplication by the nonzero residue $g^k-1$ gives

$$
(g^k-1)S_k\equiv g^{k(p-1)}-1\equiv0\pmod p.
$$

The factor $g^k-1$ is invertible modulo the [prime number](../../../number-theory.md#prime-number) $p$, so

$$
\boxed{S_k\equiv0\pmod p.}
$$

<h3 id="11i/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [11I](#11i)

<h4 id="11i/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#11i/c)

By [Fermat's little theorem](../../../number-theory.md#fermat-little-theorem),

$$
g(x,y,z)=1-f(x,y,z)^{p-1}\equiv
\begin{cases}
1&\text{if }f(x,y,z)\equiv0\pmod p,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
$$

Thus the sum of $g$ over $\mathbb F_p^3$ counts, modulo $p$, the triples on which the [ternary quadratic form](../../../linear-algebra.md#ternary-quadratic-form) $f$ vanishes.

Expand $f^{p-1}$ with the [multinomial theorem](../../../combinatorics.md#multinomial-theorem). A term indexed by $r+s+t=p-1$ contributes a constant multiple of

$$
S_{2r}S_{2s}S_{2t}.
$$

At least one of $r,s,t$ is strictly less than $(p-1)/2$. Its corresponding exponent is therefore in $\{0,1,\ldots,p-2\}$, so part (b), including its $k=0$ case, makes that power sum zero modulo $p$. Every term in the expansion consequently vanishes after summation. The constant term contributes $p^3\equiv0$, and therefore

$$
\boxed{\sum_{x,y,z\in\mathbb F_p}g(x,y,z)\equiv0\pmod p.}
$$

The zero triple is one solution of $f=0$. If it were the only solution, the displayed sum would be congruent to $1$ rather than $0$. Hence there is another triple $(x,y,z)$, not all divisible by $p$, satisfying

$$
\boxed{a x^2+b y^2+c z^2\equiv0\pmod p.}
$$

## 12H

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="12h/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [12H](#12h)

<h4 id="12h/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#12h/a)

Fix an acceptable effective enumeration $f_{e,n}$ of the $n$-ary [partial computable functions](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#computable-function). The [S-m-n theorem](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#smn-theorem) states that for every $m,n\geq1$ there is a [total computable function](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#total-computable-function) $s_n^m$ such that

$$
f_{s_n^m(e,x_1,\ldots,x_m),n}(y_1,\ldots,y_n)
\simeq f_{e,m+n}(x_1,\ldots,x_m,y_1,\ldots,y_n),
$$

where $\simeq$ means that both sides have the same value when defined and are otherwise both undefined.

The [recursion theorem](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#kleene-s-recursion-theorem) states that every total computable transformation $h$ of program indices has a semantic fixed point: there is an $e$ such that

$$
\boxed{f_{e,1}\simeq f_{h(e),1}.}
$$

<h3 id="12h/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [12H](#12h)

<h4 id="12h/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#12h/b)

[Rice theorem](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#rice-s-theorem) says that every nontrivial [extensional property of programs](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#extensional-property-of-programs) computing partial functions is undecidable. Equivalently, a nontrivial [index set](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#index-set) cannot be a [computable set](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#computable-set).

Let $A$ be such an index set. Replacing $A$ by its complement if necessary, assume that the nowhere-defined function has no index in $A$. Since the property is nontrivial, choose an index $a\in A$. If membership in $A$ were decidable, we could decide the diagonal [halting problem](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#halting-problem) as follows. For each $x$, define

$$
\psi_x(y)\simeq
\begin{cases}
f_{a,1}(y),&\text{after the computation }f_{x,1}(x)\text{ halts},\\
\text{undefined},&\text{if that computation never halts}.
\end{cases}
$$

By the [S-m-n theorem](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#smn-theorem), a total computable function $q$ produces an index $q(x)$ for $\psi_x$. If $f_{x,1}(x)$ diverges, then $\psi_x$ is nowhere defined and $q(x)\notin A$. If it halts, then $\psi_x\simeq f_{a,1}$ and extensionality gives $q(x)\in A$. A decider for $A$ would therefore decide whether $f_{x,1}(x)$ halts, a contradiction. Hence $A$ is undecidable.

↑ **Parent:** [12H](#12h)

<h4 id="12h/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#12h/c)

Apply the [S-m-n theorem](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#smn-theorem) to $g$. It gives a total computable function $h$ such that

f_{h(x),1}(y)\simeq g(x,y).
$$

The [recursion theorem](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#kleene-s-recursion-theorem) supplies an index $e$ satisfying $f_{e,1}\simeq f_{h(e),1}$. Consequently

$$
\boxed{f_{e,1}(y)\simeq g(e,y)\quad\text{for every }y\in\mathbb N_0.}
$$

**No.** The property

## 13C

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="13c/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [13C](#13c)

<h4 id="13c/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#13c/a)

<h5 id="13c/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#13c/a/i)

This is an [immigration--death process](../../../mathematical-biology.md#immigration-death-process). New individuals enter independently of the current population at the constant rate $\lambda$, producing transitions $n\to n+1$. Each existing individual dies at rate $\beta$, so the total transition rate $n\to n-1$ is $\beta n$.

<h4 id="13c/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#13c/a)

<h5 id="13c/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#13c/a/ii)

Multiply the [master equation](../../../markov-process.md#master-equation) by $s^n$ and sum over $n\geq0$. For the immigration terms,

$$
\sum_{n\geq0}s^n(p_{n-1}-p_n)=(s-1)\phi.
$$

For the death terms, shifting the first sum gives

$$
\sum_{n\geq0}s^n\bigl((n+1)p_{n+1}-np_n\bigr)
=(1-s)\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial s}.
$$

Therefore the [probability generating function](../../../probability-theory.md#probability-generating-function) obeys

$$
\boxed{\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}
=\lambda(s-1)\phi+\beta(1-s)\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial s}.}
$$

<h4 id="13c/a/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#13c/a)

<h5 id="13c/a/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#13c/a/iii)

The normalized ansatz intended in the question is

$$
\phi(s,t)=\exp\bigl((s-1)f(t)\bigr),
$$

for which $\phi(1,t)=1$. Substitution into the equation from part (ii) and cancellation of $(s-1)\phi$ gives the [linear ordinary differential equation](../../../differential-equation.md#linear-ordinary-differential-equation)

$$
f'=\lambda-\beta f.
$$

Thus

$$
f(t)=\frac\lambda\beta+\left(f(0)-\frac\lambda\beta\right)e^{-\beta t}.
$$

This generating function is that of a [Poisson distribution](../../../discrete-probability-distribution.md#poisson-distribution) with parameter $f(t)$. Equivalently, differentiating at $s=1$ gives

$$
\langle n\rangle=\partial_s\phi(1,t)=f(t),
\qquad
\sigma^2=\partial_s^2\phi(1,t)+\partial_s\phi(1,t)-\partial_s\phi(1,t)^2=f(t).
$$

Consequently

$$
\boxed{\langle n\rangle\longrightarrow\frac\lambda\beta,
\qquad \sigma^2\longrightarrow\frac\lambda\beta.}
$$

<h3 id="13c/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [13C](#13c)

<h4 id="13c/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#13c/b)

<h5 id="13c/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#13c/b/i)

The additional transition is $n\to n+k$ at rate $\epsilon(n)$. Its gain term at state $n$ comes from state $n-k$, so the new [master equation](../../../markov-process.md#master-equation) is

$$
\boxed{
\frac{dp_n}{dt}
=\lambda(p_{n-1}-p_n)
+\beta\bigl((n+1)p_{n+1}-np_n\bigr)
+\epsilon(n-k)p_{n-k}-\epsilon(n)p_n,}
$$

where the gain term is zero when $n<k$. In the generating-function equation the new contribution is

$$
(s^k-1)\sum_{n\geq0}\epsilon(n)p_n s^n.
$$

For a general state-dependent $\epsilon$, this is not closed in $\phi$. Even when it is closed, $s^k-1$ is not proportional to $s-1$ for $k>1$, so the Poisson ansatz used in part (a) is not preserved.

<h4 id="13c/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#13c/b)

<h5 id="13c/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#13c/b/ii)

Let $m(t)=\langle n\rangle=\sum_{n\geq0}np_n(t)$. Multiplying the new master equation by $n$ and summing, each immigration event changes $n$ by $+1$, each death changes it by $-1$, and each extra event changes it by $+k$. Hence

$$
\boxed{\frac{dm}{dt}=\lambda-\beta m+k\langle\epsilon(n)\rangle.}
$$

<h4 id="13c/b/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#13c/b)

<h5 id="13c/b/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#13c/b/iii)

For $\epsilon(n)=an+b$, [linearity of expectation](../../../probability-theory.md#linearity-of-expectation) gives $\langle\epsilon(n)\rangle=am+b$. The mean therefore satisfies

$$
m'=\lambda+kb-(\beta-ak)m.
$$

If $\beta>ak$, the coefficient of $m$ is negative and the [stable equilibrium](../../../dynamical-systems.md#stable-equilibrium) is

$$
\boxed{m(t)\longrightarrow m_*=\frac{\lambda+kb}{\beta-ak}.}
$$

If $\beta<ak$, the positive feedback from batch addition dominates deaths and $m(t)$ grows exponentially at rate $ak-\beta$. At the threshold $\beta=ak$, it grows linearly as $m(t)=m(0)+(\lambda+kb)t$.

## 14B

In [chemical equilibrium](../../../thermodynamics.md#chemical-equilibrium), the reaction $p+e\leftrightarrow H+\gamma$ obeys [chemical-potential balance for ionization](../../../cosmology.md#chemical-potential-balance-for-ionization)

Applying the given nonrelativistic [Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution](../../../statistical-physics.md#maxwell-boltzmann-distribution) to each massive species gives

$$
\frac{n_en_p}{n_H}
=\frac{g_eg_p}{g_H}
\left(\frac{2\pi k_BT}{h^2}\right)^{3/2}
\left(\frac{m_em_p}{m_H}\right)^{3/2}
\exp\left(-\frac{m_e+m_p-m_H}{k_BT}\right).

The [Hydrogen binding energy](../../../cosmology.md#hydrogen-binding-energy) is $I=m_e+m_p-m_H$ in units with $c=1$. Thus [Saha's equation](../../../cosmology.md#saha-ionization-equation) is

\boxed{

\left(\frac{2\pi k_BT}{h^2}\right)^{3/2}
\left(\frac{m_em_p}{m_H}\right)^{3/2}e^{-I/(k_BT)}.}

For ground-state hydrogen, $g_e=g_p=2$ and $g_H=4$, so the degeneracy factor is one. Since $m_p\simeq m_H$, the translational factor is commonly written $(2\pi m_ek_BT/h^2)^{3/2}$.

and hence

$$

$$

Combining this with the stated photon density produces

$$
\boxed{
\frac{1-X_e}{X_e^2}
=\eta\,\frac{g_H}{g_eg_p}
\frac{16\pi\zeta(3)}{(2\pi)^{3/2}}
(k_BT)^{3/2}
\left(\frac{m_H}{m_em_p}\right)^{3/2}
e^{I/(k_BT)}.}
$$

In our universe the [baryon-to-photon ratio](../../../cosmology.md#baryon-to-photon-ratio) is tiny, so there are roughly $10^9$ photons per baryon. Even when the mean photon energy is far below $I$, the high-energy [blackbody radiation](../../../statistical-physics.md#black-body-radiation) tail contains enough ionizing photons to suppress neutral hydrogen. The [recombination temperature](../../../cosmology.md#recombination-temperature) is reached only after the [Boltzmann factor](../../../statistical-physics.md#boltzmann-factor) overwhelms this large photon-to-baryon ratio, at $k_BT\simeq0.3\,\mathrm{eV}$.

For a universe with $\eta=1$, take the degeneracy factor as one, $m_H\simeq m_p$, and define $x=I/(k_BT)$. Neutral formation occurs when the boxed ratio is of order one, giving approximately

$$
1\simeq\frac{4\sqrt2\,\zeta(3)}{\sqrt\pi}
\left(\frac{I}{m_ex}\right)^{3/2}e^x.
$$

With $I=13.6\,\mathrm{eV}$ and $m_e=5.11\times10^5\,\mathrm{eV}$, this gives $x\simeq19$ and therefore

$$
\boxed{k_BT\simeq0.7\,\mathrm{eV},}
$$

to order unity. This is much hotter than recombination in our universe because there is no enormous excess of photons, but it remains below $I$ because the electron's large translational phase space still favours the ionized state.

## 16I

<h3 id="16i/solution">Solution</h3>

The [Von Neumann hierarchy](../../../set-theory.md#von-neumann-hierarchy) is defined by [transfinite recursion](../../../set-theory.md#transfinite-recursion):

$$
V_0=\varnothing,
\qquad V_{\alpha+1}=\mathcal P(V_\alpha),
\qquad V_\lambda=\bigcup_{\beta<\lambda}V_\beta
$$

for every [limit ordinal](../../../set-theory.md#limit-ordinal) $\lambda$.

We prove simultaneously by [transfinite induction](../../../set-theory.md#transfinite-induction) that every $V_\alpha$ is a [transitive set](../../../set-theory.md#transitive-set) and that $V_\alpha\subseteq V_{\alpha+1}$. The claims are immediate for $V_0$. Suppose first that $V_\alpha$ is transitive. If $x\in y\in V_{\alpha+1}=\mathcal P(V_\alpha)$, then $y\subseteq V_\alpha$, so $x\in V_\alpha$. Moreover, transitivity says that every $x\in V_\alpha$ satisfies $x\subseteq V_\alpha$, and hence

$$
x\in\mathcal P(V_\alpha)=V_{\alpha+1}.
$$

Thus $V_{\alpha+1}$ is transitive and $V_\alpha\subseteq V_{\alpha+1}$. At a limit $\lambda$, if $x\in y\in V_\lambda$, then $y\in V_\beta$ for some $\beta<\lambda$; transitivity of $V_\beta$ gives $x\in V_\beta\subseteq V_\lambda$. The inclusions at successor stages also imply

$$
\boxed{V_\alpha\subseteq V_\beta\quad\text{whenever }\alpha\leq\beta.}
$$

Finally, the [rank of a set](../../../set-theory.md#rank-of-a-set) $x$ is the [ordinal](../../../set-theory.md#ordinal)

$$
\operatorname{rank}(x)=\sup_{y\in x}\bigl(\operatorname{rank}(y)+1\bigr).
$$

Every $y\in x$ belongs to $V_{\operatorname{rank}(y)+1}\subseteq V_{\operatorname{rank}(x)}$, so $x\subseteq V_{\operatorname{rank}(x)}$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{x\in V_{\operatorname{rank}(x)+1},}
$$

which proves that every set occurs in the hierarchy.

A [graph](../../../graph.md) $G=(V,E)$ is [bipartite](../../../graph-theory.md#bipartite-graph) when its set of [vertices](../../../graph.md#vertex-graph-theory) can be partitioned as

$$
V=A\sqcup B
$$

so that every [edge](../../../graph-theory.md#edge-of-a-graph) has one endpoint in $A$ and the other in $B$. Equivalently, $G$ has a proper [graph colouring](../../../graph-theory.md#graph-coloring) with at most two colours.

<h3 id="17g/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [17G](#17g)

<h4 id="17g/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#17g/b)

Suppose first that $G$ is [bipartite](../../../graph-theory.md#bipartite-graph). As one traverses any [cycle in a graph](../../../graph-theory.md#cycle-in-a-graph), its vertices alternate between the two vertex classes. Returning to the initial class therefore requires an [even number](../../../number-theory.md#even-number) of edges, so $G$ contains no [odd cycle](../../../graph-theory.md#odd-cycle).

Conversely, suppose that $G$ has no [odd cycle](../../../graph-theory.md#odd-cycle). Work separately in each [connected component of a graph](../../../graph.md#component-graph-theory), choose a root $r$, and put

$$
A=\{v:d(r,v)\text{ is even}\},
\qquad
B=\{v:d(r,v)\text{ is odd}\},
$$

where $d$ is the [graph distance](../../../graph-theory.md#distance-graph-theory). If adjacent vertices $u,v$ were in the same class, then $|d(r,u)-d(r,v)|\leq1$ and parity would force $d(r,u)=d(r,v)$. Choose a breadth-first [spanning tree](../../../combinatorics.md#spanning-tree). The two tree paths from $r$ to $u$ and $v$ diverge at some last common vertex; their remaining segments have equal length, and adjoining the edge $uv$ gives an odd cycle. This contradiction shows that every edge joins $A$ to $B$, proving the [odd-cycle characterization of bipartite graphs](../../../graph-theory.md#odd-cycle-characterization-of-bipartite-graphs).

<h3 id="17g/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [17G](#17g)

<h4 id="17g/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#17g/c)

The [Erdős-Stone theorem](../../../graph-theory.md#erdos-stone-theorem) states precisely that, for every fixed graph $H$ with [chromatic number](../../../graph-theory.md#chromatic-number) $\chi(H)\geq2$,

$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}
\frac{\operatorname{ex}(n,H)}{\binom n2}
=1-\frac1{\chi(H)-1}.
$$

If the bipartite graph $G$ has an edge, then $\chi(G)=2$. Substitution into the theorem gives

$$
\boxed{
\frac{\operatorname{ex}(n,G)}{\binom n2}\longrightarrow
1-\frac1{2-1}=0.}
$$

If $G$ has no edges, then every graph of sufficiently large order contains $G$ as a [subgraph](../../../graph-theory.md#subgraph), so the conclusion is immediate.

<h3 id="17g/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [17G](#17g)

<h4 id="17g/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#17g/d)

For each edge $e$, let $I_e$ be the [indicator random variable](../../../probability-theory.md#indicator-random-variable) of the event that precisely one endpoint lies in $U$. Independent inclusion of the two endpoints with probability $1/2$ gives

$$
\mathbb P(I_e=1)=2\left(\frac12\right)\left(\frac12\right)=\frac12.
$$

Because $X=\sum_{e\in E(G)}I_e$, [linearity of expectation](../../../probability-theory.md#linearity-of-expectation) gives

$$
\boxed{\mathbb E X=\sum_{e\in E(G)}\mathbb E I_e=\frac m2.}
$$

Some realization therefore has $X\geq m/2$. Retaining its crossing edges gives a [subgraph](../../../graph-theory.md#subgraph) that is [bipartite](../../../graph-theory.md#bipartite-graph), with vertex classes $U$ and $V(G)\setminus U$, proving the [random cut lower bound](../../../graph-theory.md#random-cut-lower-bound).

Now suppose $n$ is an [even number](../../../number-theory.md#even-number) and instead choose $U$ uniformly among all subsets of size $n/2$. For a fixed edge $uv$,

$$
\mathbb P(u\in U,v\notin U)
=\frac{n/2}{n}\frac{n/2}{n-1},
$$

and the reverse orientation has the same probability. Thus

$$
\mathbb P(I_{uv}=1)=\frac{n}{2(n-1)}.
$$

Another application of [linearity of expectation](../../../probability-theory.md#linearity-of-expectation) gives

$$
\mathbb E X=\frac{mn}{2(n-1)}.
$$

At least one such balanced choice attains this expectation, and its crossing edges give the required [subgraph](../../../graph-theory.md#subgraph) that is [bipartite](../../../graph-theory.md#bipartite-graph). This is the [balanced random cut lower bound](../../../graph-theory.md#balanced-random-cut-lower-bound).

## 18F

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="18f/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [18F](#18f)

Let $L$ be a [splitting field](../../../galois-theory.md#splitting-field) of $f(X)=X^m-1$ over $k$. Its [formal derivative](../../../galois-theory.md#formal-derivative) is

$$
f'(X)=mX^{m-1}.
$$

The assumption on the [characteristic](../../../algebra.md#characteristic-of-a-field) of $k$ says that $m\ne0$ in $k$. Any root $\alpha$ of $f$ is nonzero, and hence $f'(\alpha)=m\alpha^{m-1}\ne0$. Thus $f$ and $f'$ have no common root, so $f$ is a [separable polynomial](../../../galois-theory.md#separable-polynomial). Since it has degree $m$ and splits over $L$, it has exactly $m$ distinct roots. This is the [separability of roots of unity](../../../galois-theory.md#separability-of-roots-of-unity).

Fix a [primitive root of unity](../../../algebra.md#primitive-root-of-unity) $\zeta_m=e^{2\pi i/m}$. Define the $m$th [cyclotomic polynomial](../../../galois-theory.md#cyclotomic-polynomial) by

$$
\boxed{\Phi_m(X)=
\prod_{\substack{1\leq a\leq m\\(a,m)=1}}
(X-\zeta_m^a).}
$$

It is [monic](../../../polynomial.md#monic-polynomial) and has degree given by the [Euler totient function](../../../number-theory.md#euler-totient-function) $\varphi(m)$. Partitioning all $m$th [roots of unity](../../../algebra.md#root-of-unity) according to their exact [multiplicative order](../../../number-theory.md#multiplicative-order) gives the [cyclotomic factorization](../../../galois-theory.md#cyclotomic-factorization)

$$
X^m-1=\prod_{d\mid m}\Phi_d(X).
$$

We now prove by [mathematical induction](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#mathematical-induction) that $\Phi_m\in\mathbb Z[X]$. The base case is $\Phi_1=X-1$. If the result holds for every proper divisor $d$ of $m$, then

$$
G(X)=\prod_{\substack{d\mid m\\d<m}}\Phi_d(X)
$$

is monic and belongs to $\mathbb Z[X]$. Polynomial division of $X^m-1$ by the monic integer polynomial $G$ produces a quotient and remainder in $\mathbb Z[X]$. The factorization over $\mathbb C$ says that the remainder is zero and the quotient is $\Phi_m$, proving the claim.

It remains to prove [irreducibility of cyclotomic polynomials](../../../galois-theory.md#irreducibility-of-cyclotomic-polynomials). Let $f\in\mathbb Z[X]$ be a monic [irreducible polynomial](../../../polynomial.md#irreducible-polynomial) dividing $\Phi_m$, let $\varepsilon$ be a root of $f$, and write $\Phi_m=fg$. We claim that $\varepsilon^p$ is a root of $f$ for every [prime number](../../../number-theory.md#prime-number) $p\nmid m$. Otherwise $\varepsilon^p$ is a root of $g$, so $\varepsilon$ is a root of $g(X^p)$. Since $f$ is the [minimal polynomial](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#minimal-polynomial) of $\varepsilon$ over $\mathbb Q$, [Gauss lemma for polynomials](../../../commutative-algebra.md#gauss-lemma-for-polynomials) gives

$$
f(X)\mid g(X^p)\qquad\text{in }\mathbb Z[X].
$$

After [reduction of an integer polynomial modulo a prime](../../../commutative-algebra.md#reduction-of-an-integer-polynomial-modulo-a-prime), the [Frobenius endomorphism](../../../galois-theory.md#frobenius-endomorphism) gives

$$
\bar f(X)\mid \bar g(X^p)=\bar g(X)^p.
$$

Choose an irreducible factor $h$ of the nonconstant monic polynomial $\bar f$. Then $h\mid\bar g$, so $h^2\mid\bar f\bar g=\bar\Phi_m$. The [cyclotomic factorization](../../../galois-theory.md#cyclotomic-factorization) would then make $h^2$ divide $X^m-1$ over $\mathbb F_p$. This is impossible because its derivative $mX^{m-1}$ is coprime to it when $p\nmid m$.

Thus $f(\varepsilon^p)=0$ for every prime $p\nmid m$. Iterating this fact over a prime factorization shows that $f(\varepsilon^a)=0$ whenever $(a,m)=1$. These are all $\varphi(m)$ primitive $m$th roots, so

$$
\deg f\geq\varphi(m)=\deg\Phi_m.
$$

Because $f$ divides $\Phi_m$, equality holds and $f=\Phi_m$. Hence every cyclotomic polynomial is irreducible over $\mathbb Q$.

Finally, direct multiplication gives

$$
(X^2-X+1)F=X^{10}-X^5+1.
$$

Also

$$
X^{15}+1=(X^5+1)(X^{10}-X^5+1).
$$

Applying the [cyclotomic factorization](../../../galois-theory.md#cyclotomic-factorization) to the two factors on the right gives

$$
X^{15}+1=\Phi_2\Phi_6\Phi_{10}\Phi_{30},
\qquad
X^5+1=\Phi_2\Phi_{10},
$$

and therefore

$$
X^{10}-X^5+1=\Phi_6(X)\Phi_{30}(X).
$$

Since $X^2-X+1=\Phi_6(X)$, cancellation yields

$$
\boxed{F(X)=\Phi_{30}(X).}
$$

The [thirtieth cyclotomic polynomial](../../../galois-theory.md#thirtieth-cyclotomic-polynomial) is irreducible over $\mathbb Q$ by the theorem just proved, so $F$ is irreducible.

<h3 id="19i/a">a</h3>

<h4 id="19i/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#19i/a)

The [Mackey's theorem](../../../representation-theory.md#mackey-restriction-formula), or Mackey restriction formula, says that if $H,K\leq G$, $W$ is a complex $K$-representation, and $D$ is a set of representatives for the [double cosets](../../../group-theory.md#double-coset) $H\backslash G/K$, then

$$
\boxed{
\operatorname{Res}_H^G\operatorname{Ind}_K^G W
\cong
\bigoplus_{x\in D}
\operatorname{Ind}_{H\cap xKx^{-1}}^H
\operatorname{Res}_{H\cap xKx^{-1}}^{xKx^{-1}}({}^xW).}
$$

Here ${}^xW$ is the representation of the [conjugate subgroup](../../../group-theory.md#conjugate-subgroup) $xKx^{-1}$ defined by

$$
{}^xW(xkx^{-1})=W(k).
$$

For the proof, use the group-algebra model

$$
\operatorname{Ind}_K^G W
=\mathbb C[G]\otimes_{\mathbb C[K]}W.
$$

The partition of $G$ into double cosets gives the direct-sum decomposition of $(H,K)$-bimodules

$$
\mathbb C[G]
=\bigoplus_{x\in D}\mathbb C[HxK].
$$

Put $L_x=H\cap xKx^{-1}$. For each $x$, define

$$
T_x:
\mathbb C[H]\otimes_{\mathbb C[L_x]}{}^xW
\longrightarrow
\mathbb C[HxK]\otimes_{\mathbb C[K]}W,
\qquad
h\otimes w\longmapsto hx\otimes w.
$$

If $\ell=xkx^{-1}\in L_x$, then

$$
T_x(h\ell\otimes w)
=h\ell x\otimes w
=hxk\otimes w
=hx\otimes kw
=T_x(h\otimes\ell w),
$$

so $T_x$ is well defined. It is visibly $H$-equivariant and surjective, and choosing representatives for $H/L_x$ shows that both sides have dimension $[H:L_x]\dim W$; hence it is an isomorphism. Summing the $T_x$ proves the formula.

The [Frobenius reciprocity](../../../representation-theory.md#frobenius-reciprocity) theorem states that for an $H$-representation $U$ and a $G$-representation $V$,

$$
\boxed{
\operatorname{Hom}_G(\operatorname{Ind}_H^G U,V)
\cong
\operatorname{Hom}_H(U,\operatorname{Res}_H^G V).}
$$

For the corresponding [character of a representation](../../../representation-theory.md#character-of-a-representation), this is

$$
\boxed{
\langle\operatorname{Ind}_H^G\chi,\psi\rangle_G
=\langle\chi,\operatorname{Res}_H^G\psi\rangle_H.}
$$

<h3 id="19i/b">b</h3>

On the orbit $G/H$, an element $g\in G$ fixes the coset $xH$ exactly when

$$

\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad

The [character of a representation](../../../representation-theory.md#character-of-a-representation) of the corresponding [permutation representation](../../../representation-theory.md#permutation-representation) therefore is

=|(G/H)^g|
=\frac1{|H|}
|\{x\in G:x^{-1}gx\in H\}|
=(1_H\mathbin{\uparrow}^G)(g).}
$$

This is the [permutation character of a coset action](../../../representation-theory.md#permutation-character-of-a-coset-action).

V=\left\{\sum_{x\in X}a_xx:\sum_{x\in X}a_x=0\right\}
$$

is also $G$-invariant, and every vector has a unique decomposition into a constant vector and an element of $V$. Hence

$$
\boxed{\mathbb C X=\mathbb C_G\oplus V.}
$$

Here $V$ is the [augmentation subrepresentation of a permutation representation](../../../representation-theory.md#augmentation-subrepresentation-of-a-permutation-representation).

<h4 id="19i/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#19i/b)

<h5 id="19i/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#19i/b/ii)

Let $D$ represent $H\backslash G/H$. Combining [Frobenius reciprocity](../../../representation-theory.md#frobenius-reciprocity) with the [Mackey's theorem](../../../representation-theory.md#mackey-restriction-formula) gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\left\langle1_H\mathbin{\uparrow}^G,
1_H\mathbin{\uparrow}^G\right\rangle_G
&=\left\langle1_H,
\operatorname{Res}_H^G(1_H\mathbin{\uparrow}^G)\right\rangle_H\\
&=\sum_{x\in D}
\left\langle1_H,
1_{H\cap xHx^{-1}}\mathbin{\uparrow}^H\right\rangle_H\\
&=\sum_{x\in D}
\left\langle1_{H\cap xHx^{-1}},
1_{H\cap xHx^{-1}}\right\rangle_{H\cap xHx^{-1}}\\
&=|D|.
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus

$$
\boxed{
\left\langle1_H\mathbin{\uparrow}^G,
1_H\mathbin{\uparrow}^G\right\rangle_G
=|H\backslash G/H|.}
$$

<h4 id="19i/b/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#19i/b)

<h5 id="19i/b/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#19i/b/iii)

Work with the selected transitive orbit $X\cong G/H$. By [Maschke's theorem](../../../representation-theory.md#maschke-s-theorem), decompose its permutation character as

$$
1_H\mathbin{\uparrow}^G
=1_G+\sum_jm_j\chi_j,
$$

where the $\chi_j$ are distinct nontrivial [irreducible characters](../../../representation-theory.md#irreducible-character). [Character orthogonality](../../../representation-theory.md#character-orthogonality) gives

$$
\left\langle1_H\mathbin{\uparrow}^G,
1_H\mathbin{\uparrow}^G\right\rangle_G
=1+\sum_jm_j^2.
$$

Consequently the augmentation summand $V$ is [irreducible](../../../representation-theory.md#irreducible-representation) exactly when this inner product equals $2$.

The [double cosets](../../../group-theory.md#double-coset) $H\backslash G/H$ are the $H$-orbits on $G/H$. Because $G$ acts transitively on $X$, these in turn correspond to the $G$-orbits on $X\times X$: move the first coordinate to the base point $H$, after which its stabilizer is $H$. One orbit is the diagonal. There are exactly two orbits precisely when $H$ is transitive on $X\setminus\{H\}$, equivalently when the action is [two-transitive](../../../group-theory.md#two-transitive-group-action). Part (ii) therefore proves

$$
\boxed{V\text{ is irreducible}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
G\text{ acts two-transitively on }X.}
$$

This is the [irreducible augmentation criterion for a transitive group action](../../../representation-theory.md#irreducible-augmentation-criterion-for-a-transitive-group-action).

Finally, transitivity implies that the fixed-vector space $\mathbb C[X]^G$ consists only of constant vectors and has dimension one. If the nonzero representation $V$ were a [trivial representation](../../../representation-theory.md#trivial-representation), it would contribute another fixed vector, a contradiction. Thus $V$ is not the trivial representation.

Because $L$ is a [simplicial subcomplex](../../../algebraic-topology.md#simplicial-subcomplex) of $K$, every face of a simplex of $L$ also belongs to $L$. The simplicial [boundary operator](../../../homology.md#boundary-operator) therefore satisfies

$$
\partial C_k(L)\subseteq C_{k-1}(L),
$$

so $C_\bullet(L)$ is a [chain subcomplex](../../../homology.md#chain-subcomplex) of the [simplicial chain complex](../../../homology.md#simplicial-chain-complex) $C_\bullet(K)$.

Define a map on the [quotient groups](../../../group-theory.md#quotient-group) by

$$
\bar\partial_k:C_k(K,L)\longrightarrow C_{k-1}(K,L),
\qquad
\bar\partial_k(c+C_k(L))
=\partial c+C_{k-1}(L).
$$

If $c'=c+\ell$ with $\ell\in C_k(L)$, then $\partial c'-\partial c=\partial\ell\in C_{k-1}(L)$, so this definition is independent of the representative. Moreover,

$$
\bar\partial_{k-1}\bar\partial_k[c]
=[\partial^2c]=0.
$$

**Thus $C_\bullet(K,L)$ is the [relative simplicial chain complex](../../../homology.md#relative-simplicial-chain-complex).**

<h3 id="20f/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [20F](#20f)

<h4 id="20f/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#20f/b)

Inclusion and passage to the quotient give a [short exact sequence of chain complexes](../../../homology.md#short-exact-sequence-of-chain-complexes)

$$
0\longrightarrow C_\bullet(L)
\xrightarrow{i}C_\bullet(K)
\xrightarrow{q}C_\bullet(K,L)
\longrightarrow0.
$$

Define the [connecting homomorphism](../../../homology.md#connecting-homomorphism) as follows. If $[c]\in H_k(K,L)$, then $\bar\partial[c]=0$, so $\partial c\in C_{k-1}(L)$. Since $\partial^2c=0$, it is a [chain cycle](../../../homology.md#chain-cycle) in $L$, and we set

$$
\delta[c]=[\partial c]\in H_{k-1}(L).
$$

Changing $c$ by a relative boundary or a chain in $L$ changes $\partial c$ by a boundary in $L$, so $\delta$ is well defined.

The resulting sequence is

$$
\boxed{
\cdots\longrightarrow H_k(L)
\xrightarrow{i_*}H_k(K)
\xrightarrow{q_*}H_k(K,L)
\xrightarrow{\delta}H_{k-1}(L)
\xrightarrow{i_*}H_{k-1}(K)
\longrightarrow\cdots.}
$$

We verify exactness directly. If a cycle of $K$ maps to zero in the relative homology, it differs from a boundary by a cycle in $L$, so it lies in $\operatorname{im}i_*$. If a relative cycle $[c]$ satisfies $\delta[c]=0$, then $\partial c=\partial\ell$ for some $\ell\in C_k(L)$; hence $c-\ell$ is a cycle in $K$ mapping to $[c]$. Finally, if a cycle $z\in C_{k-1}(L)$ becomes a boundary in $K$, say $z=\partial c$, then $[c]$ is a relative cycle with $\delta[c]=[z]$. The reverse containments follow immediately from consecutive composites being zero. This proves the [long exact sequence in relative homology](../../../homology.md#long-exact-sequence-in-relative-homology).

$$

$$

This writes the supposed complete metric space $P$ as a countable union of nowhere-dense closed subsets, contradicting the [Baire category theorem](../../../topological-analysis.md#baire-category-theorem). Thus no norm can make $P$ complete. Equivalently, this is the countable-basis obstruction that a [Banach space has uncountable Hamel dimension](../../../topological-analysis.md#banach-space-has-uncountable-hamel-dimension).

Let $X$ have basis $e_1,\ldots,e_d$, and write $x=\sum_ja_je_j$. On the compact coordinate sphere $\sum_j|a_j|=1$, the continuous positive function

$$
(a_1,\ldots,a_d)\longmapsto
\left\lVert\sum_ja_je_j\right\rVert
$$

has a positive minimum $m$. Homogeneity and the [triangle inequality](../../../topological-analysis.md#triangle-inequality) therefore give constants $m,M>0$ such that

$$
m\sum_j|a_j|
\leq\left\lVert\sum_ja_je_j\right\rVert
\leq M\sum_j|a_j|.
$$

In particular, each coordinate functional $\phi_j(x)=a_j$ belongs to the [continuous dual space](../../../continuous-dual-space.md) $X^*$.

Every member of $X^*$ is norm-continuous, so the [weak topology](../../../weak-topology.md) is coarser than the [norm topology](../../../functional-analysis.md#norm-topology). Conversely, given $x_0\in X$ and $\varepsilon>0$, the basic weak neighbourhood

$$
U=\left\{x:|\phi_j(x-x_0)|<\frac{\varepsilon}{Md}
\text{ for }1\leq j\leq d\right\}
$$

satisfies $U\subseteq\{x:\lVert x-x_0\rVert<\varepsilon\}$. Thus every norm-open set is weakly open, proving that the two topologies coincide. This is the [finite-dimensional weak and norm topologies coincide](../../../weak-topology.md#finite-dimensional-weak-and-norm-topologies-coincide) theorem.

The [Uniform boundedness principle](../../../banach-space.md#uniform-boundedness-principle) states that if $E$ is a [Banach space](../../../banach-space.md) and a family of [bounded linear operators](../../../topological-vector-space.md#continuous-linear-operator) $S_\alpha:E\to Y$ is pointwise bounded, then $\sup_\alpha\lVert S_\alpha\rVert<\infty$.

Suppose $x_n\rightharpoonup x$ in the [normed vector space](../../../functional-analysis.md#normed-vector-space) $X$. For each $n$, define the evaluation functional

$$
J_n:X^*\longrightarrow\mathbb K,
\qquad J_n(f)=f(x_n).
$$

The [completeness of the dual space](../../../continuous-dual-space.md#completeness-of-the-dual-space) says that $X^*$ is Banach even when $X$ is not. For each $f\in X^*$, the scalar sequence $J_n(f)=f(x_n)$ converges to $f(x)$ and is therefore bounded. Uniform boundedness gives

$$
\sup_n\lVert J_n\rVert<\infty.
$$

By the [canonical embedding into the bidual](../../../functional-analysis.md#canonical-embedding-into-the-bidual) and the [Hahn-Banach theorem](../../../functional-analysis.md#hahn-banach-theorem), $\lVert J_n\rVert=\lVert x_n\rVert$. Hence

$$
\boxed{\sup_n\lVert x_n\rVert<\infty,}
$$

which proves that a [weakly convergent sequence is bounded](../../../weak-topology.md#weakly-convergent-sequence-is-bounded).

Suppose $a^k\rightharpoonup a$ in $\ell^1$ but $a^k$ does not converge to $a$ in the [norm topology](../../../functional-analysis.md#norm-topology). There are $\varepsilon>0$ and a [subsequence](../../../real-analysis.md#subsequence) $(a^{m_k})$ such that

$$
\lVert a^{m_k}-a\rVert_1\geq\varepsilon
\qquad\text{for every }k.
$$

Set $x^k=a^{m_k}-a$. Then

$$
x^k\rightharpoonup0,
\qquad \lVert x^k\rVert_1\geq\varepsilon,
$$

which proves the first assertion.

We now use a [gliding hump argument](../../../continuous-dual-space.md#gliding-hump-argument). Put $b_0=0$. Having chosen $b_{n-1}$ and $k_{n-1}$, coordinatewise convergence $x_i^k\to0$ lets us choose $k_n>k_{n-1}$ so that

$$
A_n:=\sum_{i\leq b_{n-1}}|x_i^{k_n}|<\frac\varepsilon6.
$$

For this fixed element of $\ell^1$, choose $b_n>b_{n-1}$ so far out that

$$
C_n:=\sum_{i>b_n}|x_i^{k_n}|<\frac\varepsilon6.
$$

Define one sequence $y$ by

$$
y_i=\operatorname{sgn}(x_i^{k_n})
\qquad\text{when }b_{n-1}<i\leq b_n.
$$

The blocks partition the positive integers and $|y_i|\leq1$, so $y\in\ell^\infty$. On the $n$th assigned block, the signs agree; outside it, use $|y_i|\leq1$. The [duality of l1 and l infinity](../../../continuous-dual-space.md#duality-of-l1-and-l-infinity) gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle x^{k_n},y\rangle
&\geq
\sum_{b_{n-1}<i\leq b_n}|x_i^{k_n}|-A_n-C_n\\
&=\lVert x^{k_n}\rVert_1-2A_n-2C_n\\
&>\varepsilon-\frac{2\varepsilon}{6}-\frac{2\varepsilon}{6}
=\frac\varepsilon3.
\end{aligned}
$$

But $x^{k_n}\rightharpoonup0$ requires $\langle x^{k_n},y\rangle\to0$ for this fixed $y\in(\ell^1)^*=\ell^\infty$, a contradiction. Therefore every weakly convergent sequence in $\ell^1$ converges in norm. This is the [Schur property of l1](../../../continuous-dual-space.md#schur-property-of-l1).

## 23F

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="23f/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [23F](#23f)

Let $\pi:\mathbb C\to\mathbb C/\Lambda$ be the [universal covering map](../../../algebraic-topology.md#universal-cover). Since the [complex plane](../../../complex-analysis.md#complex-plane) is a [simply connected domain](../../../complex-analysis.md#simply-connected-domain), the [lifting criterion for a covering space](../../../algebraic-topology.md#lifting-criterion-for-a-covering-space) gives a [holomorphic lift between one-dimensional complex tori](../../../complex-geometry.md#affine-lift-of-a-holomorphic-map-between-one-dimensional-complex-tori) $F:\mathbb C\to\mathbb C$ such that

$$
\pi\circ F=f\circ\pi.
$$

For every $\lambda\in\Lambda$, the difference $F(z+\lambda)-F(z)$ belongs to the discrete [period lattice](../../../complex-analysis.md#period-lattice) $\Lambda$. It depends continuously on $z$, so it is constant. Differentiating shows that the [derivative](../../../calculus.md#derivative) $F'$ is $\Lambda$-periodic. It is bounded on the compact closure of a [fundamental parallelogram](../../../complex-analysis.md#fundamental-parallelogram-of-a-period-lattice), and periodicity makes it bounded on all of $\mathbb C$. The [Liouville theorem](../../../complex-analysis.md#liouville-theorem) therefore makes $F'$ constant, and hence

$$
F(z)=az+b.
$$

Thus every holomorphic map has an [affine lift of a holomorphic map between one-dimensional complex tori](../../../complex-geometry.md#affine-lift-of-a-holomorphic-map-between-one-dimensional-complex-tori). If “map of complex tori” means an identity-preserving map, choose $F(0)=0$; then $b=0$, so $F$ is the required [linear map](../../../vector-space.md#linear-map). Without that convention the statement must say affine, since a nonzero [translation in a group](../../../group.md#translation-in-a-group) of the torus lifts to $z\mapsto z+b$.

The [Weierstrass elliptic function](../../../complex-analysis.md#weierstrass-elliptic-function) of $\Lambda$ is

$$
\wp_\Lambda(z)=\frac1{z^2}+
\sum_{\omega\in\Lambda\setminus\{0\}}
\left(\frac1{(z-\omega)^2}-\frac1{\omega^2}\right).
$$

The subtracted term gives the [Normal convergence of the Weierstrass elliptic-function series](../../../complex-analysis.md#normal-convergence-of-the-weierstrass-elliptic-function-series) away from $\Lambda$. Put

$$
G_{2r}=\sum_{\omega\in\Lambda\setminus\{0\}}\omega^{-2r}.
$$

The [Laurent coefficients of the Weierstrass elliptic function](../../../complex-analysis.md#laurent-coefficients-of-the-weierstrass-elliptic-function) at zero give

$$
\wp(z)=z^{-2}+3G_4z^2+5G_6z^4+O(z^6),
\qquad
\wp'(z)=-2z^{-3}+6G_4z+20G_6z^3+O(z^5).
$$

Set $g_2=60G_4$ and $g_3=140G_6$. Direct substitution shows that the principal part and constant term of

$$
H(z)=\wp'(z)^2-4\wp(z)^3+g_2\wp(z)+g_3
$$

vanish at zero. Since $H$ is an [elliptic function](../../../complex-analysis.md#elliptic-function), translation gives the same cancellation at every point of the [period lattice](../../../complex-analysis.md#period-lattice). Every apparent [isolated singularity](../../../isolated-singularity.md) is therefore a [removable singularity](../../../isolated-singularity.md#removable-singularity), so $H$ is an [entire function](../../../complex-analysis.md#entire-function). It is periodic and hence bounded on the translates of a compact [fundamental parallelogram](../../../complex-analysis.md#fundamental-parallelogram-of-a-period-lattice). The [Liouville theorem](../../../complex-analysis.md#liouville-theorem) gives $H=0$, which proves the [Weierstrass elliptic differential equation](../../../complex-analysis.md#weierstrass-elliptic-differential-equation)

$$
\boxed{\wp'(z)^2=4\wp(z)^3-g_2\wp(z)-g_3.}
$$

Finally suppose that $f$ is a biholomorphic [group homomorphism](../../../group-theory.md#group-homomorphism). Its identity-preserving lift has the form $F(z)=\zeta z$ by the first part. Since the inverse map also lifts linearly,

$$
\zeta\Lambda=\Lambda.
$$

Choose a $\mathbb Z$-basis of $\Lambda$. Multiplication by $\zeta$ is then represented by a [unimodular matrix](../../../linear-algebra.md#unimodular-matrix) $A\in\operatorname{GL}_2(\mathbb Z)$. As a real-linear transformation of $\mathbb C$, it has [determinant](../../../linear-algebra.md#determinant) $|\zeta|^2>0$; hence $\det A=1$ and $|\zeta|=1$. The [characteristic polynomial](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#characteristic-polynomial) of $A$ and the [Cayley-Hamilton theorem](../../../mathematics.md#cayley-hamilton-theorem) give

$$
\zeta^2-\operatorname{tr}(A)\zeta+1=0.
$$

This already has the required form with $m=-\operatorname{tr}(A)\in\mathbb Z$ and $n=1$. Moreover $2\operatorname{Re}\zeta=\operatorname{tr}(A)$, so $|\zeta|=1$ forces $\operatorname{tr}(A)\in\{-2,-1,0,1,2\}$. Thus $\zeta$ is a [root of unity](../../../algebra.md#root-of-unity), of order $1$, $2$, $3$, $4$, or $6$, completing the description of an [automorphism of a one-dimensional complex torus](../../../complex-geometry.md#automorphism-of-a-one-dimensional-complex-torus).

Homogenizing the affine equation gives the [projective closure](../../../projective-space.md#projective-completion)

\qquad
F_Y=3Y^2Z,

The formula $[X:Y:Z]\mapsto[X:Z]$ is initially undefined at the unique point $P_\infty=[0:1:0]$ of $X$ at infinity. Since $X$ is a [smooth projective curve](../../../projective-space.md#smooth-projective-curve), the [extension of a rational map from a smooth projective curve](../../../projective-space.md#extension-of-a-rational-map-from-a-smooth-projective-curve) extends it uniquely to a morphism

$$
\varphi:X\longrightarrow\mathbb P^1.
$$

On the affine chart it is the rational function $x$, and the equation $y^3=x^4+1$ shows that $\varphi$ has degree three.

For each of the four roots $\alpha$ of $x^4+1$, let $P_\alpha=(\alpha,0)$. Since the root is simple, $t=y$ is a [uniformizer](../../../commutative-algebra.md#uniformizer) at $P_\alpha$, and the equation gives

$$
x-\alpha=c_\alpha t^3+O(t^6),
\qquad c_\alpha\ne0.
$$

Thus the [ramification index of a holomorphic map](../../../complex-analysis.md#ramification-index-of-a-holomorphic-map) at every $P_\alpha$ is three.

At $P_\infty$, use the chart $Y=1$ and put $u=X/Y$, $v=Z/Y$. The equation becomes

$$
v=u^4+v^4.
$$

The [implicit function theorem](../../../calculus.md#implicit-function-theorem) makes $u$ a uniformizer and gives $v=u^4$ times a local unit. Hence

$$
x=\frac XZ=\frac uv=u^{-3}\mathbin{\cdot}(\text{local unit}),
$$

so $P_\infty$ also has ramification index three. These are all the [ramification points of a holomorphic map](../../../complex-analysis.md#ramification-point-of-a-holomorphic-map) because the resulting ramification degree is $5(3-1)=10$. Indeed, the [Riemann-Hurwitz formula](../../../complex-analysis.md#riemann-hurwitz-formula) gives

$$
2g_X-2=3(2g_{\mathbb P^1}-2)+10=-6+10=4.
$$

Therefore $g_X=3$, in agreement with the [genus of a smooth plane curve](../../../algebraic-geometry.md#genus-of-a-smooth-plane-curve), and the [Euler characteristic](../../../homology.md#euler-characteristic) of the underlying compact orientable surface is

$$
\boxed{g_X=3,\qquad \chi(X)=2-2g_X=-4.}

<h5 id="25h/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#25h/a/i)

The coordinate tangent vectors are

$$
\phi_u=(-f\sin u,f\cos u,0),
\qquad
\phi_v=(f'\cos u,f'\sin u,g'),
$$

where $f,f',g'$ are evaluated at $v$. Their [cross product](../../../vector-space.md#cross-product) is

$$
\phi_u\times\phi_v
=\bigl(fg'\cos u,fg'\sin u,-ff'\bigr),
$$

and therefore

$$
\lVert\phi_u\times\phi_v\rVert
=f(v)\sqrt{f'(v)^2+g'(v)^2}>0.
$$

The positivity follows because $f(v)>0$ and the profile is a [regular curve](../../../differential-geometry.md#regular-curve). Hence $d\phi$ has rank two everywhere, so $\phi$ is an [immersion](../../../differential-geometry.md#immersion). The angle interval $(0,2\pi)$ and the absence of profile self-intersections make $\phi$ injective with a continuous local inverse. It is therefore an [embedded surface parametrization](../../../differential-geometry.md#embedded-surface-parametrization), and its image is a [regular surface](../../../differential-geometry.md#smooth-surface).

<h4 id="25h/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#25h/a)

<h5 id="25h/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#25h/a/ii)

The [first fundamental form of a surface of revolution](../../../differential-geometry.md#first-fundamental-form-of-a-surface-of-revolution) is

$$
I=f(v)^2\,du^2+q(v)^2\,dv^2,
\qquad
q(v)^2=f'(v)^2+g'(v)^2.
$$

The [geodesic Lagrangian](../../../riemannian-geometry.md#geodesic-lagrangian) is consequently

$$
L=\frac12\left(f(v)^2u'^2+q(v)^2v'^2\right).
$$

The rotation angle $u$ is a [cyclic coordinate](../../../classical-mechanics.md#cyclic-coordinate), so its [conjugate momentum](../../../classical-mechanics.md#canonical-momentum) is conserved by the [Euler-Lagrange equation](../../../analysis.md#euler-lagrange-equation):

$$
\frac d{ds}\frac{\partial L}{\partial u'}
=\frac d{ds}\left(f(v)^2u'\right)=0.
$$

Thus $f(v(s))^2u'(s)$ is constant.

The positively oriented unit tangent to the [parallel of a surface of revolution](../../../differential-geometry.md#parallel-of-a-surface-of-revolution) through $\gamma(s)$ is $e_u=\phi_u/f(v)$. Since $\gamma$ has unit speed and $\theta$ is its angle with that parallel,

$$
\cos\theta(s)
=\langle\gamma'(s),e_u\rangle
=f(v(s))u'(s).
$$

Multiplication by $f(v(s))$ yields

$$
\boxed{f(v(s))\cos\theta(s)=f(v(s))^2u'(s)=\text{constant},}
$$

which is [Clairaut's relation](../../../differential-geometry.md#clairaut-first-integral-for-a-surface-of-revolution).

<h3 id="25h/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [25H](#25h)

<h4 id="25h/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#25h/b)

<h5 id="25h/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#25h/b/i)

Because $f'(a)$ and $f'(b)$ are nonzero, $r=f(v)$ is a valid one-sided local coordinate at each endpoint. Define

$$
h_a(r)=g\bigl(f^{-1}(r)\bigr)
$$

using the inverse near $a$, and define $h_b$ analogously near $b$. The necessary and sufficient conditions are:


- $g(a)\ne g(b)$, so the two collapsed boundary circles give two distinct poles;
- near each endpoint there is a smooth function $H_*$ such that


$$
h_*(r)=h_*(0)+H_*(r^2).
$$

Indeed, the second condition makes the surface near the corresponding pole the graph

$$
z=h_*(0)+H_*(x^2+y^2),
$$

which is a smooth regular graph over the horizontal tangent plane. Together with part a(i), these two pole charts cover the closure by [regular surface](../../../differential-geometry.md#smooth-surface) charts. The surface is compact because it is the continuous image of the compact cylinder $[0,2\pi]\times[a,b]$ after each boundary circle is collapsed to its pole.

Conversely, rotational invariance forces the tangent plane at a pole to be horizontal. The surface is therefore locally the graph of a smooth radial function of $(x,y)$. A smooth rotation-invariant function near the origin is a smooth function of $x^2+y^2$, which gives the displayed condition. The poles must be distinct, since otherwise a punctured neighbourhood of their common image would have two components and could not be a surface chart. This is the [smooth endpoint criterion for a surface of revolution](../../../differential-geometry.md#smooth-endpoint-criterion-for-a-surface-of-revolution). In particular it implies the familiar first-order conditions $g'(a)=g'(b)=0$, but those conditions alone do not ensure smoothness of every order.

<h4 id="25h/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#25h/b)

<h5 id="25h/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#25h/b/ii)

Let

$$
c=f(v(s))^2u'(s)=f(v(s))\cos\theta(s)
$$

be the [Clairaut first integral for a surface of revolution](../../../differential-geometry.md#clairaut-first-integral-for-a-surface-of-revolution) from part a(ii). If $c\ne0$, then $|\cos\theta|\leq1$ gives

$$

$$

for every $s$. Since $f$ tends to zero at both poles, the geodesic avoids nonempty open neighbourhoods of the poles.

If $c=0$, then $u'(s)=0$ whenever the geodesic is away from a pole. It consequently lies in one [meridian of a surface of revolution](../../../differential-geometry.md#meridian-of-a-surface-of-revolution); after crossing a pole, the angular coordinate changes by $\pi$ but remains in the same plane through the rotation axis. A small surface neighbourhood of any point outside that meridian is disjoint from the geodesic. In either case there is a nonempty open set $U$ with

$$
\gamma(\mathbb R)\cap U=\varnothing.
$$

This proves that the [geodesic image on a compact two-pole surface of revolution is not dense](../../../differential-geometry.md#geodesic-image-on-a-compact-two-pole-surface-of-revolution-is-not-dense).

<h3 id="26k/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [26K](#26k)

<h4 id="26k/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#26k/b)

The integrands are absolutely integrable because $f$ has compact support, $g\in L^1(\mathbb R)$, and $|\varphi_Z|\leq1$. [Fubini's theorem](../../../measure-theory.md#fubini-s-theorem) therefore permits us to insert $\varphi_Z(s)=\mathbb E[e^{isZ}]$ and interchange the expectation and integrals:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\iint g(\varepsilon s)f(x)e^{-isx}\varphi_Z(s)\,ds\,dx\\
&=\mathbb E\left[
\iint g(\varepsilon s)f(x)e^{is(Z-x)}\,ds\,dx
\right].
\end{aligned}
$$

With $r=\varepsilon s$, the inner integral in $s$ is

$$
\int g(\varepsilon s)e^{is(Z-x)}\,ds
=\frac1\varepsilon
\widehat g\left(\frac{Z-x}{\varepsilon}\right).
$$

Now set $t=(Z-x)/\varepsilon$. The change of variables $x=Z-\varepsilon t$ gives

$$
\iint g(\varepsilon s)f(x)e^{-isx}\varphi_Z(s)\,ds\,dx
=\int\widehat g(t)\mathbb E[f(Z-\varepsilon t)]\,dt,
$$

which is the required identity.

Choose any $g$ with $g,\widehat g\in L^1$, $g(0)\ne0$, and for which [Fourier inversion theorem](../../../fourier-analysis.md#fourier-inversion-theorem) applies, for example a [Gaussian function](../../../calculus.md#gaussian-function). The left side factors through the [Fourier transform](../../../analysis.md#fourier-transform) of $f$ as

$$
\int g(\varepsilon s)\widehat f(-s)\varphi_Z(s)\,ds.
$$

On the right side, $f(Z-\varepsilon t)\to f(Z)$ and

$$
|\widehat g(t)\mathbb E[f(Z-\varepsilon t)]|
\leq\lVert f\rVert_\infty|\widehat g(t)|.
$$

The [dominated convergence theorem](../../../measure-theory.md#dominated-convergence-theorem) and Fourier inversion at zero give

$$
\int\widehat g(t)\mathbb E[f(Z-\varepsilon t)]\,dt
\longrightarrow
2\pi g(0)\mathbb E[f(Z)].
$$

Consequently the [Fourier-cutoff recovery of expectations from a characteristic function](../../../probability-theory.md#fourier-cutoff-recovery-of-expectations-from-a-characteristic-function) is

$$
\boxed{
\mathbb E[f(Z)]
=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow0}
\frac{1}{2\pi g(0)}
\int g(\varepsilon s)\widehat f(-s)\varphi_Z(s)\,ds.}
$$

The right side depends only on $\varphi_Z$. Equal [characteristic functions](../../../probability-theory.md#characteristic-function) therefore give equal expectations for every compactly supported continuous $f$, and part a yields the [uniqueness theorem for characteristic functions](../../../probability-theory.md#uniqueness-theorem-for-characteristic-functions).

## 27K

<h4 id="27k/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#27k/a)

A [continuous-time Markov chain](../../../markov-process.md#continuous-time-markov-chain) is a [reversible Markov chain](../../../markov-process.md#reversible-markov-chain) in equilibrium if, when started in a [stationary distribution](../../../markov-process.md#stationary-distribution) $\pi$, the process $(X_t:0\leq t\leq T)$ has the same finite-dimensional distributions as $(X_{T-t}:0\leq t\leq T)$ for every $T>0$.

For transition rates $q_{ij}$, the [detailed balance for a continuous-time Markov chain](../../../markov-process.md#detailed-balance-for-a-continuous-time-markov-chain) equations are

$$
\boxed{\pi_iq_{ij}=\pi_jq_{ji}\qquad(i\ne j).}
$$

They imply invariance directly. For each state $j$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
(\pi Q)_j
&=\sum_{i\ne j}\pi_iq_{ij}+\pi_jq_{jj}\\
&=\pi_j\sum_{i\ne j}q_{ji}
-\pi_j\sum_{k\ne j}q_{jk}=0,
\end{aligned}
$$

because $q_{jj}=-\sum_{k\ne j}q_{jk}$. Thus $\pi Q=0$, so $\pi$ is an [invariant distribution of a continuous-time Markov chain](../../../markov-process.md#invariant-distribution-of-a-continuous-time-markov-chain).

<h3 id="27k/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [27K](#27k)

<h4 id="27k/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#27k/b)

Put $K=N+s$, the total capacity. The customer count is an [M-M-s-K queue](../../../markov-process.md#m-m-s-k-queue), hence a [birth-death process](../../../markov-process.md#birth-death-process) on $\{0,1,\ldots,K\}$. Its nonzero transition rates are

$$
q_{n,n+1}=\lambda
\quad(0\leq n<K),
\qquad
q_{n,n-1}=\mu\min(n,s)
\quad(1\leq n\leq K).
$$

The first rate is zero at $K$ because an arrival finding the shop full is rejected. The second rate is the number of busy servers times the individual service rate.

Write $\rho=\lambda/\mu$. The [detailed balance for a birth-death process](../../../markov-process.md#detailed-balance-for-a-birth-death-process) recursion is

$$
\frac{\pi_n}{\pi_{n-1}}
=\frac{\lambda}{\mu\min(n,s)}.
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{\begin{gathered}
\pi_n=\frac{w_n}{Z},
\qquad
w_n=
\begin{cases}
\rho^n/n!,&0\leq n\leq s,\\
\rho^n/(s!s^{n-s}),&s<n\leq K,
\end{cases}
\end{gathered}}
$$

where

$$
Z=\sum_{n=0}^{s}\frac{\rho^n}{n!}
+\frac{\rho^s}{s!}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(\frac\rho s\right)^j.
$$

This is the [stationary distribution of an M-M-s-K queue](../../../markov-process.md#stationary-distribution-of-an-m-m-s-k-queue).

The state space is finite, and every state communicates with every other through successive births and deaths because $\lambda,\mu>0$. The chain is therefore an [irreducible Markov chain](../../../markov-process.md#irreducible-markov-chain), so its invariant distribution is unique. Finally the adjacent-state identities

$$
\pi_n\lambda
=\pi_{n+1}\mu\min(n+1,s)
$$

are detailed balance, while all nonadjacent rates vanish in both directions. Part a now shows that the queue is reversible in equilibrium.

\boxed{\widehat\theta_{\rm MLE}=\frac n{S_n}=\frac1{\overline X}.}
$$

For an [exponential distribution](../../../continuous-probability-distribution.md#exponential-distribution) of rate $\theta$,

$$
\mathbb E[X_i]=\frac1\theta,
\qquad
\operatorname{var}(X_i)=\frac1{\theta^2}.
$$

The [central limit theorem](../../../convergence-of-random-variables.md#central-limit-theorem) gives

$$
\sqrt n\left(\overline X-\frac1\theta\right)
\xrightarrow dN\left(0,\frac1{\theta^2}\right).
$$

Applying the [delta method](../../../statistical-inference.md#delta-method) to $h(x)=1/x$, for which $h'(1/\theta)=-\theta^2$, yields the [exponential-rate maximum-likelihood estimator](../../../statistical-modelling.md#exponential-rate-maximum-likelihood-estimator) limit

$$
\boxed{
\sqrt n(\widehat\theta_{\rm MLE}-\theta)
\xrightarrow dN(0,\theta^2).}
$$

<h4 id="28j/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#28j/b)

Multiplying the likelihood by the [gamma distribution](../../../continuous-probability-distribution.md#gamma-distribution) prior density gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\pi(\theta\mid x_1,\ldots,x_n)
&\propto
\theta^n e^{-\theta S_n}
\theta^{\alpha-1}e^{-\beta\theta}\\
&=\theta^{\alpha+n-1}
e^{-(\beta+S_n)\theta}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus [Gamma-exponential conjugacy](../../../statistical-inference.md#gamma-exponential-conjugacy) gives

$$
\boxed{
\theta\mid X_1,\ldots,X_n
\sim\operatorname{Gamma}(\alpha+n,\beta+S_n),}
$$

where the second parameter is the rate. Under [squared-error loss](../../../statistical-inference.md#squared-error-loss), the [Bayes estimator under squared error loss](../../../statistical-inference.md#bayes-estimator-under-squared-error-loss) is the [posterior mean](../../../statistical-inference.md#posterior-mean), hence

$$
\boxed{
\widehat\theta_\pi
=\frac{\alpha+n}{\beta+S_n}.}
$$

<h3 id="28j/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [28J](#28j)

<h4 id="28j/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#28j/c)

The [strong law of large numbers](../../../convergence-of-random-variables.md#strong-law-of-large-numbers) gives $S_n/n=\overline X\to1/\theta$ almost surely, and hence in probability. Therefore

$$
\widehat\theta_\pi
=\frac{1+\alpha/n}{\overline X+\beta/n}
\xrightarrow{p}\theta
$$

by the [continuous mapping theorem](../../../convergence-of-random-variables.md#continuous-mapping-theorem). Thus the Bayes estimator has [statistics](../../../statistical-inference.md#consistency-statistics).

It differs from the maximum-likelihood estimator by

$$
\widehat\theta_\pi-\widehat\theta_{\rm MLE}
=\frac{\alpha S_n-n\beta}{S_n(S_n+\beta)}.
$$

Since $S_n/n\to1/\theta$, the numerator is $O_p(n)$ and the denominator is $O_p(n^2)$, so this difference is $O_p(n^{-1})=o_p(n^{-1/2})$. The limit in part a and [Slutsky theorem](../../../statistical-inference.md#slutsky-theorem) now give

$$
\boxed{
\sqrt n(\widehat\theta_\pi-\theta)
\xrightarrow dN(0,\theta^2).}
$$

\quad(x\to+\infty).

\sin(t^2)

## 31E

<h3 id="31e/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [31E](#31e)

<h4 id="31e/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#31e/a)

For $a=b=0$, take the [Logarithmic first integral of the Lotka-Volterra equations](../../../dynamical-systems.md#logarithmic-first-integral-of-the-lotka-volterra-equations)

$$
\boxed{V(x,y)=r(x-\log x-1)+y-\log y-1.}
$$

Along a trajectory,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\dot V
&=r\left(1-\frac1x\right)x(1-y)
+\left(1-\frac1y\right)ry(x-1)\\
&=r(x-1)(1-y)+r(y-1)(x-1)=0.
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus $V$ is a [first integral](../../../differential-equation.md#first-integral). The scalar function $h(s)=s-\log s-1$ is nonnegative for $s>0$, vanishes only at $s=1$, and tends to infinity as $s\downarrow0$ or $s\to\infty$. Moreover,

$$
\nabla^2V=
\begin{pmatrix}r/x^2&0\\0&1/y^2\end{pmatrix}
$$

is positive definite, so $V$ is [strictly convex](../../../real-analysis.md#strictly-convex-function). It follows that $(1,1)$ is its unique critical point and every level set $V=c>0$ is a compact smooth simple closed curve in the [positive quadrant](../../../linear-algebra.md#positive-quadrant). The vector field is nonzero on such a level curve, so uniqueness of solutions makes each connected level curve one [periodic orbit](../../../dynamical-systems.md#periodic-orbit). The zero level consists of the fixed point $(1,1)$. **Hence every trajectory in $\Lambda$ is either periodic or the fixed point $(1,1)$.**

<h3 id="31e/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [31E](#31e)

<h4 id="31e/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#31e/b)

Keep the same function $V$. For arbitrary $a,b$ its [orbital derivative](../../../dynamical-systems.md#orbital-derivative) is

$$
\dot V=r\{a x(x-1)-b y(y-1)\}.
$$

Let $\gamma$ be a nonconstant orbit of the unperturbed system with period $T$. Since

$$
\frac{\dot y}{ry}=x-1,
\qquad
\frac{\dot x}{x}=1-y,
$$

integration over one period gives

$$
\int_0^T(1-x)\,dt=0,
\qquad
\int_0^T(1-y)\,dt=0.
$$

Consequently,

$$
\begin{aligned}
A&:=\int_0^T x(x-1)\,dt
=\int_0^T(1-x)^2\,dt>0,\\
B&:=\int_0^T y(y-1)\,dt
=\int_0^T(1-y)^2\,dt>0.
\end{aligned}
$$

If a perturbed periodic orbit persisted and converged to $\gamma$ as $(a,b)\to(0,0)$, its net change in $V$ over one period would vanish. The [averaged first-integral obstruction to persistence of a periodic orbit](../../../dynamical-systems.md#averaged-first-integral-obstruction-to-persistence-of-a-periodic-orbit) would therefore require

$$
0=r(aA-bB)+o(|a|+|b|).
$$

When $ab<0$, the two leading terms have the same nonzero sign and their magnitude is bounded below by $r\min(A,B)(|a|+|b|)$. **Thus no unperturbed periodic orbit persists for sufficiently small $a,b$ with $ab<0$.**

<h4 id="31e/c/solution">Solution</h4>

$$

and hence

\boxed{
\nabla\mathbin{\cdot}(\phi F)
=\frac ay-\frac{rb}{x}.}
$$

For $ab<0$, both terms have the same strict sign throughout $\Lambda$. The [Bendixson-Dulac criterion](../../../dynamical-systems.md#bendixson-dulac-theorem) therefore gives **no periodic orbit in $\Lambda$.** The same computation also excludes the case $ab=0$ unless $a=b=0$.

<h3 id="31e/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [31E](#31e)

<h4 id="31e/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#31e/d)

An interior [equilibrium point of a dynamical system](../../../dynamical-systems.md#equilibrium-point-of-a-dynamical-system) must satisfy

$$
x-by=1,
\qquad
y-ax=1.
$$

If $ab\ne1$, the unique candidate is

$$
(x_*,y_*)=
\left(\frac{1+b}{1-ab},
\frac{1+a}{1-ab}\right).
$$

It belongs to $\Lambda$ precisely in either of the two cases

$$
a>-1, b>-1, ab<1,
\qquad\text{or}\qquad
a<-1, b<-1, ab>1.
$$

At this equilibrium the [Jacobian matrix](../../../calculus.md#jacobian-matrix) has determinant

$$
\det DF(x_*,y_*)=r x_*y_*(1-ab).
$$

In the first region the equilibrium has [Poincaré index](../../../dynamical-systems.md#poincare-index) $+1$, so index considerations alone do not rule out a periodic orbit. In the second region it is a [saddle equilibrium](../../../dynamical-systems.md#saddle-equilibrium) of index $-1$. Everywhere else there is no interior equilibrium, except at $a=b=-1$, where the segment $x+y=1$ consists of equilibria.

Every simple closed curve in the convex set $\Lambda$ bounds a region contained in $\Lambda$. A periodic orbit has index $+1$, equal to the sum of the indices of the equilibria that it encloses. Thus a region with no equilibrium has total index zero, while the region with the single saddle has total index $-1$; both contradict the [index of a planar periodic orbit](../../../dynamical-systems.md#index-of-a-planar-periodic-orbit). At $a=b=-1$, a periodic orbit cannot cross the equilibrium segment and must lie in one of its two complementary convex regions, where it encloses no equilibrium, giving the same contradiction. Therefore index considerations exclude periodic orbits except possibly when

$$
\boxed{a>-1,\qquad b>-1,\qquad ab<1.}
$$

Write $u_j=u^{(j)}$. The transformation $\psi_s:(x,u)\mapsto(\widetilde x,\widetilde u)$ induces a transformation of the $n$th [jet space of a scalar ordinary differential equation](../../../differential-equation.md#jet-space-of-a-scalar-ordinary-differential-equation) by differentiating $\widetilde u$ with respect to $\widetilde x$ through order $n$. The infinitesimal generator of this induced action is the [prolongation of a vector field](../../../differential-equation.md#prolongation-of-a-vector-field)

$$
\boxed{
\operatorname{pr}^{(n)}V
=V+\sum_{j=1}^n\eta_j\frac{\partial}{\partial u_j}.}
$$

To determine its coefficients, put $\eta_0=\eta$ and use the [total derivative operator](../../../differential-equation.md#total-derivative-operator)

$$
D_x=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}
+u_1\frac{\partial}{\partial u}
+\sum_{j\geq1}u_{j+1}\frac{\partial}{\partial u_j}.
$$

Differentiating transformed derivatives by

$$
\widetilde u_j
=\frac{D_x\widetilde u_{j-1}}{D_x\widetilde x}
$$

and then differentiating with respect to the group parameter at $s=0$ gives the recursion

$$
\boxed{
\eta_j=D_x\eta_{j-1}-u_jD_x\xi,
\qquad j=1,\ldots,n.}
$$

This also proves the displayed form of the prolongation by mathematical induction on the derivative order.

↑ **Parent:** [32C](#32c)

<h4 id="32c/b/solution">Solution</h4>

For $V_1=\partial_x$, one has $\xi=1$, $\eta=0$, and hence $D_x\xi=0$. The recursion from part a gives $\eta_j=0$ for every $j$.

For $V_2=x\partial_x$, one has $D_x\xi=1$. Starting from $\eta_0=0$, induction gives

$$
\eta_j=-ju_j.
$$

For $V_3=x^2\partial_x$, one has $D_x\xi=2x$. The first coefficients are $\eta_1=-2xu_1$ and $\eta_2=-2u_1-4xu_2$, and induction gives

$$
\eta_j=-2jx u_j-j(j-1)u_{j-1}.
$$

Thus the three [prolongations of the projective vector fields on the line](../../../differential-equation.md#prolongations-of-the-projective-vector-fields-on-the-line) are

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{pr}^{(n)}V_1
&=\partial_x,\\
\operatorname{pr}^{(n)}V_2
&=x\partial_x-\sum_{j=1}^n j u_j\partial_{u_j},\\
\operatorname{pr}^{(n)}V_3
&=x^2\partial_x-\sum_{j=1}^n

The first prolonged generator is $\partial_x$, and $S$ has no explicit dependence on $x$, so $\operatorname{pr}^{(3)}V_1(S)=0$. Under $\operatorname{pr}^{(3)}V_2$, each $u_j$ has weight $-j$. Both terms in $S$ therefore have weight $-2$, giving $\operatorname{pr}^{(3)}V_2(S)=-2S$. Substitution of

\boxed{c_1=0,\qquad c_2=-2,\qquad c_3=-4x.}

For a normalized eigenstate $|n\rangle$ with a [nondegenerate energy eigenvalue](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#nondegenerate-energy-eigenvalue), [first-order nondegenerate perturbation theory](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#first-order-nondegenerate-perturbation-theory) and [second-order nondegenerate perturbation theory](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#second-order-nondegenerate-perturbation-theory) give

$$
\boxed{
E_n(H)=E_n+\langle n|V|n\rangle
+\sum_{m\ne n}
\frac{|\langle m|V|n\rangle|^2}{E_n-E_m}
+O(V^3).}
$$

<h3 id="33b/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [33B](#33b)

<h4 id="33b/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#33b/a)

Put $E_1=E_2=E$. Within the degenerate eigenspace spanned by $|1\rangle,|2\rangle$, the perturbation is

$$
V_{\rm deg}
=\varepsilon V_0
\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$

[Degenerate perturbation theory](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#degenerate-perturbation-theory) diagonalizes this matrix. Its normalized eigenstates $(|1\rangle\pm|2\rangle)/\sqrt2$ have first-order shifts $\pm\varepsilon V_0$. The coupling to $|3\rangle$ has order $\varepsilon^2$, so it cannot affect an energy through order $\varepsilon$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{
E_+=E+\varepsilon V_0,\qquad
E_-=E-\varepsilon V_0,\qquad
E_3'=E_3
\quad\text{through order }\varepsilon.}
$$

<h3 id="33b/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [33B](#33b)

<h4 id="33b/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#33b/b)

Now put $E_2=E_3=E$ and $E_1\ne E$. All diagonal matrix elements of $V$ vanish. The leading coupling is $\langle1|V|2\rangle=\varepsilon V_0$, whereas $\langle1|V|3\rangle=\varepsilon^2V_0$. Applying the second-order formula gives

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
E_1'&=E_1+\frac{\varepsilon^2V_0^2}{E_1-E}+O(\varepsilon^4),\\
E_2'&=E+\frac{\varepsilon^2V_0^2}{E-E_1}+O(\varepsilon^4),\\
E_3'&=E+O(\varepsilon^4).
\end{aligned}}
$$

The degeneracy causes no singular denominator through this order because $\langle2|V|3\rangle=0$. Equivalently, the effective perturbation on the degenerate subspace begins as

$$
\frac{V_0^2}{E-E_1}
\begin{pmatrix}
\varepsilon^2&\varepsilon^3\\
\varepsilon^3&\varepsilon^4
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Its off-diagonal mixing is only order $\varepsilon^3$, so **degenerate perturbation theory is unnecessary for energies through order $\varepsilon^2$.**

↑ **Parent:** [33B](#33b)

<h4 id="33b/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#33b/c)

The states $|2\rangle$ and $|3\rangle$ are degenerate and couple to $|1\rangle$ with amplitudes $\varepsilon V_0$ and $\varepsilon^2V_0$. The [bright and dark state reduction of a star-coupled Hamiltonian](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#bright-and-dark-state-reduction-of-a-star-coupled-hamiltonian) gives one exact dark eigenstate proportional to $-\varepsilon|2\rangle+|3\rangle$, with energy $E$. The orthogonal bright state couples to $|1\rangle$ with magnitude

$$
q=V_0\sqrt{\varepsilon^2+\varepsilon^4}
=\varepsilon V_0\sqrt{1+\varepsilon^2}.
$$

Diagonalizing the remaining two-dimensional Hermitian matrix gives the exact spectrum

$$
\boxed{
E,\qquad
E_\pm=\frac{E_1+E}{2}
\pm\sqrt{\frac{(E_1-E)^2}{4}
+\varepsilon^2V_0^2(1+\varepsilon^2)}.}
$$

Writing $\Delta=E_1-E\ne0$ and expanding the branches according to the eigenvalue to which they converge,

$$
\begin{aligned}
E_{\text{near }E_1}
&=E_1+\frac{\varepsilon^2V_0^2}{E_1-E}
+O(\varepsilon^4),\\
E_{\text{near }E}
&=E+\frac{\varepsilon^2V_0^2}{E-E_1}
+O(\varepsilon^4),\\
E_{\rm dark}&=E.
\end{aligned}
$$

These are exactly the three perturbative energies from part b through order $\varepsilon^2$.

## 34B

<h3 id="34b/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [34B](#34b)

[Bloch theorem](../../../quantum-theory.md#bloch-s-theorem) states that the energy eigenstates of a Hamiltonian invariant under translations by a [Bravais lattice](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#bravais-lattice) can be chosen as [Bloch states](../../../quantum-theory.md#bloch-state)

$$
\boxed{
T_r|\psi_k\rangle=e^{-ik\cdot r}|\psi_k\rangle,
\qquad
\psi_k(x)=e^{ik\cdot x}u_k(x),
\qquad
u_k(x+r)=u_k(x).}
$$

Indeed, the [unitary operators](../../../vector-space.md#unitary-operator) $T_r$ commute with one another because $T_rT_s=T_{r+s}=T_sT_r$, and they commute with $H$ by hypothesis. The [simultaneous diagonalization](../../../mathematics.md#simultaneous-diagonalization) theorem therefore lets us diagonalize all translations within each energy eigenspace. Their eigenvalues form a unitary character $\chi$ of the additive lattice:

$$
\chi(r+s)=\chi(r)\chi(s),
\qquad |\chi(r)|=1.
$$

Writing $\chi(a_j)=e^{-ik\cdot a_j}$ on a primitive basis gives $\chi(r)=e^{-ik\cdot r}$. In position space, with $(T_r\psi)(x)=\psi(x-r)$, this implies $\psi_k(x+r)=e^{ik\cdot r}\psi_k(x)$. Hence $u_k(x)=e^{-ik\cdot x}\psi_k(x)$ is lattice-periodic. Adding a [reciprocal lattice](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#reciprocal-lattice) vector to $k$ leaves the character unchanged, so the [crystal momentum](../../../quantum-theory.md#crystal-momentum) lies in a [Brillouin zone](../../../quantum-theory.md#brillouin-zone).

For

$$
a_1=\frac a2(\sqrt3,1),
\qquad
a_2=\frac a2(\sqrt3,-1),
$$

the equations $a_i\cdot b_j=2\pi\delta_{ij}$ give the reciprocal basis

$$
\boxed{
b_1=\left(\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt3a},\frac{2\pi}{a}\right),
\qquad
b_2=\left(\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt3a},-\frac{2\pi}{a}\right).}
$$

The reciprocal lattice is triangular, so the first Brillouin zone is its [Wigner-Seitz cell](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#wigner-seitz-cell), a regular hexagon. Its six corners are

$$
\pm\frac{2b_1+b_2}{3},
\qquad
\pm\frac{b_1+2b_2}{3},
\qquad
\pm\frac{b_1-b_2}{3}.
$$

Reciprocal-lattice translations identify these corners in two classes of three. Representatives are

$$
\boxed{
K=\frac{2b_1+b_2}{3}
=\left(\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt3a},\frac{2\pi}{3a}\right),
\qquad
K'=\frac{b_1+2b_2}{3}
=\left(\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt3a},-\frac{2\pi}{3a}\right).}
$$

For example, $(b_1-b_2)/3=K'-b_2$, while the other equivalences follow by symmetry and reciprocal translations. This gives the requested sketch: a regular hexagon with the vertical edge from $K'$ to $K$ at $k_x=2\pi/(\sqrt3a)$ and alternating $K,K'$ corner classes.

For the [tight-binding model](../../../quantum-theory.md#tight-binding), introduce the normalized Bloch sum

$$
|k\rangle=\frac1{\sqrt{N_s}}\sum_{r\in\Lambda}e^{ik\cdot r}|r\rangle.
$$

Each hop by $\pm a_j$ multiplies this state by $e^{\mp ik\cdot a_j}$. Thus the [two-direction nearest-neighbour tight-binding dispersion](../../../quantum-theory.md#two-direction-nearest-neighbour-tight-binding-dispersion) is

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
E(k)
&=E_0-2\lambda\{\cos(k\cdot a_1)+\cos(k\cdot a_2)\}\\
&=E_0-4\lambda
\cos\left(\frac{\sqrt3ak_x}{2}\right)
\cos\left(\frac{ak_y}{2}\right).
\end{aligned}}
$$

Along the boundary edge from $K'$ to $K$, $k_x=2\pi/(\sqrt3a)$ and $|k_y|\leq2\pi/(3a)$, so

$$
E(k)=E_0+4\lambda\cos\left(\frac{ak_y}{2}\right).
$$

For $\lambda>0$, it rises from $E_0+2\lambda$ at either corner to $E_0+4\lambda$ at the midpoint; for $\lambda<0$ the ordering reverses. Globally the two cosines in the first expression can simultaneously equal $1$ or $-1$, and therefore

$$
\boxed{
E_{\min}=E_0-4|\lambda|,
\qquad
E_{\max}=E_0+4|\lambda|,
\qquad
\text{band width}=8|\lambda|.}
$$

Each orbital band contains two one-electron states per lattice site because an electron has two spin states. By [band filling](../../../quantum-theory.md#band-filling), a valency of one leaves this band half-filled and the material conducts, whereas a valency of two fills it. A filled band can be insulating only if it is separated from every empty band by a positive [band gap](../../../quantum-theory.md#band-gap).

The first band's maximum is $E_0+4|\lambda|$. If the second band's minimum is $E_0+\Delta$, a gap exists precisely when $\Delta>4|\lambda|$. Consequently, among the nontrivial fillings described here,

$$
\boxed{\text{the material is a band insulator when the valency is }2
\text{ and }\Delta>4|\lambda|.}
$$

For valency one the first band is partially filled. If $\Delta\leq4|\lambda|$, the bands overlap or touch, so even at valency two the [overlapping energy bands prevent a band insulator](../../../quantum-theory.md#overlapping-energy-bands-prevent-a-band-insulator).

## 35D

<h3 id="35d/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [35D](#35d)

The [chemical potential](../../../thermodynamics.md#chemical-potential) is the change in [internal energy](../../../thermodynamics.md#internal-energy) when one particle is added at fixed [entropy](../../../thermodynamics.md#entropy) and [volume](../../../geometry-and-topology.md#volume):

$$
\mu=\left(\frac{\partial E}{\partial N}\right)_{S,V}.
$$

Equivalently, $\mu=(\partial F/\partial N)_{T,V}$ for the [Helmholtz free energy](../../../thermodynamics.md#helmholtz-free-energy).

Let a microstate $s$ have [energy](../../../classical-mechanics.md#energy) $E_s$ and particle number $N_s$. Maximizing the [Gibbs entropy](../../../statistical-physics.md#gibbs-entropy) subject to normalization and fixed mean values of $E$ and $N$ is the [maximum-entropy derivation of equilibrium ensembles](../../../statistical-physics.md#maximum-entropy-derivation-of-equilibrium-ensembles). With [Lagrange multipliers](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#lagrange-multiplier) $\alpha$, $\beta$, and $-\beta\mu$, vary

$$
-\sum_s p_s\log p_s
-\alpha\left(\sum_s p_s-1\right)
-\beta\sum_s p_s(E_s-\mu N_s).
$$

The stationarity equation is $-\log p_s-1-\alpha-\beta(E_s-\mu N_s)=0$. Normalization therefore gives the [grand canonical ensemble](../../../statistical-physics.md#grand-canonical-ensemble)

$$
\boxed{
p_s=\frac{e^{-\beta(E_s-\mu N_s)}}{\mathcal Z},
\qquad
\mathcal Z=\sum_s e^{-\beta(E_s-\mu N_s)},
\qquad
\beta=\frac1{k_BT}.}
$$

Here $\mathcal Z$ is the [grand canonical partition function](../../../statistical-physics.md#grand-canonical-partition-function) and $k_B$ is the [Boltzmann constant](../../../thermodynamics.md#boltzmann-constant).

For one fermionic [quantum state](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#quantum-state) of energy $\varepsilon$, the [Pauli exclusion principle](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#pauli-exclusion-principle) permits occupation numbers only $n=0,1$. Its two grand-canonical weights are $1$ and $e^{-\beta(\varepsilon-\mu)}$, so its mean occupation is the [Fermi-Dirac distribution](../../../statistical-physics.md#fermi-dirac-distribution)

$$
\boxed{
n_F(\varepsilon)
=\frac{e^{-\beta(\varepsilon-\mu)}}{1+e^{-\beta(\varepsilon-\mu)}}
=\frac1{e^{\beta(\varepsilon-\mu)}+1}.}
$$

For a free nonrelativistic particle, $\varepsilon=\hbar^2k^2/(2m)$. In a region of area $A$, the number of wave-vector states in the annulus from $k$ to $k+dk$, including the two [spin angular momentum](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#spin) states, is

$$
2\frac{A}{(2\pi)^2}2\pi k\,dk.
$$

Since $k\,dk=(m/\hbar^2)d\varepsilon$, the [two-dimensional free-electron density of states](../../../statistical-physics.md#two-dimensional-free-electron-density-of-states) is constant:

$$
\boxed{g(\varepsilon)=\frac{Am}{\pi\hbar^2}.}
$$

We now use units in which $k_B=1$, as in the question. At zero [temperature](../../../thermodynamics.md#temperature), the [Fermi-Dirac distribution](../../../statistical-physics.md#fermi-dirac-distribution) is a [step function](../../../measure-theory.md#step-function), and hence

$$
N=g\int_0^{\varepsilon_F}d\varepsilon=g\varepsilon_F.
$$

At positive temperature the same fixed particle number satisfies the exact relation

$$
N=g\int_0^\infty\frac{d\varepsilon}{e^{(\varepsilon-\mu)/T}+1}
=gT\log(1+e^{\mu/T}).
$$

Thus

$$
\mu=T\log(e^{\varepsilon_F/T}-1)
=\varepsilon_F+T\log(1-e^{-\varepsilon_F/T})
=\varepsilon_F+O(Te^{-\varepsilon_F/T}).
$$

This is the [low-temperature particle-number cancellation for constant density of states](../../../statistical-physics.md#low-temperature-particle-number-cancellation-for-constant-density-of-states). Therefore, for $T\ll\varepsilon_F$, the mean number of particles in the energy interval $[\varepsilon,\varepsilon+d\varepsilon]$ is

$$
\boxed{
g(\varepsilon)n_F(\varepsilon)d\varepsilon
\simeq
\frac{N}{\varepsilon_F}
\frac{d\varepsilon}{e^{(\varepsilon-\varepsilon_F)/T}+1}.}
$$

Finally, compare the [internal energy](../../../thermodynamics.md#internal-energy) with its zero-temperature value. The thermally excited particles above $\varepsilon_F$ and holes below $\varepsilon_F$ have equal leading particle numbers, so their terms proportional to $\varepsilon_F$ cancel. With $x=|\varepsilon-\varepsilon_F|/T$,

$$
U(T)-U(0)
=2gT^2\int_0^\infty\frac{x\,dx}{e^x+1}
=\frac{\pi^2}{6}gT^2,
$$

where the integral follows from the [fermion-to-boson thermal integral ratio](../../../statistical-physics.md#fermion-to-boson-thermal-integral-ratio). Differentiation gives the [linear low-temperature heat capacity of a two-dimensional Fermi gas](../../../statistical-physics.md#linear-low-temperature-heat-capacity-of-a-two-dimensional-fermi-gas):

$$
\boxed{
C_A=\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}\right)_{N,A}
=\frac{\pi^2}{3}gT
=\frac{\pi^2N}{3\varepsilon_F}T.}
$$

The requested [power law](../../../analysis.md#power-law) is therefore **linear in $T$**, with exponent one.

The [electric-dipole approximation for radiation](../../../electromagnetism.md#electric-dipole-approximation-for-radiation) requires the source size to be much smaller than the radiation wavelength:

$$
\frac{\omega a}{c}\ll1.
$$

The displayed $1/r$ fields also describe the [radiation zone](../../../electromagnetism.md#radiation-zone), so the observation point must satisfy $r\gg a$ and $r\gg c/\omega$.

Write $t_r=t-r/c$. The given vector potential and the [time derivative of the electric dipole moment](../../../electromagnetism.md#time-derivative-of-the-electric-dipole-moment) give

$$
\mathbf A(t,\mathbf x)
\simeq\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi r}\int\mathbf J(t_r,\mathbf x')\,d^3x'
=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi r}\dot{\mathbf p}(t_r).
$$

When taking the [curl](../../../calculus.md#curl), differentiating $1/r$ produces a near-field term of order $r^{-2}$, while differentiating the retarded time produces the leading radiation term because $\nabla t_r=-\widehat{\mathbf x}/c$. Hence

$$
\boxed{
\mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A
\simeq-\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi rc}
\widehat{\mathbf x}\times\ddot{\mathbf p}(t_r).}
$$

The assumed electric field $\mathbf E=-c\widehat{\mathbf x}\times\mathbf B$ and the transversality $\widehat{\mathbf x}\cdot\mathbf B=0$ make the [Poynting vector](../../../electromagnetism.md#poynting-vector)

$$
\mathbf S=\frac1{\mu_0}\mathbf E\times\mathbf B
=\frac{c}{\mu_0}|\mathbf B|^2\widehat{\mathbf x}.
$$

On a sphere of radius $R$, the power through surface element $R^2d\Omega$ is therefore

$$
\boxed{
\frac{dP}{d\Omega}
=R^2\mathbf S\cdot\widehat{\mathbf x}
=\frac{\mu_0}{16\pi^2c}
|\widehat{\mathbf x}\times\ddot{\mathbf p}(t-R/c)|^2.}
$$

Choose the polar axis along $\ddot{\mathbf p}$ and use the [angular distribution of electric dipole radiation](../../../electromagnetism.md#angular-distribution-of-electric-dipole-radiation), for which $|\widehat{\mathbf x}\times\ddot{\mathbf p}|^2=|\ddot{\mathbf p}|^2\sin^2\theta$. Since $\int\sin^2\theta\,d\Omega=8\pi/3$,

$$
\boxed{
P(t)=\frac{\mu_0}{6\pi c}
|\ddot{\mathbf p}(t-R/c)|^2.}
$$

For the charged particle in [simple harmonic motion](../../../classical-mechanics.md#simple-harmonic-motion),

$$
\mathbf p(t)=qA\sin(\omega t)\mathbf i_x,
\qquad
\ddot{\mathbf p}(t)=-qA\omega^2\sin(\omega t)\mathbf i_x.
$$

Thus

$$
\boxed{
P(t)=\frac{\mu_0q^2A^2\omega^4}{6\pi c}
\sin^2\!\bigl(\omega(t-R/c)\bigr),
\qquad
\langle P\rangle=\frac{\mu_0q^2A^2\omega^4}{12\pi c}.}
$$

Here the [angular frequency](../../../classical-mechanics.md#angular-frequency) obeys $\omega=2\pi/T$, and the average uses $\langle\sin^2\rangle=1/2$. The dipole condition is

$$
\boxed{\frac{A\omega}{c}\ll1.}
$$

Allow the amplitude to vary slowly as radiation removes energy. The mechanical energy is

$$
E=\frac12mA^2\omega^2,
$$

so the [radiation damping of a harmonically oscillating charge](../../../electromagnetism.md#radiation-damping-of-a-harmonically-oscillating-charge) gives

$$
\frac{dE}{dt}=-\langle P\rangle
=-\frac{\mu_0q^2\omega^2}{6\pi mc}E.
$$

This is [exponential decay](../../../analysis.md#exponential-decay) with rate $\gamma=\mu_0q^2\omega^2/(6\pi mc)$. Its [half-life](../../../analysis.md#half-life) is

$$
\boxed{t_{1/2}=\frac{6\pi mc\log2}{\mu_0q^2\omega^2}.}
$$

Finally, the largest particle speed is $v_{\max}=A\omega$. Therefore the dipole condition $A\omega/c\ll1$ itself implies $v_{\max}\ll c$, precisely the [nonrelativistic limit](../../../special-relativity.md#nonrelativistic-limit) required by the mechanical energy formula.

Use the continuity equation to write the axial momentum equation in conservative form:

$$

+\frac{\partial}{\partial r}(ruw)
=\nu\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r\frac{\partial w}{\partial r}\right).

Integrating from the axis to infinity, regularity at $r=0$ and decay of $u,w,w_r$ as $r\to\infty$ eliminate the boundary terms. Therefore the [conserved momentum flux of a laminar round jet](../../../fluid-mechanics.md#conserved-momentum-flux-of-a-laminar-round-jet) satisfies

$$
\boxed{\frac{dM}{dz}=0.}
$$

Let $W(z)$ be the centre-line velocity scale and $\delta(z)$ the jet width. Conservation gives $M\sim W^2\delta^2$, hence $W\delta\sim M^{1/2}$. The axial inertial and viscous terms scale as

$$
\frac{W^2}{z}\sim\frac{\nu W}{\delta^2},
$$

so $W\delta^2\sim\nu z$. Combining the two relations gives

$$
\boxed{\delta(z)\sim\frac{\nu z}{M^{1/2}},\qquad W(z)\sim\frac{M}{\nu z}.}
$$

Thus the width grows linearly and the centre-line speed decays as $z^{-1}$. The volume flux scales as

$$
Q(z)=\int_0^\infty rw\,dr\sim W\delta^2\sim\nu z,
$$

so it increases downstream. By [fluid entrainment](../../../fluid-mechanics.md#fluid-entrainment), the extra flux must be ambient fluid drawn radially into the jet.

<h3 id="38a/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [38A](#38a)

<h4 id="38a/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#38a/b)

Use the axisymmetric streamfunction convention

$$
w=\frac1r\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial r},
\qquad
u=-\frac1r\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial z}.
$$

For the [similarity solution](../../../partial-differential-equation.md#similarity-solution) $\psi=\nu z g(\eta)$, $\eta=r/z$, regularity on the axis requires $g(0)=g'(0)=0$, while matching to still ambient fluid requires $g'(\eta)/\eta\to0$ as $\eta\to\infty$. The given equation has the first integral

$$
\eta g'-2g+\frac12g^2=C.
$$

The axis conditions give $C=0$, and separation yields

$$
\frac{dg}{g(4-g)}=\frac{d\eta}{2\eta},
\qquad
g(\eta)=\frac{4a\eta^2}{1+a\eta^2}
$$

for some $a>0$.

The axial velocity is

$$
w=\frac{\nu}{\eta z}g'(\eta)
=\frac{8\nu a}{z(1+a\eta^2)^2}.
$$

Normalizing by the conserved momentum flux gives

$$
M=\int_0^\infty rw^2\,dr
=64\nu^2a^2\int_0^\infty\frac{\eta\,d\eta}{(1+a\eta^2)^4}
=\frac{32}{3}\nu^2a,
$$

so $a=3M/(32\nu^2)$. The [similarity solution for a laminar round jet](../../../fluid-mechanics.md#similarity-solution-for-a-laminar-round-jet) is therefore

$$
\boxed{g(\eta)=\frac{12M\eta^2}{32\nu^2+3M\eta^2}.}
$$

## 39A

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="39a/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [39A](#39a)

<h4 id="39a/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#39a/a)

The [linear homentropic acoustic equations](../../../linear-acoustics.md#linear-homentropic-acoustic-equations) are

$$
\rho'_t+\rho_0\nabla\mathbin\cdot\mathbf u=0,
\qquad
\rho_0\mathbf u_t=-\nabla p',
\qquad
p'=c_0^2\rho'.
$$

Differentiate the first equation in time, take the divergence of the second, and use the third. This eliminates $\rho'$ and $\mathbf u$ and gives the [acoustic pressure wave equation](../../../linear-acoustics.md#acoustic-pressure-wave-equation)

$$
\boxed{p'_{tt}=c_0^2\nabla^2p'.}
$$

<h3 id="39a/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [39A](#39a)

<h4 id="39a/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#39a/b)

For a progressive [plane wave](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#plane-wave), write

$$
p'=P e^{i(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x-\omega t)},
\qquad
\mathbf u=U\widehat{\mathbf k}e^{i(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x-\omega t)}.
$$

The momentum equation gives $\rho_0\omega U=kP$, while the [dispersion relation](../../../wave-equation.md#dispersion-relation) is $\omega=c_0k$. Therefore the pressure and longitudinal velocity amplitudes obey

$$
\boxed{\frac{P}{U}=\rho_0c_0=:Z.}
$$

The constant $Z$ is the [acoustic impedance](../../../continuum-mechanics.md#acoustic-impedance).

<h3 id="39a/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [39A](#39a)

<h4 id="39a/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#39a/c)

Let $Z_j=\rho_jc_j$, $k_j=\omega/c_j$, and $\phi=k_1L$. Use the harmonic convention $e^{-i\omega t}$ and write the pressure and velocity amplitudes as

$$
\begin{array}{lll}
x<0:&p=e^{ik_0x}+Re^{-ik_0x},&u=Z_0^{-1}(e^{ik_0x}-Re^{-ik_0x}),\\
0<x<L:&p=Ae^{ik_1x}+Be^{-ik_1x},&u=Z_1^{-1}(Ae^{ik_1x}-Be^{-ik_1x}),\\
x>L:&p=Te^{ik_0(x-L)},&u=Z_0^{-1}Te^{ik_0(x-L)}.
\end{array}
$$

Continuity of pressure and normal velocity at both interfaces is equivalently the layer transfer relation

$$
\binom{p(0)}{u(0)}=
\begin{pmatrix}
\cos\phi&-iZ_1\sin\phi\\
-iZ_1^{-1}\sin\phi&\cos\phi
\end{pmatrix}
\binom{p(L)}{u(L)}.
$$

Substituting $p(0)=1+R$, $u(0)=(1-R)/Z_0$, $p(L)=T$, and $u(L)=T/Z_0$, then adding the two resulting equations, gives

$$
2=T\left[2\cos\phi-i\left(\lambda+\lambda^{-1}\right)\sin\phi\right],
\qquad
\lambda=\frac{Z_1}{Z_0}.
$$

Hence the [acoustic transmission through a uniform layer](../../../continuum-mechanics.md#acoustic-transmission-through-a-uniform-layer) is

$$
\boxed{|T|=\left[\cos^2(k_1L)+\frac14(\lambda+\lambda^{-1})^2\sin^2(k_1L)\right]^{-1/2}.}
$$

For either $\lambda\ll1$ or $\lambda\gg1$, the strong [acoustic impedance](../../../continuum-mechanics.md#acoustic-impedance) mismatch makes transmission small except near $k_1L=n\pi$, where $|T|=1$. Away from those resonances,

$$
|T|\sim\frac{2\lambda}{|\sin k_1L|}\quad(\lambda\ll1),
\qquad
|T|\sim\frac{2}{\lambda|\sin k_1L|}\quad(\lambda\gg1).
$$

At the antiresonant points $k_1L=(n+\tfrac12)\pi$, the minimum is $2/(\lambda+\lambda^{-1})$.

## 40C

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="40c/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [40C](#40c)

<h4 id="40c/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#40c/a)

Let $U_m^n=u(mh,nk)$ be the sampled exact solution and let $A_h$ denote the update matrix of the [Forward Euler diffusion scheme](../../../finite-difference.md#forward-euler-diffusion-scheme). For $0\leq\mu\leq1/2$,

$$
(A_hv)_m=\mu v_{m-1}+(1-2\mu)v_m+\mu v_{m+1}
$$

is a convex combination, with the homogeneous boundary values included. Therefore

$$
\|A_hv\|_\infty\leq\|v\|_\infty.
$$

This is the parabolic [discrete maximum principle](../../../finite-difference.md#discrete-maximum-principle) and proves max-norm stability directly.

The [Taylor theorem](../../../calculus.md#taylor-theorem) and the [second-order central difference](../../../finite-difference.md#second-order-central-difference) give the exact-grid residual

$$
U^{n+1}=A_hU^n+k\tau^n,
\qquad
\|\tau^n\|_\infty\leq C(k+h^2)
$$

for a sufficiently smooth solution on a fixed interval $0\leq t\leq T$. If $e^n=U^n-u^n$, then

$$
\|e^{n+1}\|_\infty\leq\|e^n\|_\infty+k\|\tau^n\|_\infty.
$$

Iteration for $nk\leq T$ yields

$$
\boxed{\|e^n\|_\infty\leq\|e^0\|_\infty+CT(k+h^2)\longrightarrow0.}
$$

**Thus $\mu\leq1/2$ implies convergence when the initial grid values converge to the initial data.**

<h3 id="40c/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [40C](#40c)

<h4 id="40c/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#40c/b)

Let $L=[1,-2,1]$ be the symmetric Dirichlet second-difference matrix on the $M$ interior points. The method is

$$
\left(aI-\frac{\mu-c}{4}L\right)u^{n+1}
=\left(aI+\frac{\mu+c}{4}L\right)u^n.
$$

The orthogonal sine vectors diagonalize $L$, with eigenvalues

$$
\ell_j=-4s_j,
\qquad
s_j=\sin^2\left(\frac{j\pi}{2(M+1)}\right),
\qquad j=1,\ldots,M.
$$

Because the two time-level matrices are real symmetric polynomials in the same symmetric matrix, they share this orthonormal eigenbasis. Whenever the left matrix is invertible, the amplification matrix is therefore normal, so its Euclidean operator norm is the largest modulus of its eigenvalues. The $j$th [amplification factor](../../../finite-difference.md#amplification-factor) is

$$
G_j=\frac{a-(\mu+c)s_j}{a+(\mu-c)s_j}.
$$

For the physical Courant number $\mu>0$,

$$
|a+(\mu-c)s|^2-|a-(\mu+c)s|^2
=4\mu s(a-cs).
$$

Hence $|G_j|\leq1$ exactly when $a-cs_j\geq0$. Requiring this uniformly for every mesh, whose values $s_j$ become dense in $(0,1)$, gives

$$
a-cs\geq0\quad(0\leq s\leq1).
$$

Equivalently, $a\geq0$ and $c\leq a$. Under these inequalities the denominator is nonzero for every $0<s<1$: if $c\leq\mu$ this is immediate, while if $c>\mu$ then $a\geq c$ gives $a+(\mu-c)s\geq a+\mu-c\geq\mu>0$. Thus the necessary and sufficient mesh-uniform stability conditions are

$$
\boxed{\mu>0,\qquad a\geq0,\qquad c\leq a.}
$$

This is the [stability of a two-parameter implicit-explicit diffusion scheme](../../../finite-difference.md#stability-of-a-two-parameter-implicit-explicit-diffusion-scheme). If consistency with the stated diffusion equation is also required, comparison of the leading Taylor terms gives $ak u_t=(k/2)u_{xx}+o(k)$, so $a=1/2$ and the stable consistent family has $c\leq1/2$.
