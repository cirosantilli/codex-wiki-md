## 1I

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 2](paper-2.md)

<h3 id="1i/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1I](#1i)

If the positive [odd integer](../../../number-theory.md#odd-integer) $n$ has [prime factorization](../../../number-theory.md#fundamental-theorem-of-arithmetic)

$$
n=\prod_{r=1}^s p_r^{e_r},
$$

its [Jacobi symbol](../../../number-theory.md#jacobi-symbol) is

$$
\left(\frac an\right)=\prod_{r=1}^s\left(\frac a{p_r}\right)^{e_r},
$$

where each factor is a [Legendre symbol](../../../number-theory.md#legendre-symbol); by convention $(a/1)=1$. Thus the symbol is zero exactly when $\gcd(a,n)>1$.

The [quadratic reciprocity](../../../number-theory.md#quadratic-reciprocity) law for the [Jacobi symbol](../../../number-theory.md#jacobi-symbol) says that for coprime positive odd integers $m,n$,

$$
\boxed{\left(\frac mn\right)\left(\frac nm\right)=(-1)^{(m-1)(n-1)/4}.}
$$

To prove it, write $m=\prod_i p_i^{e_i}$ and $n=\prod_jq_j^{f_j}$. The [quadratic reciprocity](../../../number-theory.md#quadratic-reciprocity) law for distinct odd primes states

$$
\left(\frac{p_i}{q_j}\right)\left(\frac{q_j}{p_i}\right)
=(-1)^{(p_i-1)(q_j-1)/4}.
$$

Multiplying this identity over $i,j$ with multiplicities $e_i f_j$ gives the required product of [Jacobi symbols](../../../number-theory.md#jacobi-symbol). Its sign exponent modulo two is

$$
\left(\sum_i e_i\frac{p_i-1}{2}\right)
\left(\sum_j f_j\frac{q_j-1}{2}\right)
\equiv \frac{m-1}{2}\frac{n-1}{2}\pmod2,
$$

because multiplication of odd integers adds the quantities $(r-1)/2$ modulo two. This proves the law.

For the requested value, two applications of reciprocity give

$$
\left(\frac{503}{2019}\right)
=-\left(\frac7{503}\right)
=\left(\frac6{7}\right)
=-1,
$$

since $2019\equiv7\pmod{503}$, $503\equiv6\pmod7$, and $6$ is a [quadratic nonresidue](../../../number-theory.md#quadratic-nonresidue) modulo $7$.

## 2H

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 2](paper-2.md)

<h3 id="2h/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2H](#2h)

<h4 id="2h/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#2h/a)

The sum is nonempty because $A$ and $B$ are nonempty. It is a [bounded set](../../../topological-analysis.md#bounded-set): if $|a|\leq M_A$ and $|b|\leq M_B$, then $|a+b|\leq M_A+M_B$.

To prove that it is a [closed set](../../../topology.md#closed-set), let $x_j=a_j+b_j\in A+B$ be a [convergent sequence](../../../real-analysis.md#convergent-sequence) with $x_j\to x$. By the [Heine-Borel theorem](../../../topology.md#heine-borel-theorem), $A$ is a [compact set](../../../topology.md#compact-space), so $(a_j)$ has a [convergent subsequence](../../../real-analysis.md#convergent-subsequence) $a_{j_r}\to a\in A$. Then

$$
b_{j_r}=x_{j_r}-a_{j_r}\longrightarrow x-a.
$$

Since $B$ is closed, $x-a\in B$, and hence $x=a+(x-a)\in A+B$. Therefore $A+B$ is nonempty, closed and bounded, so $A+B\in\mathcal K$.

<h3 id="2h/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2H](#2h)

<h4 id="2h/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2h/b)

Every $K_n$ is contained in the bounded set $K_1$, so the [intersection](../../../set.md#set-intersection) $K=\bigcap_{n\geq1}K_n$ is bounded. It is closed because an arbitrary intersection of [closed sets](../../../topology.md#closed-set) is closed.

It remains to prove that $K$ is nonempty. Choose $x_n\in K_n$. The set $K_1$ is a [compact set](../../../topology.md#compact-space), so some [subsequence](../../../real-analysis.md#subsequence) $x_{n_j}$ converges to a point $x\in K_1$. For each fixed $m$, all sufficiently late terms satisfy $n_j\geq m$ and hence $x_{n_j}\in K_{n_j}\subseteq K_m$. Since $K_m$ is closed, $x\in K_m$. This holds for every $m$, so $x\in K$. Thus $K\in\mathcal K$.

<h3 id="2h/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2H](#2h)

<h4 id="2h/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#2h/c)

Because $K\subseteq K_n$, one of the two directed terms in the given [Hausdorff distance](../../../topological-analysis.md#hausdorff-distance) vanishes, and

$$
\rho(K_n,K)=\sup_{x\in K_n}\inf_{y\in K}|x-y|.
$$

Suppose this does not tend to zero. Then some $\varepsilon>0$, a strictly increasing sequence $(n_j)$ and points $x_j\in K_{n_j}$ satisfy

$$
\inf_{y\in K}|x_j-y|\geq\varepsilon.
$$

All $x_j$ lie in the compact set $K_1$, so after passing to a subsequence, $x_j\to x$. For every fixed $m$, eventually $n_j\geq m$, so $x_j\in K_m$ and the closedness of $K_m$ gives $x\in K_m$. Hence $x\in K$, contradicting $|x_j-x|\to0$. Therefore $\rho(K_n,K)\to0$.

## 3G

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 2](paper-2.md)

<h3 id="3g/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3G](#3g)

Let $n=2^l-1$ and let $H$ be the $l\times n$ [parity-check matrix](../../../coding-theory.md#parity-check-matrix) whose columns are the distinct nonzero vectors of the [finite field](../../../algebra.md#finite-field) vector space $\mathbb F_2^l$. The binary [Hamming code](../../../coding-theory.md#hamming-code) is

$$
C=\ker H=\{x\in\mathbb F_2^n:Hx^T=0\}.
$$

A [perfect code](../../../coding-theory.md#perfect-code) has [Hamming balls](../../../coding-theory.md#hamming-ball) of radius $t=\lfloor(d-1)/2\rfloor$ about its codewords partitioning the whole ambient space, where $d$ is its [minimum Hamming distance of a linear code](../../../coding-theory.md#minimum-hamming-distance-of-a-linear-code).

No column of $H$ is zero and no two columns agree, so $C$ has no word of [Hamming weight](../../../coding-theory.md#hamming-weight) one or two. Three suitable columns sum to zero, so $d=3$. For any received word $y$, its [syndrome](../../../coding-theory.md#syndrome) $Hy^T$ is either zero or is the unique column $h_i$ of $H$ equal to that syndrome. In the first case $y\in C$; in the second,

$$
H(y+e_i)^T=Hy^T+h_i=0,
$$

so $y$ is at [Hamming distance](../../../coding-theory.md#hamming-distance) one from a codeword. Uniqueness follows from $d=3$. Thus the radius-one balls partition $\mathbb F_2^n$, and the code is perfect.

For $l=3$, the [dual code](../../../coding-theory.md#dual-code) is the [binary simplex code](../../../coding-theory.md#binary-simplex-code) of length seven. Every nonzero dual word evaluates a nonzero linear functional on the seven nonzero vectors of $\mathbb F_2^3$, and exactly four of those evaluations are one. Hence every nonzero word of $C^\perp$ has weight

$$
\boxed{4}.
$$

## 4H

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 2](paper-2.md)

<h3 id="4h/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4H](#4h)

<h4 id="4h/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#4h/a)

A [recursive set](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#computable-set) $E\subseteq\mathbb N_0$ has an [indicator function](../../../measure-theory.md#indicator-function) computed by a [register machine](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#register-machine) that halts on every input. A [recursively enumerable set](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#recursively-enumerable-set) is the exact halting set of some register machine; equivalently, it is the domain of a [partial computable function](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#computable-function).

If $E$ is recursive, its decider immediately gives machines that halt exactly on $E$ and on $\mathbb N_0\setminus E$, so both sets are recursively enumerable. Conversely, suppose machines $M_E$ and $M_{E^c}$ recognize the two sets. On input $x$, use [dovetailing](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#dovetailing) to run one step of $M_E(x)$ and one step of $M_{E^c}(x)$ alternately. Exactly one must eventually halt. Return one if $M_E$ halts first and zero if $M_{E^c}$ halts first. This is a total decider for $E$, so $E$ is recursive.

<h3 id="4h/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4H](#4h)

<h4 id="4h/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#4h/b)

Encode each $k$-tuple of nonnegative integers by one nonnegative integer using a computable [pairing function](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#pairing-function). Define a unary partial procedure $P$ on input $m$ as follows: use [dovetailing](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#dovetailing) on the computations of $f_{n,k}$ over all encoded $k$-tuples, and halt as soon as one of them halts with output $m$. Then

$$
P(m)\downarrow
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\exists(m_1,\ldots,m_k)\in\mathbb N_0^k:
f_{n,k}(m_1,\ldots,m_k)=m.
$$

By the [Church–Turing thesis](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#church-turing-thesis), this effective procedure is implemented by a register machine with some fixed code $j$, so

$$
E=\{m\in\mathbb N_0:f_{j,1}(m)\downarrow\}.
$$

**Thus $E$ is the domain of a [partial computable function](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#computable-function) and is recursively enumerable.**

<h3 id="4h/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4H](#4h)

<h4 id="4h/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#4h/c)

The function is the [predecessor function](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#predecessor-function). It has the [primitive recursion](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#primitive-recursion) definition

$$
f(0)=0,
\qquad
f(n+1)=n.
$$

The initial function is the zero function and the recursion step is a projection function, both of which are [primitive recursive functions](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#primitive-recursive-function). Closure under primitive recursion therefore proves that $f$ is primitive recursive.

## 5J

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 2](paper-2.md)

<h3 id="5j/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [5J](#5j)

<h4 id="5j/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#5j/a)

For participants with the same recorded history, the first [linear regression](../../../linear-regression.md) estimates that one additional average mile cycled per week is associated with $0.04917$ fewer asthma episodes. The corresponding $p$-value is $0.00761$, giving evidence against a zero conditional association.

The second model estimates that, among participants with the same history, receiving the incentive is associated with $0.91387$ more episodes. The third estimates that receiving the incentive is associated with $1.73282$ more miles cycled per week. Both incentive coefficients have very small [p-values](../../../statistical-modelling.md#p-value). Because the incentive was assigned in a [randomized controlled trial](../../../causal-inference.md#randomized-controlled-trial), these last two comparisons estimate a [causal effect](../../../causal-inference.md#causal-effect) of the incentive.

<h3 id="5j/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [5J](#5j)

<h4 id="5j/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#5j/b)

The first model alone would suggest that more cycling is beneficial, but cycling was not randomized in that model: its coefficient can be distorted by [confounding](../../../causal-inference.md#confounding) or [reverse causality](../../../causal-inference.md#reverse-causality). The randomized incentive both substantially increased cycling and substantially increased asthma episodes.

If the incentive affects asthma only through cycling, it is an [instrumental variable](../../../causal-inference.md#instrumental-variable), and the corresponding [Wald estimator](../../../causal-inference.md#wald-estimator) of the effect per additional weekly mile is

$$
\frac{0.91387}{1.73282}\approx0.527
$$

additional episodes. Under that exclusion assumption the evidence therefore advises against cycling to work more often for this population. Without the exclusion assumption, the experiment establishes the harmful effect of the incentive itself but cannot by itself distinguish an effect of cycling from a direct effect of the monetary incentive.

## 6C

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 2](paper-2.md)

<h3 id="6c/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [6C](#6c)

At a positive [spatially homogeneous equilibrium](../../../diffusion-equation.md#spatially-homogeneous-equilibrium),

$$
uv^2=a,
\qquad
v-uv^2=0,
$$

so

$$
(u_*,v_*)=\left(\frac1a,a\right).
$$

The [Jacobian matrix](../../../calculus.md#jacobian-matrix) of the reaction terms there is

$$
J=\begin{pmatrix}a^2&2\\-a^2&-1\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\operatorname{tr}J=a^2-1,
\qquad
\det J=a^2.
$$

The [linear stability analysis](../../../dynamical-systems.md#linear-stability) of a two-dimensional system requires negative trace and positive determinant. Since $a>0$, the homogeneous equilibrium is stable exactly when

$$
\boxed{0<a<1}.
$$

For a spatial [Fourier mode](../../../fourier-analysis.md#fourier-mode) proportional to $\cos(kx)$, put $q=k^2$. The linearized matrix is

$$
J_q=J-q\begin{pmatrix}D&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Its trace is still negative when $0<a<1$, while

$$
\det J_q=Dq^2+(D-a^2)q+a^2.
$$

A [Turing instability](../../../diffusion-equation.md#turing-instability) occurs for some $q>0$ exactly when this quadratic has a negative minimum. This requires

$$
D<a^2,
\qquad
(a^2-D)^2>4Da^2.
$$

Writing $d=\sqrt D$, the second inequality reduces to $d<a(\sqrt2-1)$. Hence, for continuously available wave numbers, the condition is

$$
\boxed{D<a^2(\sqrt2-1)^2=a^2(3-2\sqrt2).}
$$

On a bounded spatial domain, one additionally requires an allowed wave number $k$ for which $\det J_{k^2}<0$.

## 7A

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 2](paper-2.md)

<h3 id="7a/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [7A](#7a)

Apply the [residue theorem](../../../analysis.md#residue-theorem) to the upper-half-plane semicircle, with a small semicircular indentation above the pole at $z=0$. The large arc contributes zero because $f(z)/z\to0$, so

$$
\frac{f(z)}{z(z^2+a^2)}=o(|z|^{-2}).
$$

The small indentation is traversed clockwise and contributes $-i\pi$ times the [residue](../../../analysis.md#residue) at zero. The only pole strictly inside the indented contour is $z=ia$. Therefore

$$
\operatorname{PV}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{f(x)}{x(x^2+a^2)}\,dx
=2\pi i\operatorname{Res}_{z=ia}\frac{f(z)}{z(z^2+a^2)}
+i\pi\operatorname{Res}_{z=0}\frac{f(z)}{z(z^2+a^2)}.
$$

The two residues are

$$
\operatorname{Res}_{z=ia}=-\frac{f(ia)}{2a^2},
\qquad
\operatorname{Res}_{z=0}=\frac{f(0)}{a^2}.
$$

Consequently the [Cauchy principal value](../../../complex-analysis.md#cauchy-principal-value) is

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{PV}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{f(x)}{x(x^2+a^2)}\,dx
=\frac{i\pi}{a^2}\bigl(f(0)-f(ia)\bigr).}
$$

<h4 id="8e/d/solution">Solution</h4>

$$
\frac{df}{dt}
=\sum_i\left(
\frac{\partial f}{\partial q_i}\dot q_i
+\frac{\partial f}{\partial p_i}\dot p_i
\right)+\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}
=\sum_i\left(
\frac{\partial f}{\partial q_i}\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}
-\frac{\partial f}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i}
\right)+\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}.
$$

The sum is the [Poisson bracket](../../../classical-mechanics.md#poisson-bracket) $\{f,H\}$, giving

$$
\boxed{\frac{df}{dt}=\{f,H\}+\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}.}

<h3 id="8e/e">e</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [8E](#8e)

<h4 id="8e/e/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [E](#8e/e)

Let $f$ and $g$ be [conserved quantities](../../../classical-mechanics.md#conserved-quantity), allowing explicit time dependence. Then

$$
\partial_t f=-\{f,H\},
\qquad
\partial_t g=-\{g,H\}.
$$

Using bilinearity of the [Poisson bracket](../../../classical-mechanics.md#poisson-bracket), commutation of partial differentiation with it, and the [Jacobi identity for the Poisson bracket](../../../classical-mechanics.md#jacobi-identity-for-the-poisson-bracket),

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac d{dt}\{f,g\}
&=\{\{f,g\},H\}+\{\partial_t f,g\}+\{f,\partial_t g\}\\
&=\{\{f,g\},H\}-\{\{f,H\},g\}-\{f,\{g,H\}\}=0.
\end{aligned}
$$

**Thus $\{f,g\}$ is also conserved.**

<h3 id="10d/b">b</h3>

## 12G

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 2](paper-2.md)

<h3 id="12g/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [12G](#12g)

The binary [Huffman coding](../../../information-theory.md#huffman-coding) algorithm repeatedly merges the two currently least probable symbols into one meta-symbol whose probability is their sum. After recursively constructing a code for the smaller alphabet, append $0$ and $1$ to the meta-symbol's codeword to obtain codewords for the merged pair. The result is a binary [prefix code](../../../coding-theory.md#prefix-code).

To prove optimality, begin with an optimal prefix-code tree. It may be taken to be full, since an internal vertex with one child can be suppressed and all codewords below it shortened. Two leaves of maximum depth are siblings. By exchanging labels, without changing lengths, assign two least probable symbols to such a sibling pair: moving a smaller probability to a no-shorter codeword cannot increase the expected length. Contracting this pair to one leaf replaces their contribution $p_i\ell_i+p_j\ell_j$ by $(p_i+p_j)(\ell_i-1)$ plus the constant $p_i+p_j$. If the contracted code were not optimal for the reduced alphabet, replacing it by a better one and then expanding the merged leaf would improve the original code. [Mathematical induction](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#mathematical-induction) on the number of symbols now proves that the Huffman construction minimizes expected word length.

## 13A

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 2](paper-2.md)

<h3 id="13a/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [13A](#13a)

<h4 id="13a/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#13a/a)

Take the [Hankel contour](../../../complex-analysis.md#hankel-contour) about the negative real axis: it starts at $-\infty$ just below the cut, circles the origin counterclockwise, and returns to $-\infty$ just above the cut. The notation $(0+)$ records this return side of the branch point. Choose the branch $-\pi<\arg t<\pi$ for $t^{z-1}$.

For $\operatorname{Re}z>1$, collapse the contour onto the two banks of the cut. Their phase difference is determined by

$$
(-x)^{z-1}=x^{z-1}e^{\pm i\pi(z-1)},
$$

and the resulting sine factor, together with the [Gamma reflection formula](../../../complex-analysis.md#gamma-reflection-formula) $\Gamma(z)\Gamma(1-z)=\pi/\sin(\pi z)$, recovers the integral representation (‡). The Hankel integral converges exponentially at its unbounded end and, after the standard local subtraction of the singular part at zero, depends holomorphically on $z$. The apparent poles of $\Gamma(1-z)$ are cancelled by zeros of the contour integral except at $z=1$. It therefore gives the [analytic continuation](../../../complex-analysis.md#analytic-continuation) of the [Riemann zeta function](../../../analytic-number-theory.md#riemann-zeta-function) to $\mathbb C\setminus\{1\}$.

<h3 id="13a/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [13A](#13a)

<h4 id="13a/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#13a/b)

For $n\ne0$, the denominator has derivative

$$
\left.\frac d{dt}(e^{-t}-1)\right|_{t=2\pi i n}=-e^{-2\pi i n}=-1.
$$

Hence the simple-pole [residue](../../../analysis.md#residue) is

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Res}_{t=2\pi i n}\frac{t^{-z}}{e^{-t}-1}=-(2\pi i n)^{-z}.}
$$

<h3 id="13a/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [13A](#13a)

<h4 id="13a/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#13a/c)

Apply the Hankel representation with $z$ replaced by $1-z$:

$$
\zeta(1-z)=\frac{\Gamma(z)}{2\pi i}
\int_H\frac{t^{-z}}{e^{-t}-1}\,dt.
$$

Enlarge $H$ to a modified Hankel contour that encloses the simple poles $t=2\pi i n$, $n\in\mathbb Z\setminus\{0\}$. In a strip where the deformation and residue sum converge, the outer pieces vanish and the orientation gives the Hankel integral as minus $2\pi i$ times the sum of the enclosed residues. Part (b) gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{n\ne0}\operatorname{Res}_{t=2\pi i n}
\frac{t^{-z}}{e^{-t}-1}
&=-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[(2\pi i n)^{-z}+(-2\pi i n)^{-z}\right]\\
&=-2(2\pi)^{-z}\cos\!\left(\frac{\pi z}{2}\right)\zeta(z).
\end{aligned}
$$

Substitution therefore yields

$$
\boxed{\zeta(1-z)=2^{1-z}\pi^{-z}
\cos\!\left(\frac{\pi z}{2}\right)\Gamma(z)\zeta(z).}
$$

Both sides are meromorphic functions of $z$, so the [identity theorem](../../../complex-analysis.md#identity-theorem) extends the equality from the initial strip to every point where either side is defined.

## 14E

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 2](paper-2.md)

<h3 id="14e/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [14E](#14e)

<h4 id="14e/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#14e/a)

The canonical momentum is

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{\mathbf r}}=m\dot{\mathbf r}+q\mathbf A.
$$

In components, the [Euler-Lagrange equation](../../../analysis.md#euler-lagrange-equation) is

$$
m\ddot r_i+q\left(\partial_tA_i+\dot r_j\partial_jA_i\right)
=q\left(-\partial_i\phi+\dot r_j\partial_iA_j\right).
$$

Thus

$$
m\ddot r_i=q\left[-\partial_i\phi-\partial_tA_i
+\dot r_j(\partial_iA_j-\partial_jA_i)\right].
$$

The last term is the $i$th component of $\mathbf v\times(\nabla\times\mathbf A)$, so

$$
\boxed{m\ddot{\mathbf r}=q(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B),}
$$

the [Lorentz force law](../../../electromagnetism.md#lorentz-force).

<h3 id="14e/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [14E](#14e)

<h4 id="14e/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#14e/b)

The [canonical momentum](../../../classical-mechanics.md#canonical-momentum) is $\mathbf p=m\dot{\mathbf r}+q\mathbf A$, and the [Legendre transform](../../../convex-optimization.md#convex-conjugate) gives

$$
H=\mathbf p\cdot\dot{\mathbf r}-L
=\frac{|\mathbf p-q\mathbf A|^2}{2m}+q\phi.
$$

Its [Hamilton's equations](../../../classical-mechanics.md#hamilton-s-equations) are

$$
\boxed{\dot{\mathbf r}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial\mathbf p}
=\frac{\mathbf p-q\mathbf A}{m}}
$$

and, componentwise,

$$
\boxed{\dot p_i=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial r_i}
=q\dot r_j\partial_iA_j-q\partial_i\phi.}
$$

Combining the second equation with $\mathbf p=m\dot{\mathbf r}+q\mathbf A$ recovers the [Lorentz force law](../../../electromagnetism.md#lorentz-force).

<h3 id="14e/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [14E](#14e)

<h4 id="14e/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#14e/c)

Under the [gauge transformation](../../../electromagnetism.md#gauge-transformation),

$$
\widetilde{\mathbf B}=\nabla\times(\mathbf A+\nabla f)=\mathbf B
$$

because the [curl](../../../calculus.md#curl) of a [gradient](../../../calculus.md#gradient) vanishes, and

$$
\widetilde{\mathbf E}
=-\nabla(\phi-\partial_tf)-\partial_t(\mathbf A+\nabla f)
=\mathbf E.
$$

The transformed [Lagrangian](../../../calculus-of-variations.md#lagrangian) is

$$
\widetilde L=L+q(\partial_tf+\dot{\mathbf r}\cdot\nabla f)
=L+\frac d{dt}(qf).
$$

A total time derivative changes the action only by endpoint terms, whose variation vanishes for fixed endpoints, so the [Euler-Lagrange equations](../../../analysis.md#euler-lagrange-equation) are unchanged.

The new momentum is

$$
\widetilde{\mathbf p}=m\dot{\mathbf r}+q\widetilde{\mathbf A}
=\mathbf p+q\nabla f.
$$

Use the [type-two generating function for a canonical transformation](../../../classical-mechanics.md#type-two-generating-function-for-a-canonical-transformation)

$$
F_2(\mathbf r,\widetilde{\mathbf p},t)
=\mathbf r\cdot\widetilde{\mathbf p}-qf(\mathbf r,t).
$$

It gives $\mathbf p=\partial F_2/\partial\mathbf r=\widetilde{\mathbf p}-q\nabla f$, $\widetilde{\mathbf r}=\partial F_2/\partial\widetilde{\mathbf p}=\mathbf r$, and

$$
\widetilde H=H+\partial_tF_2=H-q\partial_tf.
$$

This equals $|\widetilde{\mathbf p}-q\widetilde{\mathbf A}|^2/(2m)+q\widetilde\phi$, so the gauge change is a time-dependent [canonical transformation](../../../classical-mechanics.md#canonical-transformation).

<h4 id="15d/a/solution">Solution</h4>

The operator $D$ vanishes on the orthogonal complement of $S=\operatorname{span}\{|\alpha_0\rangle,|\alpha_1\rangle\}$, so it has rank at most two. The states are distinct rays, hence they are linearly independent and $S$ has dimension two. In an orthonormal basis of $S$, its determinant is

**Thus its two eigenvalues on $S$ are nonzero and have opposite signs. All remaining eigenvalues are zero.**

## 16I

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 2](paper-2.md)

<h3 id="16i/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [16I](#16i)

The inductive definition of [ordinal addition](../../../set-theory.md#ordinal-addition) is the [transfinite recursion](../../../set-theory.md#transfinite-recursion)

$$
\alpha+0=\alpha,
\qquad
\alpha+(\beta+1)=(\alpha+\beta)+1,
\qquad
\alpha+\lambda=\sup_{\gamma<\lambda}(\alpha+\gamma)
$$

for every nonzero [limit ordinal](../../../set-theory.md#limit-ordinal) $\lambda$. The synthetic definition takes disjoint [well-orders](../../../set.md#well-order) $A$ and $B$ of order types $\alpha$ and $\beta$, puts every member of $A$ before every member of $B$, and defines $\alpha+\beta$ to be the order type of this ordered sum.

These definitions agree by [transfinite induction](../../../set-theory.md#transfinite-induction) on $\beta$. The empty second order contributes nothing; adjoining a greatest element gives the successor clause; and a limit well-order is the union of all of its proper initial segments, so its ordered sum with $A$ has order type $\sup_{\gamma<\lambda}(\alpha+\gamma)$.

↑ **Parent:** [16I](#16i)

$$

## 17G

<h3 id="17g/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [17G](#17g)

<h4 id="17g/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#17g/a)

Choose a vertex $v$ of $K_6$. At least three of its five incident edges have one colour, say blue, by the [pigeonhole principle](../../../algebra.md#pigeonhole-principle). Let their other endpoints be $x,y,z$. If one of $xy,yz,zx$ is blue, it completes a blue triangle with $v$; if none is blue, $xyz$ is a yellow triangle. Thus every colouring of $K_6$ has a monochromatic triangle.

The statement fails for $K_5$. Colour the edges of a five-cycle blue and its complementary five-cycle yellow. Neither colour contains a triangle.

<h3 id="17g/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [17G](#17g)

<h4 id="17g/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#17g/b)

Use the two-colour [matching Ramsey number](../../../ramsey-theory.md#matching-ramsey-number)

$$
R(rK_2,sK_2)=2r+s-1
\qquad(r\geq s).
$$

Its upper bound follows by induction on $s$. If there is a blue edge, remove its endpoints and apply the induction hypothesis to the remaining $K_{2r+s-3}$: it contains either a red $r$-edge matching or a blue $(s-1)$-edge matching, and in the latter case the removed edge completes a blue $s$-edge matching. If there is no blue edge, the graph itself contains a red $r$-edge matching. For $r=s=t$, this gives the required monochromatic matching in $K_{3t-1}$.

The result never remains true for $K_{3t-2}$, including $t=1$. Partition its vertices into $A$ and $B$ with $|A|=2t-1$ and $|B|=t-1$. Colour edges within $A$ blue and every other edge yellow. A blue matching has at most $t-1$ edges, while every yellow edge meets $B$, so a yellow matching also has at most $t-1$ edges.

<h3 id="17g/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [17G](#17g)

<h4 id="17g/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#17g/c)

Let $B=\{b_1,b_2,b_3\}$ be a blue triangle and $Y=\{y_1,y_2,y_3\}$ a vertex-disjoint yellow triangle. If some $b_i$ has two yellow neighbours in $Y$, those three vertices form a yellow triangle meeting $B$. Otherwise every $b_i$ has at least two blue neighbours in $Y$, giving at least six blue cross-edges. Some $y_j$ then has two blue neighbours in $B$, and these form a blue triangle meeting $Y$. This proves the overlap assertion.

The [triangle-packing Ramsey number](../../../ramsey-theory.md#triangle-packing-ramsey-number) is

$$
R(tK_3,tK_3)=5t\qquad(t\geq2).
$$

For completeness, its induction starts from the stated $K_{10}$ result. At each step, remove two disjoint monochromatic triangles supplied on ten currently unused vertices. If their colour disagrees with the packing already constructed, apply the overlap assertion to an oppositely coloured pair; replacing that pair by the overlapping pair is the augmenting move that shifts the boundary between the two packings. Repeating the move either adds a triangle to the existing colour or converts all pairs needed for the opposite-colour packing. Thus $K_{5t}$ contains $t$ pairwise vertex-disjoint monochromatic triangles of one colour.

The bound is sharp. For $K_{5t-1}$, split the vertices into $A,B_1,B_2$ with

$$
|A|=3t-1,
\qquad |B_1|=1,
\qquad |B_2|=2t-1.
$$

Colour edges inside $A$ and between $B_1$ and $B_2$ yellow; colour all edges from $A$ to $B_1\cup B_2$ and all edges within each $B_i$ blue. Every yellow triangle lies wholly in $A$, so there are at most $t-1$ disjoint yellow triangles. Every blue triangle uses at least two vertices of $B_1\cup B_2$, and $t$ such triangles would require a blue perfect matching on those $2t$ vertices, impossible because the isolated one-vertex blue component $B_1$ and the odd component $B_2$ have no such matching. Hence there is no $t$-triangle packing of either colour for any $t\geq2$.

## 18F

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 2](paper-2.md)

<h3 id="18f/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [18F](#18f)

Let $\zeta$ be a primitive fifth root of unity. Over $\mathbb F_p$, the [Frobenius automorphism](../../../arithmetic.md#frobenius-automorphism) acts by $\zeta\mapsto\zeta^p$, so the splitting field of $X^5-1$ has degree equal to the [multiplicative order](../../../number-theory.md#multiplicative-order) of $p$ modulo $5$. Its Galois group is generated by Frobenius and is cyclic. The possible orders are therefore $1$ for $p\equiv1$, $4$ for $p\equiv2,3$, and $2$ for $p\equiv4\pmod5$.

Put $\alpha=5^{1/5}$. The polynomial $X^5-5$ satisfies the [Eisenstein criterion](../../../commutative-algebra.md#eisenstein-criterion) at $5$, so $[\mathbb Q(\alpha):\mathbb Q]=5$. Also $[\mathbb Q(\zeta):\mathbb Q]=\varphi(5)=4$. The intersection of these two fields has degree dividing both $5$ and $4$, hence is $\mathbb Q$. Since the splitting field is $\mathbb Q(\alpha,\zeta)$,

$$
\boxed{[\mathbb Q(\alpha,\zeta):\mathbb Q]=20.}
$$

Label the five roots by $\alpha\zeta^i$, $i\in\mathbb F_5$. The Galois group acts through the [affine group over a finite field](../../../group-theory.md#affine-group-over-a-finite-field):

$$
i\longmapsto ai+b,
\qquad a\in\mathbb F_5^\times,\quad b\in\mathbb F_5.
$$

Its possible nontrivial cycle types are $5$, $1+2+2$, and $1+4$; in particular, no element has a three-cycle. By the [Dedekind factorization cycle test](../../../galois-theory.md#dedekind-factorization-cycle-test), the reduction of $X^5-5$ modulo any prime $p\ne5$ therefore has no irreducible cubic factor.

The multiplier of the Frobenius element is $a=p\pmod5$. If $p\equiv2$ or $3$, then $a$ has order four; every affine map with this multiplier has one fixed point and one four-cycle. Hence $X^5-5$ is a product of a linear factor and an irreducible quartic. If $p\equiv1$, Frobenius is a translation $i\mapsto i+b$: it is the identity when $b=0$, giving complete splitting, and is a five-cycle when $b\ne0$, giving an irreducible polynomial.

## 19I

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 2](paper-2.md)

<h3 id="19i/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [19I](#19i)

<h4 id="19i/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#19i/a)

Let $\chi_{\rm reg}$ be the character of the [regular representation](../../../representation-theory.md#regular-representation). It has $\chi_{\rm reg}(1)=|G|$ and vanishes away from the identity. The multiplicity of an irreducible character $\chi_j$ in it is

$$
\langle\chi_{\rm reg},\chi_j\rangle
=\frac1{|G|}|G|\chi_j(1)=n_j.
$$

Thus the regular representation decomposes as $\bigoplus_j n_j\rho_j$. Comparing dimensions gives

$$
\boxed{|G|=\sum_{j=1}^k n_j^2.}
$$

<h3 id="19i/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [19I](#19i)

<h4 id="19i/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#19i/b)

<h5 id="19i/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#19i/b/i)

Direct matrix multiplication gives

$$
A^2=B^2=C^2=D^2=I,
\qquad
[A,B]=[C,D]=Z,
$$

while every other pair among $A,B,C,D$ commutes. Also $Z=-I$ is central. Consequently every commutator of words in the generators lies in $\langle Z\rangle$, so $G'\subseteq\langle Z\rangle$. Since $Z=[A,B]$, the reverse inclusion holds and

$$
\boxed{G'=\langle Z\rangle.}
$$

<h4 id="19i/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#19i/b)

<h5 id="19i/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#19i/b/ii)

Using the commutation relations, every element has a normal form

$$
g=Z^eA^aB^bC^cD^d,
\qquad e,a,b,c,d\in\{0,1\}.
$$

On squaring, equal generators cancel and every interchange contributes only the central element $Z$, so $g^2\in\langle Z\rangle$. There are at most $2^5=32$ normal forms, and every element has order dividing four. Hence $G$ is a [finite p-group](../../../finite-group-theory.md#finite-p-group) with $p=2$, of order at most $32$.

<h4 id="19i/b/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#19i/b)

<h5 id="19i/b/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#19i/b/iii)

Let $T$ commute with the given representation. Since $A$ and $C$ are diagonal and their four joint pairs of eigenvalues are distinct, commuting with both forces $T$ to be diagonal. Commuting with the permutation matrix $B$, which exchanges coordinates $1\leftrightarrow2$ and $3\leftrightarrow4$, equates the first two and last two diagonal entries. Commuting with $D$, which exchanges $1\leftrightarrow3$ and $2\leftrightarrow4$, makes all four entries equal. Thus the [commutant](../../../group-theory.md#centralizer) consists only of scalar matrices. Since the representation is unitary, a proper invariant subspace would have an orthogonal projection in the commutant. No such nonscalar projection exists, so the representation is irreducible.

<h4 id="19i/b/iv">iv</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#19i/b)

<h5 id="19i/b/iv/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#19i/b/iv)

The subgroups $\langle A,B\rangle$ and $\langle C,D\rangle$ each have order eight, their elements commute across the two subgroups, and their intersection is exactly $\langle Z\rangle$. Therefore

$$
|G|=\frac{8\cdot8}{2}=32.
$$

Since $G'=\langle Z\rangle$, the [abelianization](../../../group-theory.md#abelianization) $G/G'$ has order $16$ and is generated by the four commuting involutions represented by $A,B,C,D$. It is isomorphic to $(C_2)^4$, so it has sixteen one-dimensional complex representations, obtained by independently sending $A,B,C,D$ to $\pm1$ and sending $Z$ to $1$.

These characters contribute $16$ to the [sum of squares of irreducible degrees](../../../representation-theory.md#sum-of-squares-of-irreducible-degrees). The given irreducible representation has degree four and contributes another $16$. Part (a) and $|G|=32$ show that no other irreducible representations exist. Thus the complete list is the sixteen linear characters and the given four-dimensional representation.

<h3 id="21f/a">a</h3>

<h4 id="21f/a/solution">Solution</h4>

Regard the [3-sphere](../../../geometry-and-topology.md#three-sphere) as

$$
S^3=\{(z_1,z_2)\in\mathbb C^2:|z_1|^2+|z_2|^2=1\}.
$$

It is the union of the two closed solid tori

$$
U'=\{|z_1|\geq|z_2|\},
\qquad
V'=\{|z_2|\geq|z_1|\}.
$$

Explicit homeomorphisms from the two copies in the question are

$$
\Phi_U(u,w)=\frac{(u,w)}{\sqrt{1+|w|^2}},
\qquad (u,w)\in S^1\times D^2,
$$

and

$$
\Phi_V(z,v)=\frac{(z,v)}{\sqrt{1+|z|^2}},
\qquad (z,v)\in D^2\times S^1.
$$

On their boundaries both maps send $(u,v)\in S^1\times S^1$ to $(u,v)/\sqrt2$. They therefore descend through the quotient to a continuous bijection $S\to S^3$. Since $S$ is compact and $S^3$ is Hausdorff, this bijection is a homeomorphism.

<h3 id="21f/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [21F](#21f)

<h4 id="21f/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#21f/b)

Writing the square coordinates modulo one, the curve has the parametrization

$$
t\longmapsto(2t,3t)\pmod1.
$$

It is therefore the $(2,3)$ [torus knot](../../../knot-theory.md#torus-knot), or trefoil, on the common boundary of the two solid tori. Applying the [Seifert-van Kampen theorem](../../../algebraic-topology.md#seifert-van-kampen-theorem) to the complements on the two sides gives one generator $a$ for the meridian of one solid torus and one generator $b$ for the other; the boundary slope identifies two turns of the first with three turns of the second. Hence

$$
\boxed{\pi_1(S-K)\cong\langle a,b\mid a^2=b^3\rangle.}
$$

This group is nonabelian: imposing the additional relation $a^2=b^3=1$ gives a quotient

$$
\langle a,b\mid a^2=b^3=1\rangle\cong C_2*C_3,
$$

which maps onto the nonabelian group $S_3$ by sending $a$ to a transposition and $b$ to a three-cycle. An abelian group cannot have a nonabelian quotient.

## 22H

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 2](paper-2.md)

<h3 id="22h/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [22H](#22h)

<h4 id="22h/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#22h/a)

The real [Stone-Weierstrass theorem](../../../functional-analysis.md#stone-weierstrass-theorem) states that if $X$ is a compact Hausdorff space and $A\subseteq C(X,\mathbb R)$ is a subalgebra containing the constants and separating points of $X$, then $A$ is dense in $C(X,\mathbb R)$ in the [uniform norm](../../../functional-analysis.md#supremum-norm).

The [Tietze extension theorem](../../../topology.md#tietze-extension-theorem) states that if $X$ is a normal topological space, $F\subseteq X$ is closed, and $f:F\to\mathbb R$ is continuous, then $f$ extends to a continuous function on $X$. In its bounded form, an $f$ taking values in $[-M,M]$ has an extension with the same range bound. The underlying separation step is the [Urysohn lemma](../../../topology.md#urysohn-s-lemma).

<h3 id="22h/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [22H](#22h)

<h4 id="22h/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#22h/b)

Every function with the stated two linear pieces can be written

$$
f(x)=a+bx+c\left|x-\frac12\right|
$$

for suitable real constants $a,b,c$. Since

$$
\left|x-\frac12\right|=\sqrt{\left(x-\frac12\right)^2},
$$

define

$$
Q_i(x)=a+bx+cP_i\!\left(\left(x-\frac12\right)^2\right).
$$

This is a polynomial. The argument of $P_i$ lies in $[0,1/4]\subseteq[0,1]$, so

$$
\boxed{\|Q_i-f\|_\infty
\leq |c|\sup_{u\in[0,1]}|P_i(u)-\sqrt u|
\longrightarrow0.}
$$

<h3 id="22h/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [22H](#22h)

<h4 id="22h/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#22h/d)

For $F_1$, the functions satisfy $\|\sin(\pi nx)/n\|_\infty\leq1/n$, so the sequence converges uniformly to zero. Its closure is the sequence together with its limit and is compact. The same argument applies to $F_2$, since $\|\sin(\pi nx)/\sqrt n\|_\infty\leq n^{-1/2}$. Thus $F_1$ and $F_2$ are relatively compact.

The family $F_3$ is not [equicontinuous](../../../topological-analysis.md#equicontinuity). At $x=0$ its members vanish, but for $x_n=1/(2n)\to0$,

$$
\sin(\pi n x_n)=1.
$$

By the [Arzelà-Ascoli theorem](../../../topological-analysis.md#arzela-ascoli-theorem), a relatively compact subset of $C[0,1]$ must be equicontinuous. Hence $F_3$ is not relatively compact.

The [holomorphic inverse function theorem](../../../geometry-and-topology.md#holomorphic-inverse-function-theorem) therefore makes $p$ a local biholomorphism and hence a covering map in this sense.

It is not regular. The point $1$ has only the preimage $i$ in $\mathbb H$, whereas $e^{i\varepsilon}$ for small positive $\varepsilon$ has the two preimages with arguments $\varepsilon/4$ and $\pi/2+\varepsilon/4$. Thus the fibre cardinality is not locally constant near $1$, so no neighbourhood of $1$ has a preimage of the form $U\times\Delta$ for a fixed discrete set $\Delta$.

<h4 id="23f/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#23f/b)

For $|z|<1$, sum the geometric series and its derivative:

$$
\sum_{n\geq0}(-1)^nz^n=\frac1{1+z},
\qquad
\sum_{n\geq0}n(-1)^nz^n=-\frac z{(1+z)^2}.
$$

Therefore

$$

=\frac{1-z}{(1+z)^2}.
$$

This rational continuation is holomorphic across every point of the unit circle except $z=-1$, where it has a double pole. The set of singular points is consequently

$$
\boxed{\{-1\}}.
$$

<h3 id="23f/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [23F](#23f)

<h4 id="23f/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#23f/c)

The map $f$ is bounded by one near the puncture, so the [Riemann removable singularity theorem](../../../isolated-singularity.md#riemann-removable-singularity-theorem) extends it holomorphically to a map $\widetilde f:\Delta\to\mathbb C$. The [maximum modulus principle](../../../complex-analysis.md#maximum-modulus-principle) shows that its image still lies in $\Delta$: if $|\widetilde f(0)|=1$, the extension would be constant, contradicting its values in $\Delta\setminus\{0\}$ unless that constant also had modulus less than one.

If $f$ is biholomorphic, its inverse $g:\Delta\setminus\{0\}\to\Delta\setminus\{0\}$ also extends holomorphically. Were $a=\widetilde f(0)\ne0$, continuity of $\widetilde g\circ\widetilde f$ and the identity $g(f(z))=z$ on the punctured disk would give $g(a)=0$. This is impossible because $a\in\Delta\setminus\{0\}$ and $g$ takes values in the punctured disk. Hence

$$
\boxed{\widetilde f(0)=0.}
$$

<h3 id="23f/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [23F](#23f)

<h4 id="23f/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#23f/d)

View infinity as the puncture of the Riemann sphere and consider $g(1/z)$ near $z=0$. If this map had an essential singularity, the [Great Picard theorem](../../../complex-analysis.md#great-picard-theorem) in a coordinate chart would make almost every nearby value occur infinitely often, contradicting the injectivity of $g$. The singularity is therefore removable as a map into the compact Riemann surface $R$, and $g$ extends to a nonconstant holomorphic map

$$
\widetilde g:\mathbb P^1\longrightarrow R.
$$

The extension is injective. A collision between $\widetilde g(\infty)$ and $g(z_0)$, or local degree greater than one at infinity, would give nearby values two distinct preimages by the local normal form of a nonconstant holomorphic map, again contradicting injectivity on $\mathbb C$. Thus $\widetilde g$ is a holomorphic bijection between compact Riemann surfaces and hence a biholomorphism. Therefore $R\cong\mathbb P^1$, whose genus is zero.

A derivation of $A$ centred at the $k$-point $p:A\to k$ is a $k$-linear map $D:A\to k$ satisfying the twisted [Leibniz rule](../../../calculus.md#leibniz-rule)

$$
D(ab)=p(a)D(b)+p(b)D(a).
$$

The [Zariski tangent space](../../../algebraic-geometry.md#zariski-tangent-space) is

$$
T_pA=\operatorname{Der}(A,p).
$$

Since $p(f)\ne0$, $p$ extends uniquely to the [localization](../../../commutative-algebra.md#localization-of-a-ring) $A[f^{-1}]$ by $p(a/f^n)=p(a)/p(f)^n$. Restriction gives a linear map

$$
T_pA[f^{-1}]\longrightarrow T_pA.
$$

Conversely, every $D\in T_pA$ has the unique extension

$$
\widetilde D\!\left(\frac a{f^n}\right)
=\frac{D(a)}{p(f)^n}
-\frac{n\,p(a)D(f)}{p(f)^{n+1}}.
$$

The quotient rule verifies the Leibniz identity, and the assumption that $f$ is not a zero divisor makes the localization representation compatible with equality of fractions. Extension and restriction are inverse, so the natural map is an isomorphism.

The [product sigma-algebra](../../../probability-theory.md#product-sigma-algebra) is

$$
\mathcal A_1\otimes\mathcal A_2
=\sigma\{A_1\times A_2:A_i\in\mathcal A_i\}.

The [product measure](../../../probability-theory.md#product-measure) $\mu_1\otimes\mu_2$ is the probability measure on this sigma-algebra satisfying

(\mu_1\otimes\mu_2)(A_1\times A_2)=\mu_1(A_1)\mu_2(A_2).
$$

The measurable rectangles form a [pi-system](../../../measure-theory.md#pi-system) generating $\mathcal A_1\otimes\mathcal A_2$. Any two probability measures agreeing on them therefore agree on the generated sigma-algebra by the [pi-lambda theorem](../../../probability-theory.md#pi-lambda-theorem), proving uniqueness.

Saying that $U$ has law $\mu$ means that $\mu$ is the [pushforward measure](../../../measure-theory.md#pushforward-measure)

\mu=\mathbb P\circ U^{-1},
\qquad

For a real random variable, $\mu$ is a probability measure on $(\mathbb R,\mathcal B(\mathbb R))$.

The pair $U,V$ consists of [independent random variables](../../../random-variable.md#independent-random-variables) when

\mathbb P(U\in A,V\in B)

for every pair of Borel sets $A,B$; equivalently, their joint law is the product of their marginal laws.

<h3 id="26k/e">e</h3>

Take independent random variables $U_1,\ldots,U_d$, each uniform on $[0,1/2]$, and independent [Rademacher random variables](../../../probability-theory.md#rademacher-distribution) $\varepsilon_1,\ldots,\varepsilon_{d-1}$. Define

$$
X_i=\varepsilon_iU_i\quad(1\leq i<d),
\qquad
X_d=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{d-1}\varepsilon_i\right)U_d,
$$

and let $\eta$ be the joint law of $(X_1,\ldots,X_d)$.

Each $X_i$ is uniform on $[-1/2,1/2]$. If the $d$th coordinate is omitted, the remaining signs are the independent $\varepsilon_i$. If coordinate $j<d$ is omitted, the remaining signs are

(\varepsilon_i)_{i\ne j},
\qquad
\prod_{i=1}^{d-1}\varepsilon_i,

which are again uniformly distributed over all $2^{d-1}$ sign vectors because this map from $(\varepsilon_1,\ldots,\varepsilon_{d-1})$ is a bijection. The independent magnitudes then show that every $(d-1)$-coordinate marginal is Lebesgue measure.

The full law is not $d$-dimensional Lebesgue measure, because

$$
\prod_{i=1}^d\operatorname{sgn}(X_i)=1

almost surely, whereas this product is equally likely to have either sign under independent Lebesgue coordinates.

All other entries vanish.

A [reversible Markov chain](../../../markov-process.md#reversible-markov-chain) must satisfy [detailed balance](../../../markov-process.md#detailed-balance). For every $i\geq1$, the rate from $i$ to $i+1$ is positive, while the reverse rate from $i+1$ to $i$ is zero. No positive measure can satisfy $\pi_i g_{i,i+1}=\pi_{i+1}g_{i+1,i}$, so the irreducible chain is not reversible.

<h3 id="27k/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [27K](#27k)

<h4 id="27k/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#27k/c)

After leaving zero, the jump chain avoids every future reset with probability

$$
q=\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac1{1+\rho_i}.
$$

This probability is positive exactly when $\prod_{i\geq1}(1+\rho_i)<\infty$. If $q>0$, the probability of ever returning to zero is less than one, so the irreducible chain is transient. If $q=0$, a reset to zero occurs almost surely after each departure; by the [Strong Markov property](../../../markov-process.md#strong-markov-property) this happens repeatedly, so the chain is recurrent. Therefore

$$
\boxed{X\text{ is transient}\iff\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}(1+\rho_i)<\infty.}
$$

<h3 id="27k/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [27K](#27k)

<h4 id="27k/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#27k/d)

Under the transience assumption, the number of returns to zero is geometrically distributed and hence finite almost surely. The final excursion is a pure ascent through all sufficiently large states. Its holding times are independent exponential random variables with means

$$
\frac1{\lambda_i(1+\rho_i)}.
$$

The [nonexplosion criterion for a pure birth process](../../../markov-process.md#nonexplosion-criterion-for-a-pure-birth-process) says that their sum is finite almost surely exactly when the sum of their means is finite. Thus

$$
\boxed{X\text{ is explosive}\iff
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac1{\lambda_i(1+\rho_i)}<\infty.}
$$

<h3 id="27k/e">e</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [27K](#27k)

<h4 id="27k/e/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [E](#27k/e)

Let $m$ be a nonzero invariant measure. Balance at state $i\geq1$ gives

$$
m_{i-1}\lambda_{i-1}
=m_i\lambda_i(1+\rho_i),
$$

and hence, after choosing $m_0>0$,

$$
m_i=\frac{m_0\lambda_0}
{\lambda_i\prod_{j=1}^i(1+\rho_j)}.
$$

Balance at zero requires

$$
m_0\lambda_0=\sum_{i\geq1}m_i\lambda_i\rho_i
=m_0\lambda_0\sum_{i\geq1}
\frac{\rho_i}{\prod_{j=1}^i(1+\rho_j)}.
$$

The sum telescopes:

$$
\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{\rho_i}{\prod_{j=1}^i(1+\rho_j)}
=1-\frac1{\prod_{j=1}^n(1+\rho_j)}.
$$

Consequently a nonzero invariant measure exists exactly when

$$
\boxed{\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}(1+\rho_j)=\infty.}
$$

It can be normalized to an invariant probability distribution exactly when, in addition,

$$
\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}
\frac1{\lambda_i\prod_{j=1}^i(1+\rho_j)}<\infty,
$$

where the empty product for $i=0$ is one and an overall constant has been suppressed.

Define a new probability measure by the strictly positive [Radon-Nikodym derivative](../../../measure-theory.md#radon-nikodym-derivative)

$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E_{\mathbb Q}[S_1^1]
&=\mathbb E_{\mathbb P}\!\left[
e^{\widetilde\sigma Z-\widetilde\sigma^2/2}
e^{\sigma Z+m}\right]\\
&=\exp\!\left(m+\frac{\sigma^2}{2}
+\sigma\widetilde\sigma\right)=1=S_0^1.
\end{aligned}
$$

**Thus the discounted risky asset is a one-period $\mathbb Q$-martingale. The [fundamental theorem of asset pricing](../../../mathematical-finance.md#fundamental-theorem-of-asset-pricing) implies that there is no arbitrage.**

and define

so the discounted price process is a $\mathbb Q$-martingale. This equivalent martingale measure rules out arbitrage.

\qquad
r_N(\xi)=o((\xi-x_0)^N).

Integrating the finite sum gives

## 31E

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 2](paper-2.md)

<h3 id="31e/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [31E](#31e)

A map is [Devaney-chaotic](../../../dynamical-systems.md#devaney-chaos) on $\Lambda$ when it is [topologically transitive](../../../dynamical-systems.md#topological-transitivity), it has [dense periodic points](../../../dynamical-systems.md#dense-periodic-points) in $\Lambda$, and it has [sensitive dependence on initial conditions](../../../dynamical-systems.md#butterfly-effect). A one-dimensional map is [Glendinning-chaotic](../../../dynamical-systems.md#glendinning-chaos) when some iterate has a [horseshoe for an interval map](../../../dynamical-systems.md#horseshoe-for-an-interval-map): there are an interval $J$ and two disjoint subintervals $K_0,K_1\subseteq J$ such that $F^n(K_0)=F^n(K_1)=J$.

The map $F(x)=ax\pmod1$ is the [beta transformation](../../../dynamical-systems.md#beta-transformation) with slope $a>1$. On every interval of continuity of $F^n$, it is affine with slope $a^n$. The endpoints of these intervals are iterated preimages of the discontinuity set. Choosing $n$ with $a^n>2$, the standard full-branch lemma for the beta transformation supplies two disjoint continuity intervals $K_0,K_1$ on which $F^n$ maps bijectively onto $[0,1)$. Thus $F^n$ has a horseshoe, proving Glendinning chaos.

For Devaney chaos one may either use the standard theorem that a full-branch expanding interval map is Devaney-chaotic, or verify its ingredients directly. Every nonempty open interval expands by the factor $a$ on each uninterrupted iterate; after subdivision at preimages of discontinuities, one of its images contains a full branch and hence eventually covers $[0,1)$. This proves topological transitivity. Applying an inverse branch of a sufficiently high iterate to a full branch gives a contraction whose unique fixed point is periodic; choosing the branch inside any prescribed open interval proves that periodic points are dense. Finally, two sufficiently close points that follow the same branch separate by the factor $a$ on each iterate until their distance exceeds a fixed positive constant, proving sensitive dependence. Hence $F$ is also Devaney-chaotic for every real $a>1$.

## 34B

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 2](paper-2.md)

<h3 id="34b/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [34B](#34b)

For a normalized trial state $\psi$, expand it in an orthonormal basis of [energy eigenstates](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#energy-eigenstate), $\psi=\sum_nc_n\psi_n$. The [Rayleigh quotient](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#rayleigh-quotient) is then

$$
\langle H\rangle_\psi
=\sum_n|c_n|^2E_n\geq E_0\sum_n|c_n|^2=E_0.
$$

This is the [quantum variational principle](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#quantum-variational-principle): minimizing the expectation of the [Hamiltonian operator](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#hamiltonian-quantum-mechanics) over any trial family gives an upper bound on the [ground-state energy](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#ground-state-energy).

Let $\psi$ be the normalized ground-state wavefunction and form the normalized dilation $\psi_\lambda(x)=\lambda^{1/2}\psi(\lambda x)$. Its kinetic and potential expectations are

$$
T(\lambda)=\lambda^2T,
\qquad
V(\lambda)=\lambda^{-n}V.
$$

The true ground state makes the energy stationary at $\lambda=1$, so

$$
0=\left.\frac d{d\lambda}\bigl(\lambda^2T+\lambda^{-n}V\bigr)\right|_{\lambda=1}
=2T-nV.
$$

Thus the [power-law quantum virial theorem](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#power-law-quantum-virial-theorem) gives

$$
\boxed{2\langle T\rangle_0=n\langle V\rangle_0.}
$$

If $n\leq-3$ and $A>0$, the two sides have opposite signs, because $T>0$ and $V>0$. If $A<0$, the scaled energy $\lambda^2T+\lambda^{-n}V$ tends to $-\infty$ as $\lambda\to\infty$, since $-n>2$, so the Hamiltonian is not bounded below. If $A=0$, the [free particle](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#free-particle) has no normalizable ground state. Hence there is no normalizable ground state for $n\leq-3$.

For $n=1$ take the normalized [Gaussian function](../../../calculus.md#gaussian-function)

$$
\psi_\alpha(x)=\left(\frac{2\alpha^2}{\pi}\right)^{1/4}e^{-\alpha^2x^2}.
$$

The [Gaussian integral](../../../calculus.md#gaussian-integral) gives

$$
\langle T\rangle_\alpha=\frac{\hbar^2\alpha^2}{2m},
\qquad
\langle |x|\rangle_\alpha=\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}\alpha},
$$

and therefore

$$
E(\alpha)=\frac{\hbar^2\alpha^2}{2m}
+\frac{A}{\sqrt{2\pi}\alpha}.
$$

Its unique minimum satisfies

$$
\alpha^3=\frac{mA}{\sqrt{2\pi}\hbar^2}.
$$

At this point $\langle V\rangle=2\langle T\rangle$, consistently with the virial theorem, and the resulting variational estimate is

$$
\boxed{E_0\leq
\frac32\left(\frac{A^2\hbar^2}{2\pi m}\right)^{1/3}.}
$$

## 35D

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 2](paper-2.md)

<h3 id="35d/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [35D](#35d)

For one molecule whose translational motion separates from its internal degrees of freedom, the classical one-particle [partition function](../../../statistical-physics.md#canonical-partition-function) is

$$
z_1(T,V)=\frac{V}{\lambda^3}q_{\rm int}(T),
\qquad
\lambda=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi\hbar^2}{mk_BT}},
$$

where $q_{\rm int}$ contains rotational, vibrational, and other internal states. For $N$ indistinguishable noninteracting molecules, the [classical ideal-gas partition function](../../../statistical-physics.md#classical-ideal-gas-partition-function) is $Z_N=z_1^N/N!$. The thermodynamic pressure therefore obeys

$$
P=k_BT\left(\frac{\partial\log Z_N}{\partial V}\right)_{T,N}
=\frac{Nk_BT}{V},
$$

so

$$
\boxed{PV=Nk_BT.}
$$

The internal structure changes the energy, entropy, and heat capacity through $q_{\rm int}(T)$, but it does not change the [ideal gas](../../../thermodynamics.md#ideal-gas) law while the internal partition function is independent of volume and the molecules have negligible interactions.

For a dilute monatomic gas $q_{\rm int}=1$. Using the [Stirling formula](../../../real-analysis.md#stirling-formula) in $Z_N=(V/\lambda^3)^N/N!$ and differentiating the [Helmholtz free energy](../../../thermodynamics.md#helmholtz-free-energy) $F=-k_BT\log Z_N$ gives the [Sackur-Tetrode equation](../../../statistical-physics.md#sackur-tetrode-equation)

$$
\boxed{S=Nk_B\left[
\log\left(\frac{V}{N}\left(\frac{2\pi mk_BT}{h^2}\right)^{3/2}\right)
+\frac52\right].}
$$

With $N$ fixed, an [isentropic process](../../../thermodynamics.md#isentropic-process) therefore satisfies $VT^{3/2}=\text{constant}$, or $TV^{2/3}=\text{constant}$. Eliminating $T$ with the ideal-gas law yields the monatomic [reversible ideal-gas adiabat](../../../thermodynamics.md#reversible-ideal-gas-adiabat)

$$
\boxed{PV^{5/3}=\text{constant}.}
$$

Write $d_+=\sigma+h$ and $d_-=h-\sigma$ for the lower and upper layer thicknesses. A steady unidirectional velocity $\mathbf u=u(y)\mathbf e_x$ with no pressure gradient satisfies the [Navier-Stokes equations](../../../viscous-fluid-flow.md#navier-stokes-equation) through $u''=0$ in each layer. $$
\tau=\mu_+u_+'=\mu_-u_-'
=\frac{U_--U_+}{d_+/\mu_++d_-/\mu_-}.
$$

The [two-layer Couette flow](../../../viscous-fluid-flow.md#two-layer-couette-flow) is therefore

$$
\boxed{
u_+(y)=U_++\frac\tau{\mu_+}(y+h),
\quad -h\leq y\leq\sigma,}
$$



$$
\boxed{
u_-(y)=U_--\frac\tau{\mu_-}(h-y),
\quad \sigma\leq y\leq h.}
$$

<h4 id="37a/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#37a/c)

Suppose $U_+=U$ and $U_-=-U$. Integrating the two velocity profiles gives the [volume fluxes](../../../fluid-mechanics.md#volumetric-flow-rate) per unit span

$$
Q_+=d_+U+\frac{\tau d_+^2}{2\mu_+},
\qquad
Q_-=-d_-U-\frac{\tau d_-^2}{2\mu_-}.
$$

Here

$$
\tau=\frac{-2U}{d_+/\mu_++d_-/\mu_-}.
$$

Consequently

$$
Q_+=\frac{Ud_+d_-/\mu_-}{d_+/\mu_++d_-/\mu_-},
\qquad
Q_-=-\frac{Ud_+d_-/\mu_+}{d_+/\mu_++d_-/\mu_-},
$$

so all dependence on the interface position cancels from the ratio:

$$
\boxed{\frac{Q_-}{Q_+}=-\frac{\mu_-}{\mu_+}.}
$$

## 38A

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 2](paper-2.md)

<h3 id="38a/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [38A](#38a)

<h4 id="38a/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#38a/a)

Take a [shear-horizontal wave](../../../continuum-mechanics.md#shear-horizontal-wave) polarized in the $z$ direction,

$$
\mathbf u=w(x,y,t)\mathbf e_z.
$$

Then $\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0$, and the elastic-wave equation reduces to

$$
w_{tt}=c_S^2(w_{xx}+w_{yy}),
\qquad
c_S=\sqrt{\frac\mu\rho}.
$$

For $w=Y(y)e^{i(kx-\omega t)}$, the rigid boundary gives $Y(0)=0$, while vanishing shear traction at the free surface gives $Y'(h)=0$. Thus

$$
Y_n(y)=A\sin(q_ny),
\qquad
q_nh=\left(n+\frac12\right)\pi,
\qquad n=0,1,2,\ldots.
$$

The dispersion relation of each [guided shear-horizontal mode](../../../continuum-mechanics.md#guided-shear-horizontal-mode) is

$$
\boxed{\omega_n^2=c_S^2(k^2+q_n^2),
\qquad q_n=\frac{(n+1/2)\pi}{h}.}
$$

<h3 id="38a/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [38A](#38a)

<h4 id="38a/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#38a/b)

The cutoff occurs at $k=0$:

$$
\boxed{\omega_{n,c}=c_Sq_n
=\frac{c_S(n+1/2)\pi}{h}.}
$$

For $k>0$, the [phase velocity](../../../wave-equation.md#phase-velocity) and [group velocity](../../../wave-equation.md#group-velocity) are

$$
\boxed{c_{p,n}=\frac{\omega_n}{k}
=c_S\sqrt{1+\frac{q_n^2}{k^2}},
\qquad
c_{g,n}=\frac{d\omega_n}{dk}
=\frac{c_Sk}{\sqrt{k^2+q_n^2}}.}
$$

Their product is $c_{p,n}c_{g,n}=c_S^2$. On a plot against positive $k$, $c_{p,n}$ decreases from $+\infty$ to $c_S$, while $c_{g,n}$ increases from zero to $c_S$; larger mode number shifts both transitions to larger wavenumber.

<h3 id="38a/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [38A](#38a)

<h4 id="38a/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#38a/c)

Use the real mode

$$
w=A\sin(q_ny)\cos(kx-\omega t).
$$

The [kinetic energy density](../../../classical-mechanics.md#kinetic-energy-density) and [isotropic linear-elastic energy density](../../../continuum-mechanics.md#isotropic-linear-elastic-energy-density) are

$$
e_k=\frac12\rho w_t^2,
\qquad
e_p=\frac\mu2(w_x^2+w_y^2).
$$

Average over one period and integrate across the layer. Since $\int_0^h\sin^2(q_ny)\,dy=\int_0^h\cos^2(q_ny)\,dy=h/2$, the mean energy per unit horizontal area is

$$
\overline{\mathcal E}
=\int_0^h\overline{e_k+e_p}\,dy
=\frac{\rho A^2\omega^2h}{4}.
$$

The $x$-directed elastic-energy flux is $S_x=-\sigma_{zx}w_t=-\mu w_xw_t$. Its corresponding average is

$$
\overline{\mathcal F}_x
=\int_0^h\overline{S_x}\,dy
=\frac{\mu A^2k\omega h}{4}.
$$

Using $\omega^2=c_S^2(k^2+q_n^2)$ gives

$$
\frac{\overline{\mathcal F}_x}{\overline{\mathcal E}}
=\frac{\mu k}{\rho\omega}
=\frac{c_S^2k}{\omega}
=c_{g,n},
$$

and therefore

$$
\boxed{\overline{\mathcal F}_x=c_{g,n}\overline{\mathcal E}.}
$$

## 39C

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 2](paper-2.md)

<h3 id="39c/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [39C](#39c)

<h4 id="39c/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#39c/a)

Let $\theta_k=k\pi h=k\pi/(m+1)$ and define $q^{(k)}_j=\sin(j\theta_k)$. The artificial boundary values are $q^{(k)}_0=q^{(k)}_{m+1}=0$. For the [symmetric tridiagonal Toeplitz matrix](../../../linear-algebra.md#symmetric-tridiagonal-toeplitz-matrix) $H=[\beta,\alpha,\beta]$, the [sine addition formula](../../../geometry-and-topology.md#sine-addition-formula) gives

$$
(Hq^{(k)})_j
=\alpha\sin(j\theta_k)
+\beta\bigl[\sin((j-1)\theta_k)+\sin((j+1)\theta_k)\bigr]
=\bigl(\alpha+2\beta\cos\theta_k\bigr)q^{(k)}_j.
$$

Thus all such matrices have the same [discrete sine transform](../../../numerical-analysis.md#discrete-sine-transform) eigenvectors, with eigenvalues

$$
\boxed{\lambda_k=\alpha+2\beta\cos(k\pi h),
\qquad k=1,\ldots,m.}
$$

The sine vectors are mutually [orthogonal vectors](../../../linear-algebra.md#orthogonal-vectors) and nonzero, so the matrix $Q=(\sin(ij\pi h))_{i,j=1}^m$ is invertible. If $D=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_m)$, then $HQ=QD$ and therefore

$$
\boxed{H=QDQ^{-1}.}
$$

<h3 id="39c/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [39C](#39c)

<h4 id="39c/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#39c/b)

Let $u_j=(u_{1,j},\ldots,u_{m,j})^T$ collect the unknowns in grid column $j$, and define $b_j=h^2(f_{1,j},\ldots,f_{m,j})^T$. The terms within one column form the block

$$
\boxed{B=\left[\frac23,-\frac{10}3,\frac23\right]},
$$

while coupling to either adjacent column contributes

$$
\boxed{C=\left[\frac16,\frac23,\frac16\right]}.
$$

The zero boundary values remove all blocks outside the displayed range, so the [nine-point finite-difference stencil](../../../finite-difference.md#nine-point-finite-difference-stencil) becomes the [block tridiagonal matrix](../../../vector-space.md#block-tridiagonal-matrix) system

$$
\begin{pmatrix}
B&C&&\\
C&B&\ddots&\\
&\ddots&\ddots&C\\
&&C&B
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}u_1\\u_2\\\vdots\\u_m\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}b_1\\b_2\\\vdots\\b_m\end{pmatrix}.
$$

<h3 id="39c/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [39C](#39c)

<h4 id="39c/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#39c/c)

The [simultaneous diagonalization](../../../mathematics.md#simultaneous-diagonalization) from part (a) applies to both $B$ and $C$. With $\theta_k=k\pi h$,

$$
Q^{-1}BQ=D=\operatorname{diag}(d_1,\ldots,d_m),
\qquad
d_k=-\frac{10}3+\frac43\cos\theta_k,
$$

and

$$
Q^{-1}CQ=E=\operatorname{diag}(e_1,\ldots,e_m),
\qquad
e_k=\frac23+\frac13\cos\theta_k.
$$

Multiplying the $j$th block equation by $Q^{-1}$ and writing $v_j=Q^{-1}u_j$ and $c_j=Q^{-1}b_j$ gives

$$
Ev_{j-1}+Dv_j+Ev_{j+1}=c_j,
$$

with the absent boundary terms omitted. This is exactly system (3).

<h3 id="39c/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [39C](#39c)

<h4 id="39c/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#39c/d)

Each coordinate of the transformed vectors evolves independently because $D$ and $E$ are [diagonal matrices](../../../linear-algebra.md#diagonal-matrix). Reorder the unknowns by collecting the $k$th components across all grid columns:

$$
\widehat v_k=((v_1)_k,\ldots,(v_m)_k)^T,
\qquad
\widehat c_k=((c_1)_k,\ldots,(c_m)_k)^T.
$$

Then system (3) splits into the $m$ uncoupled systems

$$
\boxed{\Lambda_k\widehat v_k=\widehat c_k,
\qquad k=1,\ldots,m,}
$$

where

$$
\boxed{\Lambda_k=[e_k,d_k,e_k]
=\begin{pmatrix}
d_k&e_k&&\\
e_k&d_k&\ddots&\\
&\ddots&\ddots&e_k\\
&&e_k&d_k
\end{pmatrix}.}
$$

**Thus the original $m^2\times m^2$ problem reduces to one [discrete sine transform](../../../numerical-analysis.md#discrete-sine-transform) followed by $m$ independent tridiagonal solves and an inverse sine transform.**
