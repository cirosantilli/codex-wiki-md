<h1 id="5j/b/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [B](../b.md)

The first model alone would suggest that more cycling is beneficial, but cycling was not randomized in that model: its coefficient can be distorted by [confounding](../../../../../../confounding.md) or [reverse causality](../../../../../../reverse-causality.md). The randomized incentive both substantially increased cycling and substantially increased asthma episodes.

If the incentive affects asthma only through cycling, it is an [instrumental variable](../../../../../../instrumental-variable.md), and the corresponding [Wald estimator](../../../../../../wald-estimator.md) of the effect per additional weekly mile is

$$
\frac{0.91387}{1.73282}\approx0.527
$$

additional episodes. Under that exclusion assumption the evidence therefore advises against cycling to work more often for this population. Without the exclusion assumption, the experiment establishes the harmful effect of the incentive itself but cannot by itself distinguish an effect of cycling from a direct effect of the monetary incentive.
