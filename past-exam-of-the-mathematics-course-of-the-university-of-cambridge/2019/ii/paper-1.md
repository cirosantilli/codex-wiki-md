## 1I

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="1i/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1I](#1i)

<h4 id="1i/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1i/a)

The [Chinese remainder theorem](../../../mathematics.md#chinese-remainder-theorem) says that if the positive integers $m_1,\ldots,m_r$ are pairwise [coprime](../../../number-theory.md#coprime-integers) and $M=\prod_i m_i$, then for arbitrary residue classes $a_i\pmod{m_i}$ there is a unique residue class $x\pmod M$ satisfying

$$
x\equiv a_i\pmod{m_i}
\qquad(1\leq i\leq r).
$$

Equivalently, reduction is a [ring isomorphism](../../../algebra.md#ring-isomorphism)

$$
\mathbb Z/M\mathbb Z\longrightarrow
\prod_{i=1}^r\mathbb Z/m_i\mathbb Z.
$$

For existence, put $M_i=M/m_i$. Pairwise coprimality gives $\gcd(M_i,m_i)=1$, so [Bézout's identity](../../../algebra.md#bezout-identity) supplies an integer $u_i$ with $u_iM_i\equiv1\pmod{m_i}$. Then

$$
x=\sum_{i=1}^r a_i u_iM_i
$$

has the required residues, because every term except the $i$th is divisible by $m_i$. If $x$ and $y$ are two solutions, each $m_i$ divides $x-y$. Pairwise coprimality then implies that their product $M$ divides $x-y$, proving uniqueness modulo $M$.

<h3 id="1i/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1I](#1i)

<h4 id="1i/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1i/b)

An odd [composite number](../../../number-theory.md#composite-number) $N$ with $\gcd(b,N)=1$ is a [Fermat pseudoprime](../../../number-theory.md#fermat-pseudoprime) to base $b$ when

$$
b^{N-1}\equiv1\pmod N.
$$

For $N=35$, the [Chinese remainder theorem for unit groups](../../../mathematics.md#chinese-remainder-theorem-for-unit-groups) reduces this condition to congruences modulo $5$ and $7$. Modulo $5$, [Fermat's little theorem](../../../number-theory.md#fermat-little-theorem) gives

$$
b^{34}=b^{2}\pmod5,
$$

so $b^{34}\equiv1\pmod5$ exactly when $b\equiv\pm1\pmod5$. Modulo $7$, the unit group has order $6$, so

$$
b^{34}=b^4\pmod7.
$$

The equation $b^4=1$ in the cyclic group $(\mathbb Z/7\mathbb Z)^\times$ has $\gcd(4,6)=2$ solutions, namely $b\equiv\pm1\pmod7$.

Combining the two independent sign choices by the Chinese remainder theorem gives

$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
b\pmod5&b\pmod7&b\pmod{35}\\ \hline
1&1&1\\
1&-1&6\\
-1&1&29\\
-1&-1&34
\end{array}
$$

and hence

$$
\boxed{35\text{ is a Fermat pseudoprime to base }b
\iff b\equiv1,6,29,34\pmod{35}}.
$$

## 2H

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="2h/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2H](#2h)

The [Chebyshev polynomial](../../../numerical-analysis.md#chebyshev-polynomial) identity $T_m(\cos\theta)=\cos(m\theta)$ implies $|T_m(x)|\leq1$ on $[-1,1]$. Since $\sum_i\gamma_i<\infty$, the [Weierstrass M-test](../../../probability-and-statistics.md#weierstrass-m-test) shows that

$$
f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\gamma_iT_{3^i}(x)
$$

converges uniformly on $[-1,1]$. Every partial sum is continuous, so the [uniform limit theorem](../../../real-analysis.md#uniform-limit-theorem) makes $f$ a well-defined continuous function.

Let

$$
P_n=\sum_{i=1}^n\gamma_iT_{3^i},
\qquad
x_k=-\cos\frac{k\pi}{3^{n+1}}
\quad(0\leq k\leq3^{n+1}).
$$

Then $-1=x_0<x_1<\cdots<x_{3^{n+1}}=1$. Since every $3^i$ is odd and, for $i\geq n+1$, $3^{i-n-1}$ is an odd integer,

$$
T_{3^i}(x_k)
=-\cos\left(k\pi3^{i-n-1}\right)=(-1)^{k+1}.
$$

It follows that

$$
\boxed{f(x_k)-P_n(x_k)
=(-1)^{k+1}\sum_{i=n+1}^{\infty}\gamma_i}.
$$

Thus the error has equal alternating extrema at $3^{n+1}+1$ ordered points. The [Chebyshev alternation theorem](../../../uniform-approximation.md#equioscillation-theorem) shows that $P_n$ is a best uniform approximation among polynomials of degree at most $3^{n+1}-1$, and in particular

$$
E_{3^{n+1}-1}(f)
:=\inf_{\deg P\leq3^{n+1}-1}\lVert f-P\rVert_\infty
\geq\sum_{i=n+1}^{\infty}\gamma_i.
$$

Now let $(\delta_N)$ be the prescribed decreasing positive sequence. Define

$$
s_0=\delta_1+1,
\qquad
s_j=\delta_{3^j}+2^{-j}\quad(j\geq1),
\qquad
\gamma_j=s_{j-1}-s_j.
$$

Then $s_j$ decreases strictly to zero, so every $\gamma_j>0$, the series $\sum_j\gamma_j=s_0$ converges, and

$$
\sum_{i=j+1}^{\infty}\gamma_i=s_j\geq\delta_{3^j}.
$$

Given $N\geq1$, choose $j\geq0$ with $3^j\leq N\leq3^{j+1}-1$. Monotonicity of best-approximation errors and of $\delta_N$ gives

$$
E_N(f)\geq E_{3^{j+1}-1}(f)
\geq s_j
\geq\delta_{3^j}
\geq\delta_N.
$$

Hence this continuous $f$ satisfies

$$
\boxed{\sup_{t\in[-1,1]}|f(t)-P(t)|\geq\delta_N}
$$

for every polynomial $P$ of degree at most $N$. This is the polynomial form of [Bernstein's lethargy theorem](../../../uniform-approximation.md#bernstein-s-lethargy-theorem).

## 3G

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="3g/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3G](#3g)

For finite-valued [discrete random variables](../../../random-variable.md), the [conditional entropy](../../../information-theory.md#conditional-entropy) is

$$
H(X\mid Y)
=\sum_y\mathbb P(Y=y)H(X\mid Y=y)
=-\sum_{x,y}p(x,y)\log_2p(x\mid y).
$$

The [chain rule for information entropy](../../../information-theory.md#chain-rule-for-information-entropy) in two orders gives

$$
H(X,Z\mid Y)
=H(X\mid Y)+H(Z\mid X,Y)
=H(Z\mid Y)+H(X\mid Y,Z).
$$

Therefore

$$
H(X\mid Y)
=H(X\mid Y,Z)+H(Z\mid Y)-H(Z\mid X,Y)
\leq H(X\mid Y,Z)+H(Z),
$$

using nonnegativity of conditional entropy and the fact that [conditioning reduces entropy](../../../information-theory.md#conditioning-reduces-entropy). This proves the required inequality.

For [Fano's inequality](../../../information-theory.md#fano-s-inequality), let $X$ take values in an alphabet of size $m$, let $\widehat X=g(Y)$ be any estimator, and put

$$
E=\boldsymbol1_{\{\widehat X\ne X\}},
\qquad p_e=\mathbb P(E=1).
$$

Because $E$ is determined by $(X,Y)$,

$$
H(X\mid Y)=H(E,X\mid Y)
=H(E\mid Y)+H(X\mid E,Y).
$$

Now $H(E\mid Y)\leq H(E)=h_2(p_e)$. If $E=0$, $X$ is determined by $Y$; if $E=1$, at most $m-1$ values remain possible. Hence

$$
H(X\mid E,Y)
\leq p_e\log_2(m-1).
$$

Combining the bounds yields

$$
\boxed{H(X\mid Y)
\leq h_2(p_e)+p_e\log_2(m-1)}.
$$

## 4H

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="4h/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4H](#4h)

<h4 id="4h/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#4h/a)

The [pumping lemma for context-free languages](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#pumping-lemma-for-context-free-languages) states that for every [context-free language](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#context-free-language) $L$ there is a pumping length $p$ such that every $w\in L$ with $|w|\geq p$ can be written

$$
w=uvxyz
$$

with

$$
|vxy|\leq p,
\qquad |vy|>0,
\qquad uv^ixy^iz\in L\quad\text{for every }i\geq0.
$$

<h3 id="4h/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4H](#4h)

<h4 id="4h/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#4h/b)

<h5 id="4h/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#4h/b/i)

This language is context-free. It is the language of [even-length palindromes](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#context-free-grammar-for-even-palindromes) over $\{a,b\}$, generated by the [context-free grammar](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#context-free-grammar)

$$
S\to aSa\mid bSb\mid\epsilon.
$$

Every derivation adds matching outer symbols, and removing the matching first and last symbols gives the converse induction.

<h4 id="4h/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#4h/b)

<h5 id="4h/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#4h/b/ii)

This language is not context-free. Suppose it had pumping length $N$, and choose a prime $p>N$. Apply the pumping lemma to $w=0^p1^p$. Let $\alpha$ and $\beta$ be the total numbers of zeros and ones in the pumped pieces $v$ and $y$. If $\alpha\ne\beta$, pumping with $i=0$ produces unequal block lengths and leaves the language. If $\alpha=\beta$, then $|vy|>0$ forces $\alpha=\beta=d>0$. Pumping with $i=p+1$ produces equal block lengths

$$
p+pd=p(1+d),
$$

which are composite. Every possible pumping decomposition fails, proving the [equal prime block language is not context-free](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#equal-prime-block-language-is-not-context-free).

<h4 id="4h/b/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#4h/b)

<h5 id="4h/b/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#4h/b/iii)

The equations $3m=4l$ and $2n=5k$ are equivalent to

$$
m=4r,
\quad l=3r,
\qquad
n=5s,
\quad k=2s
$$

for nonnegative integers $r,s$. Hence the language is

$$
\{a^{4r}b^{5s}c^{2s}d^{3r}:r,s\geq0\},
$$

which is the [nested two-pair count language](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#nested-two-pair-count-language) generated by

$$
S\to aaaaSddd\mid T,
\qquad
T\to bbbbbTcc\mid\epsilon.
$$

It is therefore context-free.

<h3 id="4h/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4H](#4h)

<h4 id="4h/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#4h/c)

Take [context-free grammars](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#context-free-grammar) $G_L,G_M$ for $L,M$ and rename their nonterminals so that they are disjoint. If their start symbols are $S_L,S_M$, add a new start symbol $S$ and the production

$$
S\to S_LS_M.
$$

All productions remain context-free. A derivation from $S$ produces a word from $L$ followed by a word from $M$, and every such concatenation has a derivation. Thus the new grammar generates $LM$, proving [closure of context-free languages under concatenation](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#closure-of-context-free-languages-under-concatenation).

## 5J

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="5j/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [5J](#5j)

An [exponential dispersion family](../../../exponential-family.md#exponential-dispersion-model) has densities of the form

$$
f(y;\theta,\phi)
=a(y,\phi)\exp\left\{\frac{y\theta-b(\theta)}{\phi}\right\},
$$

where $\theta$ is the [canonical parameter](../../../exponential-family.md#natural-parameter-of-an-exponential-family), $\phi>0$ is the [dispersion parameter](../../../exponential-family.md#dispersion-parameter), and the support does not depend on $\theta$. Its mean and variance are

$$
\mathbb E Y=b'(\theta),
\qquad
\operatorname{var}(Y)=\phi b''(\theta).
$$

For the given [Gamma distribution](../../../continuous-probability-distribution.md#gamma-distribution), set

$$
\phi=\frac1\alpha,
\qquad
\theta=-\frac\lambda\alpha<0,
\qquad
b(\theta)=-\log(-\theta).
$$

Then

$$
a(y,\phi)
=\frac{y^{1/\phi-1}}{\Gamma(1/\phi)\phi^{1/\phi}}
$$

gives

$$
a(y,\phi)
\exp\left\{\frac{y\theta-b(\theta)}\phi\right\}
=\frac{\lambda^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha)}y^{\alpha-1}e^{-\lambda y}.
$$

Thus the gamma laws form the [Gamma exponential dispersion family](../../../continuous-probability-distribution.md#gamma-exponential-dispersion-family).

Directly integrating, or using the [cumulant-generating function of a gamma distribution](../../../continuous-probability-distribution.md#cumulant-generating-function-of-a-gamma-distribution),

$$

## 6C

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="6c/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [6C](#6c)

In this discrete [age-structured population model](../../../mathematical-biology.md#age-structured-population-model), individuals of age $a\geq1$ present during the breeding season each produce $b_a$ newborns, so the number entering age zero is

$$
n_{0,t}=\sum_{a=1}^{\infty}n_{a,t}b_a.
$$

An age-$a$ individual survives the following winter with probability $1-\mu_a$ and is then age $a+1$ in the next year, giving

$$
n_{a+1,t+1}=(1-\mu_a)n_{a,t}.
$$

Together with the initial age distribution, these equations determine every later cohort.

Substitute $n_{a,t}=r_a\gamma^t$ into the survival equation. Then

$$
r_{a+1}=\frac{1-\mu_a}{\gamma}r_a,
$$

and iteration gives

$$
r_a=r_0\gamma^{-a}\prod_{i=0}^{a-1}(1-\mu_i).
$$

The birth equation becomes

$$
r_0=\sum_{a=1}^{\infty}r_ab_a
=r_0\sum_{a=1}^{\infty}
\left[\prod_{i=0}^{a-1}(1-\mu_i)\right]\gamma^{-a}b_a.
$$

A nonzero population mode therefore requires the [Discrete Euler-Lotka equation](../../../mathematical-biology.md#discrete-euler-lotka-equation)

$$
\boxed{\phi(\gamma)=1},
\qquad
\phi(\gamma)=\sum_{a=1}^{\infty}
\left[\prod_{i=0}^{a-1}(1-\mu_i)\right]\gamma^{-a}b_a.
$$

For nonnegative vital rates with at least one surviving reproductive age and enough decay to make the series finite, $\phi$ is continuous and strictly decreasing on $\gamma>0$, tends to infinity as $\gamma\downarrow0$, and tends to zero as $\gamma\to\infty$. The [intermediate value theorem](../../../calculus.md#intermediate-value-theorem) gives one positive solution, and strict monotonicity makes it unique.

The value $\phi(1)$ is the expected lifetime offspring count under these fixed rates. Since $\phi$ decreases, $\phi(1)>1$ places the root at $\gamma>1$ and the population grows geometrically; $\phi(1)<1$ places it at $\gamma<1$ and the population shrinks. Equality gives replacement, $\gamma=1$.

$$

\frac{\dot a}{a}=-Ca^{-2},

\frac12(a(t)^2-1)=-C(t-t_0).

Therefore

$$
\boxed{a(t)=\sqrt{1-2C(t-t_0)}}

on the contracting interval, reaching $a=0$ at $t=t_0+1/(2C)$.

## 11G

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

A binary [linear code](../../../coding-theory.md#linear-code) of length $n$ and rank $k$ is a $k$-dimensional vector subspace $C\leq\mathbb F_2^n$. Its minimum distance $d$ is the least [Hamming weight](../../../coding-theory.md#hamming-weight) of a nonzero codeword, equivalently the least [Hamming distance](../../../coding-theory.md#hamming-distance) between distinct codewords.

<h3 id="11g/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [11G](#11g)

<h4 id="11g/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#11g/a)

Delete any $d-1$ coordinates. The resulting puncturing map

$$
\pi:C\longrightarrow\mathbb F_2^{n-d+1}
$$

is injective: if $\pi(x)=\pi(y)$ for distinct codewords, then the nonzero codeword $x-y$ is supported on at most $d-1$ coordinates, contradicting the minimum distance. Thus

$$
2^k=|C|\leq2^{n-d+1},
$$

which is the [Singleton bound](../../../coding-theory.md#singleton-bound)

$$
\boxed{n\geq d+k-1}.
$$

<h3 id="11g/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [11G](#11g)

<h4 id="11g/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#11g/b)

Let $S$ be the support of the weight-$d$ codeword $x$, and let $\pi_S$ delete all coordinates in $S$. Its kernel consists of codewords supported in $S$. If $0\ne y\in\ker\pi_S$, then $\operatorname{wt}(y)\geq d$ but $|S|=d$, so $y$ is nonzero in every coordinate of $S$. Over $\mathbb F_2$ this forces $y=x$. Hence

$$
\ker\pi_S=\langle x\rangle
$$

has dimension one, and the [punctured code](../../../coding-theory.md#punctured-code) $C'=\pi_S(C)$ has length $n-d$ and rank $k-1$ by the [rank-nullity theorem](../../../linear-algebra.md#rank-nullity-theorem).

To bound its minimum distance, take $y\in C$ whose puncture $y'$ is nonzero. If $y$ has $r$ nonzero coordinates in $S$, then $y+x$ has $d-r$ there, while both words have the same $\operatorname{wt}(y')$ nonzero coordinates outside $S$. Both $y$ and $y+x$ are nonzero codewords, so

$$
\operatorname{wt}(y')+r\geq d,
\qquad
\operatorname{wt}(y')+d-r\geq d.
$$

Adding gives $2\operatorname{wt}(y')\geq d$, and therefore

$$
\boxed{d'\geq\left\lceil\frac d2\right\rceil}.
$$

<h3 id="11g/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [11G](#11g)

<h4 id="11g/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#11g/c)

Apply part (b) repeatedly. After the first puncturing, the code has rank $k-1$, length $n-d$, and minimum distance at least $\lceil d/2\rceil$. Repeating with a minimum-weight codeword at each stage gives

$$
n\geq d+\left\lceil\frac d2\right\rceil
+\left\lceil\frac d{2^2}\right\rceil+cdots
+\left\lceil\frac d{2^{k-1}}\right\rceil,
$$

because nested ceilings obey

$$
\left\lceil\frac{\lceil d/2^j\rceil}{2}\right\rceil
=\left\lceil\frac d{2^{j+1}}\right\rceil.
$$

Thus

$$
\boxed{n\geq d+\sum_{1\leq l\leq k-1}
\left\lceil\frac d{2^l}\right\rceil},
$$

which is the binary [Griesmer bound](../../../coding-theory.md#griesmer-bound).

Let $\widehat\delta$ be the [extended transition function of a deterministic finite automaton](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#extended-transition-function-of-a-deterministic-finite-automaton). States $p,q\in Q$ are equivalent, or indistinguishable, when

$$
p\sim q
\iff
\bigl(\widehat\delta(p,w)\in F
\Longleftrightarrow
\widehat\delta(q,w)\in F\bigr)
\quad\text{for every }w\in\Sigma^*.
$$

The [quotient deterministic finite automaton by indistinguishable states](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#quotient-deterministic-finite-automaton-by-indistinguishable-states) $D/{\sim}$ has state set $Q/{\sim}$, initial state $[q_0]$, accepting states $\{[q]:q\in F\}$, and transition

$$
\overline\delta([q],a)=[\delta(q,a)].
$$

Right invariance of $\sim$ makes this well-defined, and the quotient accepts $L(D)$.

To prove minimality, choose for each state $q$ of the accessible automaton $D$ a word $w_q$ with $\widehat\delta_D(q_0,w_q)=q$. Run the same word in any DFA $A$ accepting $L(D)$, and assign the class $[q]$ the reached state of $A$. If two distinct classes $[p]\ne[q]$ reached the same state of $A$, every continuation $u$ would have the same acceptance result after $w_p$ and $w_q$. Since $A$ and $D$ accept the same language, this would make $p$ and $q$ indistinguishable in $D$, a contradiction. Thus $A$ has at least one distinct state for every class in $D/{\sim}$, proving that the quotient is the [minimal deterministic finite automaton](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#minimal-deterministic-finite-automaton).

For divisibility by seven, take states $Q=\{0,1,\ldots,6\}$, initial state $0$, accepting set $F=\{0\}$, and transitions

$$
\boxed{\delta(r,b)=2r+b\pmod7},
\qquad b\in\{0,1\}.
$$

After reading a word, the state is its binary value modulo seven, so this [binary divisibility automaton](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#binary-divisibility-automaton) accepts exactly the multiples of seven, with leading zeros allowed. Every state is accessible: the three-bit words representing $0,1,\ldots,6$ reach the corresponding residues.

For distinct residues $p,q$, let $w_p$ be the three-bit representation of $-p\pmod7$, chosen in $\{0,\ldots,6\}$. Since $2^3\equiv1\pmod7$, reading $w_p$ from state $p$ ends at

$$
2^3p+(-p)=0\pmod7,
$$

whereas reading it from $q$ ends at $q-p\ne0$. Thus $w_p$ distinguishes $p$ from $q$. All seven states are pairwise distinguishable, so the DFA is minimal by the [Myhill-Nerode theorem](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#myhill-nerode-theorem).

The [Papperitz symbol](../../../complex-analysis.md#papperitz-symbol)

$$
P\left\{
\begin{matrix}
a&b&c\\
\alpha&\beta&\gamma\\
\alpha'&\beta'&\gamma'
\end{matrix};z
\right\}
$$

represents the two-dimensional [solution space of a homogeneous linear differential equation](../../../differential-equation.md#solution-space-of-a-homogeneous-linear-differential-equation) for a second-order [Fuchsian differential equation](../../../complex-analysis.md#fuchsian-differential-equation) on the [Riemann sphere](../../../complex-analysis.md#riemann-sphere). Its only singularities are the three [regular singular points](../../../complex-analysis.md#regular-singular-point) $a,b,c$. The two entries beneath each point are its [characteristic exponents at a regular singular point](../../../complex-analysis.md#characteristic-exponent-at-a-regular-singular-point): for example, in the nonresonant case a local basis near $a$ has leading behaviours $(z-a)^\alpha$ and $(z-a)^{\alpha'}$. If one of the points is infinity, exponent $\rho$ conventionally means behaviour $z^{-\rho}$ there.

The six exponents obey the [Fuchs relation](../../../complex-analysis.md#fuchs-relation)

$$
\boxed{\alpha+\alpha'+\beta+\beta'+\gamma+\gamma'=1}.
$$

Subject to this relation, the symbol determines the corresponding Riemann differential equation; resonant differences between exponents may produce [Logarithmic Frobenius solutions](../../../complex-analysis.md#logarithmic-solution-from-a-repeated-frobenius-exponent) without changing the exponent data recorded by the symbol.

A [Möbius transformation of a Papperitz symbol](../../../complex-analysis.md#mobius-transformation-of-a-papperitz-symbol) changes the independent variable by a [Möbius transformation](../../../group-theory.md#mobius-transformation). In particular,

$$
u=\frac{(b-c)(z-a)}{(b-a)(z-c)}
$$

maps $a,b,c$ to $0,1,\infty$, respectively. Because a Möbius map is locally biholomorphic away from its pole, it carries each exponent pair with its singular point and gives

$$
P\left\{
\begin{matrix}
0&1&\infty\\
\alpha&\beta&\gamma\\
\alpha'&\beta'&\gamma'
\end{matrix};u
\right\}.
$$

Now define a [dependent-variable rescaling of a Papperitz symbol](../../../complex-analysis.md#dependent-variable-rescaling-of-a-papperitz-symbol) by writing the old dependent variable as

$$
y(u)=u^\alpha(1-u)^\beta v(u),
$$

so that $v=u^{-\alpha}(1-u)^{-\beta}y$. This [dependent-variable rescaling of a Papperitz symbol](../../../complex-analysis.md#dependent-variable-rescaling-of-a-papperitz-symbol) subtracts $\alpha$ from both exponents at zero, subtracts $\beta$ from both exponents at one, and adds $\alpha+\beta$ to both exponents at infinity. Hence $v$ has symbol

$$
P\left\{
\begin{matrix}
0&1&\infty\\
0&0&\gamma+\alpha+\beta\\
\alpha'-\alpha&\beta'-\beta&\gamma'+\alpha+\beta
\end{matrix};u
\right\}.
$$

Define

$$
A=\gamma+\alpha+\beta,
\qquad
B=\gamma'+\alpha+\beta,
\qquad
C=1+\alpha-\alpha'.
$$

The Fuchs relation gives $\alpha'-\alpha=1-C$ and $\beta'-\beta=C-A-B$. After renaming $u$ as $z$, the symbol is therefore

$$
\boxed{
P\left\{
\begin{matrix}
0&1&\infty\\
0&0&A\\
1-C&C-A-B&B
\end{matrix};z
\right\}},
$$

which is the [Gauss hypergeometric equation](../../../complex-analysis.md#gauss-hypergeometric-equation). Renaming $A,B,C$ as $a,b,c$ gives exactly the displayed symbol in the question.

In the hypergeometric symbol, select the exponent $1-c$ at zero and factor out its local power by writing

The [Exponential shifting of a Papperitz symbol](../../../complex-analysis.md#dependent-variable-rescaling-of-a-papperitz-symbol) makes the exponents of $v$ equal to $0,c-1$ at zero, $0,c-a-b$ at one, and $a-c+1,b-c+1$ at infinity. These are the hypergeometric parameters

\qquad

For noninteger $c$ this is linearly independent from $F(a,b,c;z)$; at resonant values, the usual limiting or logarithmic modification may be required.

## 16I

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="16i/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [16I](#16i)

The [completeness theorem for propositional logic](../../../mathematical-logic.md#completeness-theorem-for-propositional-logic) states that for every set of formulae $\Gamma$ and formula $\varphi$,

$$
\boxed{\Gamma\models\varphi\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\Gamma\vdash\varphi}.
$$

With the [soundness theorem for propositional logic](../../../mathematical-logic.md#soundness-theorem-for-propositional-logic), this is an equivalence. It is also equivalent to saying that every [consistent set of formulae](../../../mathematical-logic.md#consistent-set-of-formulae) has a model.

The usual countable proof lists all formulae and extends a consistent theory one formula at a time. For an uncountable set of primitive propositions, use the [uncountable-language proof of propositional completeness](../../../mathematical-logic.md#uncountable-language-proof-of-propositional-completeness): order the consistent extensions of $\Gamma$ by inclusion. The union of every chain is consistent, since a proof of a contradiction would use only finitely many assumptions and hence would already lie in one member of the chain. [Zorn lemma](../../../set-theory.md#zorn-s-lemma) gives a [maximal consistent set in propositional logic](../../../mathematical-logic.md#maximal-consistent-set-in-propositional-logic) $M$. Define a [Boolean valuation](../../../mathematical-logic.md#boolean-valuation) by $v(p)=1$ exactly when $p\in M$. The usual [structural induction](../../../foundations-of-mathematics.md#structural-induction) on formulae proves the truth lemma

$$
v(\theta)=1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\theta\in M,
$$

so $v$ is a model of $\Gamma$.

The [propositional compactness theorem](../../../mathematical-logic.md#propositional-compactness-theorem) says that a set $\Gamma$ is satisfiable if and only if every finite subset of $\Gamma$ is satisfiable. Only the forward implication is immediate. For the converse, if $\Gamma$ had no model, then $\Gamma\models\bot$, so completeness would give $\Gamma\vdash\bot$. Every [formal proof](../../../mathematical-logic.md#formal-proof) uses finitely many assumptions, so some finite $\Gamma_0\subseteq\Gamma$ would prove $\bot$ and, by soundness, would have no model, a contradiction.

The [decidability theorem for propositional logic](../../../mathematical-logic.md#decidability-theorem-for-propositional-logic) says that there is an algorithm deciding whether a propositional formula is a theorem. A formula contains only finitely many primitive propositions, even when the full language is uncountable. Its finite [truth table](../../../mathematical-logic.md#truth-table) decides whether it is valid, and completeness says that validity is equivalent to theoremhood.

Now let $(X,<)$ be the given [partially ordered set](../../../set.md#partially-ordered-set). For every distinct $x,y\in X$, introduce propositions $p_{xy}$ and $q_{xy}$. Form a propositional theory $T$ containing the following clauses:

- $p_{xy}\leftrightarrow\neg p_{yx}$ and $q_{xy}\leftrightarrow\neg q_{yx}$ for distinct $x,y$;
- $(p_{xy}\wedge p_{yz})\to p_{xz}$ and $(q_{xy}\wedge q_{yz})\to q_{xz}$ for pairwise distinct $x,y,z$;
- $p_{xy}$ and $q_{xy}$ whenever $x<y$ in the original order;
- $\neg(p_{xy}\wedge q_{xy})$ and $\neg(p_{yx}\wedge q_{yx})$ whenever $x$ and $y$ are incomparable in the original order.

The first two families say that $p$ and $q$ encode [strict total orders](../../../set.md#strict-total-order). The third makes both orders extend $<$, while the fourth makes them disagree on every incomparable pair.

Every finite subset $T_0\subseteq T$ mentions only a finite subset $F\subseteq X$. By hypothesis, the induced order on $F$ is a [two-dimensional poset](../../../set.md#two-dimensional-partially-ordered-set), so choose two total orders realizing it and assign the finitely many $p$ and $q$ variables accordingly. This satisfies $T_0$. Thus every finite subset of $T$ is satisfiable, and propositional compactness gives a model of all of $T$.

Define $x<_1y$ when $p_{xy}$ is true and $x<_2y$ when $q_{xy}$ is true. The clauses make $<_1$ and $<_2$ total orders on $X$. They both contain the original order, and their intersection contains no incomparable pair. Hence

$$
x<y\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x<_1y\text{ and }x<_2y,
$$

which proves the [finite-local characterization of two-dimensional partially ordered sets](../../../set.md#finite-local-characterization-of-two-dimensional-partially-ordered-sets).

Let $A$ be the [adjacency matrix of a graph](../../../graph-theory.md#adjacency-matrix). Since $G$ is a [regular graph](../../../graph-theory.md#regular-graph) of degree $d$, every row of $A$ sums to $d$. Therefore, for $\mathbf e=(1,\ldots,1)^T$,

$$
\boxed{A\mathbf e=d\mathbf e},
$$

so $d$ is a [graph eigenvalue](../../../graph-theory.md#graph-eigenvalue).

If $Av=dv$, then the quadratic form of the [Graph Laplacian](../../../graph-theory.md#laplacian-matrix) $L=dI-A$ gives

$$
0=v^*(dI-A)v
=\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E(G)}|v_i-v_j|^2.
$$

Every summand is nonnegative, so $v_i=v_j$ along every edge. The graph is [connected](../../../graph.md#connected-graph), hence all coordinates of $v$ are equal and $v$ is a scalar multiple of $\mathbf e$. Thus the $d$-eigenspace is one-dimensional. Since $A$ is a [symmetric matrix](../../../linear-algebra.md#symmetric-matrix), the [spectral theorem for real symmetric matrices](../../../linear-algebra.md#spectral-theorem-for-real-symmetric-matrices) makes it diagonalizable, so the algebraic multiplicity is also one. This proves the [constant eigenvector of a regular graph](../../../graph-theory.md#constant-eigenvector-of-a-regular-graph) result.

<h3 id="17g/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [17G](#17g)

<h4 id="17g/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#17g/b)

Suppose the [strongly regular graph](../../../graph-theory.md#strongly-regular-graph) has $n$ vertices, degree $d$, $r$ common neighbours for every adjacent pair and $s$ common neighbours for every nonadjacent pair. By the [walk count from powers of an adjacency matrix](../../../graph-theory.md#walk-count-from-powers-of-an-adjacency-matrix), $(A^2)_{ij}$ counts common neighbours of $i$ and $j$, while $(A^2)_{ii}=d$. Hence the [adjacency-matrix relation for a strongly regular graph](../../../graph-theory.md#adjacency-matrix-relation-for-a-strongly-regular-graph) is

$$
A^2=(d-s)I+(r-s)A+sJ,
$$

where $J$ is the [all-ones matrix](../../../vector-space.md#all-ones-matrix).

The [spectral theorem for real symmetric matrices](../../../linear-algebra.md#spectral-theorem-for-real-symmetric-matrices) gives an orthonormal eigenbasis for $A$. Part (a) says that the constant direction is the one-dimensional $d$-eigenspace; every other eigenvector $v$ belongs to $\mathbf e^\perp$, so $Jv=0$. If $Av=\theta v$, the displayed identity gives

$$
\theta^2-(r-s)\theta-(d-s)=0.
$$

There are at most two possible roots $\theta$ in addition to $d$. Thus $G$ has at most three distinct eigenvalues.

<h3 id="17g/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [17G](#17g)

<h4 id="17g/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#17g/c)

For an eigenvector $v$ with eigenvalue $\theta\in\{\lambda,\mu\}$,

$$
Bv=(A-\lambda I)(A-\mu I)v
=(\theta-\lambda)(\theta-\mu)v=0.
$$

By part (a), every vector orthogonal to $\mathbf e$ is a linear combination of such eigenvectors, so $B$ vanishes on $\mathbf e^\perp$. On the remaining eigenspace,

$$
B\mathbf e=(d-\lambda)(d-\mu)\mathbf e.
$$

The [orthogonal projection](../../../hilbert-space.md#orthogonal-projection) onto $\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf e\}$ is $J/n$. It follows that

$$
\boxed{B=\frac{(d-\lambda)(d-\mu)}nJ}.
$$

Put $\kappa=(d-\lambda)(d-\mu)/n$. For $i\ne j$, the identity $B=\kappa J$ reads

$$
(A^2)_{ij}-(\lambda+\mu)A_{ij}=\kappa,
$$

and therefore

$$
(A^2)_{ij}=
\begin{cases}
\kappa+\lambda+\mu,&i\text{ and }j\text{ adjacent},\\
\kappa,&i\text{ and }j\text{ nonadjacent}.
\end{cases}
$$

Because $(A^2)_{ij}$ is the number of common neighbours, these two constants prove that $G$ is strongly regular. This is the converse half of the [three-eigenvalue characterization of a connected strongly regular graph](../../../graph-theory.md#three-eigenvalue-characterization-of-a-connected-strongly-regular-graph).

<h3 id="17g/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [17G](#17g)

<h4 id="17g/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#17g/d)

Suppose the only distinct eigenvalues are $d$ and $\theta$. As in part (c), $A$ acts by $d$ on $\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf e\}$ and by $\theta$ on $\mathbf e^\perp$, so its [spectral decomposition](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#spectral-decomposition) is

$$
A=\theta I+\frac{d-\theta}{n}J.
$$

Every diagonal entry of an adjacency matrix is zero, giving

$$
\theta+\frac{d-\theta}{n}=0.
$$

Thus every off-diagonal entry is the same number $(d-\theta)/n=-\theta$. Since these entries belong to $\{0,1\}$ and the graph is connected with at least two vertices, that common value must be one. Hence $A=J-I$ and $G$ is the [complete graph](../../../graph-theory.md#complete-graph) $K_n$.

Conversely, $K_n$ has adjacency matrix $J-I$, with eigenvalue $n-1$ on $\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf e\}$ and eigenvalue $-1$ on $\mathbf e^\perp$. Therefore the requested graphs are precisely

$$
\boxed{K_n\quad(n\geq2)},
$$

as stated by the [connected regular graph with two adjacency eigenvalues](../../../graph-theory.md#connected-regular-graph-with-two-adjacency-eigenvalues) characterization.

Suppose otherwise and choose a relation

with the least possible number $m$ of nonzero coefficients. After relabelling and scaling, take every $\lambda_i\ne0$ and $\lambda_m=1$. Apply the relation to $xy$ and subtract $\sigma_m(y)$ times the relation at $x$. Because each [field embedding](../../../galois-theory.md#field-embedding) is multiplicative, this gives

\qquad(x\in K).
$$

The $m$th term has disappeared, while the coefficient of $\sigma_1$ is nonzero. This is a shorter nontrivial relation, contradicting minimality. View every $k$-automorphism $\sigma_i:K\to K$ as an element of the $K$-vector space

\operatorname{Hom}_k(K,K),
$$

where $K$ acts by multiplying output values. If $n=[K:k]$ and $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ is a $k$-basis of $K$, a $k$-linear map is determined freely by its $n$ values on that basis. Thus

$$
\dim_K\operatorname{Hom}_k(K,K)=n.

Part (a) says that the $m$ distinct automorphisms are [linearly independent vectors](../../../vector-space.md#linear-independence) in this space, so

$$
\boxed{m\leq[K:k]}.
$$

Now let $k=K^G$, the [fixed field](../../../galois-theory.md#fixed-field) of the finite automorphism group $G$. Its elements are fixed by every member of $G$, so the members of $G$ are distinct $K^G$-automorphisms of $K$. Applying the inequality gives the [automorphism-count bound for a finite field extension](../../../galois-theory.md#automorphism-count-bound-for-a-finite-field-extension)

$$
\boxed{|G|\leq[K:K^G]}.

<h3 id="18f/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [18F](#18f)

<h4 id="18f/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#18f/c)

The actions on the ordered pair $(x,y)$ satisfy

$$
\sigma^4=1,
\qquad
\tau^2=1,
\qquad
\tau\sigma\tau=\sigma^{-1}.
$$

Thus every element of $G$ has the form $\sigma^i$ or $\tau\sigma^i$ for $0\leq i<4$, so $|G|\leq8$. Their actions are

$$
\begin{array}{c|cccccccc}
g&1&\sigma&\sigma^2&\sigma^3&\tau&\tau\sigma&\tau\sigma^2&\tau\sigma^3\\ \hline
(g(x),g(y))
&(x,y)&(y,-x)&(-x,-y)&(-y,x)
&(x,-y)&(-y,-x)&(-x,y)&(y,x).
\end{array}
$$

Because $x,y$ are [algebraically independent elements](../../../algebra.md#algebraically-independent-elements) over $\mathbb Q$, these eight substitutions are distinct. Hence

$$
\boxed{|G|=8},
$$

and the presentation identifies $G$ with the [dihedral group](../../../finite-group-theory.md#dihedral-group) $D_8$.

Put

$$
u=x^2+y^2,
\qquad
v=x^2y^2.
$$

Both generators $\sigma$ and $\tau$ fix $u$ and $v$, so for $F=\mathbb Q(u,v)$,

$$
F\subseteq K^G.
$$

The elements $x^2$ and $y^2$ are the two roots of

$$
T^2-uT+v,
$$

so $[F(x^2,y^2):F]\leq2$. Adjoining first $x$ and then $y$ takes at most two quadratic extensions, and the [tower law](../../../algebra.md#tower-law) gives

$$
[K:F]\leq2\cdot2\cdot2=8.
$$

Part (b) gives $8=|G|\leq[K:K^G]$. Since $F\subseteq K^G\subseteq K$, another use of the tower law gives

$$
8\leq[K:K^G]\leq[K:F]\leq8.
$$

All inequalities are equalities and $[K^G:F]=1$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{K^G=\mathbb Q(x^2+y^2,x^2y^2)},
$$

the [dihedral fixed field of a two-variable rational function field](../../../galois-theory.md#dihedral-fixed-field-of-a-two-variable-rational-function-field).

## 19I

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="19i/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [19I](#19i)

<h4 id="19i/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#19i/a)

[Schur lemma](../../../representation-theory.md#schur-s-lemma) over $\mathbb C$ states that an [intertwining operator](../../../representation-theory.md#intertwining-operator) $T:V\to W$ between finite-dimensional irreducible complex representations is either zero or an isomorphism. In particular, every intertwining endomorphism of $V$ is a scalar multiple of the identity.

Indeed, $\ker T\subseteq V$ and $\operatorname{im}T\subseteq W$ are [invariant subspaces](../../../representation-theory.md#invariant-subspace). If $T\ne0$, irreducibility gives $\ker T=0$ and $\operatorname{im}T=W$, so $T$ is an isomorphism. For an endomorphism $T:V\to V$, the [fundamental theorem of algebra](../../../algebra.md#fundamental-theorem-of-algebra) gives an eigenvalue $\lambda\in\mathbb C$. Then $T-\lambda I$ is an intertwiner with nonzero kernel, so the first part forces $T-\lambda I=0$. Hence $T=\lambda I$, completing the [Proof of Schur lemma](../../../representation-theory.md#proof-of-schur-lemma).

<h3 id="19i/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [19I](#19i)

<h4 id="19i/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#19i/b)

<h5 id="19i/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#19i/b/i)

Let $G=C_3=\langle g\rangle$ and let $e_0,e_1,e_2$ be the standard basis of its real [regular representation](../../../representation-theory.md#regular-representation), with $ge_j=e_{j+1}$ modulo three. Set

$$
U=\operatorname{span}\{e_0+e_1+e_2\},
\qquad
W=\{x_0e_0+x_1e_1+x_2e_2:x_0+x_1+x_2=0\}.
$$

Both are invariant, they are orthogonal, and

$$
\boxed{\mathbb RG=U\oplus W}.
$$

The line $U$ is the [trivial representation](../../../representation-theory.md#trivial-representation). On the plane $W$, the generator acts as a rotation through $2\pi/3$. Such a rotation has no real eigenline, so $W$ has no nonzero proper invariant real subspace and is irreducible.

<h4 id="19i/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#19i/b)

<h5 id="19i/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#19i/b/ii)

The two irreducible real modules $U$ and $W$ are nonisomorphic, so the kernel-image argument from part (a), which is valid over any field, gives

$$
\operatorname{Hom}_G(U,W)=0,
\qquad
\operatorname{Hom}_G(W,U)=0.
$$

Every endomorphism of the one-dimensional trivial module is scalar:

$$
\operatorname{End}_G(U)=\mathbb R.
$$

Identify the oriented plane $W$ with $\mathbb C$, so that $g$ acts by multiplication by $e^{2\pi i/3}$. A real-linear map commutes with this rotation exactly when it is multiplication by a complex number. Equivalently,

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{End}_G(W)
=\{aI+bJ:a,b\in\mathbb R,\ J^2=-I\}\cong\mathbb C},
$$

where $J$ is rotation through $\pi/2$. Taking $J$ gives a nonscalar intertwining endomorphism of an irreducible real representation. This is the [Failure of the scalar conclusion of Schur lemma over the real numbers](../../../representation-theory.md#failure-of-the-scalar-conclusion-of-schur-lemma-over-the-real-numbers).

<h3 id="19i/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [19I](#19i)

<h4 id="19i/c/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#19i/c)

<h5 id="19i/c/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#19i/c/i)

Let $e_0,\ldots,e_{n-1}$ be the standard basis and let the generator $g$ send $e_j$ to $e_{j+1}$ modulo $n$. The constant vector

$$
c_0=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}e_j
$$

spans a trivial module. Since $n$ is even, the alternating vector

$$
c_{n/2}=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}(-1)^je_j
$$

spans a sign module on which $g$ acts by $-1$.

For each $1\leq k<n/2$, define the real [discrete Fourier modes](../../../numerical-analysis.md#discrete-fourier-mode)

$$
c_k=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\cos\left(\frac{2\pi kj}{n}\right)e_j,
\qquad
s_k=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\sin\left(\frac{2\pi kj}{n}\right)e_j.
$$

The plane $W_k=\operatorname{span}\{c_k,s_k\}$ is invariant, and $g$ acts on it by rotation through $2\pi k/n$. Since this angle is neither zero nor $\pi$, $W_k$ is irreducible over $\mathbb R$. The traces $2\cos(2\pi k/n)$ are distinct for $1\leq k<n/2$, so these modules are pairwise nonisomorphic. Orthogonality of the [discrete Fourier transform](../../../numerical-analysis.md#discrete-fourier-transform) gives

$$
\boxed{\mathbb RC_n
=\langle c_0\rangle\oplus\langle c_{n/2}\rangle
\oplus\bigoplus_{k=1}^{(n-2)/2}W_k}.
$$

The dimensions sum to $1+1+2(n-2)/2=n$, so this is the whole regular module: two nonisomorphic one-dimensional summands and $(n-2)/2$ nonisomorphic two-dimensional summands.

<h4 id="19i/c/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#19i/c)

<h5 id="19i/c/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#19i/c/ii)

When $n$ is odd there is no alternating sign mode. The same cosine-sine construction gives invariant irreducible planes $W_k$ for

$$
1\leq k\leq\frac{n-1}{2},
$$

again pairwise nonisomorphic because their generator traces are distinct. The real Fourier decomposition is

$$
\boxed{\mathbb RC_n
=\langle c_0\rangle
\oplus\bigoplus_{k=1}^{(n-1)/2}W_k}.
$$

Its dimensions sum to $1+2(n-1)/2=n$. Thus the [real regular representation of a finite cyclic group](../../../representation-theory.md#real-regular-representation-of-a-finite-cyclic-group) has one one-dimensional irreducible summand and $(n-1)/2$ nonisomorphic two-dimensional irreducible summands.

The [ring of integers of a quadratic field](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#ring-of-integers-of-a-quadratic-field) has integral basis $1,\sqrt d$ when the square-free integer $d$ is not congruent to one modulo four. Since $d=2$,

$$

This is the [ring of integers of Q of square root two](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#ring-of-integers-of-q-of-square-root-two).

<h3 id="20g/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [20G](#20g)

<h4 id="20g/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#20g/b)

The [Dirichlet unit theorem](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#dirichlet-s-unit-theorem) states that for a [number field](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#number-field) with $r$ real embeddings and $s$ conjugate pairs of complex embeddings,

$$
\mathcal O_K^\times\cong\mu(K)\times\mathbb Z^{r+s-1},
$$

where $\mu(K)$ is its finite group of [roots of unity](../../../algebra.md#root-of-unity). Here $K$ is real quadratic, so $(r,s)=(2,0)$ and $\mu(K)=\{\mathord\pm1\}$. Thus its unit group has rank one.

The element

$$
\varepsilon=1+\sqrt2
$$

is a unit because its [field norm](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#field-norm) is

$$
N_{K/\mathbb Q}(\varepsilon)
=(1+\sqrt2)(1-\sqrt2)=-1.
$$

It is the smallest unit greater than one in the chosen real embedding. Indeed, write such a unit as $u=a+b\sqrt2$ and let $u'=a-b\sqrt2=N(u)/u$ be its [algebraic conjugate](../../../algebra.md#conjugate-element-field-theory). Since $|u'|<1<u$, both $a=(u+u')/2$ and $b=(u-u')/(2\sqrt2)$ are positive integers. Hence $a,b\geq1$ and $u\geq1+\sqrt2$. The rank-one logarithmic lattice in Dirichlet's theorem is therefore generated by $\log\varepsilon$. Consequently the [units of Q of square root two](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#units-of-q-of-square-root-two) are

$$
\boxed{\mathcal O_K^\times
=\{\mathord\pm(1+\sqrt2)^n:n\in\mathbb Z\}}.
$$

<h3 id="20g/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [20G](#20g)

<h4 id="20g/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#20g/c)

Let $\varepsilon=1+\sqrt2$ and $P=(3+\sqrt2)$. The generator of this [principal ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal) has norm

$$
N_{K/\mathbb Q}(3+\sqrt2)=9-2=7.
$$

Reduction modulo $P$ gives $\sqrt2\equiv-3\equiv4\pmod7$. The map below is surjective and has $P$ in its kernel; both the kernel and $P$ have index seven, the latter by the [ideal norm](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#ideal-norm), so they are equal. Hence it induces an isomorphism of [residue fields](../../../commutative-algebra.md#residue-field)

$$
\mathcal O_K/P\cong\mathbb F_7,
\qquad
a+b\sqrt2\longmapsto a+4b\pmod7.
$$

In particular,

$$
\varepsilon\equiv5\pmod P.
$$

In the [finite field](../../../algebra.md#finite-field) $\mathbb F_7$, the element $5$ has multiplicative order six, since

$$
5^2\equiv4,
\qquad
5^3\equiv-1,
\qquad
5^6\equiv1\pmod7.
$$

By part (b), every unit is $(-1)^\delta\varepsilon^n$ for $\delta\in\{0,1\}$ and $n\in\mathbb Z$. Since $-1\equiv5^3$, its residue is $5^{n+3\delta}$, which equals one exactly when

$$
n+3\delta\equiv0\pmod6.
$$

The resulting units are $\varepsilon^{6k}$ and $-\varepsilon^{6k+3}$, and these are precisely the even and odd powers of $-\varepsilon^3$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{G=\langle-\varepsilon^3\rangle
=\left\langle-(1+\sqrt2)^3\right\rangle
=\langle-7-5\sqrt2\rangle},
$$

which is the group of [units congruent to one modulo 3 plus square root two](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#units-congruent-to-one-modulo-3-plus-square-root-two).

<h3 id="21f/a">a</h3>

<h4 id="21f/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#21f/a)

Let $p:\widehat X\to X$ be the covering map. The [uniqueness of lifts to a covering space](../../../algebraic-topology.md#uniqueness-of-lifts-to-a-covering-space) says that if $\widehat f_1,\widehat f_2:Y\to\widehat X$ satisfy

$$
p\circ\widehat f_1=f=p\circ\widehat f_2
$$

and $\widehat f_1(y_0)=\widehat f_2(y_0)$ at one point $y_0\in Y$, then

$$
\boxed{\widehat f_1=\widehat f_2}.
$$

For any $y\in Y$, choose a path $\gamma$ from $y_0$ to $y$, using path-connectedness of $Y$. Both $\widehat f_1\circ\gamma$ and $\widehat f_2\circ\gamma$ lift the same path $f\circ\gamma$ and have the same initial point. Uniqueness in the [path lifting theorem](../../../algebraic-topology.md#path-lifting-theorem) makes the lifted paths equal, so their endpoints agree. Since $y$ was arbitrary, the two lifts agree everywhere.

<h3 id="21f/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [21F](#21f)

<h4 id="21f/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#21f/b)

Take $X=S^1\vee S^1$, the [wedge of two circles](../../../topology.md#wedge-of-two-circles), and label its oriented loops $a$ and $b$. For a fixed $n\geq3$, let

$$
\alpha=(1\ 2\ \cdots\ n),
\qquad
\beta=(1\ 2)
$$

be permutations of the fibre $\{1,\ldots,n\}$. Construct a labelled graph $\widehat X_n$ with these $n$ vertices, an oriented $a$-edge from $i$ to $\alpha(i)$, and an oriented $b$-edge from $i$ to $\beta(i)$. Map every vertex to the wedge point and each labelled edge homeomorphically onto its corresponding loop. Every vertex has exactly one incoming and one outgoing edge of each label, so this is an $n$-sheeted [permutation covering of a wedge of circles](../../../algebraic-topology.md#permutation-covering-of-a-wedge-of-circles)

$$
p_n:\widehat X_n\longrightarrow X.
$$

It is connected because the $n$-cycle $\alpha$ acts transitively on the fibre.

A [deck transformation](../../../algebraic-topology.md#deck-transformation) induces a permutation $\gamma$ of the vertices that commutes with both monodromy permutations $\alpha$ and $\beta$. Conversely, such a permutation determines a deck transformation, by the [deck transformation group as a monodromy centralizer](../../../algebraic-topology.md#deck-transformation-group-as-a-monodromy-centralizer). The permutations $\alpha$ and $\beta$ generate $S_n$: the conjugates $\alpha^j\beta\alpha^{-j}$ include the adjacent transpositions, which generate the [symmetric group](../../../finite-group-theory.md#symmetric-group). Hence $\gamma$ centralizes $S_n$ and lies in its [center of a group](../../../group-theory.md#center-of-a-group). For $n\geq3$, the center of $S_n$ is trivial, so $\gamma=1$. A deck transformation fixing one point is the identity by part (a), and therefore

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Deck}(p_n)=\{1\}}.
$$

↑ **Parent:** [21F](#21f)

<h4 id="21f/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#21f/c)

**No.** The [monodromy action of a covering space](../../../algebraic-topology.md#monodromy-action-of-a-covering-space) for a two-sheeted covering takes values in $S_2$. ## 22H

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="22h/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [22H](#22h)

<h4 id="22h/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#22h/a)

For $x=(x_n)\in\ell^p$ with $1\leq p<\infty$, let

$$
x^{(N)}=(x_1,\ldots,x_N,0,0,\ldots)\in F.
$$

Then

$$
\lVert x-x^{(N)}\rVert_p^p
=\sum_{n>N}|x_n|^p\longrightarrow0
$$

because the defining nonnegative series converges. Thus $F=c_{00}$ is dense in the [l-p sequence space](../../../banach-space.md#l-p-sequence-space) for every finite $p$; this is the [density of finitely supported sequences in l-p](../../../banach-space.md#density-of-finitely-supported-sequences-in-l-p).

It is not dense in the [l-infinity sequence space](../../../banach-space.md#l-infinity-sequence-space). For the constant sequence $\mathbf1=(1,1,\ldots)$ and any finitely supported $y$, some tail of $y$ is zero, so

$$
\lVert\mathbf1-y\rVert_\infty=1.
$$

**Hence $\mathbf1$ does not belong to the closure of $F$.**

<h3 id="22h/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [22H](#22h)

<h4 id="22h/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#22h/b)

Given $x\in\ell^p$, choose a sequence $x^{(m)}\in F$ with $x^{(m)}\to x$, which is possible by part (a). The bound gives

$$
\lVert Tx^{(m)}-Tx^{(l)}\rVert_p
\leq C\lVert x^{(m)}-x^{(l)}\rVert_p,
$$

so $(Tx^{(m)})$ is a [Cauchy sequence](../../../real-analysis.md#cauchy-sequence). Since $\ell^p$ is a [Banach space](../../../banach-space.md), it converges. Define

$$
\widetilde Tx=\lim_{m\to\infty}Tx^{(m)}.
$$

The same estimate shows that this limit does not depend on the approximating sequence. Passing to the limit proves linearity and

$$
\lVert\widetilde Tx\rVert_p\leq C\lVert x\rVert_p,
$$

so $\lVert\widetilde T\rVert\leq C$. It agrees with $T$ on $F$. If two bounded extensions existed, their difference would vanish on the dense subspace $F$ and, by continuity, on its closure $\ell^p$. Thus the extension is unique. This is the [extension of a bounded linear operator from a dense subspace](../../../topological-vector-space.md#extension-of-a-bounded-linear-operator-from-a-dense-subspace).

<h3 id="22h/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [22H](#22h)

<h4 id="22h/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#22h/c)

Let $e_m$ denote the sequence with a one in coordinate $m$ and zeros elsewhere.

For $T_1$, one has $\lVert e_m\rVert_p=1$ but $\lVert T_1e_m\rVert_p=m$ for every $p$, including $p=\infty$. Thus $T_1$ is unbounded for every $p$.

For $T_2$, the sequence $T_2e_m$ has entries $-(m-1)$ and $m$ in coordinates $m-1$ and $m$, respectively. Hence $\lVert T_2e_m\rVert_p\geq m$, so $T_2$ is also unbounded for every $p$.

For $T_3$, coordinatewise division gives

$$
\lVert T_3x\rVert_p\leq\lVert x\rVert_p
$$

for every $1\leq p\leq\infty$. Thus $T_3$ is a bounded [diagonal operator on sequence space](../../../functional-analysis.md#diagonal-operator-on-sequence-space) for every $p$.

For $T_4$, write $a=(n^{-1/2})_{n\geq1}$. Then $T_4x=x_1a$, and coordinate evaluation satisfies $|x_1|\leq\lVert x\rVert_p$. By the [p-series](../../../real-analysis.md#p-series) criterion, $a\in\ell^p$ exactly when $p>2$, while $a\in\ell^\infty$. Therefore $T_4$ is bounded precisely for

$$
2<p\leq\infty.
$$

For $p\leq2$, even $T_4e_1$ does not belong to $\ell^p$.

For $T_5$, let $q$ be the [Holder conjugate exponent](../../../functional-analysis.md#conjugate-exponents) of $p$. [Holder inequality](../../../functional-analysis.md#holder-inequality) gives, for finite $p$,

$$
\left|\sum_{j=1}^nx_j\right|
\leq n^{1/q}\lVert x\rVert_p,
$$

and consequently

$$
\lVert T_5x\rVert_p^p
\leq\lVert x\rVert_p^p
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{n^{p/q}}{2^{np}}<\infty.
$$

For $p=\infty$,

$$
|(T_5x)_n|\leq\frac n{2^n}\lVert x\rVert_\infty
\leq\lVert x\rVert_\infty.
$$

Thus $T_5$ is bounded for every $1\leq p\leq\infty$. Altogether,

$$
\boxed{
\begin{array}{c|ccccc}
&T_1&T_2&T_3&T_4&T_5\\ \hline
\text{bounded on }\ell^p
&\text{never}&\text{never}&\text{all }p&2<p\leq\infty&\text{all }p
\end{array}}
$$

## 23H

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="23h/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [23H](#23h)

<h4 id="23h/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#23h/a)

For each $n\in\mathbb N$, the open interval

$$
\left(n-\frac12,n+\frac12\right)
$$

meets $\mathbb N$ only in $\{n\}$. Thus every singleton is open in the [subspace topology](../../../topology.md#subspace-topology) on $\mathbb N$. Every subset $A\subseteq\mathbb N$ is the union $A=\bigcup_{n\in A}\{n\}$ and is therefore open. Hence the induced topology is the [discrete topology](../../../topology.md#discrete-space):

$$
\boxed{\mathcal T=\mathcal P(\mathbb N)}.
$$

<h3 id="23h/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [23H](#23h)

<h4 id="23h/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#23h/b)

Because every subset of $\mathbb N$ is open, the [Borel sigma-algebra of a discrete space](../../../measure-theory.md#borel-sigma-algebra-of-a-discrete-space) is

$$
\boxed{\mathcal B(\mathbb N)=\mathcal P(\mathbb N)}.
$$

If $f:\mathbb N\to\mathbb R$ or $f:\mathbb N\to[0,\infty]$ and $B$ is a Borel subset of the codomain, then $f^{-1}(B)$ is some subset of $\mathbb N$, hence is Borel. Thus every such function is a [Borel measurable function](../../../measure-theory.md#borel-measurable-function).

<h3 id="23h/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [23H](#23h)

<h4 id="23h/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#23h/c)

Equip $\mathbb N$ with [counting measure](../../../measure-theory.md#counting-measure) $\#$. For a nonnegative function $f:\mathbb N\to[0,\infty]$, define

$$
\boxed{\sum_{n\geq1}f(n)
:=\int_{\mathbb N}f\,d\#
=\sup_{N\geq1}\sum_{n=1}^Nf(n)}.
$$

This lies in $[0,\infty]$ and agrees with the [series as a Lebesgue integral against counting measure](../../../measure-theory.md#series-as-a-lebesgue-integral-against-counting-measure).

For $f:\mathbb N\to\mathbb C$, define

$$
\sum_{n\geq1}f(n)=\int_{\mathbb N}f\,d\#
$$

when $f$ is integrable, which here means

$$
\boxed{\sum_{n\geq1}|f(n)|<\infty}.
$$

Thus the complex sum is defined under [absolute convergence](../../../real-analysis.md#absolute-convergence) and can be obtained by integrating the real and imaginary parts.

A [simple function](../../../measure-theory.md#simple-function) on $\mathbb N$ is a function with finite range, equivalently

$$
s=\sum_{j=1}^ma_j\mathbf1_{A_j}
$$

for a finite measurable partition by subsets $A_j\subseteq\mathbb N$. Its nonnegative sum is $\sum_ja_j\#A_j$. It is finite exactly when every level set with $a_j>0$ is finite, equivalently when $s$ has finite support after its zero level is discarded.

<h3 id="23h/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [23H](#23h)

<h4 id="23h/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#23h/d)

The [Beppo Levi theorem](../../../measure-theory.md#monotone-convergence-theorem), or [monotone convergence theorem](../../../measure-theory.md#monotone-convergence-theorem), states that if $(f_n)$ is a sequence of nonnegative measurable functions on a [measure space](../../../measure-theory.md#measure-space) and

$$
f_n\uparrow f
$$

pointwise almost everywhere, then $f$ is measurable and

$$
\boxed{\int f\,d\mu
=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int f_n\,d\mu},
$$

where either side may be infinite.

Measurability of $f=\sup_nf_n$ follows from measurability of countable suprema. Since $f_n\leq f$, [monotonicity of the Lebesgue integral](../../../calculus.md#monotonicity-of-the-lebesgue-integral) gives

$$
\lim_n\int f_n\,d\mu\leq\int f\,d\mu.
$$

For the reverse inequality, let $s$ be a nonnegative [simple function](../../../measure-theory.md#simple-function) with $s\leq f$, and fix $0<c<1$. Define

$$
E_n=\{x:f_n(x)\geq c,s(x)\}.
$$

Then $E_n\uparrow X$ up to the null set on which convergence fails. Since $f_n\geq c,s\mathbf1_{E_n}$,

$$
\int f_n\,d\mu
\geq c\int_{E_n}s\,d\mu
\longrightarrow c\int s\,d\mu
$$

by [continuity from below of a measure](../../../measure-theory.md#continuity-from-below-of-a-measure), applied to the finitely many level sets of $s$. Hence $\lim_n\int f_n\geq c\int s$. Letting $c\uparrow1$ and taking the supremum over all simple $s\leq f$, as in the definition of the [Lebesgue integral](../../../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-integral), gives the reverse inequality and proves the theorem.

<h3 id="23h/e">e</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [23H](#23h)

<h4 id="23h/e/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [E](#23h/e)

For integer $k\geq1$, define $f_k(n)=f(k,n)$. The assumed monotonicity in $t$ gives

$$
f_k(n)\uparrow\lim_{t\to\infty}f(t,n)
$$

for every $n$. Apply the [monotone convergence theorem](../../../measure-theory.md#monotone-convergence-theorem) on $\mathbb N$ with counting measure:

$$
\lim_{k\to\infty}\sum_{n\geq1}f(k,n)
=\sum_{n\geq1}\lim_{k\to\infty}f(k,n)
=\sum_{n\geq1}\lim_{t\to\infty}f(t,n).
$$

The function $t\mapsto\sum_nf(t,n)$ is itself nondecreasing, and the positive integers are cofinal in $\mathbb R$ as $t\to\infty$, so its limit over real $t$ equals its limit over integer $k$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{
\lim_{t\to\infty}\sum_{n\geq1}f(t,n)
=\sum_{n\geq1}\lim_{t\to\infty}f(t,n)}.
$$

Without monotonicity, take

$$
f(t,n)=\mathbf1_{[n,n+1)}(t)
\qquad(t\geq1).
$$

For each fixed $n$, $f(t,n)\to0$, so the sum of the pointwise limits is zero. At every $t\geq1$, however, exactly one term is one, so $\sum_nf(t,n)=1$ and its limit is one. This [moving-spike obstruction to interchanging a limit and an infinite sum](../../../measure-theory.md#moving-spike-obstruction-to-interchanging-a-limit-and-an-infinite-sum) gives the required counterexample.

<h3 id="24f/a">a</h3>

<h4 id="24f/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#24f/a)

Let

$$
F(x,y)=x^3y+y^3+x.
$$

Its complex partial derivatives are

$$
F_x=3x^2y+1,
\qquad
F_y=x^3+3y^2.
$$

There is no point of $X'$ at which both vanish. Indeed, if $F=F_x=F_y=0$, then $F_y=0$ and $F=0$ give

$$
x^3=-3y^2,
\qquad
x=2y^3.
$$

Neither $x$ nor $y$ can be zero because $F_x=0$. Substitution into $F_x=0$ gives $12y^7+1=0$, whereas substitution into $F_y=0$ gives $8y^7+3=0$; these equations are incompatible.

For every $p=(x_0,y_0)\in X'$, at least one partial derivative is nonzero. If $F_y(p)\ne0$, the [holomorphic implicit function theorem](../../../calculus.md#holomorphic-implicit-function-theorem) expresses the curve near $p$ as $y=g(x)$, and the restriction of the $x$-projection is a holomorphic coordinate chart. If $F_x(p)\ne0$, use the $y$-projection instead. On overlaps, the transition functions are restrictions of holomorphic coordinate functions and are holomorphic. These charts define an atlas making $X'$ a one-dimensional [complex manifold](../../../complex-geometry.md#complex-manifold), hence a [Riemann surface](../../../complex-analysis.md#riemann-surfaces).

<h3 id="24f/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [24F](#24f)

<h4 id="24f/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#24f/b)

Homogenizing the affine equation gives the [projective closure](../../../projective-space.md#projective-completion)

$$
C=\{[X:Y:Z]\in\mathbb P^2:
X^3Y+Y^3Z+Z^3X=0\}.
$$

This is the [Klein quartic](../../../algebraic-geometry.md#klein-quartic). It is smooth: if all three projective partial derivatives vanished, then

$$
3X^2Y=-Z^3,
\qquad
3Y^2Z=-X^3,
\qquad
3Z^2X=-Y^3.
$$

Multiplying them gives $28X^3Y^3Z^3=0$. If one coordinate is zero, the displayed equations force all three to vanish, which is impossible in projective space. Thus $C$ is a smooth compactification of $X'$. The smooth compactification compatible with the extended coordinate functions is its normalization, and since $C$ is already smooth it is biholomorphic to $C$.

For a direct genus computation, consider the extended coordinate map $\pi_x:X\to\mathbb C_\infty$. A generic $x$ leaves the cubic equation

$$
y^3+x^3y+x=0,
$$

so $\deg\pi_x=3$. In the affine part, ramification occurs where $F_y=0$. Solving $F=F_y=0$ gives $(0,0)$ and the seven points

$$
y^7=-\frac38,
\qquad
x=2y^3.
$$

At the origin, $x=-y^3+O(y^6)$, so the [ramification index of a holomorphic map](../../../complex-analysis.md#ramification-index-of-a-holomorphic-map) is three and the contribution is two. At each of the other seven points, $F_x=-7/2\ne0$, $F_y=0$, and $F_{yy}=6y\ne0$, so the ramification index is two and each contributes one.

There are two points at infinity. Near $P=[1:0:0]$, in coordinates $u=Y/X$, $v=Z/X$, the equation is

$$
u+u^3v+v^3=0,
$$

and $1/x=v$, so $P$ is unramified. Near $Q=[0:1:0]$, in coordinates $r=X/Y$, $s=Z/Y$, the equation is

$$
r^3+s+rs^3=0.
$$

Thus $s=-r^3+O(r^{10})$ and $1/x=s/r=-r^2+O(r^9)$, so $Q$ has ramification index two and contributes one. The total ramification is therefore

$$
2+7+1=10.
$$

The [Riemann-Hurwitz formula](../../../complex-analysis.md#riemann-hurwitz-formula) for the degree-three map to the sphere gives

$$
2g(X)-2=3(2\cdot0-2)+10=4,
$$

and hence

$$
\boxed{g(X)=3}.
$$

This also agrees with the [genus of a smooth plane curve](../../../algebraic-geometry.md#genus-of-a-smooth-plane-curve) of degree four.

↑ **Parent:** [24F](#24f)

<h4 id="24f/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#24f/c)

Let $\zeta=e^{2\pi i/7}$ and define

$$
\phi(x,y)=(\zeta^3x,\zeta y).
$$

Each term in the defining polynomial is multiplied by $\zeta^3$:

$$
(\zeta^3x)^3(\zeta y)+(\zeta y)^3+\zeta^3x
=\zeta^3(x^3y+y^3+x).
$$

Thus $\phi$ is a holomorphic automorphism of $X'$, with inverse obtained by replacing $\zeta$ by $\zeta^{-1}$. By the assumption it extends to a holomorphic automorphism of $X$; in the projective model it is

$$
[X:Y:Z]\longmapsto[\zeta^3X:\zeta Y:Z].
$$

It has order exactly seven because $\zeta$ is primitive and $y$ is not identically zero. Hence $\operatorname{Aut}(X)$ contains an element of order seven.

The extended coordinate function $\pi_x:X\to\mathbb C_\infty$ is a [meromorphic function as a holomorphic map to the Riemann sphere](../../../isolated-singularity.md#meromorphic-function-as-a-holomorphic-map-to-the-riemann-sphere). Compose it with the seventh-power map of the sphere and set

$$
\pi=\pi_x^7.
$$

This map is nonconstant and holomorphic as a map to $\mathbb C_\infty$. Since $\pi_x\circ\phi=\zeta^3\pi_x$,

$$
\boxed{\pi\circ\phi
=(\zeta^3\pi_x)^7
=\pi_x^7
=\pi}.
$$

\boxed{I(V)

## 26H

<h3 id="26h/a">a</h3>

<h4 id="26h/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#26h/a)

Define the [smooth function](../../../analysis.md#smooth-function)

$$
F:\mathbb R^{n+1}\longrightarrow\mathbb R,
\qquad
F(x)=\lVert x\rVert^2.
$$

At $x\in F^{-1}(1)$ its derivative is

$$
D_xF(v)=2x\mathbin\cdot v.
$$

This linear map is surjective because $x\ne0$; for example, $D_xF(x)=2$. Hence one is a [regular value](../../../differential-geometry.md#regular-value), and the [regular level set theorem](../../../differential-geometry.md#regular-level-set-theorem) makes

$$
S^n=F^{-1}(1)
$$

an embedded codimension-one submanifold of $\mathbb R^{n+1}$. Its tangent space is the kernel of the derivative:

$$
\boxed{T_xS^n
=\ker D_xF
=\{v:x\mathbin\cdot v=0\}
=x^\perp}.
$$

This is the [unit sphere as a regular level set](../../../geometry-and-topology.md#unit-sphere-as-a-regular-level-set).

<h3 id="26h/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [26H](#26h)

<h4 id="26h/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#26h/b)

Let $\operatorname{Sym}_n$ be the vector space of real symmetric matrices and consider

$$
\Phi:M_n(\mathbb R)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Sym}_n,
\qquad
\Phi(A)=A^TA.
$$

At $R\in O(n)$,

$$
D_R\Phi(H)=R^TH+H^TR.
$$

This derivative is surjective: for $S\in\operatorname{Sym}_n$, choosing $H=RS/2$ gives $D_R\Phi(H)=S$. Thus $I$ is a regular value and the [orthogonal group](../../../linear-algebra.md#orthogonal-group) $O(n)=\Phi^{-1}(I)$ is a submanifold of codimension

$$
\dim\operatorname{Sym}_n=\frac{n(n+1)}2.
$$

The determinant takes only the values $\pm1$ on $O(n)$, so the [special orthogonal group](../../../linear-algebra.md#special-orthogonal-group) $SO(n)=\{R\in O(n):\det R=1\}$ is an open-and-closed submanifold of $O(n)$. Therefore

$$
\dim SO(n)=n^2-\frac{n(n+1)}2=\frac{n(n-1)}2.
$$

The tangent space is the kernel of $D_R\Phi$:

$$
\boxed{T_RSO(n)
=\{H:R^TH+H^TR=0\}
=\{RA:A\in\mathfrak{so}(n)\}},
$$

where the [Special orthogonal Lie algebra](../../../semisimple-lie-algebra.md#special-orthogonal-lie-algebra) $\mathfrak{so}(n)$ is the vector space of [skew-symmetric matrices](../../../linear-algebra.md#skew-symmetric-matrix). This proves the [special orthogonal group as a submanifold](../../../linear-algebra.md#special-orthogonal-group-as-a-submanifold) description.

<h3 id="26h/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [26H](#26h)

<h4 id="26h/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#26h/c)

Choose $Q\in SO(n+1)$ with $Qe_{n+1}=v$. Conjugation by $Q$ identifies

$$
S_v=\{R\in SO(n+1):Rv=v\}
$$

with

$$
\left\{
\begin{pmatrix}A&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}:A\in SO(n)
\right\}.
$$

It follows that the [unit-vector stabilizer in a special orthogonal group](../../../linear-algebra.md#unit-vector-stabilizer-in-a-special-orthogonal-group) is an embedded submanifold diffeomorphic to $SO(n)$, and

$$
\boxed{\dim S_v=\frac{n(n-1)}2}.
$$

At $R\in S_v$, its tangent space is

$$
T_RS_v=\{RA:A^T=-A,\ Av=0\}.
$$

For any $v\ne w$, both $S_v$ and $S_w$ contain the identity. At the identity,

$$
T_IS_v=\{A\in\mathfrak{so}(n+1):Av=0\},
\qquad
T_IS_w=\{A\in\mathfrak{so}(n+1):Aw=0\}.
$$

Identify $\mathfrak{so}(n+1)$ with the exterior square $\Lambda^2\mathbb R^{n+1}$. The orthogonal complement of $T_IS_v=\Lambda^2(v^\perp)$ is $v\wedge v^\perp$. If $v$ and $w$ are linearly independent, the nonzero bivector $v\wedge w$ belongs to the orthogonal complements of both tangent spaces. Their sum is therefore a proper subspace of $T_ISO(n+1)$, so the intersection is not [transverse](../../../differential-geometry.md#transverse-intersection). If $w=-v$, then $S_w=S_v$, whose tangent space is proper, so they are again not transverse. Consequently

$$
\boxed{S_v\text{ and }S_w\text{ are never transverse when }v\ne w},
$$

as summarized by [distinct unit-vector stabilizers are not transverse](../../../linear-algebra.md#distinct-unit-vector-stabilizers-are-not-transverse).

<h3 id="27k/a">a</h3>

<h4 id="27k/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#27k/a)

For $d=1$ and $X\sim N(0,\sigma^2)$, the [characteristic function](../../../probability-theory.md#characteristic-function) is

$$
\boxed{\varphi_X(u)=e^{-\sigma^2u^2/2}}.
$$

For a [multivariate normal distribution](../../../probability-and-statistics.md#multivariate-normal-distribution) with covariance matrix $\sigma^2I_d$, the coordinates are uncorrelated and hence independent by [independence of uncorrelated jointly normal variables](../../../probability-and-statistics.md#independence-of-uncorrelated-jointly-normal-variables). Therefore

$$
\varphi_X(u)
=\prod_{j=1}^d\mathbb E[e^{iu_jX_j}]
=\prod_{j=1}^de^{-\sigma^2u_j^2/2}
=\boxed{\exp\left(-\frac{\sigma^2}{2}\sum_{j=1}^du_j^2\right)}.
$$

This is the [characteristic function of an isotropic Gaussian vector](../../../probability-theory.md#characteristic-function-of-an-isotropic-gaussian-vector).

<h3 id="27k/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [27K](#27k)

<h4 id="27k/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#27k/b)

<h5 id="27k/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#27k/b/i)

For an [orthogonal matrix](../../../linear-algebra.md#orthogonal-matrix) $A$, invariance in distribution gives

$$
\varphi_X(A^Tu)
=\varphi_{AX}(u)
=\varphi_X(u).
$$

The orthogonal group is transitive on each Euclidean sphere, so $\varphi_X(u)$ depends only on $\lVert u\rVert^2$. Define

$$
f(r)=\varphi_X(\sqrt r,e_1),
\qquad r\geq0.
$$

The [continuity of a characteristic function](../../../probability-theory.md#continuity-of-a-characteristic-function) makes $f$ continuous. Taking $A=-I$ also gives $X\stackrel d=-X$, and hence

$$
\varphi_X(u)=\varphi_X(-u)=\overline{\varphi_X(u)},
$$

so $f$ is real-valued. Thus

$$
\boxed{\varphi_X(u)=f(u_1^2+\cdots+u_d^2)},
$$

as for every [orthogonally invariant random vector](../../../probability-theory.md#orthogonally-invariant-random-vector).

<h4 id="27k/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#27k/b)

<h5 id="27k/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#27k/b/ii)

Independence of the coordinates gives factorization of their joint [characteristic function of a random vector](../../../probability-theory.md#characteristic-function-of-a-random-vector). Orthogonal invariance under coordinate permutations makes all coordinate marginals identical. Hence, for $r_1,r_2\geq0$ and $d\geq2$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(r_1+r_2)
&=\varphi_X(\sqrt{r_1}e_1+\sqrt{r_2}e_2)\\
&=\varphi_{X_1}(\sqrt{r_1})
  \varphi_{X_2}(\sqrt{r_2})\\
&=f(r_1)f(r_2).
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{f(r_1+r_2)=f(r_1)f(r_2)}.
$$

<h4 id="27k/b/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#27k/b)

<h5 id="27k/b/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#27k/b/iii)

First, $f(0)=1$. For every $r\geq0$,

$$
f(r)=f(r/2)^2\geq0.
$$

It cannot vanish: if $f(r_0)=0$ for some $r_0>0$, then

$$
0=f(r_0)=f(r_0/n)^n
$$

would imply $f(r_0/n)=0$ for every positive integer $n$, contradicting continuity at zero. Thus $f(r)>0$. Since every characteristic function has modulus at most one,

$$
\boxed{0<f(r)\leq1}.
$$

Now set $h(r)=-\log f(r)$. It is continuous, nonnegative, and additive:

$$
h(r_1+r_2)=h(r_1)+h(r_2).
$$

The classification of a [continuous additive function on the nonnegative real numbers](../../../analysis.md#continuous-additive-function-on-the-nonnegative-real-numbers) gives $h(r)=\alpha r$, where $\alpha=h(1)\geq0$. Consequently

$$
\boxed{f(r)=e^{-\alpha r}\qquad(r\geq0)}.
$$

<h4 id="27k/b/iv">iv</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#27k/b)

<h5 id="27k/b/iv/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#27k/b/iv)

Combining the preceding parts,

$$
\varphi_X(u)=e^{-\alpha\lVert u\rVert^2}.
$$

This is the characteristic function of $N(0,2\alpha I_d)$. The [uniqueness theorem for characteristic functions](../../../probability-theory.md#uniqueness-theorem-for-characteristic-functions) therefore gives

$$
\boxed{X\sim N(0,2\alpha I_d)}.
$$

When $\alpha=0$, this means the degenerate distribution concentrated at the origin. This proves the [Maxwell characterization of the normal distribution](../../../probability-theory.md#maxwell-characterization-of-the-normal-distribution).

<h4 id="28k/a/solution">Solution</h4>

Conditional on $Y_m=i$, the [holding time](../../../markov-process.md#holding-time) $V_m=U_m/g(i)$ has an [exponential distribution](../../../continuous-probability-distribution.md#exponential-distribution) of rate $g(i)$, and after that holding time the next state is $j$ with [transition probability](../../../markov-process.md#transition-probability) $p_{ij}$. The [memoryless property](../../../continuous-probability-distribution.md#memorylessness-of-the-exponential-distribution) of exponential random variables and the [Markov property](../../../markov-process.md#markov-property) of $(Y_m)$ therefore make $(X_t)$ a [continuous-time Markov chain](../../../markov-process.md#continuous-time-markov-chain).

Moreover $g(i)<1/\varepsilon$, so $V_m\geq\varepsilon U_m$. The [strong law of large numbers](../../../convergence-of-random-variables.md#strong-law-of-large-numbers) gives $\sum_mU_m=\infty$ almost surely, hence $J_m\to\infty$ and the process is [nonexplosive](../../../markov-process.md#nonexplosion-under-uniformly-bounded-jump-rates). Its [Q-matrix](../../../markov-process.md#transition-rate-matrix) is

$$
\boxed{q_{ij}=g(i)p_{ij}\quad(i\ne j),\qquad q_{ii}=-g(i).}
$$

Equivalently, if $D_g$ is the [diagonal matrix](../../../linear-algebra.md#diagonal-matrix) with entries $g(i)$, then

$$
\boxed{Q=D_g(P-I).}
$$

<h3 id="28k/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [28K](#28k)

<h4 id="28k/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#28k/b)

A [continuous-time Markov chain](../../../markov-process.md#continuous-time-markov-chain) is [irreducible](../../../markov-process.md#irreducible-markov-chain) if every state can reach every other state with positive probability. A state $i$ is [recurrent](../../../markov-process.md#recurrent-state) if, after leaving $i$, the chain returns to $i$ [with probability one](../../../convergence-of-random-variables.md#almost-sure-convergence); an irreducible chain is recurrent if one, and hence every, state is recurrent. A recurrent state is [positive recurrent](../../../markov-process.md#positive-recurrent-state) if its expected continuous [first return time](../../../markov-process.md#first-return-time) is finite, and an irreducible chain is positive recurrent when all its states are.

<h3 id="28k/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [28K](#28k)

<h4 id="28k/c/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#28k/c)

<h5 id="28k/c/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#28k/c/i)

The [jump chain](../../../markov-process.md#jump-chain) of $(X_t)$ is exactly $(Y_m)$: from $i$ it visits $j$ next with probability $p_{ij}$. Consequently the two chains have exactly the same directed paths of positive-probability transitions. Thus

$$
\boxed{X\text{ is irreducible}\iff Y\text{ is irreducible}.}
$$

<h4 id="28k/c/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#28k/c)

<h5 id="28k/c/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#28k/c/ii)

Because $J_m\to\infty$ almost surely, no infinite portion of the [jump chain](../../../markov-process.md#jump-chain) is hidden inside a finite time interval. After either chain leaves a state $i$, the event that it ever returns is therefore the same event for both chains. Hence [irreducibility and recurrence pass between a CTMC and its jump chain](../../../markov-process.md#irreducibility-and-recurrence-pass-between-a-ctmc-and-its-jump-chain) gives

$$
\boxed{X\text{ is recurrent}\iff Y\text{ is recurrent}.}
$$

<h3 id="28k/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [28K](#28k)

<h4 id="28k/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#28k/d)

Let $\nu$ be an [invariant distribution](../../../markov-process.md#stationary-distribution) for the [continuous-time Markov chain](../../../markov-process.md#continuous-time-markov-chain). Using $Q=D_g(P-I)$, its [stationarity equation](../../../markov-process.md#invariant-distribution-of-a-continuous-time-markov-chain) $\nu Q=0$ becomes

$$
(\nu D_g)P=\nu D_g.
$$

Thus the measure with weights $\nu_i g(i)$ is invariant for the irreducible positive-recurrent [jump chain](../../../markov-process.md#jump-chain). Its [invariant distribution](../../../markov-process.md#stationary-distribution) $\pi$ is unique, so $\nu_i g(i)=c\pi_i$. Normalization gives the [invariant-measure transfer between a jump chain and a CTMC](../../../markov-process.md#invariant-measure-transfer-between-a-jump-chain-and-a-ctmc) formula

$$
\boxed{\nu_i=\frac{\pi_i/g(i)}{\displaystyle\sum_{j\in S}\pi_j/g(j)}}.
$$

The denominator is finite and nonzero because $\varepsilon<g(j)<1/\varepsilon$. Thus $\nu$ is a [probability distribution](../../../probability-theory.md#probability-distribution) and $(X_t)$ is positive recurrent.

## 29J

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="29j/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [29J](#29j)

<h4 id="29j/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#29j/a)

The [Gaussian likelihood](../../../statistical-modelling.md#gaussian-likelihood) is proportional to

$$
\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\lVert Y-X\theta\rVert_2^2\right),
$$

so [maximum likelihood estimation](../../../statistical-modelling.md#maximum-likelihood-estimation) is equivalent to [ordinary least squares](../../../statistical-modelling.md#ordinary-least-squares). If $p\leq n$ and $X$ has [rank](../../../linear-algebra.md#rank-one-quadratic-form) $p$, then $X^TX$ is invertible and the [normal equations](../../../statistical-modelling.md#normal-equation) give

$$
\boxed{\widehat\theta_{\rm MLE}=(X^TX)^{-1}X^TY.}
$$

If $p>n$, then $\operatorname{rank}X<p$ and $\ker X\ne\{0\}$. A least-squares minimizer always exists, but it is not unique: by [rank-deficient ordinary least squares](../../../statistical-modelling.md#rank-deficient-ordinary-least-squares), the complete set is

$$
X^+Y+\ker X,
$$

an [affine subspace](../../../vector-space.md#affine-subspace) of dimension $p-\operatorname{rank}X>0$. Thus there are uncountably infinitely many likelihood maximizers. In the generic full-row-rank case their dimension is $p-n$.

<h3 id="29j/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [29J](#29j)

<h4 id="29j/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#29j/b)

Differentiating the [ridge regression](../../../linear-regression.md#ridge-regression) objective gives

$$
\nabla_\theta\bigl(\lVert Y-X\theta\rVert_2^2+\lambda\lVert\theta\rVert_2^2\bigr)
=2(X^TX+\lambda I_p)\theta-2X^TY.
$$

For every nonzero $z$,

$$
z^T(X^TX+\lambda I_p)z=\lVert Xz\rVert_2^2+\lambda\lVert z\rVert_2^2>0,
$$

so $X^TX+\lambda I_p$ is [positive definite](../../../linear-algebra.md#positive-definite-matrix) and hence invertible. The strictly convex objective therefore has the unique [closed-form ridge regression estimator](../../../linear-regression.md#closed-form-ridge-regression-estimator)

$$
\boxed{\widehat\theta_\lambda=(X^TX+\lambda I_p)^{-1}X^TY.}
$$

<h3 id="29j/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [29J](#29j)

<h4 id="29j/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#29j/c)

Because every column of $X$ has [sample mean](../../../variance.md#sample-mean) zero, every column $u_i$ of $U=XV$ also has sample mean zero. The [spectral theorem for real symmetric matrices](../../../linear-algebra.md#spectral-theorem-for-real-symmetric-matrices) gives

$$
U^TU=V^TX^TXV=V^T\Sigma V=\Lambda.
$$

Hence for $i\ne j$ the [sample covariance](../../../variance.md#sample-covariance) is $n^{-1}u_i^Tu_j=0$, while the [sample variance](../../../statistical-inference.md#sample-variance) of $u_i$ is

$$
\frac1n u_i^Tu_i=\frac{\Lambda_{ii}}n.
$$

**Thus the $u_i$ are pairwise uncorrelated [sample principal components](../../../statistical-learning.md#sample-principal-component), ordered by decreasing sample variance.**

<h3 id="29j/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [29J](#29j)

<h4 id="29j/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#29j/d)

Since $X=UV^T$ and $X^TX=V\Lambda V^T$,

$$
\widehat Y_{\rm MLE}
=X(X^TX)^{-1}X^TY
=U\Lambda^{-1}U^TY.
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{\widehat Y_{\rm MLE}=U\Lambda^{-1}U^TY.}
$$

Put $w_i=u_i/\sqrt{\Lambda_{ii}}$. By part (c), the $w_i$ are [normalized sample principal components](../../../statistical-learning.md#normalized-sample-principal-component) forming an [orthonormal set](../../../linear-algebra.md#orthonormal-set), and

$$
U\Lambda^{-1}U^T=\sum_{i=1}^p w_iw_i^T.
$$

This is the [orthogonal projection onto a finite-dimensional subspace](../../../linear-algebra.md#orthogonal-projection-onto-a-finite-dimensional-subspace) spanned by the $w_i$, so $\widehat Y_{\rm MLE}$ is the unique closest point in that subspace to $Y$.

<h3 id="29j/e">e</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [29J](#29j)

<h4 id="29j/e/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [E](#29j/e)

Using the estimator from part (b) and the [spectral decomposition](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#spectral-decomposition) of $X^TX$,

$$
\widehat Y_\lambda
=XV(\Lambda+\lambda I_p)^{-1}V^TX^TY
=U(\Lambda+\lambda I_p)^{-1}U^TY.
$$

Thus

$$
\boxed{\widehat Y_\lambda=U(\Lambda+\lambda I_p)^{-1}U^TY
=\sum_{i=1}^p\frac{\Lambda_{ii}}{\Lambda_{ii}+\lambda}w_iw_i^TY.}
$$

Under the stated separation of scales, the shrinkage factor is approximately one for $i\leq q$ and approximately zero for $i>q$. The [principal-component shrinkage by ridge regression](../../../linear-regression.md#principal-component-shrinkage-by-ridge-regression) formula therefore reduces approximately to

$$
\widehat Y_\lambda\approx\sum_{i=1}^q w_iw_i^TY,
$$

the [orthogonal projection](../../../hilbert-space.md#orthogonal-projection) of $Y$ onto the span of the $q$ normalized sample principal components with greatest variance.

## 30K

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="30k/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [30K](#30k)

<h4 id="30k/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#30k/a)

The pair $(M_n,\mathcal F_n)_{n\geq0}$ is a [martingale](../../../martingale.md) if $(\mathcal F_n)$ is a [filtration](../../../stochastic-process.md#filtration-probability-theory), $(M_n)$ is an [adapted process](../../../stochastic-process.md#adapted-process), every $M_n$ is [integrable](../../../probability-theory.md#integrable-random-variable), and

$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[M_n\mid\mathcal F_{n-1}]=M_{n-1}\quad\text{almost surely for every }n\geq1.}
$$

<h3 id="30k/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [30K](#30k)

<h4 id="30k/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#30k/b)

The process is adapted to its [natural filtration](../../../stochastic-process.md#natural-filtration). At each fixed time it has finite support and is therefore integrable. Conditional on $X_{n-1}=0$,

$$
\mathbb E[X_n\mid X_{n-1}=0]
=\frac1{2n}-\frac1{2n}=0=X_{n-1}.
$$

Conditional on $X_{n-1}\ne0$,

$$
\mathbb E[X_n\mid X_{n-1}]
=\frac1n(nX_{n-1})+\left(1-\frac1n\right)0
=X_{n-1}.
$$

The [Markov property](../../../markov-process.md#markov-property) now gives

$$
\mathbb E[X_n\mid\mathcal F_{n-1}]
=\mathbb E[X_n\mid X_{n-1}]=X_{n-1},
$$

so $(X_n)$ is a [martingale](../../../martingale.md).

<h3 id="30k/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [30K](#30k)

<h4 id="30k/c/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#30k/c)

<h5 id="30k/c/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#30k/c/i)

Assume (i), and let $\tau$ be a [stopping time](../../../martingale.md#stopping-time). The increments of the [stopped process](../../../martingale.md#stopped-martingale-in-discrete-time) satisfy

$$
M_{(n+1)\wedge\tau}-M_{n\wedge\tau}
=\mathbf1_{\{\tau>n\}}(M_{n+1}-M_n).
$$

Because $\{\tau>n\}\in\mathcal F_n$, taking [conditional expectation](../../../measure-theory.md#conditional-expectation) given $\mathcal F_n$ makes the right-hand side zero. Integrability follows from

$$
M_{n\wedge\tau}=M_0+\sum_{k=1}^n\mathbf1_{\{\tau\geq k\}}(M_k-M_{k-1}),
$$

a finite sum of integrable random variables. Hence $M^\tau$ is a martingale, proving (ii).

<h4 id="30k/c/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#30k/c)

<h5 id="30k/c/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#30k/c/ii)

Assume (ii). A [martingale](../../../martingale.md) has constant [expected value](../../../probability-theory.md#expected-value) by the [tower property of conditional expectation](../../../measure-theory.md#law-of-total-expectation), so for every [stopping time](../../../martingale.md#stopping-time) $\tau$ and every $n$,

$$
\mathbb E[M_{n\wedge\tau}]=\mathbb E[M_0].
$$

This is (iii).

<h4 id="30k/c/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#30k/c)

<h5 id="30k/c/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#30k/c/iii)

Assume (iii). Fix $n\geq0$ and $A\in\mathcal F_n$, and define

$$
T=n\mathbf1_A+(n+1)\mathbf1_{A^c}.
$$

This is a [stopping time](../../../martingale.md#stopping-time): $\{T\leq n\}=A\in\mathcal F_n$. Applying (iii) at time $n+1$ first to $T$ and then to the deterministic stopping time $n+1$ gives

$$
\mathbb E[\mathbf1_AM_n+\mathbf1_{A^c}M_{n+1}]
=\mathbb E[M_0]
=\mathbb E[M_{n+1}].
$$

Subtracting yields

$$
\mathbb E[\mathbf1_A(M_{n+1}-M_n)]=0
\qquad(A\in\mathcal F_n).
$$

By the defining characterization of [conditional expectation](../../../measure-theory.md#conditional-expectation), this says $\mathbb E[M_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n]=M_n$. Thus $M$ is a martingale, proving (i). Together the three implications establish the [characterization of a martingale by stopped expectations](../../../martingale.md#characterization-of-a-martingale-by-stopped-expectations).

<h3 id="31e/solution">Solution</h3>

Let $\phi_t(x)$ denote the [flow](../../../dynamical-systems.md#flow-map) through $x$. Its [alpha-limit set](../../../dynamical-systems.md#alpha-limit-set) and [omega-limit set](../../../dynamical-systems.md#omega-limit-set) are

$$
\alpha(x)=\bigcap_{T<0}\overline{\{\phi_t(x):t<T\}},
\qquad
\omega(x)=\bigcap_{T>0}\overline{\{\phi_t(x):t>T\}}.
$$

Equivalently, they contain the limits of $\phi_{t_j}(x)$ along sequences $t_j\to-\infty$ and $t_j\to+\infty$, respectively.

<h3 id="31e/i">i</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [31E](#31e)

<h4 id="31e/i/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#31e/i)

The [vector field](../../../calculus.md#vector-field) is unchanged by reflection in the $x$-axis, $(x,y)\mapsto(x,-y)$; more generally, linearity of the second equation gives the scaling symmetry $(x,y)\mapsto(x,cy)$. It is also reversible under reflection in the $y$-axis:

$$
(x(t),y(t))\longmapsto(-x(-t),y(-t)).
$$

When $k=0$, $y$ is constant, so arbitrary translations $y\mapsto y+c$ are additional symmetries.

<h3 id="31e/ii">ii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [31E](#31e)

<h4 id="31e/ii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#31e/ii)

At an [equilibrium point](../../../dynamical-systems.md#equilibrium-point-of-a-dynamical-system), $x^2-1=0$ and $kxy=0$. If $k\ne0$, the only equilibria are

$$
(1,0),\qquad(-1,0).
$$

Their [Jacobian matrix](../../../calculus.md#jacobian-matrix) is

$$
DF(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}2x&0\\ky&kx\end{pmatrix}.
$$

At $(1,0)$ its [eigenvalues](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) are $2,k$, and at $(-1,0)$ they are $-2,-k$. Hence for $k>0$, $(1,0)$ is an [unstable node](../../../dynamical-systems.md#unstable-node) and $(-1,0)$ is a [stable node](../../../dynamical-systems.md#stable-node); for $k<0$, both points are [saddles](../../../dynamical-systems.md#saddle-equilibrium).

If $k=0$, every point on each line $x=1$ and $x=-1$ is an equilibrium. The line $x=1$ is transversely repelling because the nonzero eigenvalue is $2$, while $x=-1$ is transversely attracting because the nonzero eigenvalue is $-2$; the tangential eigenvalue is zero on both nonisolated equilibrium lines.

<h3 id="31e/iii">iii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [31E](#31e)

<h4 id="31e/iii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#31e/iii)

Away from $x=\pm1$, the sign of $\dot x=x^2-1$ is fixed within each of the three invariant strips, so $x(t)$ is strictly monotone. On $x=\pm1$, the equation is $\dot y=\pm ky$, whose nonconstant solutions are exponential and hence not periodic. Therefore the [monotone coordinate excludes periodic orbits](../../../dynamical-systems.md#monotone-coordinate-excludes-periodic-orbits), and there are

$$
\boxed{\text{no nonconstant periodic orbits for any }k.}
$$

<h3 id="31e/iv">iv</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [31E](#31e)

<h4 id="31e/iv/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#31e/iv)

For $x\ne\pm1$, eliminating time gives the [separable differential equation](../../../differential-equation.md#separable-differential-equation)

$$
\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{kxy}{x^2-1},
$$

and hence

$$
y=C|x^2-1|^{k/2}.
$$

Define the possible limit at $x=s\in\{-1,1\}$ for a trajectory starting off the boundary lines by

$$
L_s=
\begin{cases}
\{(s,0)\},&k>0\text{ or }y_0=0,\\
\{(s,y_0)\},&k=0,\\
\varnothing,&k<0\text{ and }y_0\ne0.
\end{cases}
$$

The $k<0$ set is empty because $|y|\to\infty$, so there is no finite accumulation point. Since $\dot x<0$ on $(-1,1)$ and $\dot x>0$ outside $[-1,1]$, the complete classification off the boundary lines is

$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
\text{initial }x_0&\alpha(x_0,y_0)&\omega(x_0,y_0)\\ \hline
x_0<-1&\varnothing&L_{-1}\\
-1<x_0<1&L_1&L_{-1}\\
x_0>1&L_1&\varnothing.
\end{array}
$$

For $x_0>1$ the solution has forward [finite-time blow-up](../../../dynamical-systems.md#finite-time-blow-up-of-an-ordinary-differential-equation), and for $x_0<-1$ it has backward finite-time blow-up, explaining the other empty entries under the usual infinite-time definitions.

It remains to treat $x_0=s=\pm1$. Here $x(t)=s$ and $y(t)=y_0e^{kst}$. If $y_0=0$, both limit sets equal $\{(s,0)\}$. If $y_0\ne0$, then

$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
ks&\alpha(s,y_0)&\omega(s,y_0)\\ \hline
ks>0&\{(s,0)\}&\varnothing\\
ks=0&\{(s,y_0)\}&\{(s,y_0)\}\\
ks<0&\varnothing&\{(s,0)\}.
\end{array}
$$

This includes all boundary cases in both $k$ and the initial point.

<h3 id="31e/v">v</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [31E](#31e)

<h4 id="31e/v/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [V](#31e/v)

The initial point has $x_0=0$, so its orbit remains in $-1<x<1$. Integrating the orbit equation from part (iv) and using $y(0)=y_0$ gives

$$
\boxed{y(x)=y_0(1-x^2)^{k/2},\qquad -1<x<1.}
$$

This formula also covers $y_0=0$ and $k=0$ within the open strip. The endpoints are approached only as infinite time limits and have the behavior classified in part (iv).

## 32C

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="32c/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [32C](#32c)

<h4 id="32c/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#32c/a)

In canonical coordinates, [Hamilton's equations](../../../classical-mechanics.md#hamilton-s-equations) are

$$
\boxed{\dot q_i=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i},\qquad
\dot p_i=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i}\quad(1\leq i\leq n).}
$$

A function $F(q,p)$ is an [integral of motion](../../../classical-mechanics.md#integral-of-motion) if it is constant along every [Hamiltonian flow](../../../classical-mechanics.md#hamiltonian-flow), equivalently

$$
\dot F=\{F,H\}=0
$$

for a time-independent $F$. The system is an [integrable Hamiltonian system](../../../classical-mechanics.md#integrable-hamiltonian-system) when it has $n$ first integrals $F_1,\ldots,F_n$ whose [differentials](../../../calculus.md#total-differential) are linearly independent almost everywhere and which are in mutual [Poisson involution](../../../classical-mechanics.md#poisson-bracket): $\{F_i,F_j\}=0$ for all $i,j$.

<h3 id="32c/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [32C](#32c)

<h4 id="32c/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#32c/b)

For one degree of freedom, take $F_1=H$. Antisymmetry of the [Poisson bracket](../../../classical-mechanics.md#poisson-bracket) gives $\{H,H\}=0$, so [energy is conserved](../../../classical-mechanics.md#hamiltonian-conservation-law). The stated condition is exactly $dH\ne0$, which supplies the required functional independence. Hence the system is integrable at every such regular point.

<h3 id="32c/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [32C](#32c)

<h4 id="32c/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#32c/c)

Let $F_1,\ldots,F_n$ be commuting independent integrals for $(M,H)$ and let $G_1,\ldots,G_m$ be such integrals for $(\widetilde M,\widetilde H)$. Regard each as a function on the product [phase space](../../../classical-mechanics.md#phase-space). Then

$$
\{F_i,H+\widetilde H\}=\{F_i,H\}=0,
\qquad
\{G_a,H+\widetilde H\}=\{G_a,\widetilde H\}=0.
$$

The $F_i$ commute among themselves, the $G_a$ commute among themselves, and every cross bracket $\{F_i,G_a\}$ vanishes because the functions depend on disjoint canonical coordinates. Their differentials form two independent coordinate blocks, so all $n+m$ are independent on the product of the two regular sets. Thus the combined system is integrable, an instance of the [direct product of integrable Hamiltonian systems](../../../classical-mechanics.md#direct-product-of-integrable-hamiltonian-systems).

<h3 id="32c/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [32C](#32c)

<h4 id="32c/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#32c/d)

Write the [Hamiltonian](../../../classical-mechanics.md#hamiltonian) as the sum of separated one-dimensional energies

$$
H=\sum_{i=1}^n H_i,
\qquad
H_i=\frac12\left(p_i^2+\omega_i^2q_i^2\right).
$$

Each $(q_i,p_i)$ system is a [simple harmonic oscillator](../../../classical-mechanics.md#simple-harmonic-motion) and is integrable away from $(q_i,p_i)=(0,0)$ by part (b). The separated energies satisfy

$$
\{H_i,H_j\}=0,
$$

and their differentials are independent whenever every oscillator pair is nonzero. The [Poisson-commuting separated energies](../../../integrable-systems.md#poisson-commuting-separated-energies) therefore make the $n$-dimensional oscillator integrable. The exceptional coordinate strata have measure zero, so this proves [Liouville integrability](../../../classical-mechanics.md#integrable-hamiltonian-system) in its usual almost-everywhere sense; the origin is the only point where $dH$ itself vanishes.

<h3 id="32c/e">e</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [32C](#32c)

<h4 id="32c/e/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [E](#32c/e)

**No.** Consider two uncoupled oscillators with nonzero amplitudes:

$$
q_i(t)=A_i\cos(\omega_it+\phi_i),
\qquad
p_i(t)=-A_i\omega_i\sin(\omega_it+\phi_i),
\qquad i=1,2.
$$

The orbit is bounded. If it had a [period](../../../dynamical-systems.md#periodic-orbit) $T>0$, then $\omega_1T$ and $\omega_2T$ would both be integer multiples of $2\pi$, forcing $\omega_1/\omega_2\in\mathbb Q$. Choosing an irrational frequency ratio gives an [incommensurate harmonic-oscillator orbit](../../../classical-mechanics.md#incommensurate-harmonic-oscillator-orbit), which is quasiperiodic on an [invariant torus](../../../classical-mechanics.md#invariant-torus) and not periodic.

<h3 id="33b/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [33B](#33b)

The [canonical commutation relations](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#canonical-commutation-relation) are $[X_i,X_j]=[P_i,P_j]=0$ and $[X_i,P_j]=i\hbar\delta_{ij}$. Substitution into the definitions of the [creation and annihilation operators](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#creation-and-annihilation-operators) gives

$$
\boxed{[A_i^\dagger,A_j^\dagger]=0,\qquad[A_i,A_j]=0,\qquad[A_i,A_j^\dagger]=\delta_{ij}.}
$$

The normalized [ground state](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#ground-state) is defined by

$$
A_i|0\rangle=0\quad(i=1,2,3),
\qquad \langle0|0\rangle=1.
$$

Repeated use of $[A_i,A_i^\dagger]=1$ gives the [Cartesian number state of the three-dimensional isotropic harmonic oscillator](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#cartesian-number-state-of-the-three-dimensional-isotropic-harmonic-oscillator)

$$
\boxed{|n_1,n_2,n_3\rangle
=\frac{(A_1^\dagger)^{n_1}(A_2^\dagger)^{n_2}(A_3^\dagger)^{n_3}}
{\sqrt{n_1!n_2!n_3!}}|0\rangle,}
$$

with [energy eigenvalue](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#energy-eigenvalue)

$$
E_{n_1n_2n_3}=\hbar\omega\left(n_1+n_2+n_3+\frac32\right).
$$

Inverting the ladder-operator definitions yields

$$
X_i=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2\mu\omega}}(A_i+A_i^\dagger),
\qquad
P_i=i\sqrt{\frac{\mu\hbar\omega}{2}}(A_i^\dagger-A_i).
$$

The antisymmetry of the [Levi-Civita symbol](../../../calculus.md#levi-civita-symbol) then cancels the two-creation and two-annihilation terms in $\mathbf L=\mathbf X\times\mathbf P$, leaving the [orbital angular momentum in oscillator ladder operators](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#orbital-angular-momentum-in-oscillator-ladder-operators)

$$
\boxed{L_i=-i\hbar\varepsilon_{ijk}A_j^\dagger A_k.}
$$

Write $|j\rangle=A_j^\dagger|0\rangle$. Then

$$
L_i|j\rangle=-i\hbar\varepsilon_{ikj}|k\rangle,
\qquad
L^2|j\rangle=2\hbar^2|j\rangle.
$$

Since the [orbital angular momentum](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#orbital-angular-momentum) eigenvalue is $\ell(\ell+1)\hbar^2$, every first-excited state has

$$
\boxed{\ell=1.}
$$

Finally,

$$
L_z|x\rangle=i\hbar|y\rangle,
\qquad
L_z|y\rangle=-i\hbar|x\rangle.
$$

Therefore the normalized [magnetic quantum number](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#magnetic-quantum-number) $m=+1$ state can be chosen as

$$
\boxed{|\psi\rangle=\frac{|x\rangle+i|y\rangle}{\sqrt2}
=\frac{A_x^\dagger+iA_y^\dagger}{\sqrt2}|0\rangle,}
$$

which obeys $L_z|\psi\rangle=\hbar|\psi\rangle$ and $\lVert|\psi\rangle\rVert=1$.

Choose the [Landau gauge](../../../quantum-theory.md#landau-gauge-for-a-uniform-magnetic-field)

$$
\mathbf A=(0,Bx,0),\qquad \phi=0.

It preserves [translation invariance](../../../physics.md#translation-invariance) in the $y$-direction, so $p_y=\hbar k$ is conserved. With $p_z=0$, [magnetic minimal coupling](../../../quantum-theory.md#magnetic-minimal-coupling) gives

H=\frac1{2m}\left[p_x^2+(p_y-qBx)^2\right]
=\frac{p_x^2}{2m}+\frac{m\omega_c^2}{2}(x-x_k)^2,

where

$$
\omega_c=\frac{|qB|}{m},
\qquad
x_k=\frac{\hbar k}{qB}.
$$

This is a [quantum harmonic oscillator](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#quantum-harmonic-oscillator), so the [Landau levels](../../../quantum-theory.md#landau-level) are

$$
\boxed{E_n=\hbar\omega_c\left(n+\frac12\right),\qquad n=0,1,2,\ldots,}
$$

independent of $k$.

The periodic boundary condition in $y$ quantizes $k=2\pi r/L_y$. Requiring the guiding centre to lie in a strip of width $L_x$ gives a $k$-interval of width $|qB|L_x/\hbar$. The number of allowed integers is therefore approximately

$$
\boxed{D=\frac{L_y}{2\pi}\frac{|qB|L_x}{\hbar}
=\frac{|qB|A}{2\pi\hbar},}

the [degeneracy of a Landau level](../../../quantum-theory.md#degeneracy-of-a-landau-level), up to boundary corrections.

+\frac{m\omega_c^2}{2}(x-x_c)^2

## 35D

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="35d/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [35D](#35d)

<h4 id="35d/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#35d/a)

Macroscopically, an [adiabatic process](../../../thermodynamics.md#adiabatic-process) exchanges no [heat](../../../thermodynamics.md#heat) with its surroundings: $\delta Q=0$. A reversible adiabatic process is therefore [isentropic](../../../thermodynamics.md#isentropic-process), since $dS=\delta Q_{\rm rev}/T=0$. Microscopically, the system is thermally isolated, so changes in its energy arise from work done by changing external parameters rather than random energy exchange with a [heat bath](../../../thermodynamics.md#heat-bath); in a sufficiently slow change, the populations of the corresponding microscopic states are carried along without producing entropy.

For repeated operation the working substance must execute a [thermodynamic cycle](../../../thermodynamics.md#thermodynamic-cycle). Reversible isothermal contact at $T_2$ absorbs heat $Q_2$, a reversible adiabat changes the temperature to $T_1$, reversible isothermal contact at $T_1$ rejects heat $Q_1>0$, and a second adiabat returns to the initial state. This is the [Carnot cycle](../../../thermodynamics.md#carnot-cycle). Reversibility and zero net entropy change give

$$
\frac{Q_2}{T_2}-\frac{Q_1}{T_1}=0,
\qquad
\frac{Q_1}{Q_2}=\frac{T_1}{T_2}.
$$

The [thermal efficiency](../../../thermodynamics.md#thermal-efficiency) is the net work output divided by the absorbed heat:

$$
\eta=\frac{W}{Q_2}=\frac{Q_2-Q_1}{Q_2}.
$$

Consequently

$$
\boxed{\eta_{\max}=1-\frac{T_1}{T_2}.}
$$

The [Carnot theorem](../../../thermodynamics.md#carnot-s-theorem) shows that any irreversible cycle has smaller efficiency, so the two isotherms joined by two adiabats are optimal.

<h3 id="35d/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [35D](#35d)

<h4 id="35d/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#35d/b)

At fixed volume, [entropy from heat capacity](../../../thermodynamics.md#entropy-from-heat-capacity) obeys $dS=C(T)dT/T$. The [Third law of thermodynamics](../../../thermodynamics.md#third-law-of-thermodynamics) fixes $S(0)=0$ when the ground state is nondegenerate, so below the transition

$$
S(T_c^-)=\int_0^{T_c}\frac{\alpha T}{T}\,dT=\alpha T_c.
$$

Above the transition,

$$
S(T)=S(T_c^+)+\int_{T_c}^{T}\frac\beta{T'}\,dT'
=S(T_c^+)+\beta\log\frac{T}{T_c}.
$$

For a transition with no [latent heat](../../../thermodynamics.md#latent-heat), entropy is continuous, and comparison with $S=\beta\log T+\gamma$ gives the directly testable relation

$$
\boxed{\gamma=\alpha T_c-\beta\log T_c.}
$$

Thus calorimetric measurements of $C(T)$ and $T_c$ verify $\gamma$. More generally, if a measurable latent heat $L$ is present, $S(T_c^+)-S(T_c^-)=L/T_c$, and the formula becomes

$$
\boxed{\gamma=\alpha T_c+\frac{L}{T_c}-\beta\log T_c.}
$$

For a constant [electromagnetic field tensor](../../../electromagnetism.md#electromagnetic-field-tensor), the [relativistic Lorentz force](../../../electromagnetism.md#relativistic-lorentz-force) is a linear system with constant coefficients, so

$$
\boxed{u^\mu(\tau)=
\left[\exp\!\left(\frac qmF\tau\right)\right]^\mu{}_{\nu}u^\nu(0).}
$$

Put

$$
a=\frac{qE}{mc},
\qquad
b=\frac{qB}{m},
\qquad
\gamma_0=\frac1{\sqrt{1-v_0^2/c^2}}.
$$

The initial [four-velocity](../../../special-relativity.md#four-velocity) is $(u^0,u^1,u^2,u^3)=\gamma_0(c,0,v_0,0)$. For parallel fields along $x$, the field tensor has one hyperbolic block in the $(0,1)$ plane and one rotation block in the $(2,3)$ plane. With the orientation convention in the question,

$$
u^0=\gamma_0c\cosh(a\tau),
\qquad
u^1=\gamma_0c\sinh(a\tau),
$$



$$
u^2=\gamma_0v_0\cos(b\tau),
\qquad
u^3=-\gamma_0v_0\sin(b\tau).
$$

Taking the initial spacetime point to be the origin and integrating $u^\mu=dx^\mu/d\tau$ gives the [relativistic motion in parallel electric and magnetic fields](../../../electromagnetism.md#relativistic-motion-in-parallel-electric-and-magnetic-fields)

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
t(\tau)&=\frac{\gamma_0}{a}\sinh(a\tau),\\
x(\tau)&=\frac{\gamma_0c}{a}\bigl(\cosh(a\tau)-1\bigr),\\
y(\tau)&=\frac{\gamma_0v_0}{b}\sin(b\tau),\\
z(\tau)&=\frac{\gamma_0v_0}{b}\bigl(\cos(b\tau)-1\bigr).
\end{aligned}}
$$

The expressions have their continuous $a\to0$ or $b\to0$ limits when one field vanishes; reversing the sign of $q$ reverses the electric acceleration and magnetic rotation.

Eliminating [proper time](../../../special-relativity.md#proper-time) between the first two equations yields

$$
\boxed{x(t)=\frac{\gamma_0c}{a}
\left[\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{at}{\gamma_0}\right)^2}-1\right].}
$$

At early coordinate time,

$$
x(t)=\frac{qE}{2m\gamma_0}t^2+O(t^4),
$$

which is uniformly accelerated motion with the transverse relativistic inertia factor $\gamma_0$. At late positive time,

$$
x(t)=\operatorname{sgn}(qE)ct-\frac{\gamma_0mc^2}{qE}+O(t^{-1}),
$$

so the longitudinal speed approaches the [speed of light](../../../special-relativity.md#speed-of-light).

The $y$-$z$ projection satisfies

$$
y^2+\left(z+\frac{\gamma_0v_0}{b}\right)^2
=\left(\frac{\gamma_0v_0}{b}\right)^2.
$$

It is therefore a circle of radius and proper-time period

$$
\boxed{R=\frac{\gamma_0m|v_0|}{|q|B},
\qquad
\Delta\tau=\frac{2\pi m}{|q|B}.}
$$

For the pitch magnitude assume $v_0>0$ and put $\alpha=|q|E/(mc)$, $\Omega=|q|B/m$, and

$$
r=\alpha\Delta\tau=\frac{2\pi E}{cB}.
$$

Number the $n$th proper-time period as $[(n-1)\Delta\tau,n\Delta\tau]$. Its longitudinal displacement is

$$
\Delta x_n=\frac{\gamma_0c}{\alpha}
\bigl[\cosh(nr)-\cosh((n-1)r)\bigr].
$$

Consequently, as $n\to\infty$,

$$
P_n=\frac{\Delta x_n}{R}
\sim\frac{c\Omega}{2\alpha v_0}(1-e^{-r})e^{nr}
=A\exp\!\left(\frac{2\pi En}{cB}\right),
$$

where

$$
\boxed{A=\frac{c^2B}{2Ev_0}
\left(1-e^{-2\pi E/(cB)}\right).}
$$

Because the [Levi-Civita connection](../../../general-relativity.md#levi-civita-connection) is torsion-free, its [Christoffel symbols](../../../riemannian-geometry.md#christoffel-symbol) satisfy $\Gamma^\mu_{\rho\alpha}=\Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\rho}$. Substitute the stated coordinate expression for each term in the cyclic sum

$$
R^\mu{}_{\rho\alpha\beta}
+R^\mu{}_{\beta\rho\alpha}
+R^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta\rho}.
$$

The partial-derivative terms cancel pairwise by the lower-index symmetry of $\Gamma$, and the quadratic terms cancel after relabelling their dummy indices. Therefore

$$
\boxed{R^\mu{}_{\rho\alpha\beta}
+R^\mu{}_{\beta\rho\alpha}
+R^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta\rho}=0,}
$$

which is the [first Bianchi identity](../../../general-relativity.md#first-bianchi-identity).

<h3 id="37d/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [37D](#37d)

<h4 id="37d/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#37d/b)

Expanding the two [covariant derivatives](../../../general-relativity.md#covariant-derivative) of a vector, the second partial derivatives cancel in the commutator. The remaining derivatives and products of Christoffel symbols combine into the coordinate definition of the [Riemann curvature tensor](../../../general-relativity.md#riemann-curvature-tensor), giving the [Ricci identity](../../../general-relativity.md#curvature-commutator-on-a-covariant-tensor)

$$
\boxed{2\nabla_{[\alpha}\nabla_{\beta]}V^\gamma
=[\nabla_\alpha,\nabla_\beta]V^\gamma
=R^\gamma{}_{\rho\alpha\beta}V^\rho.}
$$

For a type-$(2,0)$ tensor, the connection acts on both upper indices, so the [curvature commutator on a contravariant tensor](../../../general-relativity.md#curvature-commutator-on-a-contravariant-tensor) is

$$
\boxed{2\nabla_{[\alpha}\nabla_{\beta]}T^{\mu\nu}
=R^\mu{}_{\rho\alpha\beta}T^{\rho\nu}
+R^\nu{}_{\rho\alpha\beta}T^{\mu\rho}.}
$$

↑ **Parent:** [37D](#37d)

<h4 id="37d/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#37d/c)

Lower the free index of $K$. The [Killing equation](../../../general-relativity.md#killing-equation) makes the last two indices of $\nabla_\nu\nabla_\mu K_\alpha$ antisymmetric after the appropriate derivative is moved. Combining the three resulting [Ricci identities](../../../general-relativity.md#curvature-commutator-on-a-covariant-tensor) cyclically gives

$$
\nabla_\nu\nabla_\mu K_\alpha
=\frac12\left(
[\nabla_\nu,\nabla_\mu]K_\alpha
-[\nabla_\mu,\nabla_\alpha]K_\nu
+[\nabla_\alpha,\nabla_\nu]K_\mu
\right).
$$

Applying the covector form of the Ricci identity and then the [first Bianchi identity](../../../general-relativity.md#first-bianchi-identity) reduces this to

$$
\nabla_\nu\nabla_\mu K_\alpha
=R_{\alpha\mu\nu\beta}K^\beta.
$$

Raising $\alpha$ proves the [second covariant derivative of a Killing vector](../../../general-relativity.md#second-covariant-derivative-of-a-killing-vector) identity

$$
\boxed{\nabla_\nu\nabla_\mu K^\alpha
=R^\alpha{}_{\mu\nu\beta}K^\beta.}
$$

Contracting $\mu$ and $\nu$ and using the definition and symmetries of the [Ricci tensor](../../../general-relativity.md#ricci-tensor) gives

$$
\boxed{\nabla_\mu\nabla^\mu K^\alpha
=-R^\alpha{}_{\beta}K^\beta.}
$$

<h3 id="37d/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [37D](#37d)

<h4 id="37d/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#37d/d)

The [Killing equation](../../../general-relativity.md#killing-equation) gives $\nabla_\mu K^\mu=0$ and $\nabla_{[\mu}K_{\lambda]}=\nabla_\mu K_\lambda$. Commuting derivatives once and contracting the [Ricci identity](../../../general-relativity.md#curvature-commutator-on-a-covariant-tensor) gives

$$
\nabla^\lambda\nabla_\mu K_\lambda=R_{\mu\beta}K^\beta.
$$

Therefore, using the [contracted Bianchi identity](../../../general-relativity.md#contracted-bianchi-identity) and symmetry of $R_{\mu\beta}$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
2\nabla^\mu\nabla^\lambda\nabla_{[\mu}K_{\lambda]}
&=2\nabla^\mu(R_{\mu\beta}K^\beta)\\
&=K^\beta\nabla_\beta R
+2R_{\mu\beta}\nabla^\mu K^\beta\\
&=K^\beta\nabla_\beta R.
\end{aligned}
$$

Since $\nabla_{[\mu}K_{\lambda]}$ is antisymmetric, swapping the two contracted index names shows that the left side equals its derivative-antisymmetrized form:

$$
\boxed{K^\alpha\nabla_\alpha R
=2\nabla^{[\mu}\nabla^{\lambda]}\nabla_{[\mu}K_{\lambda]}.}
$$

Now set $A_{\mu\lambda}=\nabla_{[\mu}K_{\lambda]}$. The right side is the contracted commutator $[\nabla^\mu,\nabla^\lambda]A_{\mu\lambda}$. By the [curvature commutator on a covariant tensor](../../../general-relativity.md#curvature-commutator-on-a-covariant-tensor), it is a sum of contractions of the symmetric [Ricci tensor](../../../general-relativity.md#ricci-tensor) with the antisymmetric tensor $A$, and hence vanishes. Thus

$$
\boxed{K^\alpha\nabla_\alpha R=0,}
$$

so the [Killing vector preserves scalar curvature](../../../general-relativity.md#killing-vector-preserves-scalar-curvature).

Let $u(r,z)$ and $w(r,z)$ be the radial and vertical velocity components. For steady incompressible [axisymmetric flow](../../../fluid-mechanics.md#axisymmetric-flow) without swirl, [mass conservation](../../../continuum-mechanics.md#mass-conservation) and the relevant cylindrical components of the [Navier-Stokes equation](../../../viscous-fluid-flow.md#navier-stokes-equation) are

$$
\frac1r\frac{\partial(ru)}{\partial r}+\frac{\partial w}{\partial z}=0,
$$



$$
\rho\left(u\frac{\partial u}{\partial r}+w\frac{\partial u}{\partial z}\right)
=-\frac{\partial p}{\partial r}
+\mu\left(\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial r^2}+\frac1r\frac{\partial u}{\partial r}-\frac{u}{r^2}+\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial z^2}\right),
$$



$$
\rho\left(u\frac{\partial w}{\partial r}+w\frac{\partial w}{\partial z}\right)
=-\frac{\partial p}{\partial z}
+\mu\left(\frac{\partial^2w}{\partial r^2}+\frac1r\frac{\partial w}{\partial r}+\frac{\partial^2w}{\partial z^2}\right).
$$

The vertical scale is $w\sim V$. [Incompressibility](../../../fluid-mechanics.md#incompressible-flow) over radial scale $R$ and gap scale $h$ gives $u\sim VR/h=V/\varepsilon$. In the radial equation, inertia is smaller than transverse viscous stress by

$$
\frac{\rho Vh}{\mu}=\operatorname{Re}\ll1,
$$

while radial viscous derivatives are smaller than $u_{zz}$ by $\varepsilon^2$. The pressure scale is $\mu VR^2/h^3$. In the vertical equation, both viscous and inertial terms are smaller than the corresponding cross-gap pressure-gradient scale by at least $O(\varepsilon^2)$. Thus the leading [lubrication theory](../../../viscous-fluid-flow.md#lubrication-theory) equations are

$$
\boxed{\frac1r(ru)_r+w_z=0,
\qquad p_r=\mu u_{zz},
\qquad p_z=0.}
$$

<h4 id="38a/b/solution">Solution</h4>

Away from the rim's $O(h)$ edge region, the geometry and uniform injection have no radial scale other than the factor required by continuity. It is therefore consistent to take $w=w(z)$. Regularity at $r=0$ then lets the continuity equation integrate to

$$
u(r,z)=-\frac r2w'(z).
$$

The [no-slip boundary condition](../../../viscous-fluid-flow.md#no-slip-boundary-condition) and prescribed normal velocities are

$$
w(0)=V,\quad w'(0)=0,\qquad
w(h)=0,\quad w'(h)=0.
$$

Since $p$ is independent of $z$, the radial equation implies that $w'''$ is constant. The four boundary conditions give

$$
w(z)=V\left[1-3\left(\frac zh\right)^2+2\left(\frac zh\right)^3\right],
$$

and hence

$$
u(r,z)=\frac{3Vr}{h}\frac zh\left(1-\frac zh\right).
$$

Using $w'''=12V/h^3$ in $p_r=\mu u_{zz}$ gives

$$
p_r=-\frac{6\mu Vr}{h^3}.
$$

Taking the pressure at the rim to be atmospheric, $p(R)=p_0$, produces the [porous-plate lubrication cushion](../../../viscous-fluid-flow.md#porous-plate-lubrication-cushion) pressure

$$
\boxed{p(r)-p_0=\frac{3\mu V}{h^3}(R^2-r^2).}
$$

The upward pressure force balances the disc's weight:

$$
W=\int_0^R[p(r)-p_0],2\pi r\,dr
=\frac{3\pi\mu VR^4}{2h^3}.
$$

Since $\varepsilon=h/R$,

$$
\boxed{W=\frac{3\pi\mu VR}{2\varepsilon^3}.}
$$

## 39A

The one-dimensional [Isentropic Euler equations](../../../compressible-flow.md#isentropic-euler-equations) are

$$
\rho_t+u\rho_x+\rho u_x=0,
\qquad
u_t+uu_x+\frac1\rho p_x=0.
$$

For the [polytropic equation of state](../../../astrophysical-fluid-dynamics.md#polytropic-equation-of-state),

$$
c^2=\frac{dp}{d\rho}=\frac{\gamma p}{\rho},
\qquad
\frac c{c_0}=\left(\frac\rho{\rho_0}\right)^{(\gamma-1)/2}.
$$

The continuity equation therefore implies

$$
c_t+uc_x+\frac{\gamma-1}{2}cu_x=0,
$$

while the momentum equation becomes

$$
u_t+uu_x+\frac{2c}{\gamma-1}c_x=0.
$$

Adding and subtracting these equations gives

$$
\left[\partial_t+(u\pm c)\partial_x\right]
\left[u\pm\frac{2(c-c_0)}{\gamma-1}\right]=0.
$$

Thus the [Riemann invariants](../../../compressible-flow.md#riemann-invariant)

$$
\boxed{R_\pm=u\pm\frac{2(c-c_0)}{\gamma-1}}
$$

are constant along the [characteristic curves](../../../partial-differential-equation.md#characteristic-curve)

$$
\boxed{C_\pm:\quad\frac{dx}{dt}=u\pm c.}
$$

<h4 id="39a/b/i">i</h4>

<h5 id="39a/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#39a/b/i)

The undisturbed gas has $u=0$, $c=c_0$, and hence $R_-=0$. The disturbance from the receding piston is a right-moving [simple wave](../../../compressible-flow.md#simple-wave), so this invariant remains zero throughout it:

$$
c=c_0+\frac{\gamma-1}{2}u,
\qquad
\rho=\rho_0\left(1+\frac{\gamma-1}{2c_0}u\right)^{2/(\gamma-1)}.
$$

Let $s$ be the time at which the relevant $C_+$ characteristic leaves the piston. The boundary condition is $u=\dot X_p(s)$ there. Both $u$ and $c$ are constant along this characteristic, whose speed is

$$
u+c=c_0+\frac{\gamma+1}{2}\dot X_p(s).
$$

Hence $s=s(x,t)$ is determined implicitly by the [receding-piston rarefaction wave](../../../compressible-flow.md#receding-piston-rarefaction-wave) equation

$$
\boxed{x=X_p(s)+\left[c_0+\frac{\gamma+1}{2}\dot X_p(s)\right](t-s).}
$$

Within $X_p(t)<x<c_0t$, the solution is therefore

$$
\boxed{u(x,t)=\dot X_p(s(x,t)),}
$$



$$
\boxed{\rho(x,t)=\rho_0
\left[1+\frac{\gamma-1}{2c_0}\dot X_p(s(x,t))\right]^{2/(\gamma-1)}.}
$$

For $x\geq c_0t$, the gas remains undisturbed: $u=0$ and $\rho=\rho_0$. The speed bound keeps $c$ and $\rho$ positive, and monotonic increase of $|\dot X_p|$ makes this an expanding [rarefaction wave](../../../partial-differential-equation.md#rarefaction-wave) rather than a characteristic-crossing compression.

<h4 id="39a/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#39a/b)

<h5 id="39a/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#39a/b/ii)

At $t=t_c$, the piston velocity reaches

$$
u_v=-\frac{2c_0}{\gamma-1}.
$$

The simple-wave relation then gives $c=0$ and $\rho=0$. For later times the formal continuation would require negative sound speed, so the gas can no longer follow the piston. By [vacuum formation behind a receding piston](../../../compressible-flow.md#vacuum-formation-behind-a-receding-piston), a vacuum region opens between $X_p(t)$ and the gas edge

$$
\boxed{x_v(t)=X_p(t_c)-\frac{2c_0}{\gamma-1}(t-t_c).}
$$

The earlier rarefaction solution remains to the right of $x_v(t)$, while $X_p(t)<x<x_v(t)$ is vacuum.

<h4 id="39a/b/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#39a/b)

<h5 id="39a/b/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#39a/b/iii)

Here

$$
X_p(s)=-\frac{s^{1+\alpha}}{1+\alpha},
\qquad
\dot X_p(s)=-s^\alpha.
$$

Write $A=(\gamma+1)/2$. Substituting $x=(c_0-a)t$ into the $C_+$ characteristic equation gives

$$
at=c_0s+At\,s^\alpha
-\left(A-\frac1{1+\alpha}\right)s^{1+\alpha}.
$$

For $0<a<A$ and $0<\alpha\ll1$, the relevant emission time is exponentially small. The terms containing an explicit factor $s$ are therefore beyond all algebraic orders, leaving

$$
s^\alpha\sim\frac aA.
$$

Thus the [singular small-power piston emission time](../../../compressible-flow.md#singular-small-power-piston-emission-time) is

$$
\boxed{s(x,t)\sim
\left(\frac aA\right)^{1/\alpha}
=\left(\frac{2a}{\gamma+1}\right)^{1/\alpha}.}
$$

Although $s\to0$, the limiting state is nontrivial:

$$
u\sim-\frac{2a}{\gamma+1},
\qquad
c\sim c_0-\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma+1}a,
$$

which is the centered-rarefaction state on the ray $x/t=c_0-a$ when $\alpha=0$.

## 40C

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="40c/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [40C](#40c)

<h4 id="40c/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#40c/a)

Choose a [matrix splitting](../../../numerical-analysis.md#matrix-splitting) $A=M-N$ with $M$ invertible. Then $Ax=b$ is equivalent to

$$
x=M^{-1}Nx+M^{-1}b,
$$

and the associated [stationary iterative method for a linear system](../../../numerical-analysis.md#stationary-iterative-method-for-a-linear-system) is

$$
x^{(k+1)}=Hx^{(k)}+M^{-1}b,
\qquad H=M^{-1}N.
$$

For the [Jacobi method](../../../numerical-analysis.md#jacobi-method), write $A=D+L+U$ with $D$ diagonal and $L,U$ strictly lower and upper triangular, and take

$$
M=D,\qquad N=-(L+U).
$$

Thus

$$
\boxed{x^{(k+1)}=-D^{-1}(L+U)x^{(k)}+D^{-1}b.}
$$

The iteration converges for every initial vector exactly when the [spectral radius](../../../analysis.md#spectral-radius) of its iteration matrix satisfies

$$
\boxed{\rho\bigl(-D^{-1}(L+U)\bigr)<1.}
$$

<h3 id="40c/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [40C](#40c)

<h4 id="40c/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#40c/b)

Here $D=I$, so the Jacobi iteration matrix is

$$
H=I-A=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&-\alpha&-\alpha\\
-\alpha&0&-\alpha\\
-\alpha&-\alpha&0
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The all-ones vector is an [eigenvector](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvector) with [eigenvalue](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) $-2\alpha$. On its two-dimensional [orthogonal complement](../../../hilbert-space.md#orthogonal-complement), the coordinates sum to zero and $Hv=\alpha v$, so the other eigenvalue is $\alpha$ with multiplicity two. Therefore

$$
\rho(H)=\max\{2|\alpha|,|\alpha|\}=2|\alpha|.
$$

By [Jacobi convergence for a three-by-three equicorrelation matrix](../../../numerical-analysis.md#jacobi-convergence-for-a-three-by-three-equicorrelation-matrix), convergence occurs exactly for

$$
\boxed{-\frac12<\alpha<\frac12.}
$$

At either endpoint the spectral radius is one, so convergence for arbitrary initial data fails.

<h3 id="40c/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [40C](#40c)

<h4 id="40c/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#40c/c)

The [Householder-John theorem](../../../numerical-analysis.md#householder-john-theorem) states: if $A=M-N$ is [Hermitian](../../../hilbert-space.md#hermitian-operator) positive definite and $M^*+N$ is also Hermitian positive definite, then $M$ is invertible and

$$
\rho(M^{-1}N)<1.
$$

Now let $A=D+L+L^T$ be real symmetric positive definite and tridiagonal. For the Jacobi splitting, $M=D$ and $N=-(L+L^T)$. Positive definiteness of $A$ implies every diagonal entry is positive, so $D$ is positive definite. Introduce the alternating-sign [diagonal matrix](../../../linear-algebra.md#diagonal-matrix)

$$
S=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1,1,-1,\ldots).
$$

Tridiagonality gives

$$
SAS=D-L-L^T=M^*+N.
$$

Since $S$ is orthogonal, $SAS$ is positive definite whenever $A$ is. Both hypotheses of the Householder-John theorem hold, and hence the Jacobi iteration matrix satisfies $\rho(M^{-1}N)<1$. Therefore the [Jacobi convergence for a symmetric positive-definite tridiagonal matrix](../../../numerical-analysis.md#jacobi-convergence-for-a-symmetric-positive-definite-tridiagonal-matrix) follows.
