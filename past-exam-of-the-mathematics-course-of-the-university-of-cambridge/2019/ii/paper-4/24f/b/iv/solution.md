<h1 id="24f/b/iv/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](../iv.md)

First, $1\in G(p)$. Otherwise there would be a nonconstant [rational function](../../../../../../../rational-function.md) with one simple pole and no others, defining a degree-one [finite morphism](../../../../../../../finite-morphism.md) $X\to\mathbb P^1$. Such a map is an [isomorphism](../../../../../../../isomorphism-of-algebraic-varieties.md), which would make $X$ have genus zero.

Part (iii) says that there are exactly two gaps. If $2$ is a gap, they must therefore be $1$ and $2$. If $2$ is not a gap, choose a function with its only pole at $p$, of order two. Pole orders form an [additive semigroup](../../../../../../../additive-semigroup.md) under multiplication of functions. If $3$ were also a nongap, the semigroup generated by $2$ and $3$ would contain every integer at least two, leaving only the single gap $1$, a contradiction. Hence $3$ is the second gap. This proves the [Genus-two Weierstrass gap sequence](../../../../../../../genus-two-weierstrass-gap-sequence.md)

$$
\boxed{G(p)=\{1,2\}\quad\text{or}\quad G(p)=\{1,3\}.}
$$
