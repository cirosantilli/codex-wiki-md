<h1 id="1/b/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [B](../b.md)

A [continuous phase transition](../../../../../../continuous-phase-transition.md) has a continuously vanishing [order parameter](../../../../../../order-parameter.md) and no [latent heat](../../../../../../latent-heat.md). A [first-order phase transition](../../../../../../first-order-phase-transition.md) exchanges globally stable phases with discontinuous first derivatives of a thermodynamic potential; the [order parameter](../../../../../../order-parameter.md) generally jumps, and an entropy jump gives latent heat. In [Landau theory](../../../../../../landau-theory.md) the distinction is determined by global minima, not just by the appearance of additional stationary points.

For the uniform scalar potential $U(M)=rM^2/2+uM^4/4$ with $u>0$ and $h=0$, its stationary equation is $M(r+uM^2)=0$. For $r>0$, $M=0$ is the minimum; for $r<0$, the minima are $M=\pm\sqrt{-r/u}$. They approach zero continuously as $r\uparrow0$, giving an ordinary continuous transition.

For $u<0$ and $v>0$, use the sextic potential. Write $y=M^2$. Stationarity of a nonzero phase requires $r+uy+vy^2=0$, while equality of its potential with $U(0)=0$ requires $ry/2+uy^2/4+vy^3/6=0$. Eliminating $r$ yields

$$
\boxed{r_{\rm coex}=\frac{3u^2}{16v},\qquad M_{\rm coex}^2=-\frac{3u}{4v}.}
$$

At this value the potential factors as $U(M)=\frac v6M^2(M^2+3u/(4v))^2\geq0$, proving that the three stationary zeros really are degenerate global minima. Crossing this line makes the [order parameter](../../../../../../order-parameter.md) jump: the transition is first order. The disordered and ordered [spinodal points](../../../../../../spinodal-point.md) instead occur at $r=0$ and $r=u^2/(4v)$ respectively, delimiting [metastability](../../../../../../metastability.md); these are not the equilibrium transition. If the symmetry allows a cubic term, that term can also produce a first-order transition.
