<h1 id="2/1/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [1](../1.md)

In the variational convention needed here, a [positive definite symmetric operator](../../../../../../positive-definite-symmetric-operator.md) on a real [Hilbert space](../../../../../../hilbert-space-split.md) satisfies

$$
\langle Lu,v\rangle=\langle u,Lv\rangle,\qquad
\langle Lv,v\rangle>0\quad(v\ne0).
$$

For a bounded operator on the whole space, symmetry is [self-adjointness](../../../../../../self-adjoint-operator.md). On a complex [Hilbert space](../../../../../../hilbert-space-split.md), the corresponding convention is Hermitian positivity; the quadratic expression is real. Strict positivity should be distinguished from [uniformly positive definite symmetric operator](../../../../../../uniformly-positive-definite-symmetric-operator.md) coercivity, which requires a fixed $\gamma>0$ such that $\langle Lv,v\rangle\ge\gamma\|v\|^2$.

Symmetry matters in a real space. If “positive definite” means only the inequality for the [quadratic form](../../../../../../quadratic-form.md), the next variational assertion is false: $L=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$ has $\langle Lv,v\rangle=\|v\|^2>0$, but its skew part disappears from that form. Its [Euler-Lagrange equation](../../../../../../euler-lagrange-equation.md) is $v=f$, not $Lv=f$. Thus the usual symmetric/Hermitian convention, or an explicit symmetry assumption, is required.
