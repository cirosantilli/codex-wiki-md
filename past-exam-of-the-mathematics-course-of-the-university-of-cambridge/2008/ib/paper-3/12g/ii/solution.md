<h1 id="12g/ii/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](../ii.md)

For the [Poincare disc model](../../../../../../poincare-disk-model.md), radial distance from zero to Euclidean radius $r$ is

$$
\int_0^r\frac{2\,ds}{1-s^2}=2\operatorname{artanh}r.
$$

It is minimal: any curve has length at least the integral of $2|dr|/(1-r^2)$, which in turn is at least the net radial integral. Consequently a [hyperbolic circle](../../../../../../hyperbolic-circle.md) of radius $a$ centred at zero is $|\zeta|=\tanh(a/2)$, a Euclidean [circle](../../../../../../circle.md).

Given a centre $p$, use a [Poincare disc automorphism](../../../../../../poincare-disc-automorphism.md) carrying $p$ to zero. Since it is an [isometry](../../../../../../isometry.md), it takes the given [hyperbolic circle](../../../../../../hyperbolic-circle.md) to the preceding Euclidean circle. A [Möbius transformation](../../../../../../mobius-transformation.md) takes generalized circles to generalized circles. The preimage here is compact and contained in the disc, so it cannot be a straight line. Thus

$$
\boxed{\text{every hyperbolic circle in the disc model is a Euclidean circle}.}
$$

This argument does not say that their Euclidean and hyperbolic centres coincide.
