<h3 id="1/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

The [dual linear program](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#dual-linear-program) is the [minimization problem](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#minimization-problem)

$$
\boxed{\min\{b^Ty:A^Ty\geq c,\ y\geq0\}.}
$$

Indeed, for $y\geq0$ the [optimization Lagrangian](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#optimization-lagrangian) $c^Tx+y^T(b-Ax)$ bounds the primal objective above on its [feasible points](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#feasible-point). Its supremum over $x\geq0$ is finite precisely when $A^Ty\geq c$, and then equals $b^Ty$. The two labelled parts below establish the relevant [weak duality](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#weak-duality) and [strong duality](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#strong-duality) conclusions.

For the subsequent infeasibility argument, introduce nonnegative [slack variables](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#slack-variable) $s$ and the [convex cone](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#convex-cone)

$$
C=\{Ax+s:x\geq0,\ s\geq0\}.
$$

This is generated by the columns of $A$ and the coordinate vectors. Here is why this [finitely generated cone](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#finitely-generated-cone) is closed. Any representation with a minimal number of positive coefficients uses linearly independent generators: otherwise subtract a suitable multiple of a dependence until one positive coefficient vanishes, preserving nonnegativity. For each fixed independent selection of generators, its nonnegative combinations form a closed set, because the inverse coordinate map on its span is continuous. There are finitely many selections, so their union $C$ is closed. This is the [closedness of finitely generated cones](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#closedness-of-finitely-generated-cones) used in [Farkas' lemma](../../../convex-optimization.md#farkas-lemma).

When the primal [linear program](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#linear-programming) is infeasible, $b\notin C$. Let $p$ be the nearest point of $C$ to $b$, and set $h=p-b$. The minimizing property gives $h^T(z-p)\geq0$ for every $z\in C$. Taking $z=0,2p$ gives $h^Tp=0$. Consequently

$$
h^Tz\geq0\quad(z\in C),\qquad h^Tb=-\|p-b\|^2<0.
$$

In particular $h\geq0$ and $A^Th\geq0$. Rescaling $h$ by the positive number $-h^Tb$ produces the requested [Farkas' lemma](../../../convex-optimization.md#farkas-lemma) certificate:

$$
\boxed{\lambda\geq0,\qquad A^T\lambda\geq0,\qquad\lambda^Tb=-1.}
$$

Now a [feasible point](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#feasible-point) of the relaxed inequalities would satisfy

$$
0\leq\lambda^TAx\leq\lambda^T(b+\varepsilon\mathbf1)
=-1+\varepsilon\sum_i\lambda_i.
$$

Since $\lambda^Tb=-1$, the sum of the nonnegative $\lambda_i$ is positive. Thus [robust infeasibility under uniform constraint relaxation](../../../convex-optimization.md#robust-infeasibility-under-uniform-constraint-relaxation) gives

$$
\boxed{\Pi(\varepsilon)=\varnothing\quad\text{for }0\leq\varepsilon<\frac1{\sum_i\lambda_i}.}
$$

The [ellipsoid method](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#ellipsoid-method) makes progress by reducing the volume of a containing ellipsoid. A nonempty [linear polyhedron](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#linear-polyhedron) may have empty interior, so small volume alone cannot certify that the original system is infeasible. Relaxing the inequalities remedies this difficulty, but must not make an infeasible system appear feasible. The displayed positive gap proves exactly that sufficiently small relaxations preserve infeasibility. For rational input, determinant bounds give a [Farkas' lemma](../../../convex-optimization.md#farkas-lemma) certificate of polynomial encoding length, so a sufficiently small rational relaxation can be chosen from the input encoding length. Together with a bounding box and bounds on the volume of a relaxed feasible system, this lets the [ellipsoid method](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#ellipsoid-method) distinguish infeasibility from a feasible system of very small or zero original volume in [polynomial time](../../../computer-science.md#polynomial-time). The certificate argument alone does not assert a polynomial precision bound for arbitrary unencoded real coefficients.

Fix a primal [feasible point](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#feasible-point) $x_0$. For every dual [feasible point](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#feasible-point) $y$, nonnegativity and the primal and dual inequalities give

$$
c^Tx_0\leq x_0^TA^Ty=y^TAx_0\leq y^Tb.
$$

Hence **the dual objective is bounded below by $c^Tx_0$ wherever it is feasible**, which is [weak duality](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#weak-duality). This is the meaning of “bounded” in this assertion; it does not say that the dual feasible set is bounded, or that it is nonempty. For example, with $A=0$, $b=0$, $c=1$ and a single variable, the primal is feasible and unbounded above, while its dual would require $0\geq1$ and is infeasible.

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

Let the finite primal supremum be $v$. First it is attained. The set

$$
K=\{(Ax+s,c^Tx-t):x,s,t\geq0\}
$$

is a [finitely generated cone](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#finitely-generated-cone), hence closed by the argument in the root Solution. For a sequence of primal [feasible points](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#feasible-point) whose values approach $v$, the pairs $(b,c^Tx)$ belong to $K$. Their limit $(b,v)$ therefore belongs to $K$. It gives a [feasible point](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#feasible-point) $x_*$ with $c^Tx_*\geq v$, so necessarily $c^Tx_*=v$.

Let $I$ index the tight primal inequalities at $x_*$ and let $Z$ index its zero coordinates. Consider the closed [convex cone](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#convex-cone) generated by $A_i^T$ for $i\in I$ and $-e_j$ for $j\in Z$. If $c$ were outside this cone, [separation from a closed convex cone](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#separation-from-a-closed-convex-cone) would give a direction $d$ with

$$
A_id\leq0\ (i\in I),\qquad d_j\geq0\ (j\in Z),\qquad c^Td>0.
$$

For sufficiently small positive $\delta$, $x_*+\delta d$ remains a [feasible point](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#feasible-point): the active constraints have the required signs, and every inactive constraint retains its positive slack. Its larger objective contradicts optimality. Therefore there are $y_i\geq0$, supported on $I$, and $\mu_j\geq0$, supported on $Z$, such that

$$
c=A^Ty-\mu.
$$

This makes $y$ a dual [feasible point](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#feasible-point). Moreover, the support restrictions give

$$
b^Ty=y^TAx_*=c^Tx_*+\mu^Tx_*=v.
$$

Together with [weak duality](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#weak-duality), this proves [strong duality](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#strong-duality) and dual attainment:

$$
\boxed{D\text{ is feasible, has minimum }v,\text{ and is bounded below}.}
$$

Encode a [vertex of a polytope](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#vertex-of-a-polytope) by three bits: bit $j$ records whether the lower or upper bound of coordinate $j$ is tight. Every choice is possible because $0<\varepsilon<1/2$, and the triangular active equations determine that [vertex of a polytope](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#vertex-of-a-polytope) uniquely. Its three adjacent [vertices of a polytope](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#vertex-of-a-polytope) are obtained by changing one bit. The geometric [Bland pivoting rule](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#bland-pivoting-rule) specified here chooses the smallest-index active [facet](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#facet) that can be left while increasing $x_3$. It produces the following path; the final column records the [facet](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#facet) left on arrival at the next [vertex of a polytope](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#vertex-of-a-polytope):

$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c|c}
\text{step}&\text{bits}&(x_1,x_2,x_3)&\text{next facet left}\\\hline
0&000&(0,0,0)&1\\
1&100&(1,\varepsilon,\varepsilon^2)&3\\
2&110&(1,1-\varepsilon,\varepsilon-\varepsilon^2)&2\\
3&010&(0,1,\varepsilon)&5\\
4&011&(0,1,1-\varepsilon)&1\\
5&111&(1,1-\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon+\varepsilon^2)&4\\
6&101&(1,\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon^2)&2\\
7&001&(0,0,1)&\text{none}
\end{array}
$$

For example, at the origin all three outgoing edges improve the objective, but leaving [facet](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#facet) 1 has priority. At bit string $110$, switching bit 2 decreases $x_3$, so the rule switches bit 1, leaving [facet](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#facet) 2. At $010$, neither of the earlier-coordinate switches improves $x_3$, so the rule finally leaves [facet](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#facet) 5. The upper face reverses the direction in which changes of $x_2$ improve $x_3$, and the remaining three horizontal pivots follow. The objective values in the table are strictly increasing for the stated range of $\varepsilon$.

<a id="2/image-seven-bland-pivots-through-all-eight-vertices-of-the-klee-minty-cube-with-epsilon-equal-to-one-quarter"></a>
![](../../../past-exam-of-the-mathematics-course-of-the-university-of-cambridge/2008/iii/paper-38-klee-minty.png)

**[Figure 1](#2/image-seven-bland-pivots-through-all-eight-vertices-of-the-klee-minty-cube-with-epsilon-equal-to-one-quarter). Seven Bland pivots through all eight vertices of the Klee-Minty cube, with epsilon equal to one quarter**.

Thus the [simplex method](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#simplex-method) takes **seven pivots although the optimum is directly adjacent to the starting point**. The same [Klee-Minty cube](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#klee-minty-cube) in $n$ dimensions has $2n$ inequalities and $2^n$ [vertices of a polytope](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#vertex-of-a-polytope). On its lower last-coordinate face, increasing $x_n$ means increasing $x_{n-1}$, and the first $n-1$ coordinates follow the corresponding lower-dimensional path before the last-coordinate [facet](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#facet) can be left. On the upper face, increasing $x_n$ means decreasing $x_{n-1}$, and the smallest-index rule traverses that path in reverse. The reverse-path assertion follows by the same induction, interchanging maximization and minimization on each face. Hence the pivot count satisfies $T_n=2T_{n-1}+1$, $T_1=1$, and

$$
\boxed{T_n=2^n-1.}
$$

The usual [Bland pivoting rule](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#bland-pivoting-rule) prevents cycling, including degenerate pivots, but this exponential example shows that it does not give a polynomial worst-case pivot bound. For rational fixed $\varepsilon$, the constraints have polynomial encoding length. Arithmetic cost per pivot does not remove the exponential obstruction.

For a lower bound that applies to every pivot choice, use $0\leq x_i\leq1$ and maximize $\sum_{i=1}^nx_i$, starting at the origin. Each edge of this [cube](../../../geometry-and-topology.md#cube) changes only one coordinate, whereas the unique optimal [vertex of a polytope](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#vertex-of-a-polytope) is $(1,\ldots,1)$. Every coordinate must therefore be changed along at least one edge. **Every route of adjacent pivots takes at least $n$ pivots**, and every strictly improving route takes exactly $n$.

For [Dantzig pivot rule](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#dantzig-pivot-rule), the rate is the objective increase per unit entering nonbasic variable, as in standard [reduced cost](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#reduced-cost) pricing. Rescale the three coordinates by

$$
y_1=\varepsilon^4x_1,\qquad y_2=\varepsilon^2x_2,\qquad y_3=x_3.
$$

The transformed [linear program](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#linear-programming) still maximizes $y_3$, now with bounds

$$
0\leq y_1\leq\varepsilon^4,\qquad
\varepsilon^{-1}y_1\leq y_2\leq\varepsilon^2-\varepsilon^{-1}y_1,\qquad
\varepsilon^{-1}y_2\leq y_3\leq1-\varepsilon^{-1}y_2.
$$

When the active [facet](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#facet) for coordinate $i$ is freed, the other active equations give $|dx_3/dx_i|=\varepsilon^{3-i}$. The positive rates per unit $y_i$ are therefore

$$
\varepsilon^{-2},\qquad\varepsilon^{-1},\qquad1,
$$

for $i=1,2,3$, respectively. Equivalently, use the nonnegative lower- and upper-bound [slack variables](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#slack-variable) of these displayed, normalized inequalities: freeing coordinate $i$ changes its entering slack by $|dy_i|$. Thus [Dantzig pivot rule](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#dantzig-pivot-rule) chooses the lowest coordinate index among improving edges and follows the same seven-pivot path. In $n$ dimensions set $y_i=\varepsilon^{2(n-i)}x_i$; the rates $\varepsilon^{-(n-i)}$ give the same $2^n-1$ pivots. These statements concern coordinate/slack pricing, not objective increase per unit Euclidean edge length, which defines a different pivot rule.

In a [minimum-cost flow](../../../graph-theory.md#minimum-cost-flow-problem) problem, a [directed graph](../../../graph-theory.md#directed-graph) has arc flows $f_{ij}$, unit costs $c_{ij}$, lower and upper capacities $\ell_{ij},u_{ij}$, and net supplies $b_i$ summing to zero. The [linear program](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#linear-programming) minimizes $\sum_{ij}c_{ij}f_{ij}$ subject to the [flow balances](../../../graph-theory.md#flow-balance) $\sum_j f_{ij}-\sum_j f_{ji}=b_i$ and $\ell_{ij}\leq f_{ij}\leq u_{ij}$.

Give each [graph vertex](../../../graph.md#vertex-graph-theory) a [network dual potential](../../../graph-theory.md#network-dual-potential) $\pi_i$. With the outgoing-minus-incoming [oriented incidence matrix](../../../graph.md#oriented-incidence-matrix) $B$, the [optimization Lagrangian](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#optimization-lagrangian) is

$$
L(f,\pi)=c^Tf+\pi^T(b-Bf)
=\pi^Tb+\sum_{ij}r_{ij}f_{ij},\qquad
r_{ij}=c_{ij}-\pi_i+\pi_j.
$$

Minimizing over the capacity intervals is separable. A candidate [feasible flow](../../../graph-theory.md#feasible-flow) minimizes this [optimization Lagrangian](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#optimization-lagrangian) if it takes its lower bound whenever the [network reduced cost](../../../graph-theory.md#network-reduced-cost) is positive, its upper bound whenever that [network reduced cost](../../../graph-theory.md#network-reduced-cost) is negative, and any allowed value when that [network reduced cost](../../../graph-theory.md#network-reduced-cost) is zero. Equivalently, for arcs with distinct lower and upper bounds,

$$
f_{ij}=\ell_{ij}\Rightarrow r_{ij}\geq0,\qquad
\ell_{ij}<f_{ij}<u_{ij}\Rightarrow r_{ij}=0,\qquad
f_{ij}=u_{ij}\Rightarrow r_{ij}\leq0.
$$

For every other [feasible flow](../../../graph-theory.md#feasible-flow) $g$, these conditions give $c^Tf=L(f,\pi)\leq L(g,\pi)=c^Tg$. This proves the [Lagrangian sufficiency theorem](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#lagrange-sufficiency-theorem) certificate, rather than merely stating the [capacitated flow optimality conditions](../../../graph-theory.md#capacitated-flow-optimality-conditions).

Put $t=f_{23}$. The four [flow balances](../../../graph-theory.md#flow-balance) determine

$$
(f_{12},f_{23},f_{34},f_{41})=(t+3,t,t+1,t+2).
$$

Intersecting the four capacity intervals gives $2\leq t\leq4$. In particular,

$$
\boxed{f(2)=(5,2,3,4)}
$$

is a [feasible flow](../../../graph-theory.md#feasible-flow). Write $C=c_{12}+c_{23}+c_{34}+c_{41}$. Its cost along the feasible interval is

$$
c^Tf(t)=Ct+3c_{12}+c_{34}+2c_{41}.
$$

Hence $f(2)$ is optimal precisely when **$C\geq0$**. A [network dual potential](../../../graph-theory.md#network-dual-potential) certificate is

$$
\pi_1=0,\quad\pi_2=-c_{12},\quad
\pi_3=c_{41}+c_{34},\quad\pi_4=c_{41}.
$$

The three interior arcs have zero [network reduced costs](../../../graph-theory.md#network-reduced-cost) and $r_{23}=C$, which has the required nonnegative sign at its lower capacity.

For the [network simplex algorithm](../../../graph-theory.md#network-simplex-algorithm), start with the [network simplex tree basis](../../../graph-theory.md#network-simplex-tree-basis) consisting of arcs $12,34,41$, and fix nonbasic arc $23$ at its lower bound 2. These are exactly the potentials just found. If $C\geq0$, no improving pivot is needed. If $C<0$, arc $23$ enters and its fundamental [graph cycle](../../../graph-theory.md#cycle-in-a-graph) increases all four flows by the same amount. At $f(2)$ the remaining upper capacities are $(3,3,2,4)$, so the [simplex ratio test](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#simplex-ratio-test) allows an increase of 2. Arc $34$ reaches its upper bound and leaves the tree. The new [network simplex tree basis](../../../graph-theory.md#network-simplex-tree-basis) has arcs $12,23,41$, with potentials

$$
\pi_1=0,\quad\pi_2=-c_{12},\quad
\pi_3=-c_{12}-c_{23},\quad\pi_4=c_{41}.
$$

Now only the nonbasic arc $34$ has a possibly nonzero [network reduced cost](../../../graph-theory.md#network-reduced-cost), namely $r_{34}=C<0$. It is at its upper bound, so the sign is optimal. Thus for all real costs the complete answer is

$$
\boxed{\begin{cases}
f(2)=(5,2,3,4),&C>0,\\
f(t),\quad2\leq t\leq4,&C=0,\\
f(4)=(7,4,5,6),&C<0.
\end{cases}}
$$

When $C=0$, both endpoint [basic feasible solutions](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#basic-feasible-solution) and every intermediate [feasible flow](../../../graph-theory.md#feasible-flow) have the same cost.

To count [basic solutions](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#basic-solution), a [network simplex tree basis](../../../graph-theory.md#network-simplex-tree-basis) omits one arc of the four-cycle and fixes that arc at either bound. The possibilities are

$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c|c}
\text{nonbasic arc}&\text{bound used}&t&\text{feasible?}\\\hline
12&\text{lower}&0&\text{no}\\
12&\text{upper}&5&\text{no}\\
23&\text{lower}&2&\text{yes}\\
23&\text{upper}&5&\text{no}\\
34&\text{lower}&1&\text{no}\\
34&\text{upper}&4&\text{yes}\\
41&\text{lower}&-2&\text{no}\\
41&\text{upper}&6&\text{no}
\end{array}
$$

There are **eight tree/bound basic representations, seven distinct basic flow vectors, and two basic feasible solutions**. The distinction in the first count matters: the upper bounds on $12$ and $23$ both give the identical flow $(8,5,6,7)$ at $t=5$. Thus if “basic solutions” counts vectors rather than basis representations, the answer to (i) is seven; the frequently used count of basis/bound choices is eight. The answer to (ii) is unambiguously two, since only $t=2,4$ satisfy all capacities.

<h3 id="4/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#4)

Assume the finite [graph](../../../graph.md) is connected, as is necessary for a [spanning tree](../../../combinatorics.md#spanning-tree) to exist. For a disconnected [graph](../../../graph.md), the same procedures operate separately on its [connected components of a graph](../../../graph.md#component-graph-theory) and produce a minimum spanning [forest](../../../combinatorics.md#forest).

Let $T$ be a [minimum spanning tree](../../../combinatorics.md#minimum-spanning-tree). If it omitted the unique lightest [edge](../../../graph-theory.md#edge-of-a-graph) $e$ across the specified [cut of a graph](../../../graph-theory.md#cut-graph-theory), adding $e$ would make exactly one [graph cycle](../../../graph-theory.md#cycle-in-a-graph): the [graph path](../../../graph-theory.md#path-in-a-graph) in $T$ between its endpoints, followed by $e$. Since those endpoints are on opposite sides of the [cut of a graph](../../../graph-theory.md#cut-graph-theory), that [graph path](../../../graph-theory.md#path-in-a-graph) contains another crossing [edge](../../../graph-theory.md#edge-of-a-graph) $f$. Distinct weights give $w_f>w_e$. The [graph](../../../graph.md) $T+e-f$ is still a [spanning tree](../../../combinatorics.md#spanning-tree), but has smaller total weight, contradicting minimality. Thus **every minimum spanning tree contains $e$**, the [minimum spanning tree cut property](../../../combinatorics.md#minimum-spanning-tree-cut-property).

[Prim algorithm](../../../combinatorics.md#prim-s-algorithm) starts from one [graph vertex](../../../graph.md#vertex-graph-theory), repeatedly choosing the lightest [edge](../../../graph-theory.md#edge-of-a-graph) from the reached [graph vertices](../../../graph.md#vertex-graph-theory) to an unreached [graph vertex](../../../graph.md#vertex-graph-theory). Connectivity ensures that such an [edge](../../../graph-theory.md#edge-of-a-graph) exists until all [graph vertices](../../../graph.md#vertex-graph-theory) have been reached. Each choice adds one new [graph vertex](../../../graph.md#vertex-graph-theory) and creates no [graph cycle](../../../graph-theory.md#cycle-in-a-graph). By the [minimum spanning tree cut property](../../../combinatorics.md#minimum-spanning-tree-cut-property), every chosen [edge](../../../graph-theory.md#edge-of-a-graph) belongs to every [minimum spanning tree](../../../combinatorics.md#minimum-spanning-tree). After $|V|-1$ choices the selected [edges](../../../graph-theory.md#edge-of-a-graph) form a [spanning tree](../../../combinatorics.md#spanning-tree), necessarily a [minimum spanning tree](../../../combinatorics.md#minimum-spanning-tree).

For [Kruskal algorithm](../../../combinatorics.md#kruskal-s-algorithm), suppose the next accepted [edge](../../../graph-theory.md#edge-of-a-graph) $e$ joins two different components of the current [forest](../../../combinatorics.md#forest), and let $S$ be one of those components. No previously considered lighter [edge](../../../graph-theory.md#edge-of-a-graph) crosses $S$: an accepted one already joins [graph vertices](../../../graph.md#vertex-graph-theory) within a current component, while a rejected one had its endpoints connected when it was rejected and therefore still has them in a single current component. Consequently $e$ is lightest across the [cut of a graph](../../../graph-theory.md#cut-graph-theory) defined by $S$. The [minimum spanning tree cut property](../../../combinatorics.md#minimum-spanning-tree-cut-property) again places every accepted [edge](../../../graph-theory.md#edge-of-a-graph) in every [minimum spanning tree](../../../combinatorics.md#minimum-spanning-tree). At termination the accepted [forest](../../../combinatorics.md#forest) must be connected, since otherwise a crossing [edge](../../../graph-theory.md#edge-of-a-graph) of the original connected [graph](../../../graph.md) would have been accepted when considered. It is therefore a [minimum spanning tree](../../../combinatorics.md#minimum-spanning-tree).

This reasoning also proves **the minimum spanning tree is unique**. Either procedure produces a [spanning tree](../../../combinatorics.md#spanning-tree) all of whose $|V|-1$ [edges](../../../graph-theory.md#edge-of-a-graph) belong to every [minimum spanning tree](../../../combinatorics.md#minimum-spanning-tree); another [minimum spanning tree](../../../combinatorics.md#minimum-spanning-tree) cannot have any different [edge](../../../graph-theory.md#edge-of-a-graph).

For the [minimum spanning tree cycle property](../../../combinatorics.md#minimum-spanning-tree-cycle-property), suppose a [spanning tree](../../../combinatorics.md#spanning-tree) $T$ contained the unique heaviest [edge](../../../graph-theory.md#edge-of-a-graph) $e$ of a [graph cycle](../../../graph-theory.md#cycle-in-a-graph) $C$. Removing $e$ splits $T$ into two components. The remainder of $C$ connects the endpoints of $e$, so it contains an [edge](../../../graph-theory.md#edge-of-a-graph) $f$ across these components. This [edge](../../../graph-theory.md#edge-of-a-graph) cannot belong to $T-e$, and $w_f<w_e$. Hence $T-e+f$ is a lighter [spanning tree](../../../combinatorics.md#spanning-tree). Thus **no minimum spanning tree contains $e$**.

Finally consider the [reverse-delete algorithm](../../../combinatorics.md#reverse-delete-algorithm), processing [edges](../../../graph-theory.md#edge-of-a-graph) from largest to smallest weight. Whenever an [edge](../../../graph-theory.md#edge-of-a-graph) is retained because its deletion would disconnect the current [graph](../../../graph.md), it is a [bridge in a graph](../../../graph.md#bridge-graph-theory); deleting other [edges](../../../graph-theory.md#edge-of-a-graph) later cannot create an alternative path around it, so it remains a [bridge in a graph](../../../graph.md#bridge-graph-theory). If a currently processed [edge](../../../graph-theory.md#edge-of-a-graph) $e$ is deletable, it lies on a current [graph cycle](../../../graph-theory.md#cycle-in-a-graph). None of that [graph cycle](../../../graph-theory.md#cycle-in-a-graph)'s other [edges](../../../graph-theory.md#edge-of-a-graph) can be an earlier retained heavier [edge](../../../graph-theory.md#edge-of-a-graph), since those are [bridges in a graph](../../../graph.md#bridge-graph-theory). Thus all other [edges](../../../graph-theory.md#edge-of-a-graph) on the [graph cycle](../../../graph-theory.md#cycle-in-a-graph) are lighter than $e$. The [minimum spanning tree cycle property](../../../combinatorics.md#minimum-spanning-tree-cycle-property) says that deleting $e$ removes no [edge](../../../graph-theory.md#edge-of-a-graph) of any [minimum spanning tree](../../../combinatorics.md#minimum-spanning-tree) of the original [graph](../../../graph.md). Throughout, connectivity and the presence of the unique [minimum spanning tree](../../../combinatorics.md#minimum-spanning-tree) are preserved. At termination a surviving [graph cycle](../../../graph-theory.md#cycle-in-a-graph) is impossible: any of its [edges](../../../graph-theory.md#edge-of-a-graph) was deletable when considered, since that entire surviving [graph cycle](../../../graph-theory.md#cycle-in-a-graph) already existed then. The final connected [graph](../../../graph.md) is a [tree](../../../combinatorics.md#tree-graph-theory), and hence is exactly **the minimum spanning tree**.

## 5

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 38](paper-38.md)

<h3 id="5/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#5)

Use a complete [directed graph](../../../graph-theory.md#directed-graph) on $n\geq3$ cities, with integral arc costs $c_{ij}$ and a threshold $L$. A symmetric cost matrix also represents the undirected [travelling salesman problem](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#travelling-salesman-problem) by orienting a tour. Let the binary variable $x_{ij}$ indicate that city $j$ follows city $i$, and forbid self-arcs. The decision [integer programming](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#integer-programming) formulation asks whether the following system has a solution:

$$
\begin{gathered}
x_{ij}\in\{0,1\}\quad(i\ne j),\\
\sum_{j\ne i}x_{ij}=1\quad(i=1,\ldots,n),\qquad
\sum_{i\ne j}x_{ij}=1\quad(j=1,\ldots,n),\\
\sum_{\substack{i,j\in S\\i\ne j}}x_{ij}\leq|S|-1
\quad(\varnothing\ne S\subsetneq\{1,\ldots,n\}),\\
\sum_{i\ne j}c_{ij}x_{ij}\leq L.
\end{gathered}
$$

The in-degree and out-degree equations make the selected arcs a [cycle cover of a directed graph](../../../graph-theory.md#cycle-cover-of-a-directed-graph). Any proper [graph cycle](../../../graph-theory.md#cycle-in-a-graph) on a city set $S$ would violate its subset inequality, so the selected arcs form one [Hamiltonian cycle](../../../graph-theory.md#hamilton-cycle). Conversely, a [Hamiltonian cycle](../../../graph-theory.md#hamilton-cycle) obeys every inequality: a proper subset cannot contain all the successor arcs of its [graph vertices](../../../graph.md#vertex-graph-theory). Thus the binary feasibility system is equivalent to the decision [travelling salesman problem](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#travelling-salesman-problem). The subset formulation has exponentially many inequalities, which is harmless for this formulation; verifying a proposed tour does not require listing them.

To call this decision problem [NP-complete](../../../computer-science.md#np-completeness) means two separate things. It belongs to [NP](../../../computer-science.md#np-complexity): a yes-instance has a polynomial-length [certificate in computational complexity](../../../computer-science.md#certificate-complexity), namely the cyclic ordering of the cities, and a deterministic algorithm checks distinct visits, the return to the start, and the total cost in [polynomial time](../../../computer-science.md#polynomial-time) in the binary input length. It is also [NP-hard](../../../computer-science.md#np-hardness): every decision problem in [NP](../../../computer-science.md#np-complexity) has a [polynomial-time many-one reduction](../../../computer-science.md#polynomial-time-many-one-reduction) to it, preserving yes and no answers. This is a statement about worst-case computation on finite encodings, not a proof that every individual tour is hard to find or that no polynomial algorithm exists; that last conclusion would require $\mathrm P\ne\mathrm{NP}$.

For the strict-threshold decision [Max-TSP](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#max-tsp), choose an integer $M\geq\max_{i\ne j}c_{ij}$ and replace each arc cost by $c'_{ij}=M-c_{ij}\geq0$. Every tour uses exactly $n$ arcs, so

$$
C'=nM-C,\qquad
C\leq L\ \Longleftrightarrow\ C'\geq nM-L
\ \Longleftrightarrow\ C'>nM-L-1.
$$

Set the new threshold to $L'=nM-L-1$. Its encoding length, the transformed matrix's encoding length and the computation time are all polynomial in the original input length. If thresholds are required to be nonnegative, take $M\geq\max(0,\max c_{ij},L+1)$; the same identity works. Symmetry of costs is preserved. This is a [polynomial-time many-one reduction](../../../computer-science.md#polynomial-time-many-one-reduction) from decision [travelling salesman problem](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#travelling-salesman-problem) to decision [Max-TSP](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#max-tsp), so the latter is [NP-hard](../../../computer-science.md#np-hardness). A tour is again a polynomially verifiable [certificate in computational complexity](../../../computer-science.md#certificate-complexity), now checking strict excess over $L'$, so

$$
\boxed{\text{decision Max-TSP is NP-complete}.}
$$

For the [approximation algorithm](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#approximation-algorithm), use the standard nonnegative-length setting on a complete [graph](../../../graph.md); no [triangle inequality](../../../topological-analysis.md#triangle-inequality) is required. Solve the maximum-weight [assignment problem](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#assignment-problem) with diagonal assignments forbidden, for example by applying the [Hungarian algorithm](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#hungarian-algorithm) to the negative weights. The resulting [cycle cover of a directed graph](../../../graph-theory.md#cycle-cover-of-a-directed-graph) has weight $W$ with $W\geq\operatorname{OPT}$, since every tour is an allowed assignment.

If it already has a single [graph cycle](../../../graph-theory.md#cycle-in-a-graph), it is an optimal tour. Otherwise remove a least-weight arc from each [graph cycle](../../../graph-theory.md#cycle-in-a-graph). A [graph cycle](../../../graph-theory.md#cycle-in-a-graph) of $r\geq2$ arcs and weight $W_r\geq0$ loses at most $W_r/r\leq W_r/2$. The remaining arcs form vertex-disjoint [graph paths](../../../graph-theory.md#path-in-a-graph) covering all cities. Join the endpoint of each [graph path](../../../graph-theory.md#path-in-a-graph) to the start of the next, cyclically. The required arcs exist by completeness, and their nonnegative weights cannot diminish the retained weight. The resulting single [Hamiltonian cycle](../../../graph-theory.md#hamilton-cycle) therefore satisfies

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{ALG}\geq\frac W2\geq\frac{\operatorname{OPT}}2.}
$$

The [Hungarian algorithm](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#hungarian-algorithm) takes $O(n^3)$ arithmetic operations, and finding the [graph cycles](../../../graph-theory.md#cycle-in-a-graph), cutting them and patching the [graph paths](../../../graph-theory.md#path-in-a-graph) takes $O(n)$ further operations. For rational or integer weights these operations have polynomial encoding length, giving [polynomial time](../../../computer-science.md#polynomial-time). With symmetric costs, the same directed construction is a valid undirected tour after forgetting its orientation. This is the [cycle-cover patching half-approximation for Max-TSP](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#cycle-cover-patching-half-approximation-for-max-tsp).

Nonnegativity is essential to this multiplicative guarantee. If arbitrary signed weights were allowed, giving every arc cost $-1$ would make every tour have weight $-n$, while half the optimum is $-n/2$. No tour could achieve the requested bound. Thus “length” has its usual nonnegative meaning in the approximation assertion.

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 38](paper-38.md)

<h3 id="6/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#6)

In a [finite game](../../../game-theory.md#finite-game), a profile of independent [mixed strategies](../../../game-theory.md#mixed-strategy) $\sigma=(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_n)$ is a [Nash equilibrium](../../../game-theory.md#nash-equilibrium) if no player can improve their expected payoff by changing only their own [mixed strategy](../../../game-theory.md#mixed-strategy). In symbols,

$$
U_a(\sigma_a,\sigma_{-a})\geq U_a(\tau_a,\sigma_{-a})
\quad\text{for every player }a\text{ and every strategy }\tau_a.
$$

Because expectation is linear in a player's own [mixed strategy](../../../game-theory.md#mixed-strategy), checking this inequality for all their [pure strategies](../../../game-theory.md#pure-strategy) suffices. Nothing requires the players' payoffs to sum to zero.

For the [symmetric finite game](../../../game-theory.md#symmetric-finite-game), write $e_i(p)=e(i,p)$ and $\bar e(p)=\sum_i p_i e_i(p)$. Each $e_i(p)$ is a polynomial in the opponents' independent probabilities and therefore continuous on the [probability simplex](../../../algebraic-topology.md#probability-simplex) $\Delta=\{p\geq0:\sum_i p_i=1\}$. Put

$$
r_i(p)=\max(0,e_i(p)-\bar e(p)),\qquad
S(p)=\sum_i r_i(p),\qquad
F_i(p)=\frac{p_i+r_i(p)}{1+S(p)}.
$$

This continuous map has nonnegative coordinates summing to one, so it maps the compact [convex set](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#convex-set) $\Delta$ into itself. The [Brouwer fixed-point theorem](../../../topological-analysis.md#brouwer-fixed-point-theorem) gives $p=F(p)$. At that fixed point $r_i=p_iS$. If $S>0$, every $i$ in the [strategy support](../../../game-theory.md#strategy-support) has $r_i>0$ and therefore $e_i(p)>\bar e(p)$. But

$$
\sum_i p_i(e_i(p)-\bar e(p))=0,
$$

whereas every supported term would be strictly positive, a contradiction. Thus $S=0$, all pure payoffs are at most $\bar e(p)$, and their weighted average equals $\bar e(p)$. Every supported [pure strategy](../../../game-theory.md#pure-strategy) is consequently a [best response](../../../game-theory.md#best-response) to the common opposing [mixed strategy](../../../game-theory.md#mixed-strategy). **The profile in which every player uses $p$ is a symmetric Nash equilibrium.** This proves the [gain-map proof of symmetric equilibrium](../../../game-theory.md#gain-map-proof-of-symmetric-equilibrium) for any finite number of players, including boundary fixed points of the [probability simplex](../../../algebraic-topology.md#probability-simplex).

In the [least unique bid auction](../../../game-theory.md#least-unique-bid-auction), let $p$ be the common [probability](../../../probability-theory.md#probability) of bid 1. Bidding 1 wins only when the other two bids are both 2, and its winning payoff is $3-1=2$. Bidding 2 wins only when the other two bids are both 1, and its winning payoff is $3-2=1$. Hence the two expected payoffs are

$$
e_1(p)=2(1-p)^2,\qquad e_2(p)=p^2.
$$

Neither symmetric [pure strategy](../../../game-theory.md#pure-strategy) profile is an equilibrium: against two bids of 1 a deviation to 2 earns 1 instead of 0, and against two bids of 2 a deviation to 1 earns 2 instead of 0. An interior [symmetric equilibrium](../../../game-theory.md#symmetric-equilibrium) must make the two expected payoffs equal. Solving $2(1-p)^2=p^2$ leaves just one root in $(0,1)$, giving

$$
\boxed{p=2-\sqrt2\text{ for bid 1},\qquad1-p=\sqrt2-1\text{ for bid 2}.}
$$

The expected payoff to each player is $p^2=6-4\sqrt2$.

To count all nonsymmetric [Nash equilibria](../../../game-theory.md#nash-equilibrium), let $p_i$ be player $i$'s [probability](../../../probability-theory.md#probability) of bidding 1. The difference between their expected payoffs from bids 1 and 2 is

$$
\Delta_i=2(1-p_j)(1-p_k)-p_jp_k
=2-2p_j-2p_k+p_jp_k.
$$

The [best response](../../../game-theory.md#best-response) conditions are $p_i=1$ when $\Delta_i>0$, $p_i=0$ when $\Delta_i<0$, and arbitrary $p_i$ when $\Delta_i=0$.

For any $0\leq t\leq1$, the profile $(p_1,p_2,p_3)=(1,0,t)$ satisfies these conditions: $\Delta_1=2(1-t)\geq0$, $\Delta_2=-t\leq0$, and $\Delta_3=0$. Thus every permutation of this profile is a [Nash equilibrium](../../../game-theory.md#nash-equilibrium). At $t=0,1$ these give the six pure profiles with at least one bid of each kind; for $0<t<1$, one player mixes freely and the other two use opposite [pure strategies](../../../game-theory.md#pure-strategy).

There are no other nonsymmetric equilibria. If all three probabilities are interior, every $\Delta_i$ must vanish. Subtracting two such equations gives

$$
\Delta_1-\Delta_2=(p_1-p_2)(2-p_3)=0.
$$

Since $2-p_3>0$, all probabilities are equal, yielding the symmetric solution already found. If exactly two players mix and the third uses bid 2, either mixer strictly prefers bid 1 because $\Delta=2(1-p_j)>0$. If the third uses bid 1, either mixer strictly prefers bid 2 because $\Delta=-p_j<0$. Both cases are impossible. If exactly one player mixes, their indifference requires the other players to use opposite bids. Finally, with no mixing, the all-equal pure profiles fail and the remaining six satisfy the [best response](../../../game-theory.md#best-response) conditions.

Consequently the complete [Nash equilibrium](../../../game-theory.md#nash-equilibrium) set consists of the single symmetric mixed profile together with the six closed one-parameter families given by permutations of $(1,0,t)$. Their endpoints overlap at the six pure equilibria. In particular,

$$
\boxed{\text{there are uncountably infinitely many nonsymmetric equilibria}.}
$$

Counting only the six pure ones would miss the interior points of these families in the [two-bid three-player least unique bid auction](../../../game-theory.md#two-bid-three-player-least-unique-bid-auction).
