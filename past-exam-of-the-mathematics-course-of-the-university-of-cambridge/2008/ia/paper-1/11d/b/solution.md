<h1 id="11d/b/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [B](../b.md)

At zero, $g(0)=0$ and

$$
\frac{g(h)-g(0)}h=hf(h)\longrightarrow0
$$

by [continuity](../../../../../../continuous-function.md) of $f$ at zero. Thus $g$ is always [differentiable](../../../../../../differentiable-function.md) there, with $g'(0)=0$, regardless of whether $f'(0)$ exists.

At $x\ne0$, if $f$ is [differentiable](../../../../../../differentiable-function.md), the [product rule](../../../../../../product-rule.md) applied to $t^2f(t)$ proves that $g$ is [differentiable](../../../../../../differentiable-function.md). Conversely, if $g$ is [differentiable](../../../../../../differentiable-function.md) at $x$, the function $t^{-2}$ is [differentiable](../../../../../../differentiable-function.md) on a neighborhood of $x$. Apply the [product rule](../../../../../../product-rule.md) to $f(t)=t^{-2}g(t)$ on that neighborhood. This proves the [differentiability restored by a quadratic zero](../../../../../../differentiability-restored-by-a-quadratic-zero.md) equivalence:

$$
\boxed{g\text{ is differentiable at }x\iff x=0\text{ or }f\text{ is differentiable at }x.}
$$

At a nonzero differentiability point, $g'(x)=2xf(x)+x^2f'(x)$.
