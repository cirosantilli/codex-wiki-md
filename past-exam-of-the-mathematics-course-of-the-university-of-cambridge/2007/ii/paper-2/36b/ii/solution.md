<h1 id="36b/ii/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](../ii.md)

Using local speed $A/R^2$ and length $R$, the [Reynolds number](../../../../../../reynolds-number.md) is

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Re}(R)=\frac{A}{\nu R}=\frac{Q}{2\pi(1-\cos\alpha)\nu R}.}
$$

Inertia dominates and a thin viscous [boundary layer](../../../../../../boundary-layer.md) is possible when this is large, namely **$R\ll A/\nu$**. The crossover scale is $R\sim Q/[2\pi(1-\cos\alpha)\nu]$, or $Q/\nu$ up to the fixed-angle factor. The inverse dependence on $R$ explains why this inertial sink regime occurs near the tip rather than far away. The fixed cone angle must also allow the wall layer to be small relative to the local cone radius.
