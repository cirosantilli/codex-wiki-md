<h1 id="10g/ii/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](../ii.md)

A nonzero [polynomial](../../../../../../polynomial-split.md) of degree $n$ over a [field](../../../../../../field.md) has **at most $n$ distinct roots in that [field](../../../../../../field.md)**. For a proof, the [factor theorem](../../../../../../factor-theorem.md) gives $(t-a)\mid f(t)$ whenever $f(a)=0$. Distinct linear factors are coprime in $K[t]$, a [unique factorization domain](../../../../../../unique-factorization-domain.md), so for distinct roots $a_1,\ldots,a_k$ their product divides $f$. Its degree is $k$, giving $k\leq n$. One may equivalently remove one linear factor at a time and argue by induction.

If the [field](../../../../../../field.md) has at least $n$ elements, this bound is attained by a product of $n$ distinct linear factors. For a fixed finite [field](../../../../../../field.md) with fewer than $n$ elements, the largest possible number of distinct roots is $\min(n,|K|)$ instead; multiplicities do not create more distinct roots. The zero [polynomial](../../../../../../polynomial-split.md) is excluded since it has no finite degree.
