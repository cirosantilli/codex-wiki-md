<h1 id="5b/i/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [I](../i.md)

In the normalized second-order [linear differential equation](../../../../../../linear-differential-equation.md), an [ordinary point](../../../../../../ordinary-point-criterion-for-a-second-order-equation.md) $z_0$ is one at which both $p$ and $q$ are [holomorphic functions](../../../../../../holomorphic-function.md) in a neighbourhood of $z_0$. A [singular point of a differential equation](../../../../../../singular-point-of-a-differential-equation.md) is a point that is not ordinary. A singular point $z_0$ is a [regular singular point](../../../../../../regular-singular-point.md) if $(z-z_0)p(z)$ and $(z-z_0)^2q(z)$ extend analytically to $z_0$. Equivalently, $p$ has at most a simple pole and $q$ at most a double pole there. If a singular point fails this test, it is an [irregular singular point](../../../../../../irregular-singular-point.md). The normalized form matters: zeros of a leading coefficient must first be assessed after division by that coefficient.
