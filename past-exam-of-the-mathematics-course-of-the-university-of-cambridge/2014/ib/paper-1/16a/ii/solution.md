<h1 id="16a/ii/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](../ii.md)

The [method of images](../../../../../../method-of-images.md) places an [image charge](../../../../../../image-charge.md) $-q$ at the reflected position below the plane. The candidate potential in the physical upper half-space is

$$
\Phi=\frac q{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left[\frac1{\sqrt{x^2+y^2+(z-d)^2}}-\frac1{\sqrt{x^2+y^2+(z+d)^2}}\right].
$$

At $z=0$ the two terms cancel, so $\Phi=0$ and $E_x=E_y=0$. Direct differentiation gives

$$
E_z(0^+)=-\frac{qd}{2\pi\varepsilon_0(x^2+y^2+d^2)^{3/2}},\qquad
\sigma=-\frac{qd}{2\pi(x^2+y^2+d^2)^{3/2}}.
$$

Thus the upper-side field satisfies exactly the conductor boundary conditions from part (i). The fictitious pair does have a field below the plane, but that part of its solution is not used for the physical conductor.

In $z>0$, the potential has the correct real point source, the correct zero [Dirichlet boundary condition](../../../../../../dirichlet-boundary-condition.md), and the required decay at infinity. By [Uniqueness of the Dirichlet problem](../../../../../../uniqueness-of-the-dirichlet-problem.md), it is the physical potential there. This is why the artificial charge can represent the induced conductor response without being a second physical charge.
