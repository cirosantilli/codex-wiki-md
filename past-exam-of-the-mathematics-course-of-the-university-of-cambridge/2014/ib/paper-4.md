## 1G

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 4](paper-4.md)

<h3 id="1g/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1G](#1g)

Linearity of the [integral](../../../calculus.md#integral) in each argument gives a [bilinear form](../../../linear-algebra.md#bilinear-form), and $fg=gf$ gives [symmetry](../../../physics.md#symmetry-physics). Also $(f,f)=\int_{-1}^1 f(x)^2dx\geq0$. If a [polynomial](../../../polynomial.md) $f$ is nonzero somewhere on this interval, [continuity](../../../calculus.md#continuous-function) makes $f^2$ strictly positive on a smaller interval, so the [integral](../../../calculus.md#integral) is positive. A [polynomial](../../../polynomial.md) vanishing on the whole interval is the zero [polynomial](../../../polynomial.md). Thus the form is [positive-definite](../../../linear-algebra.md#positive-definite-bilinear-form) and is an [inner product](../../../linear-algebra.md#inner-product).

Apply the [Gram-Schmidt process](../../../linear-algebra.md#gram-schmidt-process) to $1,x,x^2$. Odd [functions](../../../function.md) have zero [integral](../../../calculus.md#integral), so $x$ is already [orthogonal](../../../linear-algebra.md#orthogonal-vectors) to $1$, and the third [orthogonal polynomial](../../../numerical-analysis.md#orthogonal-polynomial) is $x^2-1/3$. Their squared [norms](../../../functional-analysis.md#norm) are

$$
 \|1\|^2=2,\qquad \|x\|^2=\frac23,\qquad
 \|x^2-1/3\|^2=\frac25-\frac49+\frac29=\frac8{45}.
$$

Therefore an **orthonormal basis** is

$$
 \boxed{\left\{\frac1{\sqrt2},\ \sqrt{\frac32}\,x,\ \sqrt{\frac58}\,(3x^2-1)\right\}.}
$$

These are the first three normalized [Legendre polynomials](../../../differential-equation.md#legendre-polynomial). Their distinct degrees also prove that they span the three-dimensional [vector space](../../../vector-space.md).

## 2E

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 4](paper-4.md)

<h3 id="2e/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2E](#2e)

The [group presentation](../../../geometric-group-theory.md#group-presentation) gives the [quotient group](../../../group-theory.md#quotient-group) $\mathbb Z^3$ by the subgroup generated by the rows of

$$
 M=\begin{pmatrix}3&6&3\\0&9&9\\-3&3&6\end{pmatrix}.
$$

For the [Smith normal form](../../../algebra.md#smith-normal-form), the first [determinantal divisor](../../../algebra.md#determinantal-divisor) is the greatest common divisor of all entries, namely $3$. The nonzero two-by-two [matrix minors](../../../vector-space.md#minor-linear-algebra) are $\pm27$, so the second [determinantal divisor](../../../algebra.md#determinantal-divisor) is $27$. The third row is the second minus the first, and a nonzero minor shows the [rank](../../../linear-algebra.md#rank-one-quadratic-form) is exactly two. Consequently the nonzero invariant factors are $3$ and $27/3=9$, with one free generator. The [Fundamental theorem of finitely generated abelian groups](../../../group.md#fundamental-theorem-of-finitely-generated-abelian-groups) gives

$$
 \boxed{G\cong\mathbb Z\times\mathbb Z/3\mathbb Z\times\mathbb Z/9\mathbb Z.}
$$

An element killed by $3$ has zero free coordinate, arbitrary coordinate in $\mathbb Z/3\mathbb Z$, and coordinate $0,3$ or $6$ in $\mathbb Z/9\mathbb Z$. There are nine such elements. Removing the identity leaves **eight elements of order three**.

## 3F

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 4](paper-4.md)

<h3 id="3f/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3F](#3f)

A [contraction mapping](../../../analysis.md#contraction-mapping) $T$ on a [metric space](../../../topological-analysis.md#metric-space) satisfies $d(Tx,Ty)\leq qd(x,y)$ for all $x,y$, with one constant $0\leq q<1$. The [contraction mapping theorem](../../../analysis.md#contraction-mapping-theorem) says that on a nonempty [complete metric space](../../../topological-analysis.md#complete-metric-space) such a map has a unique [fixed point](../../../function.md#fixed-point); successive iterates from any starting point converge to it. More precisely, if $x_n=T^nx_0$, then $d(x_n,x_*)\leq q^nd(x_1,x_0)/(1-q)$.

The [Volterra integration operator](../../../functional-analysis.md#volterra-operator) maps [continuous functions](../../../calculus.md#continuous-function) to [continuous functions](../../../calculus.md#continuous-function). In the [uniform norm](../../../functional-analysis.md#supremum-norm),

$$
 \|Af-Ag\|_\infty\leq\|f-g\|_\infty.
$$

This constant cannot be decreased: for $f=1$ and $g=0$, $Af(x)=x$, and both [uniform norms](../../../functional-analysis.md#supremum-norm) are one. Hence **$A$ is not a contraction**.

Changing the order of [integration](../../../calculus.md#integral) over the triangular region gives

$$
 A^2f(x)=\int_0^x\int_0^t f(s)\,ds\,dt
 =\int_0^x(x-s)f(s)\,ds.
$$

Thus

$$
 \|A^2f-A^2g\|_\infty\leq\sup_{0\leq x\leq1}\frac{x^2}{2}\|f-g\|_\infty
 =\frac12\|f-g\|_\infty.
$$

**$A^2$ is a contraction, with sharp constant $1/2$.** This is an example of an [iterated contraction](../../../analysis.md#iterated-contraction).

## 4G

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 4](paper-4.md)

<h3 id="4g/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4G](#4g)

For an [entire function](../../../complex-analysis.md#entire-function), the [Cauchy derivative formula](../../../complex-analysis.md#cauchy-derivative-formula) on a positively oriented circle of radius $R$ about $z_0$ is

$$
 \boxed{f^{(n)}(z_0)=\frac{n!}{2\pi i}\oint_{|\zeta-z_0|=R}
 \frac{f(\zeta)}{(\zeta-z_0)^{n+1}}\,d\zeta,\qquad n=0,1,\ldots.}
$$

The resulting [Cauchy estimate](../../../analysis.md#cauchy-estimate) is $|f^{(n)}(z_0)|\leq n!M_R/R^n$, where $M_R$ is the maximum of $|f|$ on that circle.

The [Liouville theorem](../../../complex-analysis.md#liouville-theorem) says that a bounded [entire function](../../../complex-analysis.md#entire-function) is constant. Indeed, if $|f|\leq M$ on the plane, the [Cauchy estimate](../../../analysis.md#cauchy-estimate) with $n=1$ gives $|f'(z_0)|\leq M/R$. Sending $R$ to infinity gives $f'(z_0)=0$ at every point, hence $f$ is constant.

For the growth assumption, apply the [Cauchy estimate](../../../analysis.md#cauchy-estimate) on circles centred at zero. For every integer $n>k$,

$$
 |f^{(n)}(0)|\leq n!R^{k-n}\longrightarrow0\quad(R\longrightarrow\infty).
$$

All sufficiently high [Taylor series](../../../calculus.md#taylor-series) vanish. Since the [Taylor series](../../../calculus.md#taylor-series) of an [entire function](../../../complex-analysis.md#entire-function) converges everywhere, **$f$ is a polynomial**, of degree at most $\lfloor k\rfloor$ when $k\geq0$. If a negative finite exponent is interpreted for nonzero $z$, the same estimate forces every coefficient to vanish, giving $f=0$.

## 5D

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 4](paper-4.md)

<h3 id="5d/i">i</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [5D](#5d)

<h4 id="5d/i/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#5d/i)

Away from zero the [Dirac delta distribution](../../../distribution-theory.md#dirac-delta-function) is absent. On either half-line, use $u=1+k|z|$ and the [power-law ansatz](../../../differential-equation.md#power-law-ansatz) $\psi=u^\alpha$. The [Euler-Cauchy equation](../../../differential-equation.md#euler-cauchy-equation) becomes

$$
 \alpha(\alpha-1)-\frac{15}{4}=0,
 \qquad \alpha=\frac52\ \text{or}\ -\frac32.
$$

The growing term is excluded by boundedness at either end. Thus the independently bounded half-line solutions are

$$
 \boxed{\psi(z)=C_+(1+kz)^{-3/2}\quad(z>0),\qquad
 \psi(z)=C_-(1-kz)^{-3/2}\quad(z<0).}
$$

<h3 id="5d/ii">ii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [5D](#5d)

<h4 id="5d/ii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#5d/ii)

The sign of the [Dirac delta distribution](../../../distribution-theory.md#dirac-delta-function) in the equation is positive after the outer minus sign is expanded. Integrating through zero, the nonsingular potential contributes a term tending to zero, whereas the delta term contributes $3k\psi(0)$. The [jump condition for an impulse](../../../differential-equation.md#jump-condition-for-an-impulse) is therefore

$$
 \boxed{\psi'(0^+)-\psi'(0^-)=-3k\psi(0).}
$$

[Continuity](../../../calculus.md#continuous-function) at zero gives $C_+=C_-=C$. The two one-sided derivatives are $-3kC/2$ and $+3kC/2$, whose difference is exactly $-3kC$. Hence the matching condition introduces no further restriction on $C$, and the globally bounded solution is

$$
 \boxed{\psi(z)=C(1+k|z|)^{-3/2},\qquad C\in\mathbb R\ \text{or}\ \mathbb C.}
$$

This continuous solution has precisely the derivative jump required for its second [distributional derivative](../../../distribution-theory.md#distributional-derivative) to cancel the delta term. Its amplitude remains arbitrary because no normalization has been imposed.

## 6A

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 4](paper-4.md)

<h3 id="6a/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [6A](#6a)

For a normalized state and a [self-adjoint operator](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#self-adjoint-operator) $Q$ with finite second moment, the [quantum uncertainty](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#quantum-uncertainty) is defined by $\Delta Q=\|(Q-\langle Q\rangle)\psi\|$. Since $\langle Q\rangle$ is real,

$$
 (\Delta Q)^2=\langle(Q-\langle Q\rangle)^2\rangle
 =\boxed{\langle Q^2\rangle-\langle Q\rangle^2}.
$$

In an energy [eigenstate](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#eigenstate) of the [quantum harmonic oscillator](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#quantum-harmonic-oscillator),

$$
 E=\langle H\rangle
 =\frac{(\Delta p)^2+\langle p\rangle^2}{2m}
 +\frac{m\omega^2}{2}\bigl((\Delta x)^2+\langle x\rangle^2\bigr).
$$

Discarding the nonnegative squared means proves the stated lower bound. The [Heisenberg uncertainty principle](../../../quantum-theory.md#heisenberg-uncertainty-relation) is $\Delta x\,\Delta p\geq\hbar/2$, following from $[x,p]=i\hbar$. Applying the [arithmetic-geometric mean inequality](../../../mathematical-optimization.md#arithmetic-geometric-mean-inequality) to the two variance contributions gives

$$
 E\geq\frac{(\Delta p)^2}{2m}+\frac{m\omega^2(\Delta x)^2}{2}
 \geq\omega\Delta p\Delta x\geq\boxed{\frac{\hbar\omega}{2}}.
$$

Here $\omega>0$ is the oscillator frequency. The bound is the ground-state energy; equality requires zero means and the appropriate minimum-uncertainty width.

## 7A

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 4](paper-4.md)

<h3 id="7a/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [7A](#7a)

Use the ideal long-[solenoid](../../../electromagnetism.md#solenoid) approximation: negligible pitch and end effects, vacuum permeability $\mu_0$, and negligible [displacement current](../../../electromagnetism.md#displacement-current). By cylindrical symmetry the [magnetic field](../../../electromagnetism.md#magnetic-field) is axial. An [Ampère's law](../../../electromagnetism.md#ampere-s-circuital-law) rectangular loop with one axial side inside and the other outside gives $B_{\rm in}-B_{\rm out}=\mu_0NI$. A loop wholly outside shows the outside axial field is constant with radius; requiring it to vanish far away gives

$$
 \boxed{\mathbf B_{\rm in}=\mu_0NI\,\widehat{\mathbf z},\qquad
 \mathbf B_{\rm out}=0.}
$$

The axial sign is determined by the winding direction and the [right-hand rule](../../../electromagnetism.md#right-hand-rule). The small axial component of current from the spiral pitch is neglected in this idealization.

There are $Nl$ turns, and the [magnetic flux](../../../electromagnetism.md#magnetic-flux) through each is $\pi a^2\mu_0NI$. The flux linkage is $LI$, so the [self-inductance](../../../electromagnetism.md#self-inductance) is

$$
 \boxed{L=\mu_0N^2\pi a^2l.}
$$

The induced [electromotive force](../../../electromagnetism.md#electromotive-force) opposes increasing current by [Faraday's law](../../../electromagnetism.md#faraday-s-law-of-induction). Together with [Ohm's law](../../../electromagnetism.md#ohm-s-law), the circuit equation is $L\dot I+RI=\mathcal E_0$. Solving this first-order [ordinary differential equation](../../../differential-equation.md#ordinary-differential-equation) with $I(0)=0$ gives

$$
 \boxed{I(t)=\frac{\mathcal E_0}{R}\left(1-e^{-Rt/L}\right),\qquad t\geq0.}
$$

The [time constant](../../../analysis.md#time-constant) is $L/R$, and the limiting current is $\mathcal E_0/R$.

## 8C

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 4](paper-4.md)

<h3 id="8c/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [8C](#8c)

For any distinct nodes, the [polynomial](../../../polynomial.md) $q(x)=\prod_{j=1}^\nu(x-c_j)^2$ has degree $2\nu$, is nonnegative, and vanishes at every node. The [quadrature rule](../../../numerical-analysis.md#quadrature-rule) sum for $q$ is zero, but $\int_0^\pi wq\,dx>0$ because the weight is positive and $q$ is positive except at finitely many points. Therefore **no such rule is exact for all polynomials of degree at most $2\nu$**, irrespective of the chosen weights.

For the sine weight, its first four [moments](../../../probability-theory.md#moment) are $2,\pi,\pi^2-4,\pi^3-6\pi$. A monic degree-two [orthogonal polynomial](../../../numerical-analysis.md#orthogonal-polynomial) is

$$
 p_2(x)=x^2-\pi x+2,
$$

since direct use of these [moments](../../../probability-theory.md#moment) gives $\int p_2\sin x\,dx=\int xp_2\sin x\,dx=0$. Its roots lie in $(0,\pi)$ and are

$$
 \boxed{c_{1,2}=\frac\pi2\mp\sqrt{\frac{\pi^2}{4}-2},\qquad b_1=b_2=1.}
$$

The weights follow by requiring exactness for $1$ and $x$: $b_1+b_2=2$ and $b_1c_1+b_2c_2=\pi$, and the symmetric nodes imply both weights are one. To prove cubic exactness, divide any cubic as $f=ap_2+r$, with $a,r$ of degree at most one. The [integral](../../../calculus.md#integral) of $ap_2$ is zero by [orthogonality](../../../linear-algebra.md#orthogonal-vectors), and its nodal values vanish; the remaining linear [polynomial](../../../polynomial.md) is integrated exactly. This is the [two-node Gaussian quadrature with sine weight](../../../numerical-analysis.md#two-node-gaussian-quadrature-with-sine-weight); the argument proves its [Gaussian quadrature](../../../numerical-analysis.md#gaussian-quadrature) property without assuming a general quadrature theorem.

## 9H

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 4](paper-4.md)

<h3 id="9h/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [9H](#9h)

<h4 id="9h/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#9h/a)

The [Markov property](../../../markov-process.md#markov-property) and the [Chapman-Kolmogorov equation](../../../markov-process.md#chapman-kolmogorov-equation) give

$$
 \mathbb P(X_{2n+2}=j\mid X_{2n}=i,X_{2n-2},\ldots,X_0)
 =\sum_{k\in S}p_{ik}p_{kj}=(P^2)_{ij}.
$$

For each intermediate state, one first uses the next-step [transition probability](../../../markov-process.md#transition-probability) and then the following one; the sampled history adds no information once $X_{2n}$ is known. Thus $W$ is a homogeneous [Markov chain](../../../markov-process.md#markov-chain) with [transition matrix](../../../markov-process.md#stochastic-matrix) $P^2$ and initial law $\lambda$. In particular,

$$
 \boxed{\mathbb P(W_1=0)=\sum_{i\in S}\lambda_i\sum_{j\in S}p_{ij}p_{j0}.}
$$

If zero is not a state of $S$, this probability is zero.

<h3 id="9h/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [9H](#9h)

<h4 id="9h/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#9h/b)

<h5 id="9h/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#9h/b/i)

The authoritative definition here is $Y_n=|X_n|$. On the conditioning event, $X_{n-1}=0$. Since $p_{0,1}=0$, the additional condition $Y_n=1$ then forces $X_n=-1$. The [Markov property](../../../markov-process.md#markov-property) therefore gives

$$
 \boxed{\mathbb P(Y_{n+1}=0\mid Y_n=1,Y_{n-1}=0)=p_{-1,0}.}
$$

As usual, this [conditional probability](../../../probability-theory.md#conditional-probability) is asserted when the conditioning event has positive probability.

<h4 id="9h/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#9h/b)

<h5 id="9h/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#9h/b/ii)

Starting from $Y_{n-2}=0$ forces $X_{n-2}=0$. The first nonzero value is consequently $X_{n-1}=-1$. Remaining nonzero at the next step forces $X_n=1$, because $p_{-1,-1}=0$. Hence, on an event of positive probability,

$$
 \boxed{\mathbb P(Y_{n+1}=0\mid Y_n=1,Y_{n-1}=1,Y_{n-2}=0)=p_{1,0}.}
$$

The two values are unequal by assumption. They expose memory of the hidden sign: immediately after leaving zero the sign is negative, whereas after two consecutive nonzero observations it is positive. Whenever the relevant observed histories coexist with positive probability, the [Markov property](../../../markov-process.md#markov-property) for $Y$ fails, since its next-step law is not determined by its present value alone. In particular, the absolute-value projection does not preserve the [Markov property](../../../markov-process.md#markov-property) for all initial laws; the [strong lumpability](../../../markov-process.md#strong-lumpability) condition would require $p_{-1,0}=p_{1,0}$.

There is a small qualification to a blanket conclusion about an unspecified initial law. The printed assumptions do not ensure that either conditioning event is possible, and a degenerate initial law may never reach the hidden states that cause the failure. For example, in the order $-1,0,1$, take

$$
 P=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad X_0=0.
$$

All printed constraints hold, yet $Y_n=0$ always and is a [Markov chain](../../../markov-process.md#markov-chain). Thus the correct general conclusion is **the transformed process is not necessarily Markov**; the two computed probabilities give the intended failure when their histories are available.

## 10G

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 4](paper-4.md)

<h3 id="10g/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [10G](#10g)

The algebraic [dual space](../../../linear-algebra.md#dual-space) is $V^*=\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb R}(V,\mathbb R)$, the [vector space](../../../vector-space.md) of all real [linear functionals](../../../linear-algebra.md#linear-functional). For a finite [basis](../../../vector-space.md#basis) $e_1,\ldots,e_n$, define the [dual basis](../../../linear-algebra.md#dual-basis) by $e_i^*(\sum_j a_je_j)=a_i$, so $e_i^*(e_j)=\delta_{ij}$. Every [linear functional](../../../linear-algebra.md#linear-functional) satisfies

$$
 \lambda=\sum_{i=1}^n\lambda(e_i)e_i^*.
$$

This equality follows by evaluating both sides on each vector of the [basis](../../../vector-space.md#basis). It proves spanning directly. If $\sum_i b_ie_i^*=0$, evaluation on $e_j$ gives $b_j=0$, proving linear independence without assuming a theorem on dual dimensions.

For a [vector subspace](../../../vector-space.md#vector-subspace) $U$, its [annihilator of a vector subspace](../../../linear-algebra.md#annihilator-of-a-vector-subspace) is

$$
 U^\circ=\{\lambda\in V^*: \lambda(u)=0\text{ for all }u\in U\}.
$$

Extend a [basis](../../../vector-space.md#basis) $e_1,\ldots,e_r$ of $U$ to a [basis](../../../vector-space.md#basis) of $V$. Then $U^\circ$ has [basis](../../../vector-space.md#basis) $e_{r+1}^*,\ldots,e_n^*$, so $\boxed{\dim U^\circ=n-\dim U}$.

The [dual map](../../../linear-algebra.md#transpose-of-a-linear-map) of $\alpha:V\to W$ is $\alpha^*:W^*\to V^*$, $\lambda\mapsto\lambda\circ\alpha$. In the corresponding [dual bases](../../../linear-algebra.md#dual-basis) its [matrix](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) is the transpose of the [matrix](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) for $\alpha$. A [matrix](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) and its transpose have the same [rank](../../../linear-algebra.md#rank-one-quadratic-form), giving $\operatorname{rank}\alpha^*=\operatorname{rank}\alpha$. Directly,

$$
 \alpha^*\lambda=0\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \lambda(\alpha v)=0\text{ for all }v
 \ \Longleftrightarrow\ \lambda\in(\operatorname{im}\alpha)^\circ.
$$

Thus $\boxed{\ker\alpha^*=(\operatorname{im}\alpha)^\circ}$. Also every $\lambda\circ\alpha$ vanishes on $\ker\alpha$, so $\operatorname{im}\alpha^*\subseteq(\ker\alpha)^\circ$. Both spaces have dimension $\operatorname{rank}\alpha$ by the preceding [annihilator of a vector subspace](../../../linear-algebra.md#annihilator-of-a-vector-subspace) formula and [rank-nullity theorem](../../../linear-algebra.md#rank-nullity-theorem), proving

$$
 \boxed{\operatorname{im}\alpha^*=(\ker\alpha)^\circ.}
$$

Alternatively, a [linear functional](../../../linear-algebra.md#linear-functional) vanishing on the kernel descends to the image of $\alpha$ and can be extended to $W$ by extending a [basis](../../../vector-space.md#basis).

For the infinite-dimensional [polynomial ring](../../../commutative-algebra.md#polynomial-ring), the coefficient functionals $L_i$ are linearly independent: evaluate a finite relation on each monomial. However, they do not span the algebraic [dual space](../../../linear-algebra.md#dual-space). The [linear functional](../../../linear-algebra.md#linear-functional) $p\mapsto p(1)$ takes value one on every monomial, while any finite combination of the $L_i$ vanishes on every sufficiently high-degree monomial. Thus **the $L_i$ do not form a basis of $V^*$**. An infinite formal sum of coefficient functionals is not a finite linear combination.

## 11E

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 4](paper-4.md)

<h3 id="11e/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [11E](#11e)

<h4 id="11e/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#11e/a)

With the usual commutative, unital conventions, the chain is

$$
 \boxed{\text{field}\ \Longrightarrow\ \text{principal ideal domain}
 \ \Longrightarrow\ \text{unique factorization domain}\ \Longrightarrow\ \text{integral domain}.}
$$

A [field](../../../algebra.md#field) is an [integral domain](../../../commutative-algebra.md#integral-domain), and every nonzero [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) contains an invertible element and is consequently the whole ring. Its only [ideals](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) are $(0)$ and $(1)$, so it is a [principal ideal domain](../../../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal-domain).

To prove the middle implication, first show that a [principal ideal domain](../../../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal-domain) has the [ascending chain condition](../../../algebra.md#ascending-chain-condition) on [ideals](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal). The union of an ascending chain is an [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal), hence is $(a)$; its generator belongs to one member of the chain, and all later members equal $(a)$. Now suppose a nonzero nonunit admitted no factorization into [irreducible elements](../../../commutative-algebra.md#irreducible-element). It would factor into two nonunits, at least one of which again has no such factorization. Repeating would produce a strictly increasing chain of [principal ideals](../../../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal): if $a=bc$ with $c$ a nonunit, $(a)\subsetneq(b)$, since equality and cancellation would make $c$ invertible. This contradicts the [ascending chain condition](../../../algebra.md#ascending-chain-condition). Existence of factorization follows. By the permitted fact that every [irreducible element](../../../commutative-algebra.md#irreducible-element) is a [prime element](../../../commutative-algebra.md#prime-element) in a [principal ideal domain](../../../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal-domain), any irreducible factor in one factorization divides a factor in the other; cancellation then proves uniqueness up to units and order. This is a [unique factorization domain](../../../algebra.md#unique-factorization-domain). The final implication is part of the definition of a [unique factorization domain](../../../algebra.md#unique-factorization-domain).

None of the adjacent implications reverses. The [principal ideal domain](../../../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal-domain) $\mathbb Z$ is not a [field](../../../algebra.md#field), since $2$ is not invertible. The [polynomial ring](../../../commutative-algebra.md#polynomial-ring) $\mathbb Q[x,y]$ is a [unique factorization domain](../../../algebra.md#unique-factorization-domain), by applying the polynomial extension theorem for [unique factorization domains](../../../algebra.md#unique-factorization-domain) twice, but its [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) $(x,y)$ is not principal: a generator would divide both $x$ and $y$, so would be a unit, whereas every member of this ideal vanishes at the origin. Finally, $\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-5}]$ is an [integral domain](../../../commutative-algebra.md#integral-domain) as a subring of $\mathbb C$, but is not a [unique factorization domain](../../../algebra.md#unique-factorization-domain). Its multiplicative [norm](../../../functional-analysis.md#norm) is $N(a+b\sqrt{-5})=a^2+5b^2$. There is no element of norm $2$ or $3$. Hence $2,3,1+\sqrt{-5},1-\sqrt{-5}$, of norms $4,9,6,6$, are all irreducible: a nontrivial factorization would require one of those missing norms. The only units have norm one and are $\pm1$. The two factorizations

$$
 6=2\cdot3=(1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})
$$

are therefore not related by associates and reordering.

## 12F

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 4](paper-4.md)

<h3 id="12f/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [12F](#12f)

The [Fréchet derivative](../../../calculus.md#frechet-derivative) at $p=(x_0,y_0)$ is a [linear map](../../../vector-space.md#linear-map) $L:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R$ for which

$$
 f(p+h)=f(p)+Lh+o(\|h\|)\quad(h\to0).
$$

This is [differentiability](../../../analysis.md#differentiability); if it holds, $L(h_1,h_2)=D_1f(p)h_1+D_2f(p)h_2$.

To prove the sufficient condition, take a rectangle about $p$ contained in the open domain. Split the increment along its coordinate directions:

$$
 f(x_0+h,y_0+k)-f(p)
 =[f(x_0+h,y_0+k)-f(x_0,y_0+k)]
 +[f(x_0,y_0+k)-f(p)].
$$

The one-variable [mean value theorem](../../../calculus.md#mean-value-theorem), applicable because the restrictions are differentiable, represents these two terms as

$$
 hD_1f(x_0+\theta h,y_0+k)+kD_2f(x_0,y_0+\phi k),\qquad0<\theta,\phi<1.
$$

The zero-increment cases are interpreted directly. Subtract $D_1f(p)h+D_2f(p)k$. By [continuity](../../../calculus.md#continuous-function) of both [partial derivatives](../../../calculus.md#partial-derivative) at $p$, the remainder is at most $\varepsilon(|h|+|k|)\leq\sqrt2\varepsilon\sqrt{h^2+k^2}$ once the increment is small. This is the required little-oh remainder and proves [differentiability](../../../analysis.md#differentiability).

The converse is false. Set $f(x,y)=x^2\sin(1/x)$ for $x\ne0$, and $f(0,y)=0$. At each point with $x=0$, its [Fréchet derivative](../../../calculus.md#frechet-derivative) is zero, since the function increment has magnitude at most $h^2=o(\sqrt{h^2+k^2})$. It is smooth away from this line. But

$$
 D_1f(x,y)=2x\sin(1/x)-\cos(1/x)\quad(x\ne0),\qquad D_1f(0,y)=0,
$$

is not continuous there. Thus a function can be differentiable everywhere while its [partial derivatives](../../../calculus.md#partial-derivative) are discontinuous.

For $h$, every point with $x\ne0$ is a smooth point. At the origin, $|h(x,y)|\leq|xy|\leq(x^2+y^2)/2$, so $h$ is differentiable with zero derivative. At $(0,y_0)$ with $y_0\ne0$, the putative first [partial derivative](../../../calculus.md#partial-derivative) would require the limit $y_0\sin(1/x)$, which does not exist. Consequently the exact set of differentiability is

$$
 \boxed{\{(x,y):x\ne0\}\ \cup\ \{(0,0)\}.}
$$

## 13E

A [metric space](../../../topological-analysis.md#metric-space) is [complete](../../../topological-analysis.md#completeness) if every [Cauchy sequence](../../../real-analysis.md#cauchy-sequence) in it converges to a point of that space. The stated shrinking-set characterization is the [Cantor intersection theorem](../../../topological-analysis.md#cantor-s-intersection-theorem) together with its converse.

First assume [completeness](../../../topological-analysis.md#completeness), and choose $x_n\in A_n$. If $m,n\geq N$, then both points belong to $A_N$, so $d(x_m,x_n)\leq\operatorname{diam}(A_N)$. The vanishing [diameters](../../../topological-analysis.md#diameter) imply that $(x_n)$ is a [Cauchy sequence](../../../real-analysis.md#cauchy-sequence), hence it converges to some $x\in X$. For every fixed $N$, its tail belongs to the [closed set](../../../topology.md#closed-set) $A_N$, so $x\in A_N$. Thus $x\in\bigcap_N A_N$. In fact the intersection contains only one point, since any two of its points have distance at most every diameter.

Conversely assume the stated intersection property and let $(x_n)$ be a [Cauchy sequence](../../../real-analysis.md#cauchy-sequence). Put

$$
 A_N=\overline{\{x_n:n\geq N\}}^{\,X}.
$$

These [closures](../../../topology.md#closure-topology) are nonempty, closed and nested. Taking a [closure](../../../topology.md#closure-topology) does not increase the [diameter](../../../topological-analysis.md#diameter): approximate two points of the closure by points of the set and use [continuity](../../../calculus.md#continuous-function) of distance. The [Cauchy sequence](../../../real-analysis.md#cauchy-sequence) therefore makes $\operatorname{diam}(A_N)\to0$. Choose $x$ in the intersection. For $n\geq N$, both $x_n$ and $x$ belong to $A_N$, and $d(x_n,x)\leq\operatorname{diam}(A_N)\to0$. Every [Cauchy sequence](../../../real-analysis.md#cauchy-sequence) converges in $X$, proving **completeness**.

<h3 id="14b/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [14B](#14b)

For $\operatorname{Re}s>0$, integration by parts recursively in the [Laplace transform](../../../analysis.md#laplace-transform) gives

$$
 \boxed{\mathcal L[t^n](s)=\frac{n!}{s^{n+1}},\qquad
 \mathcal L[H(t-a)](s)=\int_a^\infty e^{-st}dt=\frac{e^{-as}}s.}
$$

The initial exponential boundary term vanishes, and $\mathcal L[1]=1/s$ starts the recursion. Here $H$ is the [Heaviside step function](../../../analysis.md#heaviside-step-function).

Transform the displacement in time, writing $Y(x,s)=\mathcal L_t[y](x,s)$. Zero initial displacement and velocity give $s^2Y=c^2Y_{xx}+g/s$. A particular solution is $g/s^3$, and the spatial homogeneous solutions are $e^{\pm sx/c}$. The prescribed far-field behavior excludes the growing exponential. The fixed boundary sets the remaining coefficient to $-g/s^3$, hence

$$
 Y(x,s)=\frac g{s^3}(1-e^{-sx/c}).
$$

Since $\mathcal L[t^2/2]=1/s^3$, the [Laplace transform time-shift rule](../../../analysis.md#laplace-transform-time-shift-rule) gives

$$
 \boxed{y(x,t)=\frac g2\left[t^2-H(t-x/c)(t-x/c)^2\right].}
$$

Equivalently, $y=gt^2/2$ for $t\leq x/c$, and $y=gxt/c-gx^2/(2c^2)$ for $t\geq x/c$. This solves the forced [wave equation](../../../wave-equation.md) in each region and has continuous displacement and first derivatives across the characteristic $t=x/c$, so it also solves the equation weakly there. It satisfies the fixed-end and initial conditions and equals $gt^2/2$ sufficiently far away at each fixed time. The end's influence travels at speed $c$.

## 15F

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 4](paper-4.md)

<h3 id="15f/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [15F](#15f)

An [embedded surface parametrization](../../../differential-geometry.md#embedded-surface-parametrization) is a smooth map $F:U\subset\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R^3$, with open $U$, injective differential of rank two at each point, and $F$ a [homeomorphism](../../../topology.md#homeomorphism) onto its image with the subspace topology. Its [induced metric](../../../riemannian-geometry.md#induced-metric) is

$$
 g_{ij}=\partial_iF\cdot\partial_jF,\qquad ds^2=g_{ij}du^idu^j.
$$

In coordinates, an arc-length-parametrized [geodesic](../../../riemannian-geometry.md#geodesic) satisfies

$$
 \boxed{\ddot u^k+\Gamma^k_{ij}\dot u^i\dot u^j=0,\qquad
 g_{ij}\dot u^i\dot u^j=1,}
$$

where the [Christoffel symbols](../../../riemannian-geometry.md#christoffel-symbol) are $\Gamma^k_{ij}=\tfrac12g^{k\ell}(\partial_i g_{j\ell}+\partial_j g_{i\ell}-\partial_\ell g_{ij})$. The normalization fixes unit speed; it is compatible with the differential equations because geodesic speed is constant.

For the cone use $F(r,\theta)=(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta,\sqrt3r)$, $r>0$, restricting $\theta$ to a suitable open interval for a chart. Direct differentiation gives its [first fundamental form](../../../differential-geometry.md#first-fundamental-form)

$$
 ds^2=4\,dr^2+r^2d\theta^2.
$$

With $\rho=2r$ and $\phi=\theta/2$, this becomes $d\rho^2+\rho^2d\phi^2$, the Euclidean [metric](../../../topological-analysis.md#metric) in [polar coordinates](../../../calculus.md#polar-coordinates). The map $(r,\theta)\mapsto(2r\cos(\theta/2),2r\sin(\theta/2))$ is therefore a [local isometry](../../../differential-geometry.md#local-isometry). It is local rather than global because increasing $\theta$ by $2\pi$ increases $\phi$ by $\pi$.

Given two points, choose their angle representatives so that $|\theta_2-\theta_1|\leq\pi$. Their developed plane angles then differ by at most $\pi/2$. The straight segment between their developed positions stays away from the origin: it lies in an angular sector of width at most $\pi/2$ containing both positive-radius endpoints. Develop that segment back onto the cone. Since [local isometries](../../../differential-geometry.md#local-isometry) preserve [geodesics](../../../riemannian-geometry.md#geodesic), it gives a geodesic joining the two points; it can be parametrized by arc length. This constructs [geodesics on a punctured circular cone](../../../differential-geometry.md#geodesics-on-a-punctured-circular-cone). For a repeated point the constant curve is the degenerate geodesic. In particular, this argument does not appeal to completeness of the punctured cone.

**The joining geodesic need not be unique.** For endpoints with equal positive radius and opposite azimuth, one may use the angular changes $+\pi$ and $-\pi$. In the plane these give two straight segments from $(\rho,0)$ to $(0,\rho)$ and $(0,-\rho)$, respectively. Both avoid the origin. Their lifts pass on opposite sides of the cone and have distinct images, so a [reparametrization](../../../differential-geometry.md#reparametrization) cannot identify them.

## 16C

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 4](paper-4.md)

<h3 id="16c/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [16C](#16c)

Along a sufficiently smooth stationary curve, differentiation and the [Euler-Lagrange equation](../../../analysis.md#euler-lagrange-equation) give the [Beltrami identity](../../../analysis.md#beltrami-identity):

$$
 \frac d{dx}(f-y'f_{y'})=f_yy'-y'\frac d{dx}f_{y'}=0,
 \qquad\boxed{f-y'f_{y'}=\text{constant}.}
$$

The cancellation uses the absence of explicit $x$ dependence.

For an axisymmetric [surface of revolution](../../../differential-geometry.md#surface-of-revolution), its area is $I[y]=2\pi\int_{-l}^l y\sqrt{1+y'^2}\,dx$. A smooth positive-radius minimizer is stationary, so the [Beltrami identity](../../../analysis.md#beltrami-identity), with the constant factor omitted, gives

$$
 \frac{y}{\sqrt{1+y'^2}}=k>0.
$$

Equivalently the [Euler-Lagrange equation](../../../analysis.md#euler-lagrange-equation) gives $yy''=1+y'^2$, whence $y''=y/k^2$. The first integral selects

$$
 y=k\cosh((x-x_*)/k).
$$

Equal radii at $\pm l$ imply $x_*=0$ (subtract the two cosh values), giving

$$
 \boxed{y(x)=k\cosh(x/k),\qquad r=k\cosh(l/k).}
$$

This is a [catenoid](../../../differential-geometry.md#catenoid). The conclusion is a necessary form for a smooth connected minimizer; it does not declare every stationary catenoid a minimum.

Put $a=l/k>0$. The boundary equation is $r/l=\cosh a/a$. Its derivative is $(a\sinh a-\cosh a)/a^2$, so its unique critical point solves $a\tanh a=1$. The left side increases strictly from zero to infinity. Thus $\cosh a/a$ decreases to a unique minimum and then increases, diverging as $a\downarrow0$ and $a\to\infty$. There are exactly two positive values of $k$ when

$$
 r/l>\min_{a>0}\frac{\cosh a}{a}\simeq1.50888.
$$

At equality there is one value, and below it there is none.

To match the printed [second variation](../../../calculus-of-variations.md#second-variation) convention, write $I[y+\varepsilon\eta]=I[y]+\varepsilon\delta I+\varepsilon^2\delta^2I+o(\varepsilon^2)$; its $\delta^2I$ is the quadratic coefficient, half the second derivative with respect to $\varepsilon$. Expanding the area integrand gives

$$
 \delta^2I=\pi\int_{-l}^l\left[\frac{y}{(1+y'^2)^{3/2}}\eta'^2
 +2\frac{y'}{\sqrt{1+y'^2}}\eta\eta'\right]dx.
$$

On the [catenoid](../../../differential-geometry.md#catenoid) these coefficients are $k\operatorname{sech}^2(x/k)$ and $2\tanh(x/k)$. Integrate the mixed term by parts; the boundary term is zero because $\eta(\pm l)=0$. Since $(\tanh(x/k))'=\operatorname{sech}^2(x/k)/k$, this yields

$$
 \boxed{\delta^2I=\pi\int_{-l}^l\left(k\eta'^2-\frac{\eta^2}{k}\right)\operatorname{sech}^2(x/k)\,dx.}
$$

This is the [axisymmetric second variation of a catenoid](../../../differential-geometry.md#axisymmetric-second-variation-of-a-catenoid), with the explicitly stated quadratic-coefficient convention.

The [complex Fourier series](../../../fourier-series.md#complex-fourier-series) of a $2L$-periodic function is

$$
 \boxed{f(x)\sim\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}c_ne^{in\pi x/L},\qquad
 c_n=\frac1{2L}\int_{-L}^L f(x)e^{-in\pi x/L}\,dx.}
$$

The coefficient formula follows from [orthogonality](../../../linear-algebra.md#orthogonal-vectors) of the exponential functions. For an arbitrary [square-integrable function](../../../measure-theory.md#square-integrable-function), this representation means convergence in the $L^2$ [norm](../../../functional-analysis.md#norm). With appropriate additional regularity it also gives pointwise convergence, to the mean of the one-sided limits at a jump. No pointwise representation is asserted for an unrestricted function merely from periodicity.

<h3 id="17d/ii">ii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [17D](#17d)

<h4 id="17d/ii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#17d/ii)

The [Parseval identity](../../../fourier-analysis.md#parseval-identity) for a [complex Fourier series](../../../fourier-series.md#complex-fourier-series) is

$$
 \boxed{\frac1{2L}\int_{-L}^L|f(x)|^2dx=\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}|c_n|^2.}
$$

It holds for every [square-integrable function](../../../measure-theory.md#square-integrable-function), with complex conjugation expressed by the absolute squares. It is the [Pythagorean theorem](../../../geometry-and-topology.md#pythagorean-theorem) extended to the complete orthonormal system of complex exponentials.

<h3 id="17d/iii">iii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [17D](#17d)

<h4 id="17d/iii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#17d/iii)

Integrate the two exponentials in $\cos(\alpha x)$ against $e^{-inx}$. For $\alpha\notin\mathbb Z$ there are no zero denominators, and

$$
 c_n=\frac1{2\pi}\left[\frac{\sin\pi(\alpha-n)}{\alpha-n}
 +\frac{\sin\pi(\alpha+n)}{\alpha+n}\right]
 =\boxed{\frac{(-1)^n\alpha\sin(\pi\alpha)}{\pi(\alpha^2-n^2)}}.
$$

Thus the [complex Fourier series](../../../fourier-series.md#complex-fourier-series) is

$$
 \boxed{\cos(\alpha x)=\frac{\alpha\sin(\pi\alpha)}\pi
 \sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}\frac{(-1)^ne^{inx}}{\alpha^2-n^2},\qquad -\pi\leq x\leq\pi.}
$$

This is the periodic extension, not a globally nonperiodic cosine formula. For each fixed complex $\alpha$, the coefficients are $O(n^{-2})$, giving absolute and uniform convergence. The extension is continuous at the endpoints because the two endpoint values agree; piecewise smooth [pointwise convergence of a piecewise smooth Fourier series](../../../fourier-series.md#pointwise-convergence-of-a-piecewise-smooth-fourier-series) identifies the sum with it, including those endpoints.

Since $\sin(\pi\alpha)\ne0$ for nonintegral complex $\alpha$, rearranging proves

The last term means $\pi\cot(\pi\alpha)/(2\alpha)$, so half-integer arguments are included by the finite cotangent value zero.

## 18B

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 4](paper-4.md)

<h3 id="18b/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [18B](#18b)

Apply [mass conservation](../../../continuum-mechanics.md#mass-conservation) to a fixed horizontal region $D$. The layer mass is $\rho\int_Dh\,dx\,dy$ and the outward horizontal flux is $\rho\int_{\partial D}h(u,v)\cdot\mathbf n\,ds$. The [divergence theorem](../../../calculus.md#divergence-theorem) and arbitrary choice of $D$ give

$$
 \boxed{h_t+(hu)_x+(hv)_y=0.}
$$

This depth-integrated law retains free-surface motion; neglecting the vertical velocity in horizontal momentum is not the assumption that the depth cannot change.

Take the free-surface pressure to be constant. [hydrostatic pressure](../../../fluid-mechanics.md#hydrostatic-pressure) gives $p=p_{\rm atm}+\rho g(h-z)$, so the horizontal pressure forces are $-g\eta_x,-g\eta_y$. Linearization about a resting layer gives the [linearized shallow water equations](../../../physics.md#linearized-shallow-water-equations)

$$
 u_t-fv=-g\eta_x,\qquad v_t+fu=-g\eta_y,\qquad
 \eta_t+h_0(u_x+v_y)=0.
$$

Taking the curl of the momentum equations yields $\zeta_t=-f(u_x+v_y)$. Combining with continuity gives the [linearized shallow-water potential-vorticity anomaly](../../../geophysical-fluid-dynamics.md#linearized-shallow-water-potential-vorticity-anomaly)

$$
 \boxed{\partial_t\left(\zeta-\frac f{h_0}\eta\right)=0,\qquad Q=Q_0(x,y).}
$$

For $Q_0=0$, $\zeta=f\eta/h_0$. Taking the divergence of momentum gives $\partial_t(u_x+v_y)-f\zeta=-g\nabla_h^2\eta$. Eliminate the divergence with continuity to obtain

$$
 \boxed{\eta_{tt}-gh_0\nabla_h^2\eta+f^2\eta=0.}
$$

For $\theta=k(x-ct)$, substitution of $\eta=\eta_0\cos\theta$ gives the [inertia-gravity wave](../../../geophysical-fluid-dynamics.md#inertia-gravity-wave) [dispersion relation](../../../wave-equation.md#dispersion-relation)

$$
 \boxed{\omega^2=gh_0k^2+f^2,\qquad\omega=kc,\qquad c^2=gh_0+f^2/k^2.}
$$

The associated purely oscillatory [velocity field](../../../fluid-mechanics.md#velocity-field) is

$$
 u=U\cos\theta,\qquad v=V\sin\theta,\qquad
 U=\frac{c\eta_0}{h_0},\quad V=\frac{f\eta_0}{kh_0}=\frac f\omega U.
$$

These formulas satisfy all three linearized equations and the zero-anomaly condition. A separately added spatially uniform inertial oscillation is not part of this monochromatic wave.

To first order in amplitude evaluate the wave at a particle's equilibrium coordinate $x_*$, so $\theta_* = kx_*-\omega t$. Integrating its [velocity](../../../classical-mechanics.md#velocity) gives displacements relative to the orbit centre

$$
 \xi=-\frac U\omega\sin\theta_*,\qquad
 \upsilon=\frac V\omega\cos\theta_*,\qquad
 \boxed{\frac{\xi^2}{(U/\omega)^2}+\frac{\upsilon^2}{(V/\omega)^2}=1.}
$$

These are [particle ellipses for rotating shallow-water waves](../../../geophysical-fluid-dynamics.md#particle-ellipses-for-rotating-shallow-water-waves), with axis ratio $V/U=f/\omega<1$. For the stipulated positive signs, the velocity at phases $0,\pi/2,\pi,3\pi/2$ points respectively **right, up, left, down**, with magnitudes $U,V,U,V$. The particle orbits are clockwise as time advances, since the phase decreases.

## 19H

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 4](paper-4.md)

<h3 id="19h/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [19H](#19h)

For the [normal linear model](../../../statistical-modelling.md#normal-linear-model), differentiating the [ordinary least squares](../../../statistical-modelling.md#ordinary-least-squares) objective gives $X^TX\widehat\beta=X^TY$. Full column [rank](../../../linear-algebra.md#rank-one-quadratic-form) makes $X^TX$ invertible, so

$$
 \boxed{\widehat\beta=(X^TX)^{-1}X^TY,\qquad
 \widehat\beta\sim N_p\left(\beta,\sigma^2(X^TX)^{-1}\right).}
$$

The distribution follows because $\widehat\beta=\beta+(X^TX)^{-1}X^T\varepsilon$ is a linear transformation of a [multivariate normal distribution](../../../probability-and-statistics.md#multivariate-normal-distribution). Its [expectation](../../../probability-theory.md#expected-value) is $\beta$, and direct multiplication gives the displayed [covariance matrix](../../../variance.md#covariance-matrix).

Let $H=X(X^TX)^{-1}X^T$, the orthogonal projection onto the design's column space. The [residual sum of squares](../../../linear-regression.md#residual-sum-of-squares) is $RSS=\|Y-X\widehat\beta\|^2=Y^T(I-H)Y$. Since $(I-H)X\beta=0$, symmetry and idempotence give $\mathbb E[RSS]=\sigma^2\operatorname{tr}(I-H)=(n-p)\sigma^2$. Thus

$$
 \boxed{\widehat\sigma^2=\frac{RSS}{n-p}}
$$

is an [unbiased estimator](../../../statistical-modelling.md#unbiased-estimator).

For the two independent regressions choose $\beta=(a,b,c,d)^T$. The first $m$ rows of the [design matrix](../../../linear-regression.md#design-matrix) are $(1,u_i,0,0)$ and the last $m$ rows are $(0,0,1,w_i)$. The centering conditions and distinct predictors give

$$
 X^TX=\operatorname{diag}(m,S_u,m,S_w),\qquad
 S_u=\sum_i u_i^2>0,\quad S_w=\sum_i w_i^2>0.
$$

It follows that

$$
 \boxed{\widehat a=\overline V,\quad\widehat b=\frac{\sum_i u_iV_i}{S_u},\quad
 \widehat c=\overline Z,\quad\widehat d=\frac{\sum_i w_iZ_i}{S_w}.}
$$

The two estimated slopes are independent [normal random variables](../../../probability-theory.md#gaussian-random-variable), as they depend on independent error blocks. Therefore

$$
 \boxed{\widehat b-\widehat d\sim N\left(b-d,\sigma^2\left(\frac1{S_u}+\frac1{S_w}\right)\right).}
$$

For these data use the pooled [residual sum of squares](../../../linear-regression.md#residual-sum-of-squares)

$$
 RSS=\sum_i(V_i-\widehat a-\widehat b u_i)^2
 +\sum_i(Z_i-\widehat c-\widehat d w_i)^2,\qquad s^2=\frac{RSS}{2m-4}.
$$

Its scaled value is $\chi^2_{2m-4}$ and is independent of the slope estimates by the permitted normal-model result. Hence the standardized difference has a [Student t-distribution](../../../continuous-probability-distribution.md#student-s-t-distribution) with $2m-4$ degrees of freedom, giving the exact **95 percent confidence interval**

$$
 \boxed{(\widehat b-\widehat d)\ \pm\ t_{2m-4,\,0.975}\,
 s\sqrt{\frac1{S_u}+\frac1{S_w}}.}
$$

Here $t_{\nu,0.975}$ is the 0.975 [quantile](../../../probability-theory.md#quantile-function) of that distribution. This is a [confidence interval for the difference of independent regression slopes](../../../statistical-inference.md#confidence-interval-for-the-difference-of-independent-regression-slopes). As required by the original $p<n$ framework, this specialization needs $m>2$. Distinct predictors alone allow $m=2$, but then the fit is saturated, $RSS=0$ and there are no residual degrees of freedom to estimate the unknown variance or form this interval.

## 20H

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 4](paper-4.md)

<h3 id="20h/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [20H](#20h)

On a directed [flow network](../../../graph-theory.md#flow-network) with capacities $c_e$, choose [flows](../../../graph-theory.md#flow) $0\leq f_e\leq c_e$ satisfying inflow equals outflow at every vertex except source $s$ and sink $t$, and maximize the net source outflow $|f|$. For a [cut of a flow network](../../../graph-theory.md#cut-of-a-flow-network) $S$ containing $s$ but not $t$, its capacity is $\sum_{u\in S,v\notin S}c_{uv}$. The [max-flow min-cut theorem](../../../graph-theory.md#max-flow-min-cut-theorem) states

$$
 \boxed{\max_f |f|=\min_{s\in S,\ t\notin S}c(S,S^c).}
$$

Every feasible [flow](../../../graph-theory.md#flow) is bounded above by every cut capacity; equality for a feasible flow and a cut proves optimality.

The [Ford-Fulkerson algorithm](../../../graph-theory.md#ford-fulkerson-algorithm) starts with a feasible flow, for instance zero. A forward edge has residual capacity $c_{uv}-f_{uv}$ and its reverse edge has residual capacity $f_{uv}$. Find a source-to-sink [augmenting path](../../../graph-theory.md#augmenting-path) in this [residual network](../../../graph-theory.md#residual-network), and increase the flow along it by the minimum residual capacity on the path, reducing original-edge flows when reverse edges are used. Repeat until no such path exists. The vertices reachable from the source then define a saturated minimum [cut of a flow network](../../../graph-theory.md#cut-of-a-flow-network). With integer capacities and zero initial flow, all residual capacities and augmentations are integers. Each augmentation increases $|f|$ by at least one, while $|f|$ is bounded by the finite sum of source capacities, so termination takes finitely many steps.

For the original roads, a feasible maximizing flow is given below; every table entry is flow/capacity in vehicles per minute.

| Road | Before storm | After storm |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $A\to R_1$ | $40/60$ | $30/60$ |
| $A\to R_4$ | $50/50$ | $30/50$ |
| $R_1\to R_3$ | $10/10$ | $10/10$ |
| $R_1\to R_2$ | $30/30$ | $20/30$ |
| $R_4\to R_2$ | $20/40$ | $0/40$ |
| $R_4\to R_5$ | $30/30$ | $30/30$ |
| $R_2\to R_3$ | $30/40$ | $20/40$ |
| $R_2\to R_5$ | $20/30$ | $0/30$ |
| $R_3\to B$ | $40/40$ | $30/40$ |
| $R_5\to B$ | $50/50$ | $30/50$ |

All road capacities are respected, and direct summation verifies [flow conservation](../../../graph-theory.md#flow-conservation) at each roundabout. The original flow value is $90$. The cut separating $B$ from every other vertex consists of its two incoming roads and has capacity $40+50=90$. Therefore the original maximum is **90 vehicles per minute**.

To impose a [vertex capacity](../../../graph-theory.md#vertex-capacity), split $R_2$ into $R_2^-$ and $R_2^+$. Send its incoming roads to $R_2^-$ and its outgoing roads from $R_2^+$, and add a directed internal edge $R_2^-\to R_2^+$ of capacity $20$. Every unit passing through the roundabout must traverse this internal edge. This gives the standard [maximum flow with vertex capacities](../../../graph-theory.md#maximum-flow-with-vertex-capacities) construction.

Starting from zero, apply the [Ford-Fulkerson algorithm](../../../graph-theory.md#ford-fulkerson-algorithm) with the following [augmenting paths](../../../graph-theory.md#augmenting-path) in order. First use $A\to R_1\to R_3\to B$: its residual capacities are $60,10,40$, so augment by $10$. Next use $A\to R_4\to R_5\to B$: the residual capacities are $50,30,50$, so augment by $30$. Finally use $A\to R_1\to R_2^-\to R_2^+\to R_3\to B$: the residual capacities at this stage are $50,30,20,40,30$, so augment by $20$. No reverse edge is needed in this valid run. The resulting original-road flows are precisely the after-storm column above, and the internal edge carries $20$.

For termination and optimality, take the source-side set $\{A,R_1,R_4,R_2^-\}$. Its outgoing edges are $R_1\to R_3$, $R_4\to R_5$ and $R_2^-\to R_2^+$, of capacities $10,30,20$. They are all saturated, and no positive-flow original edge enters this set. Consequently no residual path leaves the set, and its cut capacity equals the constructed flow value. The new maximum is therefore

$$
 \boxed{60\ \text{vehicles per minute}.}
$$
