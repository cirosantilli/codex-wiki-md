<h1 id="12a/c/ii/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](../ii.md)

**The antisymmetric part need not be a [tensor](../../../../../../../tensor.md).** Its contraction with every symmetric test is zero, so the premise imposes no transformation law on it. This is the [blindness of symmetric contraction tests to antisymmetric arrays](../../../../../../../blindness-of-symmetric-contraction-tests-to-antisymmetric-arrays.md), now with a third free index.

For a concrete example, prescribe the same numerical array in every orthonormal frame, with only

$$
d^a_{121}=1,\qquad d^a_{211}=-1
$$

nonzero, and take $d^s=0$. Every contraction $d^a_{ijk}s_{ij}$ vanishes, and the zero result is a genuine [vector](../../../../../../../vector.md) in every frame. But under $R=\operatorname{diag}(-1,-1,1)$ the rank-three [tensor](../../../../../../../tensor.md) transformation law would send the $121$ component to $-1$, whereas our framewise prescription leaves it equal to $1$. Thus the array is not a [tensor](../../../../../../../tensor.md) despite satisfying every stipulated contraction test. An antisymmetric part could be a [tensor](../../../../../../../tensor.md) if an additional transformation law were supplied; it is simply not forced to be one.
