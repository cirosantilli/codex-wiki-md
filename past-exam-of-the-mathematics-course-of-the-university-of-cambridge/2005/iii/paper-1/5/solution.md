<h1 id="5/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [5](../5.md)

A [subnormal series](../../../../../subnormal-series.md) of a [finite group](../../../../../finite-group.md) is a chain $1=G_0\triangleleft G_1\triangleleft\cdots\triangleleft G_r=G$. A [normal series of a group](../../../../../normal-series-of-a-group.md) requires every $G_i$ normal in the whole group. This distinction matters: a [composition series](../../../../../composition-series.md) is a [subnormal series](../../../../../subnormal-series.md) with nontrivial simple factors, whereas a [chief series](../../../../../chief-series.md) is a strict, unrefinable [normal series of a group](../../../../../normal-series-of-a-group.md), whose factors are [minimal normal subgroups](../../../../../minimal-normal-subgroup.md) of the appropriate ambient quotients. Both exist in a [finite group](../../../../../finite-group.md), by successively choosing maximal proper [normal subgroups](../../../../../normal-subgroup.md) or a maximal chain of ambient-normal subgroups. The direct-product result proved above shows that [chief factors](../../../../../chief-factor.md) are powers of isomorphic [simple groups](../../../../../simple-group.md), rather than necessarily simple themselves.

The [Jordan–Hölder theorem](../../../../../jordan-holder-theorem.md) says that any two [composition series](../../../../../composition-series.md) have the same multiset of simple factors, up to isomorphism and order. Here is an induction proof. The assertion is trivial for the identity group. Let $A,B$ be the penultimate terms of two series for $G$; they are maximal proper [normal subgroups](../../../../../normal-subgroup.md). If $A=B$, apply induction there. Otherwise $AB=G$, since its image is a nontrivial [normal subgroup](../../../../../normal-subgroup.md) in each simple quotient. Put $C=A\cap B$. The maps to the quotients give

$$
A/C\cong G/B,\qquad B/C\cong G/A.
$$

For example, $a\mapsto aB$ has kernel $C$ and is onto because $AB=G$. Take a [composition series](../../../../../composition-series.md) of $C$ and append $A$, obtaining a [composition series](../../../../../composition-series.md) of $A$ because $A/C$ is simple; do the same with $B$. Induction inside these smaller groups shows that the factors in the two original series are, in both cases, the factors of $C$ together with $G/A$ and $G/B$. This proves the theorem and justifies the phrase [Jordan–Hölder factors](../../../../../jordan-holder-factor.md) independently of a chosen series.

The [derived series](../../../../../derived-series.md) is $G^{(0)}=G$, $G^{(i+1)}=[G^{(i)},G^{(i)}]$. Its terms are [characteristic subgroups](../../../../../characteristic-subgroup.md), hence normal. A group is soluble if this series reaches the identity. Equivalently it admits a [subnormal series](../../../../../subnormal-series.md) with abelian factors. One direction uses the [derived series](../../../../../derived-series.md) itself. For the other, note the extension principle: if $N\triangleleft G$ has derived length $a$ and $G/N$ has derived length $b$, then $G^{(b)}\le N$, so $G^{(a+b)}=1$. Applying this principle along an abelian-factor series proves solubility.

Subgroups and quotients of a [soluble group](../../../../../solvable-group.md) are soluble: $H^{(i)}\le G^{(i)}$ for a subgroup, and the [commutator subgroup](../../../../../commutator-subgroup.md) of a quotient is the image of the [commutator subgroup](../../../../../commutator-subgroup.md). A finite soluble [simple group](../../../../../simple-group.md) is abelian, since its [commutator subgroup](../../../../../commutator-subgroup.md) is normal and cannot equal the whole group if the [derived series](../../../../../derived-series.md) terminates. An abelian simple [finite group](../../../../../finite-group.md) is cyclic of prime order. Therefore **a [finite group](../../../../../finite-group.md) is soluble exactly when all its [Jordan–Hölder factors](../../../../../jordan-holder-factor.md) are cyclic of prime order**: necessity follows from the subgroup/quotient property, and sufficiency follows from the extension principle. Its [chief factors](../../../../../chief-factor.md) are elementary abelian, by the minimal-normal-subgroup result.

For nilpotence, the [lower central series](../../../../../lower-central-series.md) is $\gamma_1(G)=G$, $\gamma_{i+1}(G)=[\gamma_i(G),G]$. A group is nilpotent if $\gamma_{c+1}=1$ for some $c$. An ascending [central series](../../../../../central-series.md) $1=N_0\le\cdots\le N_c=G$ satisfies $[N_{i+1},G]\le N_i$. Reversing a terminating [lower central series](../../../../../lower-central-series.md) gives such a [central series](../../../../../central-series.md). Conversely, induction gives $\gamma_i(G)\le N_{c-i+1}$, so any such [central series](../../../../../central-series.md) forces $\gamma_{c+1}=1$. The [upper central series](../../../../../upper-central-series.md) $Z_0=1$, $Z_{i+1}/Z_i=Z(G/Z_i)$ contains every ascending [central series](../../../../../central-series.md) term by term, and thus reaches $G$ exactly in the nilpotent case. Because $[\gamma_i,\gamma_i]\le[\gamma_i,G]=\gamma_{i+1}$, induction gives $G^{(i)}\le\gamma_{i+1}$: **[nilpotent groups](../../../../../nilpotent-group.md) are soluble**.

For [finite groups](../../../../../finite-group.md) there is the stronger structural characterization: **a [finite group](../../../../../finite-group.md) is nilpotent exactly when it is the [direct product of groups](../../../../../direct-product-of-groups.md) formed from its [Sylow subgroups](../../../../../sylow-subgroup.md)**. To prove the forward direction, the [upper central series](../../../../../upper-central-series.md) gives the [normalizer](../../../../../normalizer.md) condition. If $H<G$, choose its first term $Z_i$ not contained in $H$, with $Z_{i-1}\le H$. An element of $Z_i\setminus H$ has all [group commutators](../../../../../group-commutator.md) with $H$ in $Z_{i-1}$, so normalizes $H$. Hence $H<N_G(H)$.

For a [Sylow subgroup](../../../../../sylow-subgroup.md) $P$, its [normalizer](../../../../../normalizer.md) is self-normalizing. Indeed, if $x$ normalizes $N_G(P)$, the two [Sylow subgroups](../../../../../sylow-subgroup.md) $P^x,P$ of that [normalizer](../../../../../normalizer.md) are conjugate there; adjusting $x$ by an element of $N_G(P)$ puts it in $N_G(P)$ already. Applying the strict [normalizer](../../../../../normalizer.md) condition to $N_G(P)$ shows that it cannot be proper, so $P$ is normal. Normal [Sylow subgroups](../../../../../sylow-subgroup.md) of distinct primes commute, since their [group commutators](../../../../../group-commutator.md) lie in their trivial intersection. Their product has [cardinality](../../../../../cardinality.md) $|G|$ and is direct.

Conversely, a nontrivial finite $p$-group has nontrivial centre: in the class equation every noncentral [conjugacy class](../../../../../conjugacy-class.md) has size divisible by $p$, so $p$ divides the centre's order. Induct on its order through the quotient by its centre to obtain an [upper central series](../../../../../upper-central-series.md). Thus every finite $p$-group is nilpotent, and a finite [direct product of groups](../../../../../direct-product-of-groups.md) of them is nilpotent because its [upper central series](../../../../../upper-central-series.md) is obtained componentwise.

A finite [supersolvable group](../../../../../supersolvable-group.md) has a [normal series of a group](../../../../../normal-series-of-a-group.md) with cyclic prime-order factors. It is soluble, but the ambient-normal condition is stronger than the condition on a [composition series](../../../../../composition-series.md). Finite [nilpotent groups](../../../../../nilpotent-group.md) are supersolvable: a $p$-group has a central subgroup of order $p$, and induction in its quotient builds an ambient-normal prime-factor series; [direct products of groups](../../../../../direct-product-of-groups.md) of the Sylow factors combine these series. The converses fail. The group $S_3$ is supersolvable via $1<C_3<S_3$, but is not nilpotent because its Sylow $2$-subgroups are not normal. The group $A_4$ is soluble via $1<V_4<A_4$, but is not supersolvable: no subgroup of order two is normal, since the three involutions are conjugate, and no subgroup of order three is normal, since there are four such subgroups. In this example $1<C_2<V_4<A_4$ is a [composition series](../../../../../composition-series.md) whose first $C_2$ is not normal in $A_4$, while $1<V_4<A_4$ is a [chief series](../../../../../chief-series.md) with a nonsimple [chief factor](../../../../../chief-factor.md) $V_4$.

Finally, a [perfect group](../../../../../perfect-group.md) satisfies $G'=G$. In any [finite group](../../../../../finite-group.md), the [derived series](../../../../../derived-series.md) eventually stabilizes at a perfect [characteristic subgroup](../../../../../characteristic-subgroup.md), because a descending sequence of finite subgroup orders eventually stops decreasing. It is trivial exactly when the group is soluble. A nonabelian [simple group](../../../../../simple-group.md) is perfect: its nontrivial [commutator subgroup](../../../../../commutator-subgroup.md) is normal and must be the whole group. Thus series distinguish successively stronger notions, summarize the building blocks through [Jordan–Hölder factors](../../../../../jordan-holder-factor.md), and separate soluble behaviour from a persistent perfect part.
