<h3 id="1/i">i</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/i/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#1/i)

For the [Weierstrass equation of an elliptic curve](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#weierstrass-equation-of-an-elliptic-curve) in this part, $a_1=a_2=a_6=0$, $a_3=1$, $a_4=-1$. Hence

$$
b_2=0,\qquad b_4=-2,\qquad b_6=1,\qquad c_4=48,\qquad c_6=-216.
$$

The [elliptic-curve discriminant](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#elliptic-curve-discriminant) is therefore

$$
\boxed{\Delta=\frac{48^3-216^2}{1728}=37.}
$$

Every [prime](../../../number-theory.md#prime-number) other than $37$ has unit discriminant, so this integral equation gives [good reduction](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#good-reduction-of-an-elliptic-curve). At $37$ the [valuation](../../../algebra.md#valuation) is one. An [Admissible change of Weierstrass coordinates](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#admissible-change-of-weierstrass-coordinates) changes discriminant [valuations](../../../algebra.md#valuation) by a multiple of twelve; consequently no integral model can lower this value to zero. Thus the equation is minimal there and the reduction is genuinely bad, rather than an artifact of a nonminimal model. Explicitly $(5,18)$ is singular on the reduced affine cubic: both partial derivatives vanish there. The set of [bad reduction](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#bad-reduction-of-an-elliptic-curve) primes is

$$
\boxed{\{37\}.}
$$

<h3 id="1/ii">ii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/ii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#1/ii)

Over $\mathbb F_2$, both $x=0,1$ give $x^3-x=0$, and both $y=0,1$ give $y^2+y=0$. There are four affine [rational points](../../../algebraic-geometry.md#rational-point), together with the point $O$ at infinity. Over $\mathbb F_3$, each $x=0,1,2$ gives $x^3-x=0$, while precisely $y=0,2$ solve $y^2+y=0$. Thus

$$
\boxed{\#\widetilde E(\mathbb F_2)=5,\qquad\#\widetilde E(\mathbb F_3)=7.}
$$

Both reductions are nonsingular by the discriminant calculation, so these are the point counts of the reduced [elliptic curves](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#elliptic-curve), with their [group](../../../group.md) laws.

<h3 id="1/iii">iii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/iii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#1/iii)

Use [reduction of torsion points on an elliptic curve](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#reduction-of-torsion-points-on-an-elliptic-curve), which is injective on torsion of order [prime](../../../number-theory.md#prime-number) to the residue characteristic. Any two-primary [torsion point of an elliptic curve](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#torsion-point-of-an-elliptic-curve) must inject into the [group](../../../group.md) of order seven at three, so is zero. Any three-primary [torsion point of an elliptic curve](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#torsion-point-of-an-elliptic-curve) injects into the [group](../../../group.md) of order five at two, so is zero. For a [prime](../../../number-theory.md#prime-number) $q\ne2,3$, its primary torsion injects into both reduced [groups](../../../group.md) and would have order dividing both five and seven, so is also zero. Decomposing the cyclic [group](../../../group.md) generated by any [torsion point of an elliptic curve](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#torsion-point-of-an-elliptic-curve) into its primary parts shows that $E(\mathbb Q)$ has no nonidentity torsion at all. This is the [two-prime bound on rational elliptic torsion](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#two-prime-bound-on-rational-elliptic-torsion), with the residue-characteristic exceptions handled explicitly. Since $P=(0,0)$ is not $O$,

$$
\boxed{P\text{ has infinite order}.}
$$

<h3 id="1/iv">iv</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/iv/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#1/iv)

The inverse on this [Weierstrass equation of an elliptic curve](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#weierstrass-equation-of-an-elliptic-curve) is $(x,y)\mapsto(x,-y-1)$. A line $y=\lambda x+\nu$ through the points being added has third intersection $R$ with $x(R)=\lambda^2-x_1-x_2$, and the sum is $-R$.

At $P=(0,0)$, implicit differentiation gives $\lambda=(3x^2-1)/(2y+1)=-1$. The tangent is $y=-x$. Substitution into the equation gives $x^2=x^3$, so its third intersection is $(1,-1)$, whose inverse is $(1,0)$. Next the line through $(0,0)$ and $(1,0)$ is $y=0$; its remaining intersection is $(-1,0)$, whose inverse is $(-1,-1)$. Consequently

$$
\boxed{2P=(1,0),\qquad3P=(-1,-1).}
$$

<h3 id="1/v">v</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/v/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [V](#1/v)

The [good-reduction map respects elliptic addition](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#good-reduction-map-respects-elliptic-addition). Since the [finite groups](../../../group.md#finite-group) at two and three have orders five and seven, respectively, $5P$ reduces to $O$ at two and $7P$ reduces to $O$ at three. These multiples are not $O$, since $P$ has infinite order. The [formal kernel of a minimal Weierstrass equation](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#formal-kernel-of-a-minimal-weierstrass-equation) gives, for every nonidentity point in the reduction kernel at $p$,

$$
v_p(x)=-2r,\qquad v_p(y)=-3r,\qquad r>0.
$$

Thus both coordinates of $5P$ have negative two-adic [valuation](../../../algebra.md#valuation), and both coordinates of $7P$ have negative three-adic [valuation](../../../algebra.md#valuation). In particular

$$
\boxed{x(5P),y(5P),x(7P),y(7P)\notin\mathbb Z.}
$$

There is also an elementary way to see that both coordinates must fail integrality here. An integral affine pair cannot reduce to the point at infinity. If one coordinate had negative [valuation](../../../algebra.md#valuation) and the other nonnegative [valuation](../../../algebra.md#valuation), the equation would give different [valuations](../../../algebra.md#valuation) on its two sides. When both are negative the lowest-valuation terms give $2v_p(y)=3v_p(x)$, so both are indeed negative.

For a direct numerical confirmation, continuing the [chord-and-tangent group law](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#chord-and-tangent-group-law) calculation gives

$$
4P=(2,-3),\qquad5P=(1/4,-5/8),\qquad
6P=(6,14),\qquad7P=(-5/9,8/27).
$$

The original printed part repeats its request about $7P$ at the end; the two distinct requested multiples are $5P$ and $7P$.

<h3 id="2/i">i</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/i/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#2/i)

An [isogeny of elliptic curves](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#isogeny-of-elliptic-curves) over $k$ is a nonconstant $k$-morphism $\varphi:E_1\to E_2$ which sends $O_1$ to $O_2$. A nonconstant morphism of smooth projective curves is finite and surjective. Its compatibility with the [elliptic curve group law from Riemann-Roch](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#elliptic-curve-group-law-from-riemann-roch) can be seen without assuming it in the definition.

Identify a point $P$ with the degree-zero [divisor class](../../../algebraic-geometry.md#divisor-class) $[P-O_1]$. A [finite morphism](../../../algebraic-geometry.md#finite-morphism) pushes forward divisors, and pushes a [principal divisor](../../../algebraic-geometry.md#principal-divisor-on-an-algebraic-curve) to the [principal divisor](../../../algebraic-geometry.md#principal-divisor-on-an-algebraic-curve) of the norm of its rational function. It therefore induces a [group homomorphism](../../../group-theory.md#group-homomorphism) on [degree-zero Picard groups of a curve](../../../ringed-space.md#degree-zero-picard-group-of-a-curve). On geometric points, the pushforward of $[P-O_1]$ is $[\varphi(P)-O_2]$. Since point addition is addition of these [divisor classes](../../../algebraic-geometry.md#divisor-class), this proves

$$
\boxed{\varphi(P+Q)=\varphi(P)+\varphi(Q).}
$$

The equality is defined over $k$, so in particular it holds on the [groups](../../../group.md) of $k$-rational points and after every [field](../../../algebra.md#field) extension. The argument also covers inseparable [isogenies of elliptic curves](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#isogeny-of-elliptic-curves); separability is not part of the definition.

<h3 id="2/ii">ii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/ii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#2/ii)

The squares in $\mathbb F_5$ are $0,1,4$. The following table counts affine solutions at each $x$, with one point at infinity to be added:

$$
\begin{array}{c|ccccc}
x&0&1&2&3&4\\\hline
x^3-x&0&0&1&4&0\\
\#\{y:y^2=x^3-x\}&1&1&2&2&1\\
x^3-x+1&1&1&2&0&1\\
\#\{y:y^2=x^3-x+1\}&2&2&0&1&2
\end{array}
$$

Hence $\#E_1(\mathbb F_5)=\#E_2(\mathbb F_5)=8$. In characteristic five, a finite point has order two precisely when $y=0$. The first cubic has three roots $0,1,4$, giving four elements killed by two, including $O$. The second cubic has only the root $3$, giving two elements killed by two. An isomorphism of [groups](../../../group.md) would preserve these counts, so the [groups](../../../group.md) are not isomorphic.

More precisely $(2,1)\in E_1$ doubles to $(0,0)$ and has order four; the independent two-torsion point $(1,0)$ gives $C_4\times C_2$. On $E_2$, the point $(0,1)$ doubles to $(4,1)$, which doubles to $(3,0)$; it therefore has order eight. Thus the [eight-point isogenous elliptic curves over F5](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#eight-point-isogenous-elliptic-curves-over-f5) have point [groups](../../../group.md)

$$
\boxed{E_1(\mathbb F_5)\cong C_4\times C_2,\qquad E_2(\mathbb F_5)\cong C_8.}
$$

<h3 id="2/iii">iii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/iii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#2/iii)

Put $u=x_1-1$, $v=y_1$. Then $v^2=u(u^2+3u+2)$. After using the curve equation, the displayed rational functions become

$$
X=x_1+\frac2{x_1-1}=u+1+\frac2u,\qquad
Y=v\left(1-\frac2{u^2}\right).
$$

Set $X_0=X+2=u+3+2/u$. Direct substitution into $v^2=u(u^2+3u+2)$ gives

$$
Y^2=X_0(X_0^2-6X_0+1)=X_0^3-X_0^2+X_0
$$

over $\mathbb F_5$. Replacing $X_0$ by $X+2$ makes this $Y^2=X^3-X+1$. This verifies the target equation explicitly, and identifies the functions with the [two-isogeny formula](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#two-isogeny-formula) after translating the source's two-torsion point to zero and then translating the target coordinate.

We must also check the omitted affine points. At $T=(1,0)$, $v$ is a [local parameter](../../../projective-space.md#local-parameter-on-a-smooth-algebraic-curve), and $u=v^2/(x_1(x_1+1))$ has order two, since $x_1(x_1+1)=2$ at $T$. Thus $X$ has a pole of order two and $Y$ one of order three. At $O_1$, the usual [pole orders on a smooth cubic in Weierstrass form](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#pole-orders-on-a-smooth-cubic-in-weierstrass-form) give poles two and three as well: the correction terms have higher order and do not cancel their leading terms. There are no other poles. A rational map from a smooth curve into a projective curve extends across these points; the indicated pole orders show that both $T$ and $O_1$ map to $O_2$. Hence this is a nonconstant identity-preserving morphism, and part (i) makes it an [isogeny of elliptic curves](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#isogeny-of-elliptic-curves).

The pullback of the pole divisor $2O_2$ of $X$ is $2O_1+2T$. Degrees of divisors multiply by the morphism degree, so $2\deg\varphi=4$. We obtain

$$
\boxed{\deg\varphi=2,\qquad\ker\varphi=\{O_1,(1,0)\}.}
$$

The degree is [prime](../../../number-theory.md#prime-number) to five, so the [elliptic isogeny](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#isogeny-of-elliptic-curves) is separable. Equivalently $dX/Y=dx_1/y_1$ is a nonzero pullback of an invariant differential.

<h3 id="2/iv">iv</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/iv/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#2/iv)

For a [Short Weierstrass form](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#short-weierstrass-form) $y^2=x^3+Ax+B$, use $c_4=-48A$ and $\Delta=-16(4A^3+27B^2)$. Both curves have $A=-1$, so $c_4=48=3$ in $\mathbb F_5$. For $E_1$, $B=0$ gives $\Delta_1=64=4$; for $E_2$, $B=1$ gives $\Delta_2=-368=2$. Thus

$$
\boxed{j(E_1)=3^3/4=3,\qquad j(E_2)=3^3/2=1\quad\text{in }\mathbb F_5.}
$$

The [j-invariant of an elliptic curve](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#j-invariant-of-an-elliptic-curve) is preserved by every isomorphism over the algebraic closure. Since these values differ, **the two curves are not geometrically isomorphic**, even though the preceding part gives a degree-two [elliptic isogeny](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#isogeny-of-elliptic-curves).

## 3

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 32](paper-32.md)

<h3 id="3/i">i</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/i/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#3/i)

Choose an integral [Minimal Weierstrass equation](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#minimal-weierstrass-equation) whose discriminant is a $p$-adic unit. Given $P=[X:Y:Z]\in E(\mathbb Q_p)$, scale the coordinates so that $X,Y,Z\in\mathbb Z_p$ and at least one is a unit. Define

$$
\varphi(P)=[\bar X:\bar Y:\bar Z]\in\widetilde E(\mathbb F_p).
$$

Another primitive representative differs by a unit scalar, so the projective reduction is well defined. The reduced cubic is smooth by [good reduction](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#good-reduction-of-an-elliptic-curve).

To prove the [group homomorphism](../../../group-theory.md#group-homomorphism) property, view this smooth projective cubic with its section $O$ as the integral elliptic model $\mathcal E/\mathbb Z_p$. Its addition law is defined over $\mathbb Z_p$, by adding degree-zero [divisor classes](../../../algebraic-geometry.md#divisor-class), and thus is a regular morphism $\mathcal E\times\mathcal E\to\mathcal E$. Properness identifies $E(\mathbb Q_p)$ with the integral sections just described. Reducing the addition morphism is the [group operation](../../../group.md#group-operation) of the smooth special fiber. Consequently

$$
\boxed{\varphi(P+Q)=\varphi(P)+\varphi(Q),\qquad\varphi(O)=\widetilde O.}
$$

This is the [good-reduction map respects elliptic addition](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#good-reduction-map-respects-elliptic-addition) argument; it covers tangent, vertical and infinity cases where reducing a single affine slope formula would involve division by a nonunit. Smoothness also makes reduction surjective: at each reduced point a partial derivative is a unit, and the [Hensel lemma](../../../arithmetic.md#hensel-s-lemma) lifts that point in a local affine chart.

<h3 id="3/ii">ii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/ii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#3/ii)

Near $O$, put $t=-x/y$ and $w=-1/y$, or projectively $t=-X/Y$, $w=-Z/Y$. The integral [Weierstrass equation of an elliptic curve](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#weierstrass-equation-of-an-elliptic-curve) becomes

$$
w=t^3+a_1tw+a_2t^2w+a_3w^2+a_4tw^2+a_6w^3.
$$

The linear coefficient of $w$ at the origin is a unit, so successive coefficient comparison gives a unique series $w(t)\in\mathbb Z_p[[t]]$ beginning $t^3$. Expressing the addition morphism in the local parameters of its two inputs gives

$$
F(t_1,t_2)=t_1+t_2+\text{terms of total degree at least two}\in\mathbb Z_p[[t_1,t_2]].
$$

Its identity, inverse, commutativity and associativity identities follow from the corresponding elliptic [group](../../../group.md) identities. This one-dimensional commutative [formal group law](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#formal-group-law) is the [formal group of an elliptic curve](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#formal-group-of-an-elliptic-curve) $\widehat E$ attached to the model.

A point reducing to $O$ lies in the chart with $Y$ a unit and has $t,w\in p\mathbb Z_p$. The implicit equation determines $w$ uniquely from $t$. Conversely every $t\in p\mathbb Z_p$ gives the convergent solution $w(t)$ and the point $[-t:1:-w(t)]$; $t=0$ gives $O$. Hence the parameter is a bijection from the reduction kernel to $p\mathbb Z_p$. The series $F$ converges there and describes its addition. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\ker\varphi=E_1(\mathbb Q_p)\cong\widehat E(p\mathbb Z_p).}
$$

For nonzero $t$ with $v_p(t)=r>0$, $w(t)=t^3$ times a unit, so $v_p(x)=-2r$ and $v_p(y)=-3r$. These are the [formal kernel of a minimal Weierstrass equation](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#formal-kernel-of-a-minimal-weierstrass-equation) [valuations](../../../algebra.md#valuation) used earlier.

<h3 id="3/iii">iii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/iii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#3/iii)

Write $E[q^\infty]$ for the points killed by some power of $q$, rather than any notion of all points which are $q$-divisible. On the reduction kernel, formal multiplication is

$$
[q^m](t)=q^mt+O(t^2).
$$

Since $q\ne p$, its linear coefficient is a unit. Recursive inversion gives an integral inverse series, convergent on $p\mathbb Z_p$; equivalently the [Hensel lemma](../../../arithmetic.md#hensel-s-lemma) gives a unique solution to $[q^m](t)=s$ for every $s\in p\mathbb Z_p$. Thus multiplication by $q^m$ is bijective on the kernel, proving that its $q$-primary torsion is zero. The reduction [group homomorphism](../../../group-theory.md#group-homomorphism) is therefore injective on $E(\mathbb Q_p)[q^\infty]$.

For surjectivity on this torsion, take a reduced point $\widetilde P$ killed by $q^m$. Lift it to $P\in E(\mathbb Q_p)$ using smooth reduction. Then $q^mP$ belongs to the kernel, so choose its unique kernel division point $R$ with $q^mR=q^mP$. The point $P-R$ is killed by $q^m$ and reduces to $\widetilde P$. This proves the [prime-to-p torsion lifts uniquely at good reduction](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#prime-to-p-torsion-lifts-uniquely-at-good-reduction) statement:

$$
\boxed{E(\mathbb Q_p)[q^\infty]\xrightarrow{\sim}\widetilde E(\mathbb F_p)[q^\infty].}
$$

The right side is a subgroup of a [finite group](../../../group.md#finite-group). Thus the left side is finite and has exactly the requested order. No claim that the whole formal kernel is torsion-free at $p=2$ is needed.

## 4

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 32](paper-32.md)

<h3 id="4/i">i</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#4)

<h4 id="4/i/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#4/i)

Translate the [rational point](../../../algebraic-geometry.md#rational-point) of order two to $(0,0)$ and complete the square. After clearing denominators by admissible scaling, write

$$
E:y^2=x(x^2+ax+b),\qquad a,b\in\mathbb Z,\qquad b(a^2-4b)\ne0.
$$

The companion curve is $E':Y^2=X(X^2-2aX+a^2-4b)$. The [two-isogeny formula](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#two-isogeny-formula) gives maps $\phi:E\to E'$ and $\psi:E'\to E$ satisfying $\psi\phi=[2]$.

For the [Weak Mordell-Weil theorem](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#weak-mordell-weil-theorem) in this setting, use the [two-torsion square-class homomorphism](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#two-torsion-square-class-homomorphism)

$$
\alpha(O)=1,\quad\alpha((0,0))=[b],\quad\alpha((x,y))=[x]\in\mathbb Q^*/\mathbb Q^{*2},
$$

and its counterpart $\alpha'$ on $E'$. These are homomorphisms: a nonvertical line intersecting the cubic in three points has the product of their $x$-coordinates a square, by comparison of the constant term of the intersection cubic. For a line through $(0,0)$, the product of the other two coordinates is $b$, explaining the special value there. The kernels are $\psi E'(\mathbb Q)$ and $\phi E(\mathbb Q)$, respectively, as follows by solving the [elliptic isogeny](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#isogeny-of-elliptic-curves) preimage quadratic and testing its discriminant for a square.

The [prime-support bound in two-isogeny descent](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#prime-support-bound-in-two-isogeny-descent) makes both images finite. Indeed at a [prime](../../../number-theory.md#prime-number) not dividing $b$, a negative [valuation](../../../algebra.md#valuation) of $x$ makes the leading cubic term dominate, giving $2v(y)=3v(x)$; a positive [valuation](../../../algebra.md#valuation) makes $x^2+ax+b$ a unit, giving $2v(y)=v(x)$. In either case $v(x)$ is even. Thus the [square class](../../../galois-theory.md#square-class) of $x$ is supported only on primes dividing $b$, together with a possible sign. The same argument uses $b'=a^2-4b$ on $E'$. Since $2E=\psi\phi E$, the two finite [elliptic isogeny](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#isogeny-of-elliptic-curves) quotients imply that $E(\mathbb Q)/2E(\mathbb Q)$ is finite.

Finite generation still needs a descent on height. Let $h_x$ be the [logarithmic height](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#absolute-logarithmic-weil-height) of the $x$-coordinate. The duplication formula has a degree-four rational $x$-coordinate map with coprime numerator and denominator. Polynomial height estimates, with the resultant supplying the lower bound, give the [height growth under a morphism of the projective line](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#height-growth-under-a-morphism-of-the-projective-line) relation $h_x(2P)=4h_x(P)+O(1)$. Thus the convergent telescoping limit

$$
\widehat h(P)=\tfrac12\lim_{n\to\infty}4^{-n}h_x(2^nP)
$$

defines the [canonical height of an elliptic curve](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#canonical-height-of-an-elliptic-curve), without assuming finite generation. It is nonnegative, satisfies $\widehat h(2P)=4\widehat h(P)$ and the [height parallelogram identity](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#height-parallelogram-identity), and differs from $h_x/2$ by a bounded amount. Consequently its bounded subsets of [rational points](../../../algebraic-geometry.md#rational-point) are finite by the [Northcott theorem](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#northcott-theorem).

Choose the finite list $R_i$ of representatives modulo $2E(\mathbb Q)$ and let $H=\max_i\widehat h(R_i)$. For $P=2Q+R_i$, the [canonical height pairing](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#canonical-height-pairing) inequality gives

$$
\widehat h(Q)=\tfrac14\widehat h(P-R_i)\le\tfrac12\bigl(\widehat h(P)+H\bigr).
$$

Repeated halving brings the height below $H+1$. There are only finitely many such terminal points, and recovering $P$ expresses it as an integer combination of those points and the $R_i$. This proves the [canonical-height proof of Mordell-Weil finite generation](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#canonical-height-proof-of-mordell-weil-finite-generation), so

$$
\boxed{E(\mathbb Q)\cong\mathbb Z^{g_E}\oplus T,\qquad T\text{ finite}.}
$$

<h3 id="4/ii">ii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#4)

<h4 id="4/ii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#4/ii)

Retain $E:y^2=x(x^2+ax+b)$ and put $b'=a^2-4b$. The explicit [two-isogeny formula](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#two-isogeny-formula) is

$$
\phi(x,y)=\left(x+a+\frac bx,\ y\left(1-\frac b{x^2}\right)\right),\qquad
\psi(X,Y)=\left(\frac{X-2a+b'/X}{4},\ \frac{Y(1-b'/X^2)}8\right),
$$

with the maps extended at the kernel points. Direct substitution verifies the equations and $\psi\phi=[2]$.

Here is a concrete kernel test for the descent homomorphisms. A preimage of $(X,Y)\in E'$ has $x$ satisfying $x^2+(a-X)x+b=0$, whose discriminant is $(X-a)^2-4b$. The target equation says $Y^2=X((X-a)^2-4b)$, so for nonexceptional points this quadratic has a rational solution precisely when $X$ is a square. The resulting $y$ follows from the displayed [elliptic isogeny](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#isogeny-of-elliptic-curves) formula. For a nonzero target two-torsion point, the preimage quadratic has a double root $x=(X-a)/2$, and the source equation gives $y^2=x^2X$, so the same square condition still applies. At $(0,0)\in E'$, a rational preimage exists precisely when $b'$ is a square, matching the special square-class value $\alpha'((0,0))=[b']$. The dual argument gives the other kernel. Thus

$$
A:=\alpha(E(\mathbb Q))\cong E(\mathbb Q)/\psi E'(\mathbb Q),\qquad
B:=\alpha'(E'(\mathbb Q))\cong E'(\mathbb Q)/\phi E(\mathbb Q).
$$

Let $\delta=[\ker\psi:\ker\psi\cap\phi E(\mathbb Q)]$. The quotient map induced by $\psi$ has this kernel, giving

$$
[E(\mathbb Q):2E(\mathbb Q)]=\frac{\#A\,\#B}{\delta}.
$$

If $b'$ is a rational square, $E$ has four rational two-torsion points and $\delta=1$; otherwise it has two and $\delta=2$. In either case $\delta\,\#E(\mathbb Q)[2]=4$. Combining with finite generation therefore proves the [two-isogeny rank formula](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#square-class-index-formula-for-two-isogeny-descent)

$$
\boxed{2^{g_E}=\frac{\#A\,\#B}{4}.}
$$

This derivation checks the factor four, including curves with full rational two-torsion.

To compute these images, enumerate the signed square-free divisors $d$ of $b$ and $b'$. Substituting $x=d(U/V)^2$ yields the [quartic covering in a two-isogeny descent](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#quartic-covering-in-a-two-isogeny-descent)

$$
W^2=dU^4+aU^2V^2+(b/d)V^4,\qquad(U,V)\ne(0,0).
$$

The class $d$ occurs precisely when this covering has a [rational point](../../../algebraic-geometry.md#rational-point), with the identity and two-torsion handled by the boundary cases. Test at the real place and at primes dividing $2bb'$ to rule out candidates, and search for [rational points](../../../algebraic-geometry.md#rational-point) to prove that surviving classes occur. The locally soluble classes form a finite [elliptic isogeny](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#isogeny-of-elliptic-curves) [Selmer group of an elliptic curve](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#n-selmer-group), giving an upper rank bound. [rational points](../../../algebraic-geometry.md#rational-point) and independent height pairings give a lower bound. When the bounds agree the rank is proved. **Local solubility alone does not prove a [rational point](../../../algebraic-geometry.md#rational-point) exists**: if a gap remains, a higher descent or further information about the covering obstructions is needed. This is why the procedure usually computes the rank but is not an unjustified universal termination claim.

<h3 id="4/iii">iii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#4)

<h4 id="4/iii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#4/iii)

For a rank-zero example, take $E_0:y^2=x^3+x$, with rational two-torsion $(0,0)$. Its [two-isogeny formula](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#two-isogeny-formula) companion is $E'_0:y^2=x^3-4x$. The [prime-support bound in two-isogeny descent](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#prime-support-bound-in-two-isogeny-descent) allows only $\pm1$ on $E_0$; all nonzero affine $x$ are positive because $x(x^2+1)$ is a square. Hence $A_0=\{1\}$. For $E'_0$ the possible classes are $\{1,-1,2,-2\}$, all realized: $O$ gives $1$, $(0,0)$ gives $[-4]=-1$, and $(2,0),(-2,0)$ give $2,-2$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{2^{g_{E_0}}=\frac{1\cdot4}{4}=1,\qquad g_{E_0}=0.}
$$

This is the [rank-zero elliptic curve with cubic x cubed plus x](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#rank-zero-elliptic-curve-with-cubic-x-cubed-plus-x) certificate; no unresolved locally soluble covering is being counted as soluble.

For a positive-rank example, take $E_1:y^2=x^3-2x$. Its companion is $E'_1:y^2=x^3+8x$. The first image is contained in $\{1,-1,2,-2\}$. The identity gives $1$, $(0,0)$ gives $-2$, and $(2,2)$ gives $2$; multiplicativity of the square-class [group homomorphism](../../../group-theory.md#group-homomorphism) also gives $-1$. Thus $A_1$ has all four classes. On $E'_1$, the same prime-support bound allows $\pm1,\pm2$, but $x(x^2+8)$ is nonnegative only for $x\ge0$, eliminating the negative classes. The identity gives $1$ and $(0,0)$ gives $[8]=2$, so $B_1=\{1,2\}$. Consequently

$$
\boxed{2^{g_{E_1}}=\frac{4\cdot2}{4}=2,\qquad g_{E_1}=1.}
$$

The [rank-one elliptic curve with cubic x cubed minus twice x](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#rank-one-elliptic-curve-with-cubic-x-cubed-minus-twice-x) therefore supplies the required positive-rank example. The exhibited point $(2,2)$ is itself nontorsion: doubling gives $(9/4,-21/8)$, whose nonintegral coordinates contradict the [Nagell–Lutz theorem](../../../normalization-of-an-algebraic-curve.md#nagell-lutz-theorem) if it were torsion. No assertion that this point generates the full free part is needed for the rank calculation.
