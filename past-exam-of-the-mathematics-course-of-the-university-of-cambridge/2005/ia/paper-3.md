## 1D

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="1d/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1D](#1d)

By the [spectral theorem for real symmetric matrices](../../../linear-algebra.md#spectral-theorem-for-real-symmetric-matrices), choose an [orthonormal eigenbasis](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#orthonormal-eigenbasis) $u_1,u_2,u_3$ with $Au_i=\lambda_i u_i$. If $x=\sum_i y_i u_i$, then

$$
1=x^TAx=\sum_i\lambda_i y_i^2
\leq\lambda_1\sum_i y_i^2=\lambda_1|x|^2.
$$

Thus every point of the [ellipsoid](../../../geometry-and-topology.md#ellipsoid) has distance at least $\lambda_1^{-1/2}$ from the origin. Equality holds precisely when

$$
(\lambda_1-\lambda_2)y_2^2+(\lambda_1-\lambda_3)y_3^2=0.
$$

Both coefficients are strictly positive, so $y_2=y_3=0$. The surface equation then gives $y_1=\pm\lambda_1^{-1/2}$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{d_{\min}=\frac1{\sqrt{\lambda_1}},\qquad
x=\pm\frac{u_1}{\sqrt{\lambda_1}}.}
$$

**Exactly two points attain the minimum.** The strict ordering makes the largest-eigenvalue [eigenspace](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenspace) one-dimensional; this is the equality case in [nearest points to the origin on a positive-definite ellipsoid](../../../geometry-and-topology.md#nearest-points-to-the-origin-on-a-positive-definite-ellipsoid).

## 2D

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="2d/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2D](#2d)

A [cyclic group](../../../group.md#cyclic-group) is a [group](../../../group.md) generated by a single element: there is $g\in G$ such that every element is $g^n$ for some integer $n$. For a [finite group](../../../group.md#finite-group) of prime order $p$, choose $g\ne e$. Its [cyclic subgroup](../../../group.md#cyclic-subgroup) $\langle g\rangle$ has order dividing $p$ by [Lagrange's theorem](../../../group-theory.md#lagrange-s-theorem), and its order is greater than one. Hence $|\langle g\rangle|=p$, and $\langle g\rangle=G$. Thus **every [group](../../../group.md) of prime order is cyclic**.

A [group homomorphism](../../../group-theory.md#group-homomorphism) from $C_{11}=\langle a\rangle$ is determined by $h=\varphi(a)$, since $\varphi(a^j)=h^j$. Its image generator must satisfy $h^{11}=e$, so its [order of a group element](../../../group-theory.md#order-of-a-group-element) divides $11$. As an element of $C_{14}$ its order also divides $14$. These integers are coprime, so the only possible order is one. Thus $h=e$, and

$$
\boxed{\varphi(a^j)=e\quad\text{for every }j.}
$$

This map is indeed a [group homomorphism](../../../group-theory.md#group-homomorphism), so **there is exactly one**, the trivial homomorphism.

## 3A

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="3a/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3A](#3a)

Let $e=\tfrac12(|A|^2+|B|^2)$. The time equations and the [dot product](../../../linear-algebra.md#dot-product) [differentiation](../../../calculus.md#differentiation) rule give the local identity

$$
\partial_t e=A\cdot(\nabla\times B)-B\cdot(\nabla\times A).
$$

The [divergence of a cross product](../../../calculus.md#divergence-of-a-cross-product) is

$$
\nabla\cdot(A\times B)=B\cdot(\nabla\times A)-A\cdot(\nabla\times B).
$$

Combining them yields $\partial_t e+\nabla\cdot(A\times B)=0$. On a fixed bounded region with a [boundary](../../../topology.md#boundary-of-a-set) regular enough for the [divergence theorem](../../../calculus.md#divergence-theorem), differentiating under the [integral](../../../calculus.md#integral) and applying that theorem gives

$$
\boxed{\frac d{dt}\left[\frac12\int_V(|A|^2+|B|^2)\,dV\right]
=-\int_{\partial V}(A\times B)\cdot n\,dS.}
$$

Here $n$ is the outward [unit normal](../../../differential-geometry.md#unit-normal), so the sign expresses loss of the interior quadratic quantity by outward flux. No vanishing-boundary assumption is required.

## 4A

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="4a/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4A](#4a)

For the unit-speed [curve](../../../topology.md#curve), the [unit tangent vector](../../../differential-geometry.md#unit-tangent-vector), [curvature of a space curve](../../../differential-geometry.md#curvature-of-a-space-curve), [principal normal vector](../../../differential-geometry.md#principal-normal-vector) and [binormal vector](../../../differential-geometry.md#binormal-vector) are

$$
\boxed{t=\gamma',\qquad \kappa=|\gamma''|,\qquad
p=\frac{\gamma''}{|\gamma''|},\qquad b=t\times p.}
$$

Differentiating $t\cdot t=1$ gives $t\cdot\gamma''=0$, so $t,p,b$ form a positively oriented [orthonormal basis](../../../linear-algebra.md#orthonormal-basis). The hypothesis $\gamma''\ne0$ makes the [principal normal vector](../../../differential-geometry.md#principal-normal-vector) well-defined.

To determine the sign of the [torsion of a space curve](../../../differential-geometry.md#torsion-of-a-curve) without relying on a pictorial handedness convention, take $R>0$, $h\ne0$, $L=\sqrt{R^2+h^2}$ and use the unit-speed [circular helix](../../../differential-geometry.md#circular-helix)

$$
\gamma_h(s)=\bigl(R\cos(s/L),\,R\sin(s/L),\,hs/L\bigr).
$$

Writing $u=s/L$, direct [differentiation](../../../calculus.md#differentiation) gives its [Frenet frame](../../../differential-geometry.md#frenet-frame):

$$
\begin{aligned}
t&=\frac1L(-R\sin u,R\cos u,h),&
p&=(-\cos u,-\sin u,0),\\
b&=\frac1L(h\sin u,-h\cos u,R),&
b'&=\frac h{L^2}(\cos u,\sin u,0).
\end{aligned}
$$

Consequently

$$
\boxed{\kappa=\frac R{R^2+h^2},\qquad
\tau=-b'\cdot p=\frac h{R^2+h^2}.}
$$

At $s=0$ the marked point is $(R,0,0)$, with $t=(0,R/L,h/L)$, $p=(-1,0,0)$, $b=(0,-h/L,R/L)$ and $b'=(h/L^2,0,0)$. For $h>0$, $b'$ points opposite to $p$ and the [torsion of a space curve](../../../differential-geometry.md#torsion-of-a-curve) is positive. Replacing $h$ by $-h$ reflects the [circular helix](../../../differential-geometry.md#circular-helix) across the horizontal plane and makes the [torsion of a space curve](../../../differential-geometry.md#torsion-of-a-curve) negative; then $b'$ points in the same direction as $p$. The direction of increasing $s$ is fixed in both panels.

## 5D

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="5d/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [5D](#5d)

A [group action](../../../group-theory.md#group-action) is a map $(g,x)\mapsto g\cdot x$ from $G\times X$ to $X$ satisfying $e\cdot x=x$ and $(gh)\cdot x=g\cdot(h\cdot x)$. Define the [orbit of a group action](../../../group-theory.md#orbit-of-a-group-action) $Gx=\{g\cdot x:g\in G\}$ and the [stabilizer subgroup](../../../group-theory.md#stabilizer-subgroup) $G_x=\{g:g\cdot x=x\}$. The latter is a [subgroup](../../../group.md#subgroup): the identity fixes $x$, products of elements fixing $x$ fix $x$, and inverses do too.

For finite $G$, the [orbit-stabilizer theorem](../../../group-theory.md#orbit-stabilizer-theorem) states

$$
\boxed{|Gx|=\frac{|G|}{|G_x|}.}
$$

To prove it, send a left [coset](../../../group-theory.md#coset) $gG_x$ to $g\cdot x$. If $gG_x=hG_x$, then $h^{-1}g\in G_x$ and $g\cdot x=h\cdot x$. Conversely, equality of the two images implies $h^{-1}g\in G_x$, hence equality of the [cosets](../../../group-theory.md#coset). Every point of the [orbit of a group action](../../../group-theory.md#orbit-of-a-group-action) has such a preimage. This is therefore a [bijection](../../../function.md#bijection), and each [coset](../../../group-theory.md#coset) has $|G_x|$ elements, proving the formula.

For the action on [subgroups](../../../group.md#subgroup), conjugation maps a [subgroup](../../../group.md#subgroup) $K$ to a [subgroup](../../../group.md#subgroup) $gKg^{-1}$ because it preserves products and inverses. Furthermore $eKe^{-1}=K$ and

$$
(gh)K(gh)^{-1}=g(hKh^{-1})g^{-1}.
$$

Thus this is the [conjugation action](../../../group-theory.md#conjugation-action). The [stabilizer subgroup](../../../group-theory.md#stabilizer-subgroup) of $H$ is its [normalizer](../../../group-theory.md#normalizer) $N_G(H)$, and $H\subseteq N_G(H)$ since each $h\in H$ conjugates $H$ onto itself. Hence the number $k$ of distinct [conjugate subgroups](../../../group-theory.md#conjugate-subgroup) is

$$
k=\frac{|G|}{|N_G(H)|}\leq\frac{|G|}{|H|}.
$$

Now suppose $H$ is proper. Put $m=|G|/|H|\geq2$ and list the $k$ distinct [conjugate subgroups](../../../group-theory.md#conjugate-subgroup) as $H_1,\ldots,H_k$. Each has $|H|$ elements and all contain the same identity. Counting the identity just once, even if other elements overlap, gives

$$
\left|\bigcup_{i=1}^kH_i\right|
\leq1+k(|H|-1)
\leq1+m(|H|-1)
=|G|-m+1<|G|.
$$

Thus **some element of $G$ lies in no conjugate of $H$**. This proves [conjugates of a proper subgroup do not cover a finite group](../../../group-theory.md#conjugates-of-a-proper-subgroup-do-not-cover-a-finite-group) by an explicit count; it does not require the conjugates to intersect only at the identity.

## 6D

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="6d/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [6D](#6d)

Represent the [Riemann sphere](../../../complex-analysis.md#riemann-sphere) by the complex [projective line](../../../finite-group-theory.md#projective-line), with finite $z$ represented by $[z:1]$ and infinity by $[1:0]$. An invertible [matrix](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) $A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ acts by $[u:v]\mapsto[au+bv:cu+dv]$, giving the stated fractional map on finite points. Applying $B$ and then $A$ is exactly [matrix multiplication](../../../vector-space.md#matrix-multiplication) $AB$, including at poles and infinity. Therefore

$$
\theta(AB)=\theta(A)\circ\theta(B),
$$

so $\theta$ is a [group homomorphism](../../../group-theory.md#group-homomorphism) from the [special linear group](../../../group-theory.md#special-linear-group) to the [Möbius group](../../../group-theory.md#mobius-group).

Every [Möbius transformation](../../../group-theory.md#mobius-transformation) has an invertible complex [matrix](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) representative $A$. Choose $\rho\in\mathbb C$ with $\rho^2\det A=1$. Such a nonzero [square root](../../../algebra.md#square-root) exists in $\mathbb C$, and $\rho A$ has [determinant](../../../linear-algebra.md#determinant) one while representing the same projective map. This proves surjectivity. If the map is the identity, fixing zero and infinity forces $b=c=0$, and fixing one forces $a=d$. The determinant-one condition gives $a^2=1$. Conversely both [scalar matrices](../../../linear-algebra.md#scalar-matrix) $I$ and $-I$ act trivially. Hence

$$
\boxed{\ker\theta=\{I,-I\},\qquad
\mathcal M\cong SL_2(\mathbb C)/\{I,-I\}.}
$$

For the normal forms, use the fact that a complex $2\times2$ [matrix](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) has a [Jordan normal form](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#jordan-normal-form): either it is diagonalizable or it has one nontrivial [Jordan block](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#jordan-block). For a determinant-one representative, distinct [eigenvalues](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) are $\lambda,\lambda^{-1}$. Diagonalizing gives $\operatorname{diag}(\lambda,\lambda^{-1})$, whose projective action is $z\mapsto\lambda^2z$. Distinctness means $\lambda^2\ne1$, so $\mu=\lambda^2$ is neither zero nor one.

If the [eigenvalue](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) is repeated, the [determinant](../../../linear-algebra.md#determinant) condition gives $\lambda=\pm1$. A diagonalizable representative would be $\lambda I$ and would give the identity, excluded here. A nontrivial [Jordan block](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#jordan-block) is similar to $\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\lambda&1\\0&\lambda\end{smallmatrix}\right)$, giving $z\mapsto z+1/\lambda=z\pm1$. Any invertible conjugating [matrix](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) gives a [Möbius transformation](../../../group-theory.md#mobius-transformation); rescaling it to [determinant](../../../linear-algebra.md#determinant) one does not change that map. Thus these [matrix similarities](../../../linear-algebra.md#matrix-similarity) are genuine conjugacies in the [Möbius group](../../../group-theory.md#mobius-group), proving the requested [Möbius conjugacy normal forms](../../../group-theory.md#mobius-conjugacy-normal-forms).

Conjugacy preserves [fixed points](../../../function.md#fixed-point) bijectively. A nonidentity scaling has exactly the two [fixed points](../../../function.md#fixed-point) zero and infinity, while either translation has exactly one, infinity. The identity fixes every point. This proves all three possibilities and excludes any further finite number of [fixed points](../../../function.md#fixed-point).

Finally, suppose $P\circ T\circ P^{-1}=S$, with $S(z)=z+\varepsilon$ and $\varepsilon=\pm1$. Then $S^n(z)=z+n\varepsilon\to\infty$ for every finite $z$ in the topology of the [Riemann sphere](../../../complex-analysis.md#riemann-sphere); infinity remains infinity. Since $P^{-1}$ is continuous on that [sphere](../../../geometry-and-topology.md#sphere),

$$
\boxed{T^n(z)=P^{-1}\bigl(S^n(P(z))\bigr)\longrightarrow
P^{-1}(\infty)=z_0\quad\text{for every }z.}
$$

This is [iteration of a one-fixed-point Möbius transformation](../../../group-theory.md#iteration-of-a-one-fixed-point-mobius-transformation). The limiting statement includes the unique [fixed point](../../../function.md#fixed-point) itself and uses spherical convergence, which also handles intermediate iterates equal to infinity.

## 7D

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="7d/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [7D](#7d)

The identity belongs to the [center of a group](../../../group-theory.md#center-of-a-group). If $a,b$ are central, then for every $g\in G$,

$$
g(ab^{-1})=a\,gb^{-1}=ab^{-1}g,
$$

so the center is a [subgroup](../../../group.md#subgroup). For a central $a$, $gag^{-1}=a$ for every $g$, giving $gZ(G)g^{-1}=Z(G)$. Thus **the center is a [normal subgroup](../../../group-theory.md#normal-subgroup)**.

Write a [matrix](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) of the [Heisenberg group](../../../lie-algebra.md#heisenberg-group) as $h(x,y,z)$. Direct [matrix multiplication](../../../vector-space.md#matrix-multiplication) and inversion give

$$
h(x,y,z)h(a,b,c)=h(x+a,y+b+xc,z+c),
\qquad
h(x,y,z)^{-1}=h(-x,xz-y,-z).
$$

The identity is $h(0,0,0)$ and each [matrix](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) has [determinant](../../../linear-algebra.md#determinant) one, so these formulas show closure under products and inverses inside $GL_3(\mathbb R)$. Hence $H$ is a [subgroup](../../../group.md#subgroup).

The two products $h(x,y,z)h(a,b,c)$ and $h(a,b,c)h(x,y,z)$ differ only in their upper-right entries, by $xc-az$. Commutation with every $h(a,b,c)$ therefore requires $xc=az$ for all real $a,c$. Setting $a=0,c=1$ forces $x=0$; setting $a=1,c=0$ forces $z=0$. These conditions are also sufficient. Thus

$$
\boxed{Z(H)=\{h(0,y,0):y\in\mathbb R\}.}
$$

Define the surjective [group homomorphism](../../../group-theory.md#group-homomorphism) $F:H\to(\mathbb R^2,+)$ by $F(h(x,y,z))=(x,z)$. The multiplication formula gives $F(hh')=F(h)+F(h')$, and its [kernel of a group homomorphism](../../../group-theory.md#kernel-of-a-group-homomorphism) is precisely $Z(H)$. The [first isomorphism theorem](../../../group-theory.md#first-isomorphism-theorem) yields

$$
\boxed{H/Z(H)\cong(\mathbb R^2,+),\qquad
h(x,y,z)Z(H)\longmapsto(x,z).}
$$

This [center quotient of the real Heisenberg group](../../../lie-algebra.md#center-quotient-of-the-real-heisenberg-group) can also be checked directly: two elements have the same $(x,z)$ coordinates exactly when they differ by multiplication by a central element, and quotient multiplication adds these coordinates.

## 8D

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="8d/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [8D](#8d)

First $E=\ker(A+I)$ is a [linear subspace](../../../vector-space.md#vector-subspace). To prove it is nonzero, use [orthogonality](../../../linear-algebra.md#orthogonal-vectors) and $\det A=-1$:

$$
\det(A+I)=\det A\,\det(I+A^{-1})
=-\det(I+A^T)=-\det(A+I).
$$

Hence $\det(A+I)=0$. If $u\in E$ then $Au=-u$ also implies $A^Tu=-u$. For $x\perp E$,

$$
\langle Ax,u\rangle=\langle x,A^Tu\rangle=-\langle x,u\rangle=0,
$$

so $E^\perp$ is invariant. If $E$ had dimension two, its one-dimensional [orthogonal complement](../../../hilbert-space.md#orthogonal-complement) would be invariant and $A$ would act there as a scalar $\eta=\pm1$. The [determinant](../../../linear-algebra.md#determinant) would be $(-1)^2\eta=-1$, so $\eta=-1$, forcing $A=-I$. Dimension three also forces $A=-I$. Both contradict the hypothesis. Consequently

$$
\boxed{\dim E=1.}
$$

Choose an [orthonormal basis](../../../linear-algebra.md#orthonormal-basis) beginning with a [unit vector](../../../vector-space.md#unit-vector) in $E$ and followed by a basis of $\Pi=E^\perp$. In this basis the [orthogonal matrix](../../../linear-algebra.md#orthogonal-matrix) has block form

$$
A\sim\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\0&R\end{pmatrix},
\qquad R^TR=I_2,\qquad \det R=1.
$$

The first column of $R$ is some $(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)^T$. Its perpendicular unit second column with positive orientation must be $(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta)^T$. Thus the restriction is the plane [rotation](../../../riemannian-geometry.md#rotation-mathematics)

$$
R=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\
\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}.
$$

[Trace](../../../linear-algebra.md#matrix-trace) invariance gives $\operatorname{tr}A=-1+2\cos\theta$, proving

$$
\boxed{\cos\theta=\tfrac12(\operatorname{tr}A+1).}
$$

A [Euclidean reflection](../../../linear-algebra.md#reflection-mathematics) in $\Pi$ fixes that plane pointwise and reverses $E$, so it occurs exactly when $R=I_2$. This is equivalent to $\cos\theta=1$, hence **exactly to $\operatorname{tr}A=1$**. Finally,

$$
\det(A-I)=(-2)\det(R-I_2)
=-2\bigl((\cos\theta-1)^2+\sin^2\theta\bigr)
=\boxed{4(\cos\theta-1)}.
$$

This exhibits the [three-dimensional improper orthogonal transformation](../../../linear-algebra.md#three-dimensional-improper-orthogonal-transformation) as a [rotation](../../../riemannian-geometry.md#rotation-mathematics) about the axis $E$ composed with [Euclidean reflection](../../../linear-algebra.md#reflection-mathematics) in $\Pi$.

## 9A

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="9a/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [9A](#9a)

Assume sufficient smoothness and a regular [boundary](../../../topology.md#boundary-of-a-set) for the [integrations](../../../calculus.md#integral) below, with $n$ outward. Put $\delta=w-\phi$, so $\delta=0$ on $S$. Expanding the [Dirichlet energy](../../../differential-geometry.md#dirichlet-energy) gives

$$
\int_V|\nabla w|^2
=\int_V|\nabla\phi|^2
+2\int_V\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla\delta+\int_V|\nabla\delta|^2.
$$

By [Green's first identity](../../../partial-differential-equation.md#green-s-first-identity), the middle [integral](../../../calculus.md#integral) is

$$
\int_V\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla\delta
=\int_S\delta\,\partial_n\phi-\int_V\delta\,\Delta\phi=0.
$$

Thus the [Dirichlet principle](../../../calculus-of-variations.md#dirichlet-principle) follows with an explicit remainder:

$$
\boxed{\int_V|\nabla w|^2-\int_V|\nabla\phi|^2
=\int_V|\nabla(w-\phi)|^2\geq0.}
$$

Equality means $w-\phi$ is constant on each [connected component](../../../geometry-and-topology.md#connected-component); its zero [boundary](../../../topology.md#boundary-of-a-set) value forces that constant to be zero.

Now let $w$ solve the [heat equation](../../../diffusion-equation.md#heat-equation) and set $E(t)=\int_V|\nabla w|^2\,dV$. [Differentiation](../../../calculus.md#differentiation) followed by [Green's first identity](../../../partial-differential-equation.md#green-s-first-identity) gives

$$
E'(t)=2\int_V\nabla w\cdot\nabla w_t
=2\int_Sw_t\,\partial_nw-2\int_Vw_t\Delta w.
$$

Under time-independent [Dirichlet boundary conditions](../../../differential-equation.md#dirichlet-boundary-condition), $w_t=0$ on $S$, while $w_t=\Delta w$ in $V$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{E'(t)=-2\int_V(\Delta w)^2\,dV\leq0.}
$$

If equality holds at a particular time, [continuity](../../../calculus.md#continuous-function) makes $\Delta w=0$ throughout $V$ at that time. Its [boundary](../../../topology.md#boundary-of-a-set) values are still $f$, so uniqueness of the [Dirichlet problem](../../../analysis.md#dirichlet-problem) implies $w=\phi$. Conversely $w=\phi$ has $\Delta w=0$ and gives equality. This proves the full equality criterion for [Dirichlet energy dissipation for the heat equation](../../../diffusion-equation.md#dirichlet-energy-dissipation-for-the-heat-equation).

For the [dynamic boundary condition for the heat equation](../../../differential-equation.md#dynamic-boundary-condition-for-the-heat-equation), substitute $w_t=-\alpha\partial_nw$ directly into the [boundary term](../../../calculus.md#boundary-term). The interior equation is unchanged, so

$$
\boxed{E'(t)=-2\int_Vw_t^2\,dV
-2\int_S\alpha(\partial_nw)^2\,dS\leq0.}
$$

This is [Dirichlet energy dissipation with a dynamic heat boundary condition](../../../differential-equation.md#dirichlet-energy-dissipation-with-a-dynamic-heat-boundary-condition). It remains valid when $\alpha$ vanishes, because no division by $\alpha$ is used. The last part preserves energy monotonicity; its stationary states need not have the earlier prescribed [Dirichlet data](../../../differential-equation.md#dirichlet-boundary-data). For example, with strictly positive $\alpha$ on a connected [boundary](../../../topology.md#boundary-of-a-set), a stationary harmonic state has zero outward [normal derivative](../../../differential-geometry.md#normal-derivative) and is constant, as follows by applying [Green's first identity](../../../partial-differential-equation.md#green-s-first-identity) to that state itself.

## 10A

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="10a/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [10A](#10a)

For an oriented piecewise [smooth surface](../../../differential-geometry.md#smooth-surface) $S$ and a [continuously differentiable](../../../calculus.md#continuously-differentiable-function) [vector field](../../../calculus.md#vector-field), [Stokes theorem](../../../calculus.md#stokes-theorem) states

$$
\int_S(\nabla\times B)\cdot n\,dS
=\oint_{\partial S}B\cdot dr,
$$

where the [boundary orientation](../../../differential-geometry.md#boundary-orientation) is induced by $n$ through the [right-hand rule](../../../electromagnetism.md#right-hand-rule). Choose the upward orientation of the [elliptic paraboloid](../../../geometry-and-topology.md#elliptic-paraboloid). Its band has [cylindrical radius](../../../calculus.md#cylindrical-radius) $1/2\leq r\leq1$, and a parametrization is

$$
X(r,\varphi)=(r\cos\varphi,r\sin\varphi,r^2),
\qquad 0\leq\varphi<2\pi.
$$

Its [oriented surface element](../../../calculus.md#oriented-surface-element) and scalar area element are

$$
\boxed{d\mathbf S=(X_r\times X_\varphi)\,dr\,d\varphi
=(-2r^2\cos\varphi,-2r^2\sin\varphi,r)\,dr\,d\varphi,}
$$



$$
dS=r\sqrt{1+4r^2}\,dr\,d\varphi.
$$

The requested sketch shows the open band, not a capped solid.

![](../../../past-exam-of-the-mathematics-course-of-the-university-of-cambridge/2005/ia/paper-3-paraboloid.png)

**[Figure 2](#10a/image-upward-oriented-annular-paraboloid-band-with-counterclockwise-outer-boundary-and-clockwise-inner-boundary). Upward-oriented annular paraboloid band, with counterclockwise outer boundary and clockwise inner boundary**.

For the specified [vector field](../../../calculus.md#vector-field), direct [differentiation](../../../calculus.md#differentiation) gives $\nabla\times B=(0,0,3x^2+3y^2)$. Hence

$$
I=\int_0^{2\pi}\int_{1/2}^1 3r^3\,dr\,d\varphi
=\boxed{\frac{45\pi}{32}}.
$$

To check this by [Stokes theorem](../../../calculus.md#stokes-theorem), parametrize a counterclockwise circle of radius $\rho$ at height $\rho^2$ by $r_\rho(\varphi)=(\rho\cos\varphi,\rho\sin\varphi,\rho^2)$. Along it $dz=0$, and

$$
B\cdot dr_\rho
=\rho^4(\sin^4\varphi+\cos^4\varphi)\,d\varphi.
$$

Using $\int_0^{2\pi}\sin^4\varphi\,d\varphi =\int_0^{2\pi}\cos^4\varphi\,d\varphi=3\pi/4$ gives $3\pi\rho^4/2$. The induced outer circle is counterclockwise as viewed from above, and the inner circle is clockwise. Therefore

$$
I=\oint_{C_1}B\cdot dr-\oint_{C_{1/2}}B\cdot dr
=\frac{3\pi}{2}\left(1-\frac1{16}\right)
=\boxed{\frac{45\pi}{32}}.
$$

This verifies the direct [surface integral](../../../calculus.md#surface-integral) and the [Stokes flux through an annular paraboloid](../../../calculus.md#stokes-flux-through-an-annular-paraboloid). Reversing the chosen surface orientation reverses the final sign.

## 11A

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="11a/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [11A](#11a)

At a [critical point](../../../analysis.md#critical-point) $p$ of a twice [continuously differentiable](../../../calculus.md#continuously-differentiable-function) real function, $\nabla f(p)=0$ and the [Taylor expansion](../../../calculus.md#taylor-expansion) is

$$
f(p+h)=f(p)+\tfrac12h^THh+o(|h|^2),
\qquad H=D^2f(p).
$$

The real symmetric [Hessian matrix](../../../calculus.md#hessian-matrix) has an [orthonormal eigenbasis](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#orthonormal-eigenbasis), so writing $h=\sum_i a_i u_i$ gives $h^THh=\sum_i\lambda_i a_i^2$. If every [eigenvalue](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) is positive, this quadratic form is at least $\lambda_{\min}|h|^2$, and the remainder cannot change its sign for sufficiently small nonzero $h$. Thus $p$ is a strict [local minimum](../../../analysis.md#local-minimum). If every [eigenvalue](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) is negative, the analogous argument gives a strict [local maximum](../../../analysis.md#local-maximum). If positive and negative [eigenvalues](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) both occur, displacement along the corresponding [eigenvectors](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvector) gives values above and below $f(p)$, so $p$ is a [saddle point of a scalar function](../../../analysis.md#saddle-point-of-a-scalar-function).

The [second-derivative test](../../../calculus.md#second-derivative-test) can be inconclusive when the [Hessian matrix](../../../calculus.md#hessian-matrix) is singular and semidefinite: higher-order terms along directions in the [kernel of a linear map](../../../linear-algebra.md#kernel-of-a-linear-map) matter. For example $x^2+y^4$ and $x^2-y^4$ have the same Hessian $\operatorname{diag}(2,0)$ at zero, but the first has a strict minimum and the second a [saddle point of a scalar function](../../../analysis.md#saddle-point-of-a-scalar-function). A zero [eigenvalue](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) does not prevent a saddle conclusion if other [eigenvalues](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) already have both signs.

For the given function write $r^2=x^2+y^2$. Its first [derivatives](../../../calculus.md#derivative) are

$$
f_x=y e^{-\alpha r^2}(1-2\alpha x^2),\qquad
f_y=x e^{-\alpha r^2}(1-2\alpha y^2).
$$

The origin is a [critical point](../../../analysis.md#critical-point) for every real $\alpha$, with

$$
D^2f(0,0)=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Its [eigenvalues](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) are $1,-1$, so it is always a [saddle point of a scalar function](../../../analysis.md#saddle-point-of-a-scalar-function). If one coordinate at a [critical point](../../../analysis.md#critical-point) is zero, the other must also be zero. If both are nonzero, the [derivative](../../../calculus.md#derivative) equations force $x^2=y^2=1/(2\alpha)$, possible only for $\alpha>0$. Hence for $\alpha\leq0$ the origin is the only [critical point](../../../analysis.md#critical-point).

For $\alpha>0$ there are four additional [critical points](../../../analysis.md#critical-point),

$$
(x,y)=\frac1{\sqrt{2\alpha}}(\varepsilon,\eta),
\qquad \varepsilon,\eta\in\{-1,1\}.
$$

At such a point,

$$
f=\frac{\varepsilon\eta}{2\alpha e},\qquad
D^2f=-4\alpha xy\,e^{-1}I_2
=-\frac{2\varepsilon\eta}{e}I_2.
$$

Thus **the two equal-sign points are strict maxima, and the two opposite-sign points are strict minima**. They are global: $2|xy|\leq r^2$ implies

$$
|f(x,y)|\leq\frac{r^2}{2}e^{-\alpha r^2}
\leq\frac1{2\alpha e},
$$

where the last maximum occurs at $r^2=1/\alpha$ and equality also requires $x^2=y^2$.

As $\alpha\downarrow0$, the four nonzero [critical points](../../../analysis.md#critical-point) have distance $\alpha^{-1/2}$ from the origin and **escape to infinity**, rather than merging with the origin. Their values diverge as $\pm1/(2e\alpha)$. As $\alpha\uparrow0$ from below there are no extra [critical points](../../../analysis.md#critical-point), while the origin stays a saddle throughout. This is [critical points escaping to infinity in a Gaussian-weighted bilinear function](../../../calculus.md#critical-points-escaping-to-infinity-in-a-gaussian-weighted-bilinear-function).

## 12A

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="12a/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [12A](#12a)

The substitution is nonsingular in the interior $x>0$, $0<y<1$, $0<z<x$. Its inverse is

$$
x=\sqrt{\alpha\beta},\qquad
y=\sqrt{\frac\beta\alpha},\qquad
z=\gamma\sqrt{\frac\alpha\beta}.
$$

Thus $y<1$ is equivalent to $\beta<\alpha$, while $z<x$ is equivalent to $\gamma<xy=\beta$. Positivity gives the full transformed domain

$$
\boxed{0<\gamma<\beta<\alpha<\infty.}
$$

The forward [Jacobian determinant](../../../calculus.md#jacobian-determinant) is

$$
\frac{\partial(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}{\partial(x,y,z)}
=\det\begin{pmatrix}
1/y&-x/y^2&0\\
y&x&0\\
0&z&y
\end{pmatrix}
=2x>0.
$$

Therefore $x\,dx\,dy\,dz=\tfrac12\,d\alpha\,d\beta\,d\gamma$. The [boundary](../../../topology.md#boundary-of-a-set) faces where the formula fails have measure zero. The [change of variables formula](../../../calculus.md#change-of-variables-formula) gives

$$
I=\frac12\int_0^\infty d\alpha\int_0^\alpha d\beta\int_0^\beta d\gamma\,
e^{-A\alpha-B\beta-C\gamma}.
$$

The integrand is nonnegative, so [Tonelli's theorem](../../../measure-theory.md#tonelli-theorem) permits reordering the [integration](../../../calculus.md#integral) even before convergence has been established. Integrating first in $\alpha$, then in $\beta$, gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
I
&=\frac12\int_0^\infty e^{-C\gamma}\,d\gamma
\int_\gamma^\infty e^{-B\beta}\,d\beta
\int_\beta^\infty e^{-A\alpha}\,d\alpha\\
&=\frac1{2A}\int_0^\infty e^{-C\gamma}\,d\gamma
\int_\gamma^\infty e^{-(A+B)\beta}\,d\beta\\
&=\frac1{2A(A+B)}
\int_0^\infty e^{-(A+B+C)\gamma}\,d\gamma\\
&=\boxed{\frac1{2A(A+B)(A+B+C)}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The assumptions $A,B,C>0$ make all three integrals of [exponential functions](../../../calculus.md#exponential-function) converge. Equivalently, the [ordered-cone substitution by products and ratios](../../../calculus.md#ordered-cone-substitution-by-products-and-ratios) can be followed by the increment variables $\alpha-\beta$, $\beta-\gamma$, $\gamma$, all independently positive and with [Jacobian determinant](../../../calculus.md#jacobian-determinant) one; the exponent then separates into three linear terms with coefficients $A$, $A+B$ and $A+B+C$.
