## 1E

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="1e/i">i</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1E](#1e)

<h4 id="1e/i/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#1e/i)

Take the [integral domain](../../../commutative-algebra.md#integral-domain) $R=\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-5}]$, embedded in $\mathbb C$. It exhibits [failure of unique factorization in the ring of integers generated by square root of minus five](../../../algebra.md#failure-of-unique-factorization-in-the-ring-of-integers-generated-by-square-root-of-minus-five):

$$
\boxed{6=2\cdot3=(1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5}).}
$$

Here is why these are genuinely distinct factorizations into [irreducible elements](../../../commutative-algebra.md#irreducible-element). The multiplicative [algebraic norm](../../../algebraic-number-theory.md#algebraic-norm) $N(a+b\sqrt{-5})=a^2+5b^2$ is a nonnegative integer and equals one only for $\pm1$, which are therefore the only [units](../../../algebra.md#unit-in-a-ring). Neither two nor three is a value of $N$: if $b\ne0$ the value is at least five, and if $b=0$ it is a square. A factorization of $2$ into nonunits would require norms two and two; one of $3$ would require norms three and three; one of $1\pm\sqrt{-5}$ would require norms two and three. All are impossible. Thus all four factors are irreducible. The factors of norms four and nine cannot be [associate elements](../../../commutative-algebra.md#associate-element) of those of norm six, and $1+\sqrt{-5}$ and $1-\sqrt{-5}$ are not related by a sign. Therefore $R$ is **an [integral domain](../../../commutative-algebra.md#integral-domain) which is not a [unique factorization domain](../../../algebra.md#unique-factorization-domain)**.

<h3 id="1e/ii">ii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1E](#1e)

<h4 id="1e/ii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#1e/ii)

Use the standard convention that an [integral domain](../../../commutative-algebra.md#integral-domain) has $1\ne0$. Since $(n)=(-n)$, the sign of a nonzero $n$ makes no difference. For $|n|=p$ prime, every nonzero residue has an inverse by [Bézout's identity](../../../algebra.md#bezout-identity), so the [quotient ring](../../../commutative-algebra.md#quotient-ring) is a [field](../../../algebra.md#field) and hence an [integral domain](../../../commutative-algebra.md#integral-domain). If $|n|=ab$ is composite with $1<a,b<|n|$, the nonzero classes of $a$ and $b$ multiply to zero, giving [zero divisors](../../../mathematics.md#zero-divisor). For $n=\pm1$, the quotient is the [zero ring](../../../commutative-algebra.md#zero-ring), excluded by the convention above. Finally, $n=0$ gives $\mathbb Z/(0)\cong\mathbb Z$, an [integral domain](../../../commutative-algebra.md#integral-domain). Thus

$$
\boxed{n=0\quad\text{or}\quad |n|\text{ is prime}.}
$$

If a convention restricts this notation to positive moduli, the answer is simply the positive primes.

## 2H

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="2h/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2H](#2h)

Parametrize the surface by $r(x,y)=(x,y,xy)$. Its tangent vectors are $r_x=(1,0,y)$ and $r_y=(0,1,x)$. Choose the upward [unit normal](../../../differential-geometry.md#unit-normal)

$$
N=\frac{(-y,-x,1)}{\sqrt{1+x^2+y^2}}.
$$

The [first fundamental form](../../../differential-geometry.md#first-fundamental-form) coefficients are

$$
E=1+y^2,\qquad F=xy,\qquad G=1+x^2,
\qquad EG-F^2=1+x^2+y^2.
$$

Since $r_{xx}=r_{yy}=0$ and $r_{xy}=(0,0,1)$, the [second fundamental form](../../../second-fundamental-form.md) coefficients are $e=g=0$ and $f=(1+x^2+y^2)^{-1/2}$. The determinant of the [shape operator](../../../second-fundamental-form.md#shape-operator) gives the [Gaussian curvature](../../../second-fundamental-form.md#gaussian-curvature):

$$
\boxed{K=\frac{eg-f^2}{EG-F^2}=-\frac1{(1+x^2+y^2)^2}.}
$$

Changing the normal's sign changes both second-form factors and leaves this determinant unchanged. Although the question calls the surface a hyperboloid, $z=xy$ is a [hyperbolic paraboloid](../../../geometry-and-topology.md#hyperbolic-paraboloid); the calculation uses the stated surface equation.

## 3F

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="3f/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3F](#3f)

The function is [Fréchet differentiable](../../../calculus.md#frechet-differentiability) at $(a,b)$ when there is a [linear map](../../../vector-space.md#linear-map) $L:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R$ such that

$$
f(a+h,b+k)=f(a,b)+L(h,k)+r(h,k),\qquad
\lim_{(h,k)\to(0,0)}\frac{|r(h,k)|}{\sqrt{h^2+k^2}}=0.
$$

If it exists, $L(h,k)=f_x(a,b)h+f_y(a,b)k$, as is seen by taking increments along each coordinate axis.

To prove [differentiability from continuous partial derivatives](../../../analysis.md#differentiability-from-continuous-partial-derivatives), take a small rectangle in the given neighbourhood and split the increment along its sides. The one-variable [mean value theorem](../../../calculus.md#mean-value-theorem) gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(a+h,b+k)-f(a,b)
&=f(a+h,b+k)-f(a,b+k)+f(a,b+k)-f(a,b)\\
&=h f_x(a+\theta h,b+k)+k f_y(a,b+\eta k),
\end{aligned}
$$

for some $0<\theta,\eta<1$ when the corresponding increment is nonzero. If an increment is zero, its term is simply omitted. The required one-variable continuity follows from existence of the corresponding [partial derivative](../../../calculus.md#partial-derivative) on the segment. Subtracting $L(h,k)$ gives a remainder whose absolute value, by continuity of both [partial derivatives](../../../calculus.md#partial-derivative) at $(a,b)$, is at most

$$
\epsilon(|h|+|k|)\le\sqrt2\epsilon\sqrt{h^2+k^2}
$$

for sufficiently small increments. Since $\epsilon$ is arbitrary, the normalized remainder tends to zero. Thus **the function is differentiable at $(a,b)$**, with the displayed derivative.

## 4F

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="4f/i">i</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4F](#4f)

<h4 id="4f/i/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#4f/i)

**This does not define a [topology](../../../topology.md) on $\mathbb Z$.** Every singleton $\{2k\}$ is declared open, but their union over $k\in\mathbb Z$ is $2\mathbb Z$, an infinite proper subset. It is therefore not declared open, violating closure under arbitrary unions. This is the example that [finite subsets do not define a topology on an infinite set](../../../topology.md#finite-subsets-do-not-define-a-topology-on-an-infinite-set). The empty set, whole space and finite-intersection requirements do hold, but they do not compensate for the failed union axiom. This is different from the [cofinite topology](../../../topology.md#cofinite-topology), which uses finite complements rather than finite sets.

<h3 id="4f/ii">ii</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4F](#4f)

<h4 id="4f/ii/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#4f/ii)

If an open set contains an even integer, invariance under every even translation makes it contain all even integers. Similarly, containing an odd integer makes it contain all odd integers. Conversely each parity class is invariant under those translations. Thus the proposed [open sets](../../../topology.md#open-set) are exactly

$$
\boxed{\varnothing,\quad 2\mathbb Z,\quad 2\mathbb Z+1,\quad\mathbb Z.}
$$

They form a [partition topology](../../../topology.md#partition-topology). Both the empty and whole sets are included, and any union or finite intersection is again a union of parity classes. Hence **this is a [topological space](../../../topology.md#topological-space)**. It is not the [discrete topology](../../../topology.md#discrete-space): no singleton is open.

## 5D

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="5d/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [5D](#5d)

Write $z=x+iy$ and $w=u+iv$. Multiplying the numerator and denominator of the [Möbius transformation](../../../group-theory.md#mobius-transformation) by $1+\bar z$ gives

$$
u=\frac{2y}{(1+x)^2+y^2},\qquad
v=\frac{1-x^2-y^2}{(1+x)^2+y^2}>0.
$$

Pull back the given upper-half-plane solution to obtain the [harmonic measure of a semicircle in a disk](../../../analysis.md#harmonic-measure-of-a-semicircle-in-a-disk):

$$
\boxed{F(x,y)=\frac12+\frac1\pi\arctan\left(\frac{2y}{1-x^2-y^2}\right),\qquad x^2+y^2<1.}
$$

The principal real arctangent is appropriate because $v>0$. By [conformal invariance of harmonicity](../../../complex-analysis.md#conformal-invariance-of-harmonicity), composing a [harmonic function](../../../partial-differential-equation.md#harmonic-function) with this analytic conformal map is harmonic. More explicitly, $\Delta_z(f\circ w)=|w'(z)|^2(\Delta_w f)\circ w=0$. At a boundary point on the open upper semicircle the ratio tends to $+\infty$, yielding one; on the lower semicircle it tends to $-\infty$, yielding zero.

At $z=\pm1$ the two boundary prescriptions jump, so no continuous boundary value can be imposed there and the interior limit depends on the approach. The bounded harmonic solution is interpreted with the specified values away from those endpoints. Boundedness also supplies the usual uniqueness for this [Dirichlet problem](../../../analysis.md#dirichlet-problem); without a boundedness or comparable regularity restriction, singular harmonic functions at the omitted endpoints would allow extra solutions.

## 6A

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="6a/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [6A](#6a)

Choose the coordinate vectors $b=e_i$ and $c=e_j$ in the assumed bilinear identity. It gives $T_{ij}=0$ for each pair of indices, hence **the tensor is zero**.

Let $\Omega$ be the enclosed region. Apply the [divergence theorem](../../../calculus.md#divergence-theorem) to the vector [field](../../../algebra.md#field) $(b\cdot x)c$. Since $b,c$ are constant,

$$
\nabla\cdot[(b\cdot x)c]=b\cdot c.
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{\int_S(b\cdot x)(c\cdot n)\,dS=V b\cdot c.}
$$

Writing the left side as $b_i c_j\int_S x_i n_j\,dS$ and using the initial tensor argument gives the [boundary integral of position and normal components](../../../calculus.md#boundary-integral-of-position-and-normal-components)

$$
\boxed{\int_S x_i n_j\,dS=V\delta_{ij}.}
$$

Contract with the [Kronecker delta](../../../linear-algebra.md#kronecker-delta) to obtain $\int_S x\cdot n\,dS=3V$. Contract with the [Levi-Civita symbol](../../../calculus.md#levi-civita-symbol) to obtain

$$
\left(\int_S x\times n\,dS\right)_k
=\epsilon_{kij}V\delta_{ij}=0.
$$

Thus the requested integrals are **$3V$ and the zero vector**, respectively. These applications require the usual sufficiently regular closed boundary and outward-normal orientation of the divergence theorem.

## 7B

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="7b/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [7B](#7b)

In the position representation, $\hat p=-i\hbar\nabla$. The [orbital angular momentum](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#orbital-angular-momentum) operator and its squared magnitude are

$$
\boxed{\hat L=\hat x\times\hat p=-i\hbar\hat x\times\nabla,\qquad
\hat L^2=\sum_{i=1}^3\hat L_i^2.}
$$

For example, with $D=x\cdot\nabla$, the latter is $-\hbar^2[r^2\Delta-D^2-D]$. Operator products here act on smooth [wavefunctions](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#wave-function) in a common invariant domain.

To calculate the [angular momentum commutation relations](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#angular-momentum-commutation-relations), use $[x_a,p_b]=i\hbar\delta_{ab}$ and the vanishing position-position and momentum-momentum [commutators](../../../lie-algebra.md#commutator). Expanding the products gives

$$
[x_ap_b,x_cp_d]=i\hbar(\delta_{ad}x_cp_b-\delta_{bc}x_ap_d).
$$

Multiplication by $\epsilon_{iab}\epsilon_{jcd}$ then gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
[L_i,L_j]
&=i\hbar\epsilon_{iab}\epsilon_{jcd}
(\delta_{ad}x_cp_b-\delta_{bc}x_ap_d)\\
&=i\hbar(x_i p_j-x_j p_i)
=i\hbar\epsilon_{ijk}L_k.
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, the product commutator rule yields

$$
[L^2,L_i]=\sum_j\bigl(L_j[L_j,L_i]+[L_j,L_i]L_j\bigr)
=i\hbar\sum_{j,k}\epsilon_{jik}(L_jL_k+L_kL_j)=0.
$$

The final cancellation pairs the antisymmetric [Levi-Civita symbol](../../../calculus.md#levi-civita-symbol) with an expression symmetric in $j,k$. Thus

$$
\boxed{[L_i,L_j]=i\hbar\epsilon_{ijk}L_k,\qquad [L^2,L_i]=0.}
$$

## 8C

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="8c/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [8C](#8c)

<h4 id="8c/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#8c/a)

Under the null hypothesis the three preference probabilities for girls are each $1/3$. Conditional on their total of sixty, the counts have a [multinomial distribution](../../../discrete-probability-distribution.md#multinomial-distribution), giving expected counts $(20,20,20)$. The [Pearson chi-squared goodness-of-fit test](../../../statistical-modelling.md#pearson-chi-squared-goodness-of-fit-test) statistic is

$$
\boxed{X^2=\frac{(13-20)^2+(14-20)^2+(33-20)^2}{20}=12.7.}
$$

No probabilities have been fitted, so the asymptotic null distribution is [chi-squared distribution](../../../probability-theory.md#chi-squared-distribution) with $3-1=2$ degrees of freedom. All expected counts are comfortably large for this approximation. For two degrees of freedom its upper-tail probability is $e^{-X^2/2}$, so

$$
\boxed{p=e^{-6.35}\simeq0.00175.}
$$

At the conventional five-percent or one-percent significance level, **reject equal attractiveness**: there is substantial evidence against the null distribution. No level was specified, so reporting the p-value makes the decision rule explicit. The test assumes independent responses and a sampling interpretation appropriate to the children surveyed.

<h3 id="8c/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [8C](#8c)

<h4 id="8c/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#8c/b)

Use the [Pearson chi-squared test of homogeneity](../../../statistical-modelling.md#pearson-chi-squared-test-of-homogeneity) for two multinomial samples. Under equal preference distributions, estimate the common probabilities from pooled counts, giving $(1/4,1/4,1/2)$. With row totals forty and sixty, the expected table is

$$
E=\begin{pmatrix}10&10&20\\15&15&30\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The [Pearson chi-squared statistic for contingency tables](../../../statistical-modelling.md#pearson-chi-squared-statistic-for-contingency-tables) is

$$
\begin{aligned}
X^2&=\frac4{10}+\frac1{10}+\frac9{20}+\frac4{15}+\frac1{15}+\frac9{30}\\
&=\frac{19}{12}\simeq1.58333.
\end{aligned}
$$

There are $(2-1)(3-1)=2$ degrees of freedom, after fitting the common category probabilities. All expected counts exceed five. Thus

$$
\boxed{p=e^{-19/24}\simeq0.4531.}
$$

At five percent, **do not reject the common preference distribution**. These data are consistent with that hypothesis; failure to reject does not prove equality. This conclusion differs from part a because a common nonuniform distribution, especially an overall preference for chocolate, is allowed here.

## 9C

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="9c/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [9C](#9c)

Order the states as sandwich $N$, Hall $H$, and own cooking $C$. The [transition matrix](../../../markov-process.md#stochastic-matrix), with current state indexing the row, is

$$
P=\begin{pmatrix}1/2&1/4&1/4\\1/3&1/3&1/3\\1&0&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The two probabilities $1/4$ in the first row are unconditional alternatives to the probability $1/2$ of returning. Let $a_n=(P^n)_{HH}$, so $n$ counts days after the initial Hall lunch. Expanding the determinant gives the [characteristic polynomial](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#characteristic-polynomial)

$$
\det(\lambda I-P)=\lambda(\lambda-1)(\lambda+1/6).
$$

The three distinct [eigenvalues](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) imply that the sequence has the form

$$
a_n=A+B(-1/6)^n+C0^n,
$$

using the specified $0^0=1$. Directly, $a_0=1$, $a_1=1/3$ and $a_2=7/36$. The last two equations give $A=3/14$ and $B=-5/7$; then $C=3/2$. Consequently

$$
\boxed{a_{60}=\frac3{14}-\frac5{7\,6^{60}}.}
$$

The limiting value is also the Hall entry of the [stationary distribution](../../../markov-process.md#stationary-distribution) $(4/7,3/14,3/14)$. The tiny finite-time correction is retained in the boxed exact probability. Sixty days later means sixty transitions, rather than the sixtieth day including the initial day.

## 10H

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="10h/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [10H](#10h)

<h4 id="10h/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#10h/a)

The [matrix trace](../../../linear-algebra.md#matrix-trace) is the sum of diagonal entries, $\operatorname{tr}A=\sum_{i=1}^nA_{ii}$. For any two square [matrices](../../../vector-space.md#matrix),

$$
\operatorname{tr}(BC)=\sum_{i,j}B_{ij}C_{ji}
=\sum_{j,i}C_{ji}B_{ij}=\operatorname{tr}(CB).
$$

Apply this [cyclic property of the trace](../../../linear-algebra.md#cyclic-property-of-the-trace) with $B=T$ and $C=AT^{-1}$:

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{tr}(TAT^{-1})=\operatorname{tr}(AT^{-1}T)=\operatorname{tr}A.}
$$

Thus [matrix similarity](../../../linear-algebra.md#matrix-similarity) preserves the [matrix trace](../../../linear-algebra.md#matrix-trace); no assumption that either [matrix](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) is diagonalizable is needed.

<h3 id="10h/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [10H](#10h)

<h4 id="10h/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#10h/b)

Suppose $v=(v_1,\ldots,v_r)^t$ belongs to the [kernel](../../../linear-algebra.md#kernel-of-a-linear-map) of the displayed [matrix](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) $\Lambda$. Define the [polynomial](../../../polynomial.md) $q(z)=\sum_{k=1}^r v_kz^k$. Then $q(\lambda_j)=0$ for all $j$, and $q(0)=0$. These are $r+1$ distinct roots of a [polynomial](../../../polynomial.md) of degree at most $r$. Repeated application of the [factor theorem](../../../polynomial.md#factor-theorem) therefore makes $q$ identically zero, so every $v_k=0$. Hence $\Lambda$ is injective and, by finite-dimensional [rank-nullity theorem](../../../linear-algebra.md#rank-nullity-theorem), invertible. Its transpose is invertible with inverse $(\Lambda^{-1})^t$, proving **both kernels are trivial**.

For the second [matrix](../../../vector-space.md#matrix), membership of the all-ones vector in the [kernel](../../../linear-algebra.md#kernel-of-a-linear-map) means

$$
\sum_{j=1}^n\lambda_j^k=0\qquad(1\le k\le n).
$$

Group the distinct nonzero values as $\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_r$, with multiplicities $m_1,\ldots,m_r>0$. Zero values contribute nothing to these positive powers. The first $r$ equations are

$$
\sum_{j=1}^r m_j\mu_j^k=0\qquad(1\le k\le r),
$$

or $\Lambda(\mu)^t m=0$. The proved transpose-kernel statement implies $m=0$, contradicting its positive entries if $r>0$. Therefore $r=0$ and

$$
\boxed{\lambda_1=\cdots=\lambda_n=0.}
$$

Conversely, all zero values plainly give a zero [matrix](../../../vector-space.md#matrix). This proves the [zero power sums force a finite complex multiset to vanish](../../../galois-theory.md#zero-power-sums-force-a-finite-complex-multiset-to-vanish) criterion, including repetitions and zero values rather than assuming all [eigenvalues](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) distinct.

<h3 id="10h/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [10H](#10h)

<h4 id="10h/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#10h/c)

Use the permitted triangularization to write $A=TUT^{-1}$ with $U$ upper triangular and diagonal $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n$. Products of upper-triangular [matrices](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) are upper triangular, and their diagonal entries multiply, so

$$
0=\operatorname{tr}(A^k)=\operatorname{tr}(U^k)=\sum_{j=1}^n\lambda_j^k
\qquad(1\le k\le n).
$$

The result of part b makes every $\lambda_j$ zero. Thus $U$ is strictly upper triangular. To see directly that $U^n=0$, a potentially nonzero entry in a product of $n$ factors requires a chain

$$
i=i_0<i_1<\cdots<i_n=j
$$

of $n+1$ indices in $\{1,\ldots,n\}$, which is impossible. Hence

$$
\boxed{A^n=TU^nT^{-1}=0.}
$$

This is [nilpotence from vanishing traces of powers](../../../linear-algebra.md#nilpotence-from-vanishing-traces-of-powers). It proves nilpotence without substituting an unproved diagonalization for the allowed triangularization.

## 11E

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="11e/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [11E](#11e)

Use the usual commutative unital-ring convention for [Noetherian rings](../../../algebra.md#noetherian-ring). A ring is Noetherian exactly when every [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) is finitely generated, equivalently when ascending chains of [ideals](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) stabilize. Indeed, the union of an ascending chain is an [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal); finitely many generators all lie in a single chain member. Conversely, if an [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) cannot be finitely generated, adjoining a new element at each stage constructs a strictly ascending chain.

First assume $R[X]$ is Noetherian. Evaluation at zero gives a surjective [ring homomorphism](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ring-homomorphism) $R[X]\to R$ with [kernel](../../../linear-algebra.md#kernel-of-a-linear-map) $(X)$. A quotient of a [Noetherian ring](../../../algebra.md#noetherian-ring) is Noetherian: lift an [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) to the source, take finitely many generators there, and map those generators down. Thus $R\cong R[X]/(X)$ is Noetherian.

For the converse, assume $R$ is Noetherian and take any [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) $I\subseteq R[X]$. For $d\ge0$, let $J_d$ be the set of coefficients of $X^d$ in elements of $I$ of degree at most $d$. It is an [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) of $R$, since sums and scalar multiples of such [polynomials](../../../polynomial.md) stay in $I$. Multiplication by $X$ shows $J_d\subseteq J_{d+1}$. The ascending chain stabilizes, say at $N$.

For each $0\le d\le N$, choose finitely many generators $a_{d,j}$ of $J_d$ and corresponding $f_{d,j}\in I$ of degree at most $d$ having coefficient $a_{d,j}$ at $X^d$. Discard zero generators. We claim these finitely many [polynomials](../../../polynomial.md) generate $I$. If a nonzero $f\in I$ has degree $m\le N$, express its leading coefficient as $\sum_jr_j a_{m,j}$ and subtract $\sum_jr_jf_{m,j}$. The degree decreases. If $m>N$, use $J_m=J_N$, express the leading coefficient using the $a_{N,j}$, and subtract

$$
\sum_jr_jX^{m-N}f_{N,j}.
$$

Again the leading term cancels and the degree decreases. Induction on degree, including degree zero, expresses every $f$ in the claimed [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal). Therefore every [ideal](../../../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) of $R[X]$ is finitely generated, proving the [Hilbert basis theorem](../../../algebra.md#hilbert-basis-theorem) and

$$
\boxed{R[X]\text{ is Noetherian}\iff R\text{ is Noetherian}.}
$$

The argument is the [bounded-degree coefficient proof of Hilbert basis theorem](../../../algebra.md#bounded-degree-coefficient-proof-of-hilbert-basis-theorem); it never divides by a leading coefficient and therefore does not require $R$ to be an [integral domain](../../../commutative-algebra.md#integral-domain). With the analogous left-ideal convention the same proof applies to left-Noetherian rings with a central indeterminate.

Use the unit [sphere](../../../geometry-and-topology.md#sphere) $X^2+Y^2+Z^2=1$, its north pole $(0,0,1)$, and the equatorial complex plane $Z=0$. The line from the north pole to a [sphere](../../../geometry-and-topology.md#sphere) point meets that plane at

$$
\boxed{z=\frac{X+iY}{1-Z}.}
$$

The north pole maps to $\infty$. The inverse [stereographic projection](../../../complex-analysis.md#stereographic-projection) is

$$
(X,Y,Z)=\left(\frac{2\Re z}{1+|z|^2},\frac{2\Im z}{1+|z|^2},\frac{|z|^2-1}{1+|z|^2}\right).
$$

The antipodal point has coordinate $w=-(X+iY)/(1+Z)$. Since $X^2+Y^2=(1-Z)(1+Z)$, this is $-1/\bar z$ when both sides are finite and nonzero. Zero and infinity correspond to the two poles and are exchanged by the same extended convention. The inverse formula also proves the converse, giving the [antipodal stereographic coordinate relation](../../../complex-analysis.md#antipodal-stereographic-coordinate-relation)

$$
\boxed{w=-1/\bar z\iff\text{the sphere points are antipodal}.}
$$

The requested rotation result is that [sphere](../../../geometry-and-topology.md#sphere) rotations correspond exactly to [Möbius transformations](../../../group-theory.md#mobius-transformation)

$$
z\longmapsto\frac{az+b}{-\bar b z+\bar a},\qquad |a|^2+|b|^2=1.
$$

The [matrices](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) $\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\-\bar b&\bar a\end{pmatrix}$ form $SU(2)$, and opposite [matrices](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) induce the same transformation. Thus the rotation group $SO(3)$ is identified with $SU(2)/\{\pm I\}$. This is the [sphere rotations as special-unitary Möbius transformations](../../../complex-analysis.md#sphere-rotations-as-special-unitary-mobius-transformations) result, which need not be proved here.

For a planar [circle](../../../topology.md#circle) $|z-c|=r$, $c=a+ib$ and $r>0$, substitute $|z|^2=(1+Z)/(1-Z)$ and $\Re(\bar cz)=(aX+bY)/(1-Z)$. Multiplication by $1-Z$ gives the [circle-plane relation under stereographic projection](../../../complex-analysis.md#circle-plane-relation-under-stereographic-projection):

$$
-2aX-2bY+(1-|c|^2+r^2)Z+1+|c|^2-r^2=0.
$$

The lift lies in this plane, and conversely its intersection with the [sphere](../../../geometry-and-topology.md#sphere) projects onto the [circle](../../../topology.md#circle). It is a nondegenerate [circle](../../../topology.md#circle) on the [sphere](../../../geometry-and-topology.md#sphere). The north pole is not on the displayed plane, since substitution there gives two rather than zero.

A [great circle](../../../geometry-and-topology.md#great-circle) is the intersection with a plane through the [sphere](../../../geometry-and-topology.md#sphere)'s centre. Here this holds exactly when

$$
\boxed{r^2=1+|c|^2.}
$$

The unit [circle](../../../topology.md#circle) lifts to the equator. If a different [circle](../../../topology.md#circle) lifts to a [great circle](../../../geometry-and-topology.md#great-circle), its plane and the equatorial plane are distinct planes through the origin, so their intersection cuts the [sphere](../../../geometry-and-topology.md#sphere) at two antipodal equatorial points. Their planar coordinates are $z$ and $-z$ on $|z|=1$.

Conversely, if the planar [circle](../../../topology.md#circle) meets the unit [circle](../../../topology.md#circle) in $z$ and $-z$, their lifts are antipodal equatorial points. The plane of the lifted [circle](../../../topology.md#circle) contains their midpoint, the origin, so that [circle](../../../topology.md#circle) is great. Equivalently, adding $|z-c|^2=r^2$ and $|-z-c|^2=r^2$ gives $r^2=1+|c|^2$. The exclusion of the unit [circle](../../../topology.md#circle) ensures the two intersection points are a genuine pair rather than coincident circles. This proves the [antipodal equatorial intersection criterion for a great circle](../../../complex-analysis.md#antipodal-equatorial-intersection-criterion-for-a-great-circle).

<h3 id="13f/solution">Solution</h3>

The [inverse function theorem](../../../calculus.md#inverse-function-theorem) says: if $U\subseteq\mathbb R^2$ is open, $F:U\to\mathbb R^2$ is smooth, $a\in U$, and $DF(a)$ is invertible, then there are open neighbourhoods $V\subseteq U$ of $a$ and $W$ of $F(a)$ for which $F:V\to W$ is a bijection with a smooth inverse. Its derivative is $D(F^{-1})(F(x))=[DF(x)]^{-1}$ after shrinking $V$ if necessary. A smooth function here has derivatives of every order.

For the given map,

$$
DF(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}3x^2-1&-2y\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\det DF(x,y)=3x^2-1.
$$

Thus its local smooth-invertibility set is

$$
\boxed{\{(x,y):x\ne\pm1/\sqrt3\}.}
$$

A differentiable local inverse is impossible at either excluded line by the [chain rule](../../../calculus.md#chain-rule). Even a merely continuous local inverse is impossible there: at fixed $y$, the cubic first component has a strict local extremum at these $x$ values, giving equal values at two nearby $x$ coordinates and preventing local injectivity.

On the curve, $y^2=x^3-x=x(x-1)(x+1)\ge0$. Its sign intervals give exactly $x\in[-1,0]\cup[1,\infty)$, so the two prescribed subsets cover the curve and are disjoint. For fixed $y$, the function $x^3-x$ is strictly increasing on $[1,\infty)$, starts at zero and tends to infinity. The [intermediate value theorem](../../../calculus.md#intermediate-value-theorem) therefore gives a unique $p(y)\ge1$ with $p(y)^3-p(y)=y^2$.

At every point of this second component, $3x^2-1\ge2$, so the [inverse function theorem](../../../calculus.md#inverse-function-theorem) applies. Locally its smooth inverse evaluated at $(0,y)$ has the form $(p(y),y)$, by the proved uniqueness. These local descriptions agree on overlaps, proving **$p$ is smooth on all of $\mathbb R$**. For example,

$$
p'(y)=\frac{2y}{3p(y)^2-1},
$$

whose nonzero denominator also makes the smoothness mechanism transparent.

Let $p$ first be an arbitrary degree-$n$ [polynomial](../../../polynomial.md) with leading coefficient $a_n\ne0$. Choose $R$ so large that on $|z|=R$,

$$
|p(z)-a_nz^n|<|a_n|R^n.
$$

The homotopy $a_nz^n+s[p(z)-a_nz^n]$, $0\le s\le1$, never meets zero on that [circle](../../../topology.md#circle). Hence the image curve $p(Re^{i\theta})$ has [winding number](../../../complex-analysis.md#winding-number) $n$, the same as $a_nR^ne^{in\theta}$. The [argument principle](../../../complex-analysis.md#argument-principle) counts precisely $n$ zeros inside, with multiplicity, since there are no poles. Choose $R$ large enough that the same leading-term domination holds for every $|z|\ge R$; the lower-term ratios decrease with radius, so this also excludes all exterior zeros. This proves the [fundamental theorem of algebra](../../../algebra.md#fundamental-theorem-of-algebra) with its multiplicity count. A degree-zero nonzero [polynomial](../../../polynomial.md) has no zeros.

Now use the specified bound for the monic quartic. Compare $\Gamma$ with the closed curve

$$
\gamma_0(t)=\begin{cases}R^4e^{4\pi it},&0\le t\le1/2,\\R^4,&1/2\le t\le1.\end{cases}
$$

Its first piece travels once counterclockwise around a [circle](../../../topology.md#circle), and its second stays at the endpoint, so its [winding number](../../../complex-analysis.md#winding-number) is one. On the first piece, $|\Gamma-\gamma_0|<R^4$. On the second, both $p(R)$ and $p(iR)$ differ from $R^4$ by less than $R^4$; every convex combination of those errors also has modulus less than $R^4$. Thus the quoted [winding number](../../../complex-analysis.md#winding-number) stability result gives

$$
\boxed{n(\Gamma,0)=1.}
$$

For real coefficients define $q(y)=y^4-by^2+d$. It has no real zeros and positive leading coefficient, so $q(y)>0$ for every real $y$, including $d=q(0)>0$. Therefore

$$
p(iy)=q(y)+i(cy-ay^3)
$$

lies strictly in the right half-plane for every real $y$. Also $p(x)>0$ for $x\ge0$: there are no zeros there, and the [polynomial](../../../polynomial.md) is positive for sufficiently large positive $x$. These facts exclude roots on the positive real and imaginary axes.

Consider the positively oriented quarter-disk boundary: first the arc from $R$ to $iR$, then the imaginary axis to zero, then the real axis back to $R$. Under $p$, its last two pieces form a path from $p(iR)$ to $p(R)$ entirely within the right half-plane. The chord closing $\Gamma$ also lies there. Since this half-plane is convex and excludes zero, deforming one closing path into the other changes no [winding number](../../../complex-analysis.md#winding-number). Thus the full image of the quarter-disk boundary has [winding number](../../../complex-analysis.md#winding-number) one. By the [argument principle](../../../complex-analysis.md#argument-principle) there is exactly one root inside that quarter-disk, counted with multiplicity.

Finally, all [polynomial](../../../polynomial.md) roots lie in $|z|<R$ under the given strict coefficient bound: for $|z|\ge R$, the sum of lower terms divided by $|z|^4$ is smaller than one. Hence

$$
\boxed{\text{exactly one zero lies in the open first quadrant}.}
$$

Its multiplicity is one. This is the [quarter-sector winding test for a real quartic](../../../complex-analysis.md#quarter-sector-winding-test-for-a-real-quartic); it accounts for the axis exclusions and does not replace the sector boundary by a chord without justifying the deformation.

For the [Fourier sine series](../../../fourier-series.md#fourier-sine-series) on $(0,1)$, [orthogonality](../../../linear-algebra.md#orthogonal-vectors) gives

$$
b_n=2\int_0^1x\sin(n\pi x)\,dx
=2\left[-\frac{x\cos(n\pi x)}{n\pi}+\frac{\sin(n\pi x)}{n^2\pi^2}\right]_0^1
=\frac{2(-1)^{n+1}}{n\pi}.
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{x=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{2(-1)^{n+1}}{n\pi}\sin(n\pi x),\qquad 0<x<1.}
$$

The series is that of the odd, two-periodic extension. At $x=0$ its value is zero, agreeing with $f(0)$; at $x=1$ every summand is zero and the series converges to zero, the average of the two one-sided extension limits. Thus it represents the function in the interior and in the square-integrable sense, **not the prescribed value one at the endpoint $x=1$**. This endpoint issue matters when using sine expansions with homogeneous boundary conditions.

<h3 id="15g/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [15G](#15g)

<h4 id="15g/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#15g/b)

Set $y=e^{-x/2}v$, the [Dirichlet gauge transform for constant drift](../../../diffusion-equation.md#dirichlet-gauge-transform-for-constant-drift). Direct differentiation gives

$$
y''+y'=e^{-x/2}(v''-v/4).
$$

The homogeneous endpoint conditions on $y$ become $v(0)=v(1)=0$. Thus the eigenvalue equation is $v''=(\lambda+1/4)v$. To exclude other branches without assuming the eigenvalue real, multiply by $\bar v$ and integrate by parts:

$$
(\lambda+1/4)\int_0^1|v|^2\,dx=-\int_0^1|v'|^2\,dx<0
$$

for a nonzero [eigenfunction](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenfunction). Therefore $\lambda+1/4=-k^2$ with $k>0$. The boundary conditions in $v=A\sin(kx)+B\cos(kx)$ give $B=0$ and $k=n\pi$, $n\ge1$. Hence

$$
\boxed{\lambda_n=-n^2\pi^2-\frac14,\qquad y_n(x)=e^{-x/2}\sin(n\pi x).}
$$

Equivalently, $L[y]=\lambda y$ is the [Sturm-Liouville form](../../../analysis.md#sturm-liouville-form) $(e^xy')'=\lambda e^xy$, with positive weight $e^x$. Its [orthogonality](../../../linear-algebra.md#orthogonal-vectors) relation is

$$
\boxed{\int_0^1e^x y_m(x)y_n(x)\,dx=\frac12\delta_{mn}.}
$$

It is the weighted inner product, not the unweighted one, that makes these [eigenfunctions](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenfunction) orthogonal.

Expand $xe^{-x/2}=\sum_nb_ny_n$ with the coefficients from part a. Completeness and the nonzero [eigenvalues](../../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) give the [Sturm-Liouville eigenfunction expansion](../../../analysis.md#sturm-liouville-eigenfunction-expansion)

$$
\boxed{y(x)=-e^{-x/2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}
\frac{2(-1)^{n+1}}{n\pi(n^2\pi^2+1/4)}\sin(n\pi x).}
$$

The coefficients are $O(n^{-3})$, so the function series converges uniformly and vanishes at both endpoints. Its first derivative also converges uniformly. Applying the transformed operator in the square-integrable sense gives the part-a expansion of $x$; equivalently $v''-v/4=x$ on the interior. Integrating this continuous right-hand side gives the classical twice-differentiable solution there. No nonzero homogeneous solution satisfies both endpoints, so this solution is unique. The mismatch of the forcing's sine series at a single endpoint does not affect the interior equation or these boundary conditions.

For a normalized state and an [observable](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#observable) $A$, define $\langle A\rangle=\langle\psi,A\psi\rangle$ and $\Delta_\psi A=\|(A-\langle A\rangle)\psi\|$. Suppose first that the position and momentum [variances](../../../variance.md) are finite and the state has the regularity needed for the usual position-momentum quadratic forms. Put $a=\langle x\rangle$, $b=\langle p\rangle$, $u=(x-a)\psi$ and $v=(-i\hbar\partial_x-b)\psi$. The [Cauchy-Schwarz inequality](../../../probability-and-statistics.md#cauchy-schwarz-inequality) gives

$$
(\Delta_\psi x)^2(\Delta_\psi p)^2\ge|\langle u,v\rangle|^2\ge(\Im\langle u,v\rangle)^2.
$$

The term involving $b$ has zero imaginary part. Integration by parts gives

$$
2\Re\int_{\mathbb R}(x-a)\bar\psi\psi'\,dx
=\int_{\mathbb R}(x-a)(|\psi|^2)'\,dx=-1.
$$

Consequently $\Im\langle u,v\rangle=\hbar/2$, and

$$
\boxed{(\Delta_\psi x)(\Delta_\psi p)\ge\frac\hbar2.}
$$

This proves the [Heisenberg uncertainty relation](../../../quantum-theory.md#heisenberg-uncertainty-relation). For smooth rapidly decaying states the boundary terms vanish directly. The same proof extends to $x\psi\in L^2$ and weak derivative $\psi'\in L^2$ using smooth cutoffs and taking their radius to infinity; the necessary mixed products are integrable by Cauchy-Schwarz. Normalization alone need not give finite [variances](../../../variance.md) or an operator-domain momentum expectation. A state with infinite spread is interpreted through the corresponding extended bound, rather than assigning a finite uncertainty to an undefined expression.

For the specified [Gaussian wave packet](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#gaussian-wave-packet), write $s=\hbar t$ and $a_t=1+is$. Its [probability density](../../../quantum-mechanics.md#probability-density) is

$$
|\psi(x,t)|^2=\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}\sqrt{1+s^2}}
\exp\left[-\frac{x^2}{2(1+s^2)}\right].
$$

It is a normalized centered [normal distribution](../../../probability-theory.md#normal-distribution) with [variance](../../../variance.md) $1+s^2$. Symmetry gives $\langle x\rangle=0$, and hence

$$
\boxed{\Delta_\psi x=\sqrt{1+\hbar^2t^2}.}
$$

Also $\partial_x\psi=-x\psi/(2a_t)$, so $p\psi=i\hbar x\psi/(2a_t)$ and $\langle p\rangle=0$ by oddness. Integration by parts, or directly taking the squared norm, gives

$$
\langle p^2\rangle=\hbar^2\int|\psi'|^2\,dx
=\frac{\hbar^2}{4|a_t|^2}\langle x^2\rangle=\frac{\hbar^2}{4}.
$$

Thus

$$
\boxed{\Delta_\psi p=\frac\hbar2,\qquad
(\Delta_\psi x)(\Delta_\psi p)=\frac\hbar2\sqrt{1+\hbar^2t^2}\ge\frac\hbar2.}
$$

The packet saturates the bound at $t=0$ and has a larger position-momentum uncertainty product at every nonzero time. Its spatial spreading does not change the momentum distribution.

In SI [units](../../../algebra.md#unit-in-a-ring), the source-free [Maxwell equations](../../../electromagnetism.md#maxwell-equations) in vacuum are

$$
\nabla\cdot E=0,\qquad \nabla\cdot B=0,\qquad
\nabla\times E=-\partial_tB,\qquad
\nabla\times B=\mu_0\epsilon_0\partial_tE.
$$

Use complex amplitudes with phase $e^{i(\omega t-k\cdot x)}$ and take real parts at the end. The electric divergence equation gives $k\cdot E_0=0$. Faraday's equation gives $k\times E_0=\omega B_0$, so

$$
\boxed{B(x,t)=\Re\left[\frac{k\times E_0}{\omega}e^{i(\omega t-k\cdot x)}\right].}
$$

The magnetic divergence equation follows because $k\cdot(k\times E_0)=0$. Ampere's equation becomes $k\times B_0=-\mu_0\epsilon_0\omega E_0$. Since $k\times(k\times E_0)=-|k|^2E_0$, a nonzero wave satisfies

$$
\boxed{\omega^2=c^2|k|^2,\qquad c=(\mu_0\epsilon_0)^{-1/2}.}
$$

Thus both [fields](../../../algebra.md#field) are transverse, mutually perpendicular for linear polarization, and obey the vacuum plane-wave dispersion relation.

For the interface let $n=e_z$ point from the minus side to the plus side and write $[E]=E_+-E_-$, similarly for $B$. Applying the integral [Maxwell equations](../../../electromagnetism.md#maxwell-equations) to a thin pillbox and a thin loop gives the [electromagnetic boundary conditions](../../../physics.md#electromagnetic-boundary-condition)

$$
\boxed{n\cdot[E]=\sigma/\epsilon_0,\quad n\times[E]=0,\quad
n\cdot[B]=0,\quad n\times[B]=\mu_0 j.}
$$

Here $j$ is the tangential surface current. The jump notation fixes the signs, including the direction of the normal.

For the [perfect conductor](../../../electromagnetism.md#perfect-conductor), take the time-varying interior [fields](../../../algebra.md#field) to vanish. Choose positive $k$ with $\omega=ck$. The incident [electric field](../../../electromagnetism.md#electric-field) has complex form $E_0e_xe^{i\omega t+ikz}$. To cancel its tangential value at the surface, use the reflected [field](../../../algebra.md#field) $-E_0e_xe^{i\omega t-ikz}$, whose wavevector points upward. The total vacuum [fields](../../../algebra.md#field) are therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
E&=\Re[2iE_0e^{i\omega t}\sin(kz)]e_x,\\
B&=\Re[-(2E_0/c)e^{i\omega t}\cos(kz)]e_y.
\end{aligned}
$$

The sign of each magnetic contribution follows from $B_0=k\times E_0/\omega$. At $z=0$, the [electric field](../../../electromagnetism.md#electric-field) is zero and the [magnetic field](../../../electromagnetism.md#magnetic-field) is tangential. The jump conditions yield

$$
\boxed{\sigma=0,\qquad
j(t)=\frac{2}{\mu_0c}\Re(E_0e^{i\omega t})e_x.}
$$

For a real incident amplitude this is $j=(2E_0/\mu_0c)\cos(\omega t)e_x$. It supplies the reflected wave while satisfying the normal-field and tangential-electric conditions. Any independently imposed static interior [field](../../../algebra.md#field) is separate from this oscillatory response.

<h3 id="18a/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [18A](#18a)

For an [inviscid flow](../../../fluid-mechanics.md#inviscid-flow) of constant density $\rho$, driven by a conservative body force $-\nabla\Phi$, the [Euler equation](../../../fluid-mechanics.md#euler-equations-for-an-inviscid-fluid) is

$$
\partial_tu+(u\cdot\nabla)u=-\nabla(p/\rho)-\nabla\Phi.
$$

The vector identity $(u\cdot\nabla)u=\nabla(|u|^2/2)-u\times(\nabla\times u)$ and irrotationality give the [Unsteady Bernoulli equation](../../../fluid-mechanics.md#unsteady-bernoulli-equation). Specifically, wherever a single-valued potential $u=\nabla\phi$ is available,

$$
\nabla\left(\phi_t+\frac12|u|^2+\frac p\rho+\Phi\right)=0.
$$

On a connected potential-flow region this means

$$
\boxed{\phi_t+\frac12|u|^2+\frac p\rho+\Phi=C(t).}
$$

The integration function is spatially constant but need not be constant in time; it can be removed by changing the potential's time-dependent gauge. A simply connected irrotational region provides the requisite global potential. For a barotropic fluid the [pressure](../../../thermodynamics.md#pressure) term is replaced by $\int dp/\rho(p)$; here the liquid density is constant.

For the tube, let $U(t)$ be its approximately uniform axial [velocity](../../../classical-mechanics.md#velocity). The large reservoir's surface [velocity](../../../classical-mechanics.md#velocity) is negligible and gravity is neglected as stipulated. Entrance-region inertia contributes an effective length of order $a$, negligible against $L$. The potential difference between the outlet and the reservoir is consequently $LU$ to leading order. Subtract Bernoulli at the reservoir surface, where [pressure](../../../thermodynamics.md#pressure) is $P+p_a$, from its value at the outlet, where [pressure](../../../thermodynamics.md#pressure) is $p_a$ and speed is $U$. This gives

$$
L\dot U+\frac12U^2=\frac P\rho,\qquad U(0)=0.
$$

The inertial term cannot be dropped during startup. With $U_\infty=\sqrt{2P/\rho}$, separate variables:

$$
\frac{d(U/U_\infty)}{1-(U/U_\infty)^2}=\frac{U_\infty}{2L}\,dt.
$$

Hence the [inviscid startup in a pressure-driven tube](../../../fluid-mechanics.md#inviscid-startup-in-a-pressure-driven-tube) gives

$$
\boxed{Q(t)=\pi a^2\sqrt{\frac{2P}{\rho}}
\tanh\left(\frac tL\sqrt{\frac P{2\rho}}\right).}
$$

Initially $U\sim Pt/(\rho L)$, the acceleration of the stationary liquid column. At long times $U\to\sqrt{2P/\rho}$, the inviscid pressure-to-kinetic-energy limit. The result applies while the tube remains full and reservoir dimensions are large enough that surface motion and the neglected entrance inertia remain small.

<h4 id="19d/a/solution">Solution</h4>

A full [QR decomposition](../../../linear-algebra.md#qr-decomposition) of a real $m$ by $n$ [matrix](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) with $m\ge n$ writes

$$
A=Q\begin{pmatrix}R\\0\end{pmatrix},
$$

where $Q$ is an orthogonal $m$ by $m$ [matrix](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) and $R$ is upper triangular $n$ by $n$. In the full-column-rank case $R$ is invertible. Equivalently the reduced factorization is $A=Q_1R$ with $Q_1^TQ_1=I_n$. Householder transformations can construct the full factorization.

Let $Q^Tb=\binom{c_1}{c_2}$ with $c_1\in\mathbb R^n$. Since orthogonal transformations preserve the [Euclidean norm](../../../functional-analysis.md#euclidean-norm),

$$
\|Ax-b\|_2^2=\|Rx-c_1\|_2^2+\|c_2\|_2^2.
$$

Therefore the [linear least-squares problem](../../../linear-algebra.md#linear-least-squares-problem) has the full-rank solution

$$
\boxed{Rx^*=c_1,\qquad \min_x\|Ax-b\|_2=\|c_2\|_2.}
$$

Back substitution solves this triangular system without forming $A^TA$. If the [matrix](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) is rank deficient, use a rank-revealing pivoted factorization and minimize over its independent columns; the minimizer need not be unique. The original PDF minimizes over $x\in\mathbb R^n$; the converted TeX's minimization over $b$ is a transcription error.

<h3 id="19d/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [19D](#19d)

<h4 id="19d/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#19d/b)

For a nonzero real vector $v$, its [Householder reflection](../../../linear-algebra.md#householder-transformation) is

$$
\boxed{H=I-2\frac{vv^T}{v^Tv}.}
$$

Let $P=vv^T/(v^Tv)$. Then $P^T=P$ and $P^2=P$, so

$$
H^TH=(I-2P)^2=I-4P+4P^2=I.
$$

Thus **$H$ is an [orthogonal matrix](../../../linear-algebra.md#orthogonal-matrix)**, and it is also symmetric with $H^{-1}=H$. It sends $v$ to $-v$ and fixes every vector perpendicular to $v$, explaining the reflection in the hyperplane $v^\perp$. For a target reduction of a vector $a$ to $\|a\|e_1$, choose $v=a-\|a\|e_1$ when this vector is nonzero; an alternative sign avoids cancellation in numerical implementations.

<h3 id="19d/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [19D](#19d)

<h4 id="19d/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#19d/c)

The first column is $a=(2,1,2)^T$ with length three. Choose $v=a-3e_1=(-1,1,2)^T$, so $v^Tv=6$. Its [Householder reflection](../../../linear-algebra.md#householder-transformation) is

$$
H_1=I-\frac13vv^T=
\begin{pmatrix}2/3&1/3&2/3\\1/3&2/3&-2/3\\2/3&-2/3&-1/3\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Direct multiplication gives

$$
H_1A=\begin{pmatrix}3&3\\0&0\\0&3\end{pmatrix},\qquad
H_1b=\begin{pmatrix}3\\3\\-3\end{pmatrix}.
$$

To eliminate the remaining third-row entry in the second column, reflect the last two coordinates using $v_2=(0,1,-1)^T$. Then

$$
H_2=I-v_2v_2^T=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&0&1\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix},
$$

and

$$
H_2H_1A=\begin{pmatrix}3&3\\0&3\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
H_2H_1b=\begin{pmatrix}3\\-3\\3\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Both steps are [Householder reflections](../../../linear-algebra.md#householder-transformation); the second happens to exchange two coordinates. Solve the leading triangular equations $3x_1+3x_2=3$ and $3x_2=-3$ to obtain

$$
\boxed{x^*=\begin{pmatrix}2\\-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\min_x\|Ax-b\|_2=3.}
$$

For an independent check, $Ax^*-b=(-1,-2,2)^T$, whose squared norm is nine and which is orthogonal to both columns of $A$. Thus $A^T(Ax^*-b)=0$, confirming the least-squares minimizer.

## 20C

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 3](paper-3.md)

<h3 id="20c/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [20C](#20c)

In a [zero-sum game](../../../game-theory.md#zero-sum-game), an entry $a_{ij}$ is the net amount received by the row player when the players choose actions $i,j$; the column player receives its negative. A [mixed strategy](../../../game-theory.md#mixed-strategy) $p$ has nonnegative entries summing to one. Against column strategy $q$, expected row payoff is $p^TAq$. An optimal row strategy maximizes its guaranteed payoff $\min_qp^TAq$; an optimal column strategy minimizes $\max_pp^TAq$.

The betting payoff must subtract the player's own stake: a winner receives both stakes but has already paid its own. Its net gain is the opponent's stake, and a loser loses its own. Ties give zero. Thus the row-payoff [matrix](../../../vector-space.md#matrix) is

$$
\boxed{A=\begin{pmatrix}
0&-1&-1&4\\
1&0&-2&-2\\
1&2&0&-3\\
-4&2&3&0
\end{pmatrix}.}
$$

It is antisymmetric. Interchanging players therefore changes the sign of the game value while leaving the game unchanged, so its value is zero. An optimal column strategy $p$ must prevent any pure row choice from gaining positively, namely $(Ap)_i\le0$. Antisymmetry then also gives $p^TA\ge0$, so the same vector is an optimal row strategy. This is the [support certificate for an antisymmetric matrix game](../../../game-theory.md#support-certificate-for-an-antisymmetric-matrix-game).

Seek a mixture supported on actions $1,3,4$ making those three opposing choices indifferent. Their zero-payoff equations are

$$
-p_3+4p_4=0,\qquad p_1-3p_4=0,\qquad -4p_1+3p_3=0.
$$

With $p_1+p_3+p_4=1$, these give

$$
\boxed{p=\begin{pmatrix}3/8\\0\\1/2\\1/8\end{pmatrix}.}
$$

Check every opponent action, including the omitted second one:

$$
Ap=\begin{pmatrix}0\\-7/8\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}\le0.
$$

This verifies optimality rather than simply assuming that a support choice is valid.

It is also unique. Let $r$ be any other optimal strategy, so $Ar\le0$. For the displayed $p$, antisymmetry gives $p^TAr=-(Ap)^Tr=(7/8)r_2$. The left side is nonpositive and the right side nonnegative, forcing $r_2=0$. Since the other three entries of $p$ are positive, $p^TAr=0$ also forces $(Ar)_1=(Ar)_3=(Ar)_4=0$. Their equations and normalization recover the same probabilities. Thus **both players' unique optimal strategy uses bets one, three and four in the proportions $3:4:1$**.
