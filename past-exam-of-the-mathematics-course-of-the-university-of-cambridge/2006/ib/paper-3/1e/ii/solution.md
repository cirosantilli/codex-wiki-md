<h1 id="1e/ii/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](../ii.md)

Use the standard convention that an [integral domain](../../../../../../integral-domain.md) has $1\ne0$. Since $(n)=(-n)$, the sign of a nonzero $n$ makes no difference. For $|n|=p$ prime, every nonzero residue has an inverse by [Bézout's identity](../../../../../../bezout-identity.md), so the [quotient ring](../../../../../../quotient-ring.md) is a [field](../../../../../../field.md) and hence an [integral domain](../../../../../../integral-domain.md). If $|n|=ab$ is composite with $1<a,b<|n|$, the nonzero classes of $a$ and $b$ multiply to zero, giving [zero divisors](../../../../../../zero-divisor.md). For $n=\pm1$, the quotient is the [zero ring](../../../../../../zero-ring.md), excluded by the convention above. Finally, $n=0$ gives $\mathbb Z/(0)\cong\mathbb Z$, an [integral domain](../../../../../../integral-domain.md). Thus

$$
\boxed{n=0\quad\text{or}\quad |n|\text{ is prime}.}
$$

If a convention restricts this notation to positive moduli, the answer is simply the positive primes.
