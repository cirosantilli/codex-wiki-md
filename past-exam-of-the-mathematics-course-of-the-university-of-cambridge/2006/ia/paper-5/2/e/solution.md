<h1 id="2/e/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [E](../e.md)

**True in the intended comparison with traditional Unix protection mechanisms.** [Windows XP](../../../../../../windows-xp.md) provides object-specific [access control lists](../../../../../../access-control-list.md), subject tokens containing identities and privileges, and a security reference monitor. Rights can distinguish operations on files, registry keys, processes and other protected objects. Traditional [Unix](../../../../../../unix.md) file permissions distinguish read, write and execute for owner, group and everyone else; unrestricted root authority also makes privilege separation coarse. The richer [Windows XP](../../../../../../windows-xp.md) mechanisms can express finer least-privilege policies. Cambridge's [access-control notes](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/teaching/0809/IntroSec/slides-2up.pdf) describe this architectural contrast.

This is an architectural capability comparison, not a claim that every [Windows XP](../../../../../../windows-xp.md) installation resists attacks better than every [Unix](../../../../../../unix.md) installation. More complex authorization is not itself a proof of security; configuration and implementation matter, and [Unix](../../../../../../unix.md) variants can add [access control lists](../../../../../../access-control-list.md) and other protection mechanisms. Without specifying that traditional model, the blanket comparison is underspecified.
