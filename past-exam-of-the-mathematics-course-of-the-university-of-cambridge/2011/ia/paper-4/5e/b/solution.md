<h1 id="5e/b/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [B](../b.md)

**Yes.** A concrete example of [nonunique factorization in a congruence submonoid](../../../../../../nonunique-factorization-in-a-congruence-submonoid.md) is

$$
\boxed{21\cdot221=51\cdot91=4641.}
$$

The four factors are distinct and each is congruent to one modulo ten. The primes $3,7,13,17$ can be checked by trial division by two and three. Their elementary prime decompositions are

$$
21=3\cdot7,\quad221=13\cdot17,\quad51=3\cdot17,\quad91=7\cdot13.
$$

For any one of these products $pq$, where $p,q$ are the indicated distinct [primes](../../../../../../prime-number.md), its positive divisors are $1,p,q,pq$. This divisor assertion does not require unique factorization: if $d\mid pq$ and $p\nmid d$, then $p\mid pq/d$ by the [Euclid lemma](../../../../../../euclid-lemma.md) proved in (a), so $d\mid q$ and $d$ is either one or $q$. If $p\mid d$, write $d=pe$ and obtain $e\mid q$, giving $d=p$ or $pq$.

None of $3,7,13,17$ is congruent to one modulo ten. Consequently no listed product has a proper nonunit divisor in $S$. Thus each is an [irreducible element of a commutative monoid](../../../../../../irreducible-element-of-a-commutative-monoid.md), and the displayed equality proves the claim. Ordinary prime decompositions and irreducibility relative to the smaller [commutative monoid](../../../../../../commutative-monoid.md) $S$ are different notions.
