<h1 id="section-a/6/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [6](../6.md)

For [background-subtracted radiative temperature uncertainty](../../../../../background-subtracted-radiative-temperature-uncertainty.md), assume the two quoted precisions are independent [standard uncertainties](../../../../../standard-uncertainty.md), and treat the radiative proportionality factor as known. Write $P_B=B$ and $P_T=10B$. Then $P_S=9B$, while both absolute input uncertainties equal $0.5B$. Consequently

$$
\sigma_S=\sqrt{(0.05\times10B)^2+(0.50B)^2}=\frac{B}{\sqrt2},\qquad
\frac{\sigma_T}{T}=\frac14\frac{\sigma_S}{P_S}.
$$

Thus **the [temperature](../../../../../temperature-split.md) precision is about $1.96\%$, or $2\%$**. Subtraction amplifies fractional uncertainty even though $P_T$ itself is precise.

Independence is a measurement assumption, not a consequence of the two percentages. For [correlation](../../../../../pearson-correlation-coefficient.md) coefficient $r_c$, the result is $\sigma_T/T=\sqrt{2(1-r_c)}/72$. If the percentages are strict error bounds combined pessimistically, the corresponding bound is $2.78\%$. Unknown calibration [correlations](../../../../../pearson-correlation-coefficient.md) cannot be inferred from the source.
