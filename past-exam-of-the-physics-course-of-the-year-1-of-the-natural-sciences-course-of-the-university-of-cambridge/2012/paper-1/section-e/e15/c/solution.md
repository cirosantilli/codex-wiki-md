<h1 id="section-e/e15/c/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [C](../c.md)

In [electrostatic equilibrium](../../../../../../electrostatic-equilibrium.md) a conductor has $\mathbf E=0$ throughout its bulk and constant [electric potential](../../../../../../electric-potential.md) on each connected component. Otherwise free charge would continue moving. [Gauss's law](../../../../../../gauss-s-law.md) then excludes bulk excess charge; it resides on surfaces. Just outside a conductor the tangential field vanishes and the normal jump is

$$
\boxed{\mathbf E_{\rm out}=\frac{\sigma}{\epsilon_0}\widehat{\mathbf n}}
$$

for a vacuum interface with outward normal $\widehat{\mathbf n}$. Surface charge rearranges in an external field to enforce these [boundary conditions](../../../../../../boundary-condition.md). Greater curvature often concentrates charge, but the actual distribution is a global boundary-value problem.

An empty closed cavity inside a conductor has constant potential and zero field, even in an external electrostatic field. If a charge $q$ is placed in the cavity, a [Gaussian surface](../../../../../../gaussian-surface.md) in the metal shows that the cavity wall carries total induced charge $-q$; its detailed distribution depends on geometry. An initially neutral isolated conductor then has $+q$ on its exterior surface. A grounded conductor can exchange charge with [Earth](../../../../../../earth.md), so this isolation condition no longer applies.

A useful example is a charge $q$ at height $d$ above an infinite grounded plane. In the physical half-space, the [method of images](../../../../../../method-of-images.md) replaces the induced charges by an image $-q$ at height $-d$, giving the attractive [force](../../../../../../force-split.md) $q^2/(16\pi\epsilon_0d^2)$. The image is a mathematical construction, not an actual charge behind the conductor. **Equipotential surfaces and [charge conservation](../../../../../../charge-conservation.md) determine conductor fields together.**
