<h1 id="section-a/2/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [2](../2.md)

The spacecraft clock measures [proper time](../../../../proper-time.md) $\tau$, while a clock at rest with Earth measures the launch-to-emission interval $t$. Neglect Earth's subsequent motion and gravity, as in the intended [special relativity](../../../../special-relativity-split.md) model. The [Lorentz factor](../../../../lorentz-factor.md) and [time dilation](../../../../time-dilation.md) give

$$
\gamma=\frac1{\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}}=\frac3{\sqrt8},\qquad
t=\gamma\tau=\frac3{\sqrt8}(12\,\mathrm h)=12.7279\,\mathrm h.
$$

In Earth's [inertial frame](../../../../inertial-frame.md), the spacecraft's [displacement](../../../../displacement.md) at emission is $d=vt$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{d=\frac{c\tau}{\sqrt8}=4.24264\ \text{light-hours}
\simeq4.58\times10^{12}\,\mathrm m.}
$$

Here $\tau=43200\,\mathrm s$ and the [speed of light](../../../../speed-of-light.md) is $c=2.99792458\times10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. The distance is evaluated at the emission event, using simultaneous Earth-frame endpoints, as required by [relativity of simultaneity](../../../../relativity-of-simultaneity.md); the return signal has not yet reached Earth. A distance assigned on the spacecraft's simultaneous time slice is different: Earth has moved away at $c/3$ for $12\,\mathrm h$ in that frame, giving $c\tau/3=4$ light-hours. Specifying the [reference frame](../../../../reference-frame.md) is essential to the numerical answer.
