<h3 id="section-a/a1">A1</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [Section A](#section-a)

<h4 id="section-a/a1/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A1](#section-a/a1)

For the [gravitational-electrostatic balance of equal charged spheres](../../newton-s-law-of-universal-gravitation.md#gravitational-electrostatic-balance-of-equal-charged-spheres), the [shell theorem](../../mathematical-physics.md#spherical-shell-theorem) and [Coulomb's law](../../electrostatics.md#coulomb-s-law) give $F_g=GM^2/r^2$ and $F_e=q^2/(4\pi\epsilon_0r^2)$ for well-separated spherical particles. Their separation cancels at balance. Since $M=4\pi\rho a^3/3$,

$$
\boxed{a=\left(\frac{9q^2}{64\pi^3\epsilon_0G\rho^2}\right)^{1/6}
=\left[\frac{3|q|}{4\pi\rho\sqrt{4\pi\epsilon_0G}}\right]^{1/3}.}
$$

The two charges have the same sign, so electrostatic repulsion opposes gravitational attraction. A zero charge gives no nontrivial force balance. Spherical symmetry of the external charge distribution, or the leading far-field monopole approximation, is understood.

<h3 id="section-a/a2">A2</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [Section A](#section-a)

<h4 id="section-a/a2/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A2](#section-a/a2)

By symmetry, the two grounded plates take equal induced surface charge and the [electric field](../../electric-field.md) has magnitude $\sigma/(2\epsilon_0)$ between either plate and the sheet. The [grounded-plate potential of a central charged sheet](../../electric-field.md#grounded-plate-potential-of-a-central-charged-sheet) is therefore

$$
V_{\rm sheet}=\frac{\sigma d}{4\epsilon_0}\simeq282\,\mathrm V.
$$

An [electron](../../physics.md#electron) released at zero potential gains [kinetic energy](../../energy.md#kinetic-energy) $eV_{\rm sheet}$. Thus

$$
\boxed{v=\sqrt{\frac{e\sigma d}{2\epsilon_0m_e}}\simeq9.97\times10^6\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.}
$$

The planar infinite-sheet approximation neglects edge effects. Here $e>0$ denotes charge magnitude; the electron's negative charge makes it accelerate toward the positive sheet. Relativistic corrections are below0.1%. Numerical constants use rounded CODATA values, not a claim about the historical handbook: $\epsilon_0=8.85419\times10^{-12}\,\mathrm{F\,m^{-1}}$, $e=1.602176634\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm C$ and $m_e=9.10938\times10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}$. [Vacuum permittivity, elementary charge and electron mass](../../physics-bibliography.md#codata-recommended-values-of-the-fundamental-physical-constants-2022-by-national-institute-of-standards-and-technology)

<h3 id="section-a/a3">A3</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [Section A](#section-a)

<h4 id="section-a/a3/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A3](#section-a/a3)

In the initial [centre-of-mass frame](../../special-relativity.md#center-of-momentum-frame), equal fragments acquire opposite velocities $\pm w$ by [conservation of momentum](../../momentum.md#momentum-conservation). The released energy is

$$
\Delta K=2\left[\frac12\frac m2w^2\right]=\frac12mw^2.
$$

The [equal-mass fragmentation energy budget](../../momentum.md#equal-mass-fragmentation-energy-budget) needs $w=500\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ merely to match the other ship, costing $1.25\,\mathrm{GJ}$. To close an existing gap requires a little more than this. Using the full reserve gives

$$
\boxed{w_{\max}=\sqrt{\frac{2(2\times10^9)}{10^4}}=632\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}},\qquad
v_{\rm front}=2.632\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}.}
$$

**The reserve is sufficient to overtake.** The rear half moves at $1.368\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, preserving momentum. The kinetic-energy increase is the same in any inertial frame because total momentum is unchanged. Subsequent capture or braking would require additional interaction details; no target mass is needed for the interception test.

<h3 id="section-a/a4">A4</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [Section A](#section-a)

<h4 id="section-a/a4/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A4](#section-a/a4)

The PDF's apex angle is $30^\circ$, so each contacted face makes $\alpha=15^\circ$ with the vertical. Let N be the inward [normal force](../../force.md#normal-force) per face. Its vertical component is downward, $-N\sin\alpha$; limiting upward [static friction](../../force.md#static-friction) contributes $\mu N\cos\alpha$. Symmetric contacts cancel horizontal forces and torque. Incipient lift consequently requires

$$
\boxed{N_{\min}=\frac{mg}{2(\mu\cos\alpha-\sin\alpha)}\simeq3.08\,\mathrm N\quad\text{per face}.}
$$

This is the usual **normal gripping force** convention, using $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$. Friction can lift the prism only if $\mu>\tan\alpha$, which is satisfied here.

If “force applied” means the magnitude of the entire finger-contact resultant, including its tangential component, the [normal versus resultant force in a frictional pinch](../../force.md#normal-versus-resultant-force-in-a-frictional-pinch) gives

$$
\boxed{F_{\min}=N_{\min}\sqrt{1+\mu^2}=3.31\,\mathrm N\quad\text{per face}.}
$$

The optimal resultant lies at the limiting [friction angle](../../force.md#friction-angle) $\arctan\mu$ from the inward normal. Stating which force is being measured avoids confusing these two legitimate quantities.

<h3 id="section-b/b7">B7</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [Section B](#section-b)

<h4 id="section-b/b7/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B7](#section-b/b7)

For a steady uniform jet, the mass flow rate is $\dot m=\rho Au$. The transported [momentum flux](../../mathematical-physics.md#momentum-flux) and [kinetic energy flux of a uniform jet](../../mathematical-physics.md#kinetic-energy-flux-of-a-uniform-jet) are

$$
\boxed{\dot p=\rho Au^2,\qquad\dot K=\frac12\rho Au^3.}
$$

Incompressibility gives piston speed $v_p=Au/S$. Equating piston power $Fv_p$ to the jet's kinetic power gives

$$
\boxed{F=\frac12\rho Su^2.}
$$

This ideal result neglects losses and the kinetic energy of fluid moving slowly inside the large cylinder. Retaining an inlet speed correction would multiply the pressure expression by $1-(A/S)^2$.

A horizontal impact occurs at the projectile apex. Write $u_x=u\cos\theta$ and $u_y=u\sin\theta$. At its height h, $u_y^2=2gh$ and the flight time is $t=u_y/g$, so

$$
u_y=\sqrt{2gh},\qquad u_x=w\sqrt{\frac g{2h}},\qquad
\boxed{u=9.90\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}},\quad\theta=45^\circ,\quad F=4.91\,\mathrm N.}
$$

Here $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$ and $u_x=u_y=7.00\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. The [apex-target condition for a ballistic jet](../../classical-mechanics.md#apex-target-condition-for-a-ballistic-jet) fixes both launch speed and angle.

If the target stops the horizontal momentum of the arriving water, the force is

$$
F_{\rm hit}=\dot m\,u_x=\rho A u u_x=0.277\,\mathrm N,
\qquad \mu m_tg=0.147\,\mathrm N.
$$

**This exceeds the limiting friction, so the target can be dislodged** in the ideal stopping-impact model. The [jet-area adjustment during ballistic slowing](../../classical-mechanics.md#jet-area-adjustment-during-ballistic-slowing) matters: the nozzle area sets $\dot m$; replacing u by $u_x$ in that mass-flow factor would incorrectly assume unchanged jet area during deceleration. Reflection or a specified deflection would change the transferred momentum. Air drag, breakup and splash losses are excluded from the ballistic estimate.

<h3 id="section-b/b8">B8</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [Section B](#section-b)

<h4 id="section-b/b8/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B8](#section-b/b8)

The [moment of inertia](../../rigid-body-dynamics.md#moment-of-inertia) about an axis is $I=\int r_\perp^2\,dm$. The [parallel axis theorem](../../rigid-body-dynamics.md#parallel-axis-theorem) states $I=I_{\rm CM}+Ma^2$, where a is the perpendicular separation from a parallel axis through the centre of mass, and M is total mass.

Since $\rho=kr^2$, the density coefficient has units $\mathrm{kg\,m^{-5}}$. The [density-power cylindrical moment of inertia](../../rigid-body-dynamics.md#density-power-cylindrical-moment-of-inertia) follows from cylindrical shells:

$$
M=\int_0^R2\pi Lkr^3\,dr=\frac{\pi kLR^4}{2},\qquad
I_{\rm CM}=\int_0^R2\pi Lkr^5\,dr=\frac{\pi kLR^6}{3}
=\boxed{\frac23MR^2}.
$$

For an offset pivot, the [physical pendulum](../../mathematical-physics.md#physical-pendulum) equation is $(I_{\rm CM}+Ma^2)\ddot\theta=-Mga\sin\theta$. At small amplitude,

$$
\boxed{T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{a^2+2R^2/3}{ga}}.}
$$

For the [minimum-period offset of a physical pendulum](../../mathematical-physics.md#minimum-period-offset-of-a-physical-pendulum), minimizing $T^2$ is equivalent to minimizing $a+2R^2/(3a)$. Its derivative vanishes at

$$
\boxed{a_{\min}=R\sqrt{2/3},\qquad
T_{\min}=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{2R\sqrt{2/3}}g}.}
$$

The second derivative is positive. The period diverges as $a^{-1/2}$ near zero and grows as $a^{1/2}$ for large offset, giving one minimum. This assumes the stated ideal pivot can pass inside the body; an externally constrained pivot would restrict the allowable a. [§15.4, physical-pendulum torque equation and period](../../physics-bibliography.md#university-physics-volume-1-by-william-moebs-and-others)

↑ **Parent:** [B9](#section-b/b9)

For the stated standard configuration, the [Lorentz transformation](../../special-relativity.md#lorentz-transformation) is

$$
\boxed{t'=\gamma(t-vx/c^2),\quad x'=\gamma(x-vt),\quad y'=y,\quad z'=z,\quad
\gamma=(1-v^2/c^2)^{-1/2}.}
$$

Differentiate along the moving object's worldline and divide $dx'$ by $dt'$ to obtain the [velocity-addition formula](../../special-relativity.md#velocity-addition-formula):

$$
\boxed{u_x'=\frac{u_x-v}{1-u_xv/c^2}.}
$$

For a receding source, two emissions separated by proper time $1/f'$ occur $\gamma/f'$ apart in S. The second signal travels an additional distance $v\gamma/f'$, so its reception interval is $\gamma(1+v/c)/f'$. Hence the longitudinal [relativistic Doppler effect](../../wave.md#relativistic-doppler-effect) is

$$
\boxed{f=\frac{f'}{\gamma(1+v/c)}=f'\sqrt{\frac{1-v/c}{1+v/c}}.}
$$

The inverse velocity transformation for the forward throw gives

$$
\boxed{u=\frac{c/2+c/7}{1+1/14}=\frac35c.}
$$

The ball approaches the stationary receiver, so its forward light is blueshifted. The received wavelength is $400\sqrt{(1-3/5)/(1+3/5)}=\boxed{200\,\mathrm{nm}}$, in the [ultraviolet](../../optics.md#ultraviolet). **The stated monochromatic emission is outside the visible band.** Other reflected or emitted frequencies have not been specified. <h4 id="section-c/c10/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C10](#section-c/c10)

For sinusoidal steady state with the $e^{i\omega t}$ convention, [complex impedance](../../electrical-circuit.md#electrical-impedance) is the phasor ratio $Z=\widetilde V/\widetilde I$, encoding both amplitude ratio and phase. From $V=L\,dI/dt$, an ideal inductor has $Z_L=i\omega L$; from $I=C\,dV/dt$, a capacitor has $Z_C=(i\omega C)^{-1}$.

The original diagram has $R_1,L_1$ in series, and $R_4,C_4$ in parallel. Thus

$$
Z_1=R_1+i\omega L_1,\qquad Z_4=\frac{R_4}{1+i\omega R_4C_4}.
$$

With the right supply node as the reference, the two unloaded [voltage divider](../../electrical-engineering.md#voltage-divider) outputs are $\widetilde V_A=V_0R_2/(Z_1+R_2)$ and $\widetilde V_B=V_0Z_4/(R_3+Z_4)$. Subtracting gives

$$
\boxed{V_{AB}=V_0\left[\frac{R_2}{Z_1+R_2}-\frac{Z_4}{R_3+Z_4}\right].}
$$

The [series-RL parallel-RC bridge balance](../../electrical-engineering.md#series-rl-parallel-rc-bridge-balance) is $Z_1Z_4=R_2R_3$. Equating its real and imaginary coefficients gives

$$
\boxed{R_1=\frac{R_2R_3}{R_4}=25\,\Omega,\qquad
L_1=R_2R_3C_4=0.250\,\mathrm H.}
$$

These ideal components balance the bridge at any frequency, not just the test frequency.

At $\omega=200\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$, the current amplitude through $R_2$ is $5/|50+50i|=0.0707\,\mathrm A$. Using peak rather than RMS supply amplitude,

$$
\boxed{\overline P_{R_2}=\frac12|\widetilde I|^2R_2=0.0625\,\mathrm W.}
$$

In the DC steady state the inductor is a short and the capacitor is open. Both dividers have equal resistors, so each midpoint is at2.5V and the bridge stays balanced. The upper-branch current is0.100A, giving $\boxed{P_{R_2,\rm DC}=0.250\,\mathrm W}$.

For the high-input-resistance voltmeter, hold the other components fixed. At the DC balance,

$$
\frac{dV_{AB}}{dR_1}=-\frac{V_0R_2}{(R_1+R_2)^2}=-0.0500\,\mathrm{V\,\Omega^{-1}},\qquad
\boxed{\delta R_1\simeq\frac{0.001}{0.0500}=0.020\,\Omega.}
$$

The [DC bridge resistance resolution](../../electrical-engineering.md#dc-bridge-resistance-resolution) is a local readout resolution, or0.08% of $R_1$, not a total uncertainty allowing for supply, component calibration or voltmeter loading. [§15.2, resistor/inductor/capacitor phasors and peak/RMS distinction](../../physics-bibliography.md#university-physics-volume-2-by-samuel-j-ling-and-others)

## Section D

<h3 id="section-d/d12">D12</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [Section D](#section-d)

<h4 id="section-d/d12/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D12](#section-d/d12)

For a small-slope string element, the transverse net [tension](../../force.md#tension) is $T[y_x(x+\delta x)-y_x(x)]\simeq Ty_{xx}\delta x$. Its mass is $\rho\delta x$, so [Newton's second law](../../force.md#newton-s-second-law) gives

$$
\boxed{y_{tt}=\frac T\rho y_{xx},\qquad c^2=T/\rho.}
$$

Its extra length is $[\sqrt{1+y_x^2}-1]\delta x\simeq y_x^2\delta x/2$. Work against the approximately constant tension gives the [elastic energy of a small-slope string](../../wave-equation.md#elastic-energy-of-a-small-slope-string), while transverse motion gives its kinetic energy:

$$
\boxed{dU=\frac T2y_x^2\,\delta x,\qquad dK=\frac\rho2y_t^2\,\delta x.}
$$

For a single travelling wave $y=f(x\pm ct)$, $y_t^2=c^2y_x^2$, so $dK=dU$ pointwise to this order. A standing wave need not have pointwise or instantaneous kinetic–potential equality.

For a fixed-end string of length L, taking the stated note to be its fundamental gives $c=2Lf$. Hence

$$
\boxed{T=\rho(2Lf)^2=16.5\,\mathrm N.}
$$

If98Hz were instead the nth harmonic, the required tension would be $16.5/n^2\,\mathrm N$; the usual fundamental interpretation is explicit.

For the specified standing wave, $\dot y=A\omega\sin kx\cos\omega t$. Every allowed nonzero fixed-end mode has $\int_0^L\sin^2kx\,dx=L/2$, yielding

$$
\boxed{K_{\max}=\frac14\rho LA^2\omega^2=4.25\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm J,\qquad\omega=2\pi(98\,\mathrm{Hz}).}
$$

At this instant the ideal mode's elastic energy is zero; at maximum displacement all its energy is elastic. [Chapter16, transverse-string wave speed, wave energy and fixed-end normal modes](../../physics-bibliography.md#university-physics-volume-1-by-william-moebs-and-others)

<h3 id="section-d/d13">D13</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [Section D](#section-d)

<h4 id="section-d/d13/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D13](#section-d/d13)

In the time-independent [Schrödinger equation](../../mathematical-physics.md#schrodinger-equation), $-\hbar^2\psi''/(2m)$ is the kinetic-energy operator acting on the state, $V\psi$ its potential-energy contribution and $E\psi$ the energy-eigenvalue term. The complex [wave function](../../wave-function.md) is a probability amplitude: the [Born rule](../../wave-function.md#born-rule) assigns density $|\psi|^2$ for a normalized single-particle state. A plane-wave beam uses an incident-amplitude or flux normalization instead of a unit-integral wavefunction.

For a free plane wave, $\psi''=-k^2\psi$, so $\boxed{E=\hbar^2k^2/(2m)}$ and its time factor obeys $\hbar\omega=E$. Above the upward [potential step](../../quantum-scattering.md#step-potential), the bounded subthreshold solution has decay constant

$$
\boxed{\alpha=\frac{\sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}}\hbar.}
$$

For the PDF's unit incident amplitude, write $\psi=e^{ikx}+Re^{-ikx}$ on the left and $\psi=Te^{-\alpha x}$ on the right, omitting their common time phase. Continuity of both $\psi$ and $\psi'$ at this finite step gives

$$
1+R=T,\qquad ik(1-R)=-\alpha T,\qquad
R=\frac{k-i\alpha}{k+i\alpha},\quad T=\frac{2k}{k+i\alpha}.
$$

Consequently

$$
\boxed{|R|^2=1,\qquad |T|^2=\frac{4k^2}{k^2+\alpha^2}=\frac{4E}{V_0}.}
$$

The [evanescent amplitude versus transmitted current](../../quantum-scattering.md#evanescent-amplitude-versus-transmitted-current) distinction matters: the second quantity is a squared amplitude, **not a transmitted-flux probability**. The decaying tail has zero [probability current](../../wave-function.md#probability-current), despite nonzero density and the possible value $|T|^2>1$.

Using $\hbar=1.05457\times10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}$ and the electron mass and elementary charge specified above, the energy difference1eV gives $\alpha=5.12\,\mathrm{nm^{-1}}$. Thus at the stated position

$$
\boxed{|\psi_t(1\,\mathrm{nm})|^2=\frac83e^{-2(5.123)(1)}\simeq9.46\times10^{-5}.}
$$

This is relative to the unit incident density in the source figure, not an absolute normalized probability per metre. [Reduced Planck constant, electron mass and electron volt conversion](../../physics-bibliography.md#codata-recommended-values-of-the-fundamental-physical-constants-2022-by-national-institute-of-standards-and-technology)

<h3 id="section-e/e14">E14</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [Section E](#section-e)

<h4 id="section-e/e14/a">a</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [E14](#section-e/e14)

<h5 id="section-e/e14/a/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#section-e/e14/a)

[Gauss's law](../../maxwell-equations.md#gauss-s-law) states

$$
\boxed{\oint_{\partial V}\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf A=\frac{Q_{\rm enc}}{\epsilon_0},\qquad
\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\rho_e/\epsilon_0.}
$$

The surface is closed and oriented outward, and $Q_{\rm enc}$ includes all enclosed charge. The integral law follows from the local form through the [divergence theorem](../../multivariable-calculus.md#divergence-theorem). It is universally valid in classical electromagnetism, but calculating the field directly from the flux requires enough symmetry.

For spherical charge symmetry, $E(r)=Q(<r)/(4\pi\epsilon_0r^2)$. A uniform infinite line gives $E=\lambda/(2\pi\epsilon_0r)$ from a coaxial cylinder. An infinite sheet gives $E=\sigma/(2\epsilon_0)$ on either side from a pillbox, with the direction set by charge sign. The normal field jump is $\sigma/\epsilon_0$; the electrostatic field inside a conductor is zero. Zero enclosed charge implies zero net flux, not generally zero field. [Chapter6, Gaussian surfaces and symmetry applications](../../physics-bibliography.md#university-physics-volume-2-by-samuel-j-ling-and-others)

<h4 id="section-e/e14/b">b</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [E14](#section-e/e14)

<h5 id="section-e/e14/b/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#section-e/e14/b)

For a steady thin-wire current in vacuum, the [Biot-Savart law](../../electromagnetism.md#biot-savart-law) is

$$
\boxed{\mathbf B(\mathbf r)=\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi}\int\frac{d\boldsymbol\ell'\times(\mathbf r-\mathbf r')}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|^3}.}
$$

The line element follows conventional current direction. More generally replace $I\,d\boldsymbol\ell'$ with $\mathbf J(\mathbf r')d^3r'$. The cross product gives the circulating field direction and superposition allows contributions from separate wire segments to be added.

Integration gives $B=\mu_0I/(2\pi r)$ around a long straight wire. On the axis of a circular loop of radius a, $B_z=\mu_0Ia^2/[2(a^2+z^2)^{3/2}]$, including the centre value $\mu_0I/(2a)$. Coil turns multiply the field when their geometries coincide; distributed coils and solenoids follow by integration. This is a magnetostatic or quasistatic calculation: rapidly changing currents require retarded electrodynamics and appropriate displacement-current effects, not an instantaneous wire formula. [Chapter12, steady-current fields and Biot–Savart applications](../../physics-bibliography.md#university-physics-volume-2-by-samuel-j-ling-and-others)

<h4 id="section-e/e14/c">c</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [E14](#section-e/e14)

<h5 id="section-e/e14/c/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#section-e/e14/c)

For a stationary closed circuit, [Faraday's law of induction](../../maxwell-equations.md#faraday-s-law-of-induction) is

$$
\boxed{\mathcal E=\oint\mathbf E\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell=-\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt},\qquad
\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t}.}
$$

The loop orientation and magnetic-flux normal obey the right-hand convention. For a moving material circuit, the emf is instead $\oint(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell$, with the same negative derivative of the flux through the moving circuit. The additional magnetic force term accounts for motional emf.

[Lenz's law](../../maxwell-equations.md#lenz-s-law) expresses the minus sign physically: an induced current opposes the change in flux that produces it. It does not always oppose the field itself. Increasing positive flux drives a current producing negative flux; decreasing it reverses that response. A straight conducting rod of length l moving across a perpendicular uniform field has emf magnitude Blv. A resistive return circuit then develops a magnetic braking force, and the supplied mechanical power equals Joule heating in the ideal steady limit. This connects induction with [conservation of energy](../../mathematical-physics.md#conservation-of-energy). [Chapter13, Faraday induction, Lenz direction and motional emf](../../physics-bibliography.md#university-physics-volume-2-by-samuel-j-ling-and-others)

<h4 id="section-e/e14/d">d</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [E14](#section-e/e14)

<h5 id="section-e/e14/d/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#section-e/e14/d)

A [conservative force](../../energy.md#conservative-force) has work depending only on the endpoints, so it can be written

$$
\boxed{\mathbf F=-\nabla U,\qquad W_{A\to B}=U(A)-U(B),\qquad
\oint\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=0.}
$$

The arbitrary additive constant in [potential energy](../../energy.md#potential-energy) has no effect on force. A smooth curl-free force is conservative on a simply connected domain; a hole or excluded singularity requires checking global circulation as well.

Uniform gravity has $U=mgz$, central gravity has $U=-GMm/r$ with zero at infinity, and a linear spring has $U=kx^2/2$ relative to equilibrium. If the potential is time independent and no other force does work, $K+U$ is conserved by the [work-energy theorem](../../energy.md#work-energy-theorem). A time-dependent potential or nonconservative work can change mechanical energy; having an instantaneous gradient force alone is not sufficient for energy conservation. [Chapter8, conservative forces, potential energy and mechanical-energy conservation](../../physics-bibliography.md#university-physics-volume-1-by-william-moebs-and-others)

<h3 id="section-e/e15">E15</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [Section E](#section-e)

<h4 id="section-e/e15/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [E15](#section-e/e15)

Treat Earth as a stationary spherical source, with $\mu=GM_E$, and neglect drag and other bodies. The conserved quantities are total mechanical energy and the [angular momentum](../../momentum.md#angular-momentum) vector, implying a fixed orbital plane and constant areal velocity. The Kepler problem additionally conserves the [Laplace-Runge-Lenz vector](../../classical-mechanics.md#laplace-runge-lenz-vector). The orbiting object's linear momentum is not constant because gravity exerts a force.

For a circular orbit, $mv_c^2/r_0=\mu m/r_0^2$, so $K=\mu m/(2r_0)=-U/2$. Hence

$$
\boxed{E_0=K+U=-\frac{\mu m}{2r_0}.}
$$

At the closest approach of a noncircular orbit, the velocity is tangential and $v_0=k\sqrt{\mu/r_0}$. Its energy and specific angular momentum are

$$
\boxed{E=\frac{\mu m}{2r_0}(k^2-2)=E_0(2-k^2),\qquad
h=r_0v_0=k\sqrt{\mu r_0}.}
$$

The [pericentre-speed qualification of Kepler orbit classes](../../celestial-mechanics.md#pericentre-speed-qualification-of-kepler-orbit-classes) requires $k\ge1$ when $r_0$ is genuinely the closest approach. For an ellipse, conservation of h gives $v_m=v_0r_0/r_m$. Equating energies at the two turning points and writing $x=r_m/r_0$ gives

$$
k^2-2=\frac{k^2}{x^2}-\frac2x,\qquad
(x-1)[(k^2-2)x+k^2]=0.
$$

The distinct apocentre root is therefore

$$
\boxed{\frac{r_m}{r_0}=\frac{k^2}{2-k^2},\qquad 1<k<\sqrt2.}
$$

For an unbound orbit, $v^2(r)=2E/m+2\mu/r$ decreases toward infinity. The [asymptotic minimum speed of an unbound Kepler orbit](../../celestial-mechanics.md#asymptotic-minimum-speed-of-an-unbound-kepler-orbit) is

$$
\boxed{v_\infty=\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{r_0}(k^2-2)}.}
$$

It is the limiting infimum, reached only asymptotically: positive for a hyperbola and zero for a parabola. The turning-point interpretation of $r_0$ is essential; if $0<k<1$, the same instantaneous energy describes a bound orbit whose $r_0$ is the apocentre instead. [Chapter13, gravitational energy, escape and Kepler orbits](../../physics-bibliography.md#university-physics-volume-1-by-william-moebs-and-others)

The orbit is **circular for $\boxed{k=1}$**. The speed equals $\sqrt{GM_E/r_0}$ and radial acceleration balances the changing direction of the velocity.

<h4 id="section-e/e15/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [E15](#section-e/e15)

A noncircular **ellipse with $r_0$ at pericentre has $\boxed{1<k<\sqrt2}$**. Its energy is negative. Merely specifying $k<1$ would make the stated closest-approach point an apocentre, so it is excluded by the geometry; the circular case is listed separately.
