# Bijection

↑ **Parent:** [Function](function-split.md)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bijection)

A [bijection](bijection.md) $f:X\to Y$ is a [function](function-split.md) that is both [injective](injective-function.md) and [surjective](surjective-function.md): each element of its [codomain](codomain.md) is the output of exactly one element of its [domain of a function](domain-of-a-function.md). The two requirements exclude unused targets and merged inputs respectively. The finite example $f(A)=1$, $f(B)=2$, $f(C)=3$ is a [bijection](bijection.md) from $\{A,B,C\}$ to $\{1,2,3\}$, whereas adding an unused target $4$ makes that same rule fail [surjectivity](surjective-function.md).

A [bijection](bijection.md) gives an [inverse function](inverse-function.md) by reversing every pairing. Conversely, a two-sided [inverse function](inverse-function.md) implies a [bijection](bijection.md), as the proof under [inverse function](inverse-function.md) shows. This is useful for transporting structures without losing or identifying elements. It also defines equality of [cardinality](cardinality.md): two [sets](set-split.md) have equal [cardinality](cardinality.md) exactly when a [bijection](bijection.md) pairs them. An infinite example is $n\mapsto2n$ from the [integers](integer.md) to the even [integers](integer.md), with inverse $m\mapsto m/2$ on that specified [codomain](codomain.md). A proper [subset](subset.md) can therefore have the same [cardinality](cardinality.md) as an infinite [set](set-split.md). [Levin, functions: injections, surjections and bijections](https://discrete.openmathbooks.org/preview/sec_intro-functions.html).
