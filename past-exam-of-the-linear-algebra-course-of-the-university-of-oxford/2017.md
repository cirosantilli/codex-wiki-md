<h3 id="1/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1/a)

<h5 id="1/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#1/a/i)

For a [linear transformation](../vector-space.md#linear-map) $T$ of a [finite-dimensional vector space](../vector-space.md#finite-dimensional-vector-space) over a [field](../algebra.md#field) $F$, its [minimal polynomial](../linear-operator-theory.md#minimal-polynomial) $m_T$ is the unique [monic polynomial](../polynomial.md#monic-polynomial) of least degree satisfying $m_T(T)=0$. There is at least one nonzero [polynomial](../polynomial.md) with this property because the powers $I,T,T^2,\ldots$ lie in the finite-dimensional [vector space](../vector-space.md) of linear maps $V\to V$. Dividing any other such [polynomial](../polynomial.md) by $m_T$ leaves a remainder of smaller degree that also vanishes at $T$, so that remainder must be zero. This also proves uniqueness.

If $C$ represents $T$ in any [basis](../vector-space.md#basis), the paper's [characteristic polynomial](../linear-operator-theory.md#characteristic-polynomial) is

$$
\boxed{\chi_T(x)=\det(C-xI).}
$$

It is independent of the [basis](../vector-space.md#basis), since changing the [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) replaces $C-xI$ by a [similar matrix](../linear-algebra.md#matrix-similarity), preserving its [determinant](../multilinear-algebra.md#determinant). Its degree is $\dim V$ and its leading coefficient is $(-1)^{\dim V}$; $m_T$ is always [monic](../polynomial.md#monic-polynomial).

<h4 id="1/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1/a)

<h5 id="1/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#1/a/ii)

The [Cayley-Hamilton theorem](../cayley-hamilton-theorem.md) states

$$
\boxed{\chi_T(T)=0.}
$$

Let $d=\dim V$. Extend the [field](../algebra.md#field) to an [algebraically closed field](../algebra.md#algebraically-closed-field) and choose a [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) in which the [matrix](../vector-space.md#matrix) of $T$ is an [upper triangular matrix](../linear-algebra.md#upper-triangular-matrix), as permitted. Write its diagonal entries as $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_d$ and put $V_i=\operatorname{span}(e_1,\ldots,e_i)$. Each $V_i$ is an [invariant subspace](../representation-theory.md#invariant-subspace), and

$$
(T-\lambda_iI)V_i\subseteq V_{i-1}.
$$

Apply $T-\lambda_dI$ first, then $T-\lambda_{d-1}I$, and continue. The resulting product sends $V_d$ into $V_0=\{0\}$. These factors are [commuting operators](../vector-space.md#commuting-operators), so

$$
\prod_{i=1}^d(T-\lambda_iI)=0.
$$

The [determinant](../multilinear-algebra.md#determinant) of an [upper triangular matrix](../linear-algebra.md#upper-triangular-matrix) gives $\chi_T(x)=(-1)^d\prod_i(x-\lambda_i)$. Thus $\chi_T(T)=0$ after the [field extension](../algebra.md#field-extension). Its entries were in the original [field](../algebra.md#field), whose embedding is injective, so they were already zero there. The zero-dimensional case is immediate as well.

<h3 id="1/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

<h5 id="1/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#1/b/i)

The displayed [matrix](../vector-space.md#matrix) is the transpose convention for a [companion matrix](../linear-operator-theory.md#companion-matrix). Expanding $\det(xI-A)$ along its last row gives

$$
\det(xI-A)=x^n+\alpha_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+\alpha_1x+\alpha_0=f(x).
$$

Indeed, the last entry contributes $(x+\alpha_{n-1})x^{n-1}$ and the entry in column $j<n$ contributes $\alpha_{j-1}x^{j-1}$; the cofactor signs cancel those from the superdiagonal entries $-1$. Consequently

$$
\boxed{\chi_T(x)=(-1)^nf(x).}
$$

The [Cayley-Hamilton theorem](../cayley-hamilton-theorem.md) gives $f(A)=0$, so $m_T$ divides $f$. To exclude a smaller [minimal polynomial](../linear-operator-theory.md#minimal-polynomial), let $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ be the [standard basis](../vector-space.md#standard-basis). The first $n-1$ rows of $A$ show

$$
e_1^{\mathsf T}A^j=e_{j+1}^{\mathsf T}\qquad(0\leq j<n).
$$

If $\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}c_jA^j=0$, multiplying on the left by $e_1^{\mathsf T}$ gives $\sum_jc_je_{j+1}^{\mathsf T}=0$, so every coefficient is zero. No nonzero [polynomial](../polynomial.md) of degree below $n$ vanishes at $A$. Since $f$ and $m_T$ are [monic polynomials](../polynomial.md#monic-polynomial),

$$
\boxed{m_T=f.}
$$

<h4 id="1/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

<h5 id="1/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#1/b/ii)

Write $B=D+K$, where $D$ has $A$ in each diagonal block and zero elsewhere, while $K$ has $I$ immediately above the diagonal and zero elsewhere. These [block matrices](../vector-space.md#block-matrix) satisfy $DK=KD$: either product has $A$ immediately above the diagonal. Also, $K^i$ has $I$ on its $i$th superdiagonal and is zero elsewhere.

The [binomial theorem for commuting matrices](../binomial-coefficient.md#binomial-theorem-for-commuting-matrices) therefore yields

$$
B^j=\sum_{r=0}^j\binom jrD^{j-r}K^r.
$$

Only $r=i$ can contribute to block $(k,k+i)$, provided that block lies in the [matrix](../vector-space.md#matrix). Hence

$$
\boxed{(B^j)_{k,k+i}=\begin{cases}
0,&j<i,\\
\binom ji A^{j-i},&j\geq i.
\end{cases}}
$$

Thus $\beta_{i,j}=\binom ji$, interpreted as an element of the [field](../algebra.md#field). The formula remains valid in positive [field characteristic](../algebra.md#characteristic-of-a-field): some of these coefficients can then vanish. This possibility matters in the next part.

<h4 id="1/b/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

<h5 id="1/b/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#1/b/iii)

Write $f=m_T$. Part (ii) identifies the first superdiagonal block exactly:

$$
\boxed{\bigl(f(B)\bigr)_{k,k+1}=f'(A),}
$$

where $f'$ is the [formal derivative](../galois-theory.md#formal-derivative). Thus the candidate is $g=f'$. If the [irreducible polynomial](../polynomial.md#irreducible-polynomial) $f$ has $f'\ne0$, its [polynomial degree](../polynomial.md#degree-of-a-polynomial) makes $f$ and $f'$ coprime. Equivalently, $f$ is a [separable polynomial](../galois-theory.md#separable-polynomial). This includes every irreducible [polynomial](../polynomial.md) over a [field](../algebra.md#field) of characteristic zero, and every irreducible [polynomial](../polynomial.md) over a [perfect field](../algebra.md#perfect-field).

Under this separability hypothesis, [Bézout's identity for polynomials](../polynomial.md#bezout-s-identity-for-polynomials) gives $a(x)f(x)+b(x)f'(x)=1$. Evaluating at $A$ proves that $C=f'(A)$ is an [invertible matrix](../linear-algebra.md#invertible-matrix). Moreover, $f(B)$ has zero diagonal blocks, because $f(A)=0$. It is therefore a [nilpotent matrix](../linear-operator-theory.md#nilpotent-matrix) with $f(B)^N=0$. The only product of $N-1$ strictly upward block steps that reaches block $(1,N)$ uses the first superdiagonal every time, so

$$
\bigl(f(B)^{N-1}\bigr)_{1,N}=C^{N-1}\ne0.
$$

For $N=1$, the corresponding assertion is simply $f(B)^0=I\ne0$. The [block upper triangular matrix](../linear-algebra.md#block-upper-triangular-matrix) $B$ has

$$
\chi_U(x)=\det(B-xI)=\det(A-xI)^N=(-1)^{nN}f(x)^N.
$$

The [minimal polynomial](../linear-operator-theory.md#minimal-polynomial) divides this [characteristic polynomial](../linear-operator-theory.md#characteristic-polynomial). Since $f$ is an [irreducible polynomial](../polynomial.md#irreducible-polynomial), it must be a power of $f$; the exact [nilpotency index](../linear-operator-theory.md#nilpotency-index) just found forces

$$
\boxed{m_U=f^N,\qquad \chi_U=(-1)^{nN}f^N\quad\text{if }f\text{ is separable}.}
$$

**The arbitrary-field qualification is necessary.** In the [rational function field](../algebra.md#rational-function-field) $F=\mathbb F_2(t)$, take $f(x)=x^2-t$. This is an [irreducible polynomial](../polynomial.md#irreducible-polynomial): a square of a rational function has even order of vanishing at $t=0$, while $t$ has odd order. Its [formal derivative](../galois-theory.md#formal-derivative) is zero. With $N=2$,

$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\t&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
B=\begin{pmatrix}A&I\\0&A\end{pmatrix},\qquad
B^2=tI_4.
$$

The off-diagonal block in $f(B)$ is zero, and $m_U=f$, rather than $f^2$. No alternative [polynomial](../polynomial.md) $g$ coprime to $f$ could represent that block: $g(A)=0$ would imply $m_T=f\mid g$. Thus the printed coprimality claim fails as stated over a general [field](../algebra.md#field).

There is also a precise general replacement. In characteristic $p>0$, let $q=p^r$ be the largest power for which $f(x)=h(x^q)$, so that $h'\ne0$; set $q=1$ in characteristic zero. Irreducibility of $f$ implies that $h$ is an [irreducible polynomial](../polynomial.md#irreducible-polynomial), so $h$ and $h'$ are coprime. The [binomial theorem](../binomial-coefficient.md#binomial-theorem) in characteristic $p$ gives $B^q=D^q+K^q$. Expanding $h(D^q+K^q)$, with the coefficients equivalently supplied by the [Hasse derivatives](../polynomial.md#hasse-derivative), gives

$$
f(B)=K^qH,
$$

where $H$ commutes with $K$ and is a [block upper triangular matrix](../linear-algebra.md#block-upper-triangular-matrix) whose diagonal blocks are $h'(A^q)$. These blocks are invertible by [Bézout's identity for polynomials](../polynomial.md#bezout-s-identity-for-polynomials), since $h(A^q)=f(A)=0$. Thus $H$ is invertible, and

$$
f(B)^s=K^{qs}H^s=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad qs\geq N.
$$

The same [characteristic polynomial](../linear-operator-theory.md#characteristic-polynomial) and divisibility argument now prove the corrected arbitrary-field answer:

$$
\boxed{m_U(x)=f(x)^{\lceil N/q\rceil},\qquad
\chi_U(x)=(-1)^{nN}f(x)^N.}
$$

This is the [minimal polynomial of a companion block chain](../linear-operator-theory.md#minimal-polynomial-of-a-companion-block-chain); $q=1$ recovers the intended separable case.

<h5 id="2/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#2/a/i)

The [dual space](../dual-space.md) is

$$
V'=\operatorname{Hom}_F(V,F),
$$

the [vector space](../vector-space.md) of [linear functionals](../dual-space.md#linear-functional), with pointwise operations

$$
(\phi+\psi)(v)=\phi(v)+\psi(v),\qquad
(a\phi)(v)=a\phi(v).
$$

Given the [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) $e_1,\ldots,e_n$, its [dual basis](../dual-space.md#dual-basis) consists of the coordinate [linear functionals](../dual-space.md#linear-functional)

$$
\boxed{f_i\!\left(\sum_{j=1}^na_je_j\right)=a_i,
\qquad f_i(e_j)=\delta_{ij}.}
$$

In particular, $\dim V'=\dim V=n$.

<h4 id="2/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#2/a)

<h5 id="2/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#2/a/ii)

The [dual map](../dual-space.md#transpose-of-a-linear-map) acts by [function composition](../function.md#function-composition):

$$
\boxed{T':W'\longrightarrow V',\qquad T'(\phi)=\phi\circ T.}
$$

The composition is a [linear functional](../dual-space.md#linear-functional) on $V$, and pointwise operations give $T'(a\phi+b\psi)=aT'\phi+bT'\psi$. Thus this really is a [linear transformation](../vector-space.md#linear-map); its direction reverses that of $T$.

<h4 id="2/a/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#2/a)

<h5 id="2/a/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#2/a/iii)

For $\phi\in V'$ and $v\in V$,

$$
(I'\phi)(v)=\phi(Iv)=\phi(v),
$$

so $I'$ is the [identity map](../function.md#identity-function) on the [dual space](../dual-space.md). If $S:U\to V$ and $T:V\to W$, then, for $\psi\in W'$ and $u\in U$,

$$
\bigl((TS)'\psi\bigr)(u)=\psi(T(Su))
=\bigl(S'(T'\psi)\bigr)(u).
$$

Equality on all vectors proves

$$
\boxed{(TS)'=S'T'.}
$$

Apply this to $RR^{-1}=R^{-1}R=I$. It gives both $R'(R^{-1})'=I'$ and $(R^{-1})'R'=I'$. Consequently the [dual map](../dual-space.md#transpose-of-a-linear-map) is invertible and

$$
\boxed{(R')^{-1}=(R^{-1})'.}
$$

<h3 id="2/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

<h5 id="2/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#2/b/i)

The [annihilator of a vector subspace](../dual-space.md#annihilator-of-a-vector-subspace) is

$$
U^\circ=\{\phi\in V':\phi(u)=0\text{ for every }u\in U\}.
$$

Choose a [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) $e_1,\ldots,e_r$ of $U$ and extend it to a [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) $e_1,\ldots,e_d$ of $V$. In the [dual basis](../dual-space.md#dual-basis), precisely $f_{r+1},\ldots,f_d$ span $U^\circ$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\dim U^\circ=\dim V-\dim U.}
$$

The canonical restriction [linear transformation](../vector-space.md#linear-map)

$$
\rho:V'\longrightarrow U',\qquad \rho(\phi)=\phi|_U
$$

has [kernel of a linear map](../kernel-of-a-linear-map.md) $U^\circ$. It is surjective: extend any [linear functional](../dual-space.md#linear-functional) on $U$ by assigning arbitrary values, for instance zero, to the additional [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) vectors. The [First isomorphism theorem for vector spaces](../vector-space.md#first-isomorphism-theorem-for-vector-spaces) gives the explicit [linear isomorphism](../vector-space.md#linear-isomorphism)

$$
\boxed{V'/U^\circ\ \longrightarrow U',\qquad
\phi+U^\circ\longmapsto\phi|_U.}
$$

It is natural because the displayed map itself makes no [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) choice; a [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) was used only to prove surjectivity. In particular, $U'$ is canonically a [quotient vector space](../vector-space.md#quotient-vector-space) of $V'$, rather than canonically a subspace of $V'$.

<h4 id="2/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

<h5 id="2/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#2/b/ii)

If $\phi=T'\psi$ and $v\in\ker T$, then $\phi(v)=\psi(Tv)=0$. Thus $\operatorname{im}T'\subseteq(\ker T)^\circ$, using the [annihilator of a vector subspace](../dual-space.md#annihilator-of-a-vector-subspace).

Conversely, let $\phi$ vanish on the [kernel of a linear map](../kernel-of-a-linear-map.md) $\ker T$. Define a [linear functional](../dual-space.md#linear-functional) on $\operatorname{im}T$ by $\psi_0(Tv)=\phi(v)$. This is well-defined: $Tv=Tw$ implies $v-w\in\ker T$, so $\phi(v)=\phi(w)$. Extend $\psi_0$ to a [linear functional](../dual-space.md#linear-functional) $\psi$ on $W$, using the extension argument from part (i). Then $T'\psi=\phi$, proving

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{im}T'=(\ker T)^\circ.}
$$

The dimension formula from part (i) and the [rank-nullity theorem](../kernel-of-a-linear-map.md#rank-nullity-theorem) now give

$$
\operatorname{rank}T'=\dim(\ker T)^\circ
=\dim V-\dim\ker T=\operatorname{rank}T.
$$

To deduce the [eigenvalue](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) statement, apply this equality of [rank of a linear map](../vector-space.md#rank-of-a-linear-map) to $T-\lambda I$. Its [dual map](../dual-space.md#transpose-of-a-linear-map) is $T'-\lambda I$, so their [kernel of a linear map](../kernel-of-a-linear-map.md) dimensions agree. Thus the same $\lambda$ are [eigenvalues](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue), with the same [geometric multiplicities](../linear-operator-theory.md#geometric-multiplicity).

For [algebraic multiplicities](../linear-operator-theory.md#algebraic-multiplicity) as well, part (a)(iii) gives

$$
\bigl((T-\lambda I)^r\bigr)'=(T'-\lambda I)^r,
$$

so equality of [rank](../vector-space.md#rank-of-a-linear-map) applies to every power. Hence

$$
\dim\ker(T-\lambda I)^r=\dim\ker(T'-\lambda I)^r.
$$

After extending to a [field](../algebra.md#field) where the [characteristic polynomials](../linear-operator-theory.md#characteristic-polynomial) split, take $r\geq\dim V$. These [kernel of a linear map](../kernel-of-a-linear-map.md) spaces are the [generalized eigenspaces](../linear-operator-theory.md#generalized-eigenspace); the [primary decomposition theorem for linear maps](../linear-operator-theory.md#primary-decomposition-theorem-for-linear-maps) identifies their dimensions with the [algebraic multiplicities](../linear-operator-theory.md#algebraic-multiplicity). The same proof works over the original [field](../algebra.md#field) for every [eigenvalue](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) in it. We obtain

$$
\boxed{\text{$T$ and $T'$ have the same nonzero eigenvalues,
with both kinds of multiplicity equal.}}
$$

In fact the argument also includes the zero [eigenvalue](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue).

<h3 id="3/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

<h5 id="3/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#3/a/i)

Use the convention that the [inner product](../inner-product.md) is conjugate-linear in its first argument and linear in its second, as in part (b). The [adjoint operator](../hilbert-space.md#adjoint-operator) is characterized by

$$
\boxed{\langle Tv,w\rangle=\langle v,T^*w\rangle
\qquad(v,w\in V).}
$$

Existence and uniqueness follow from the finite-dimensional representation of [linear functionals](../dual-space.md#linear-functional) by the [inner product](../inner-product.md). More concretely, choose an [orthonormal basis](../inner-product.md#orthonormal-basis) $e_i$ and let $a_{ij}=\langle e_i,Te_j\rangle$. Then

$$
\langle e_i,T^*e_j\rangle=\langle Te_i,e_j\rangle
=\overline{a_{ji}},
$$

which defines $T^*$ uniquely and verifies the required identity by expansion in that [basis](../vector-space.md#basis). Thus its [matrix](../vector-space.md#matrix) is the conjugate transpose, proved here rather than assumed. A [self-adjoint operator](../linear-operator-theory.md#self-adjoint-operator) satisfies $T^*=T$, equivalently $\langle Tv,w\rangle=\langle v,Tw\rangle$ for every pair of vectors.

<h4 id="3/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

<h5 id="3/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#3/a/ii)

Proceed by induction on $\dim V$. The result is immediate for dimension zero. In positive dimension, the [characteristic polynomial](../linear-operator-theory.md#characteristic-polynomial) has a root over the [complex numbers](../complex-analysis.md#complex-number), so there is a nonzero [eigenvector](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvector) $v$ with [eigenvalue](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) $\lambda$. Normalize it to have norm one. [Self-adjointness](../linear-operator-theory.md#self-adjoint-operator) gives

$$
\overline\lambda\langle v,v\rangle
=\langle Tv,v\rangle
=\langle v,Tv\rangle
=\lambda\langle v,v\rangle,
$$

so $\lambda$ is real.

The [orthogonal complement](../hilbert-space.md#orthogonal-complement) $v^\perp$ is an [invariant subspace](../representation-theory.md#invariant-subspace): if $w\perp v$, then

$$
\langle v,Tw\rangle=\langle Tv,w\rangle
=\overline\lambda\langle v,w\rangle=0.
$$

The restriction of $T$ to $v^\perp$ is again [self-adjoint](../linear-operator-theory.md#self-adjoint-operator), since the same [inner product](../inner-product.md) identity holds on that subspace. By induction it has an [orthonormal basis](../inner-product.md#orthonormal-basis) of [eigenvectors](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvector). Adjoining $v$ gives

$$
\boxed{\text{an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of $T$}.}
$$

The existence of the first [eigenvector](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvector) is the essential use of the [complex numbers](../complex-analysis.md#complex-number) in this proof of the finite-dimensional [spectral theorem](../hilbert-space.md#spectral-theorem).

<h4 id="3/a/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

<h5 id="3/a/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#3/a/iii)

Let $e_1,\ldots,e_d$ be the given [orthonormal basis](../inner-product.md#orthonormal-basis), with $Te_j=\lambda_je_j$. From the defining identity of the [adjoint operator](../hilbert-space.md#adjoint-operator),

$$
\langle e_i,T^*e_j\rangle
=\langle Te_i,e_j\rangle
=\overline\lambda_i\delta_{ij}.
$$

These coordinates prove $T^*e_j=\overline\lambda_je_j$ without assuming a [matrix](../vector-space.md#matrix) formula. Therefore

$$
T^*Te_j=|\lambda_j|^2e_j=TT^*e_j
$$

for every [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) vector. Thus $T$ is a [normal operator](../hilbert-space.md#normal-operator), and

$$
\boxed{T^*T=TT^*,\qquad
T^*=T\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \lambda_j\in\mathbb R\text{ for every }j.}
$$

The condition of possessing an [orthonormal basis](../inner-product.md#orthonormal-basis) of [eigenvectors](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvector) is already part of the hypothesis; real [eigenvalues](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) alone would not imply [self-adjointness](../linear-operator-theory.md#self-adjoint-operator) for an arbitrary [linear transformation](../vector-space.md#linear-map).

<h3 id="3/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

<h5 id="3/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#3/b/i)

The integral is conjugate-linear in $f$, linear in $g$, and conjugate-symmetric, by the corresponding properties of the integral and of [complex conjugation](../complex-analysis.md#complex-conjugation). Also,

$$
\langle f,f\rangle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|f(x)|^2e^{-x^2}\,dx\geq0.
$$

If a [polynomial](../polynomial.md) $f$ is not zero, there is a real point $x_0$ with $f(x_0)\ne0$: a nonzero [polynomial](../polynomial.md) cannot vanish at every real point. By [continuity](../calculus.md#continuous-function), $|f|$ has a positive lower bound on a sufficiently small closed interval about $x_0$. The continuous function $e^{-x^2}$ also has a positive lower bound there. The integral over this interval is strictly positive. Hence

$$
\boxed{\langle f,f\rangle=0\ \Longleftrightarrow\ f=0.}
$$

Together with the finiteness allowed in the question, these properties prove that the formula defines an [inner product](../inner-product.md) on $V=\mathbb C[x]$.

<h4 id="3/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

<h5 id="3/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#3/b/ii)

Differentiate the expression from the source before applying any finite-dimensional result. The exponential factors cancel:

$$
\boxed{Tf=f''-2xf'-2f.}
$$

This is a [polynomial](../polynomial.md) whenever $f$ is a [polynomial](../polynomial.md), and its [polynomial degree](../polynomial.md#degree-of-a-polynomial) is at most that of $f$. Differentiation and multiplication are linear, so $T$ is a [linear transformation](../vector-space.md#linear-map) $V\to V$, and every $V_n$ is an [invariant subspace](../representation-theory.md#invariant-subspace).

For [self-adjointness](../linear-operator-theory.md#self-adjoint-operator), put $w(x)=e^{-x^2}$. Then $w'=-2xw$ and $wTf=(wf')'-2wf$. [Integration by parts](../calculus.md#integration-by-parts) therefore gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle Tf,g\rangle
&=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\bigl((w\overline{f'})'-2w\overline f\bigr)g\,dx\\
&=-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}w\overline{f'}g'\,dx
-2\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}w\overline f g\,dx\\
&=\langle f,Tg\rangle.
\end{aligned}
$$

The discarded endpoint terms are $w\overline{f'}g$ and $w\overline f g'$. They vanish at both infinities because exponential decay dominates every [polynomial](../polynomial.md). The same decay justifies the intermediate integrals. Consequently

$$
\boxed{T(V_n)\subseteq V_n,\qquad
\langle Tf,g\rangle=\langle f,Tg\rangle\quad(f,g\in V).}
$$

Here the question's [self-adjointness](../linear-operator-theory.md#self-adjoint-operator) is in the sense of an [adjoint on an inner product space](../hilbert-space.md#adjoint-on-an-inner-product-space): $T$ itself represents the adjoint on the polynomial space. Each finite-dimensional restriction $T|_{V_n}$ is a [self-adjoint operator](../linear-operator-theory.md#self-adjoint-operator). No choice of operator domain in a completed [Hilbert space](../hilbert-space.md) is needed for this argument. The resulting [Gaussian polynomial operator](../orthogonal-polynomial.md#gaussian-polynomial-operator) will produce [Hermite polynomials](../orthogonal-polynomial.md#hermite-polynomial) in part (iv).

<h4 id="3/b/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

<h5 id="3/b/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#3/b/iii)

If $f\in V_n\setminus V_{n-1}$, write $f(x)=a_nx^n+\cdots$ with $a_n\ne0$. In the expression $Tf=f''-2xf'-2f$, the first term has [polynomial degree](../polynomial.md#degree-of-a-polynomial) at most $n-2$, while the other two contribute

$$
-2na_nx^n-2a_nx^n=-2(n+1)a_nx^n.
$$

Thus, if $Tf=\lambda f$, comparison of the leading coefficients gives

$$
\boxed{\lambda=-2(n+1).}
$$

For $n=0$ take $V_{-1}=\{0\}$; the same result follows from $T1=-2$. This identifies the [eigenvalue](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) conditional on the existence of the [eigenvector](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvector); existence is established next.

<h4 id="3/b/iv">iv</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

<h5 id="3/b/iv/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#3/b/iv)

Construct compatible choices in the nested spaces, rather than choosing unrelated [orthonormal bases](../inner-product.md#orthonormal-basis) for different $V_n$. Set $V_{-1}=\{0\}$ and define

$$
L_n=V_n\cap V_{n-1}^{\perp}.
$$

The [orthogonal complement](../hilbert-space.md#orthogonal-complement) is taken for the [inner product](../inner-product.md) from part (i). Since $\dim V_n=n+1$ and $\dim V_{n-1}=n$, the space $L_n$ has dimension one and $V_n=V_{n-1}\oplus L_n$.

It is an [invariant subspace](../representation-theory.md#invariant-subspace). Indeed, if $v\in L_n$ and $u\in V_{n-1}$, part (ii) gives

$$
\langle u,Tv\rangle=\langle Tu,v\rangle=0,
$$

because $Tu\in V_{n-1}$. Also $Tv\in V_n$. Hence $Tv\in L_n$. Any nonzero vector in this one-dimensional space is consequently an [eigenvector](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvector). Choose a unit vector $g_n\in L_n$. It has [polynomial degree](../polynomial.md#degree-of-a-polynomial) exactly $n$: membership in $V_{n-1}$ would make it orthogonal to itself and thus zero. Part (iii) now supplies its [eigenvalue](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue).

For $m<n$, $g_m\in V_{n-1}$ and $g_n\perp V_{n-1}$, so the choices are pairwise orthogonal. We have proved

$$
\boxed{\langle g_m,g_n\rangle=\delta_{mn},\qquad
\deg g_n=n,\qquad Tg_n=-2(n+1)g_n\quad(n\geq0).}
$$

An explicit compatible construction is the [Gram-Schmidt process](../inner-product.md#gram-schmidt-process) on $1,x,x^2,\ldots$:

$$
r_n=x^n-\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\langle g_j,x^n\rangle g_j,
\qquad g_n=\frac{r_n}{\sqrt{\langle r_n,r_n\rangle}}.
$$

The leading term of $r_n$ is $x^n$, so it is never zero. This fixes the positive real leading coefficient and yields the normalized [Hermite polynomials](../orthogonal-polynomial.md#hermite-polynomial). For example, the [Gaussian integral](../integral.md#gaussian-integral) gives

$$
g_0=\pi^{-1/4},\qquad
g_1=\sqrt2\,\pi^{-1/4}x,\qquad
g_2=\frac{2x^2-1}{\sqrt2\,\pi^{1/4}}.
$$

Every [polynomial](../polynomial.md) is a finite linear combination of these [eigenvectors](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvector), since $g_0,\ldots,g_n$ form an [orthonormal basis](../inner-product.md#orthonormal-basis) of $V_n$ for each $n$.
