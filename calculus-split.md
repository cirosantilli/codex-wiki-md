# Calculus

↑ **Parent:** [Real analysis](real-analysis-split.md)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calculus)

Calculus connects **local change** with **accumulated change**. A [derivative](derivative.md) asks how fast a [function](function-split.md) changes near one input; an [integral](integral-split.md) adds its contributions across an [interval](interval-mathematics.md). The [fundamental theorem of calculus](fundamental-theorem-of-calculus.md) explains why these operations fit together: accumulating a continuous rate produces a quantity whose [derivative](derivative.md) is that rate.

For example, suppose a particle moves along a [straight line](straight-line.md) with [position](position.md) $s(t)=t^2+t$ metres at time $t$ seconds. Its [velocity](velocity.md) is $s'(t)=2t+1$ metres per second. Between $t=0$ and $t=3$, adding the [velocity](velocity.md) over time gives

$$
\int_0^3(2t+1)\,dt=s(3)-s(0)=12\text{ metres}.
$$

Here the [velocity](velocity.md) stays positive, so the [displacement](displacement.md) also equals the distance travelled. If the particle reverses direction, the [integral](integral-split.md) of its signed [velocity](velocity.md) still gives [displacement](displacement.md), while the [integral](integral-split.md) of its [speed](speed.md) gives total distance.

This is useful because local rules are often easier to specify than whole trajectories. A [differential equation](differential-equation-split.md) can prescribe a particle's [velocity](velocity.md) or a population's growth rate; [integration](integral-split.md) then reconstructs its evolution, subject to an [initial condition](initial-condition.md). In the other direction, a [derivative](derivative.md) extracts local information from a known [function](function-split.md), and [optimization](mathematical-optimization-split.md) uses that information to locate or approach promising inputs. The same two operations therefore connect measurement, prediction and design.

The visual connection is developed in [derivative](derivative.md), [integral](integral-split.md) and [fundamental theorem of calculus](fundamental-theorem-of-calculus.md). The definitions depend on [limits](limit-of-a-function.md), which turn progressively finer finite measurements into exact mathematical statements. A graph with a corner need not have a [derivative](derivative.md) there, and the conditions permitting [integration](integral-split.md) or interchange of [limits](limit-of-a-function.md) matter; calculus is more than a collection of symbolic rules.

Historically, Isaac Newton described changing quantities and their rates in the language of fluents and fluxions; his manuscript _History of the Method of Fluxions_ records that approach.[Isaac Newton, History of the Method of Fluxions, Cambridge University Library MS Add. 3968](https://www.newtonproject.ox.ac.uk/view/texts/normalized/NATP00356) Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz's _Nova methodus pro maximis et minimis_ appeared in _Acta Eruditorum_ in October 1684, pp. 467–473; the original article is preserved in the HKUST Library collection.[Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, Nova methodus pro maximis et minimis, 1684](https://digitalcollections.hkust.edu.hk/Detail/objects/b540295) The modern [limit](limit-of-a-function.md) definitions used here should not be read back into those seventeenth-century formulations.
