<h3 id="1/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1/a)

Write the rational expression as $(x^2+4)^{-1}$ and apply the [chain rule](../../calculus.md#chain-rule):

$$
\boxed{\frac d{dx}\frac1{x^2+4}=-\frac{2x}{(x^2+4)^2}.}
$$

The denominator is positive for every real $x$, so the [derivative](../../calculus.md#derivative) is defined throughout the real line.

<h4 id="1/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

The outer [exponential function](../../calculus.md#exponential-function) differentiates to itself, and the inner [sine](../../geometry-and-topology.md#sine) has [derivative](../../calculus.md#derivative) [cosine](../../geometry-and-topology.md#cosine). The [chain rule](../../calculus.md#chain-rule) gives

$$
\boxed{\frac d{dx}e^{\sin x}=e^{\sin x}\cos x.}
$$

## 2

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="2/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#2/a)

For positive $a$, write $a^{-x}=\exp(-x\ln a)$. Differentiating by the [chain rule](../../calculus.md#chain-rule) gives

$$
\boxed{\frac d{dx}a^{-x}=-(\ln a)a^{-x}.}
$$

The [natural logarithm](../../calculus.md#natural-logarithm) of the constant base supplies the factor; it must not be omitted when $a\ne e$.

<h3 id="2/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

Set $u=\ln x$, so $du=dx/x$. The real domain is $x>1$, ensuring $u>0$. The [integration by substitution](../../integral.md#integration-by-substitution) followed by [integration by parts](../../calculus.md#integration-by-parts) gives

$$
\int\frac{\ln(\ln x)}x\,dx=\int\ln u\,du=u\ln u-u+C.
$$

Thus

$$
\boxed{\ln x\,\ln(\ln x)-\ln x+C,\qquad x>1.}
$$

## 3

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h4 id="3/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

Since the [derivative](../../calculus.md#derivative) of $\ln\cos x$ is $-\tan x$ on this interval,

$$
\int_0^{\pi/4}\tan x\,dx
=\big[-\ln\cos x\big]_0^{\pi/4}

$$

<h3 id="3/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

Use the real [antiderivative](../../integral.md#antiderivative) $\ln|x|$, valid on either half-line away from zero:

$$
\int_{-2}^{-1}\frac{dx}{x}
=\big[\ln|x|\big]_{-2}^{-1}=\ln1-\ln2=\boxed{-\ln2}.
$$

The negative answer is consistent with a negative integrand and increasing [integration](../../integral.md) limits.

## 4

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="4/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#4)

<h4 id="4/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#4/a)

The specified range ensures $\cos y\ne0$, so the [separable differential equation](../../differential-equation.md#separable-differential-equation) can be written $\sec^2y\,dy=\sin x\,dx$. Integrating gives $\tan y=C-\cos x$. The range selects the principal [inverse tangent](../../geometry-and-topology.md#inverse-tangent), hence

$$
\boxed{y(x)=\arctan(C-\cos x),\qquad C\in\mathbb R.}
$$

Every finite $C$ produces a solution within the required range. Constant solutions at the excluded values $y=\pm\pi/2$ are not part of this family or of the question's domain.

<h3 id="4/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#4)

<h4 id="4/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#4/b)

The [general solution](../../linear-ordinary-differential-equation.md#general-solution) of this first-order [linear ordinary differential equation](../../linear-ordinary-differential-equation.md) is $y=Ce^{3x}$. Its [initial condition](../../differential-equation.md#initial-condition) gives $C=3$, so

$$
\boxed{y(x)=3e^{3x}.}
$$

[Differentiation](../../calculus.md#differentiation) verifies $y'=3y$ and substitution at zero gives the required value.

↑ **Parent:** [A](#6/a)

The [sphere](../../euclidean-geometry.md#sphere) has surface area $4\pi R^2=400\pi\,\mathrm{m^2}$. Dividing the given surface-patch area by that total gives the dimensionless fraction

$$
\boxed{\frac{10}{400\pi}=\frac1{40\pi}\approx0.00796,}
$$

or about **$0.796\%$** of the surface. The patch area is already measured on the surface, so no planar projection or spherical-cap conversion is needed.

<h3 id="6/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#6)

<h4 id="6/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#6/b)

Interpret the stated side lengths as all eight edges of the [square pyramid](../../euclidean-geometry.md#square-pyramid). The equal lateral edges put its apex above the base [centre](../../euclidean-geometry.md#center-geometry). The base-centre-to-vertex distance is half the diagonal, $1/\sqrt2\,\mathrm m$. The [Pythagorean theorem](../../euclidean-geometry.md#pythagorean-theorem) therefore gives

$$
h=\sqrt{1^2-(1/\sqrt2)^2}=\frac1{\sqrt2}\,\mathrm m.
$$

Using the [pyramid](../../euclidean-geometry.md#pyramid) volume formula with a $1\,\mathrm{m^2}$ base,

$$
\boxed{V=\frac13(1)\frac1{\sqrt2}
=\frac1{3\sqrt2}\,\mathrm{m^3}\approx0.2357\,\mathrm{m^3}.}
$$

## 7

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="7/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [7](#7)

<h4 id="7/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#7/a)

Where the original expression is defined, multiplication by $\cos x$ gives

$$
y=\frac{\cos x}{\cos x+\sin x}
=\frac12\left[1+\cot\left(x+\frac\pi4\right)\right].
$$

Thus the graph repeats with period $\pi$ and has vertical [asymptotes](../../topology.md#asymptote)

$$
\boxed{x=-\frac\pi4+k\pi,\qquad k\in\mathbb Z.}
$$

Between consecutive [asymptotes](../../topology.md#asymptote) the extended [cotangent](../../geometry-and-topology.md#cotangent) branch decreases from $+\infty$ to $-\infty$, since $y'=-1/(\cos x+\sin x)^2<0$. It passes through $(k\pi,1)$ and $(\pi/4+k\pi,1/2)$.

It is essential to [preserve the domain when simplifying a function](../../function.md#preserve-the-domain-when-simplifying-a-function). At $x=\pi/2+k\pi$, the original tangent is undefined. The finite limiting value is zero, so the graph has **holes at $(\pi/2+k\pi,0)$**, rather than actual zeros. The simplified expression extends across those points, but the original one does not.

↑ **Parent:** [7](#7)

<h4 id="7/b/solution">Solution</h4>

This [exponential function](../../calculus.md#exponential-function) is positive everywhere, with $y(0)=1$, $y\to\infty$ as $x\to-\infty$ and $y\to0$ as $x\to\infty$. Its [derivatives](../../calculus.md#derivative) are

$$
y'=-3x^2e^{-x^3}\leq0,\qquad
y''=3x(3x^3-2)e^{-x^3}.

It is strictly decreasing overall, with a horizontal stationary [inflection point](../../topology.md#inflection-point) at **$(0,1)$**. A second, nonstationary [inflection point](../../topology.md#inflection-point) occurs at $x=(2/3)^{1/3}$, where $y=e^{-2/3}$. The only horizontal [asymptote](../../topology.md#asymptote) is **$y=0$ toward positive infinity**; the graph is not symmetric about the vertical axis.

<h4 id="9/a/solution">Solution</h4>

On $0\leq x\leq1/2$, the [quadratic polynomial](../../polynomial.md#quadratic-polynomial) is positive. The required area is therefore its [definite integral](../../integral.md#definite-integral) above the horizontal axis:

## 11

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="11/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [11](#11)

<h4 id="11/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#11/a)

Expanding the [determinant](../../multilinear-algebra.md#determinant) along the first row gives

$$
\det A=1(4-16)+4(-4+28)+7(16-28)
=-12+96-84=\boxed{0}.
$$

The [matrix trace](../../linear-algebra.md#matrix-trace) is the sum of the diagonal entries, **$\operatorname{tr}A=1+4+1=6$**. Since the [determinant](../../multilinear-algebra.md#determinant) is the product of the [eigenvalues](../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue), at least one [eigenvalue](../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) is zero. Thus $A$ is singular; the [determinant](../../multilinear-algebra.md#determinant) alone does not specify the two other [eigenvalues](../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue).

<h3 id="11/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [11](#11)

<h4 id="11/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#11/b)

The symmetry between the first and third coordinates makes $(1,0,-1)^{\mathsf T}$ an [eigenvector](../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvector), with [eigenvalue](../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) $-6$. In the remaining invariant [plane](../../geometry-and-topology.md#plane), write a [vector](../../vector-space.md#vector) as $(s,t,s)^{\mathsf T}$. Its image is $(8s-4t,-8s+4t,8s-4t)^{\mathsf T}$, so the reduced [eigenvalue](../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) equation has [determinant](../../multilinear-algebra.md#determinant)

$$
\det\begin{pmatrix}8-\lambda&-4\\-8&4-\lambda\end{pmatrix}
=\lambda(\lambda-12).
$$

The corresponding directions have $t=2s$ for $\lambda=0$ and $t=-s$ for $\lambda=12$. Normalizing gives

$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|c}
\lambda=-6&\mathbf e_-=(1,0,-1)^{\mathsf T}/\sqrt2\\[2pt]
\lambda=0&\mathbf e_0=(1,2,1)^{\mathsf T}/\sqrt6\\[2pt]
\lambda=12&\mathbf e_+=(1,-1,1)^{\mathsf T}/\sqrt3.
\end{array}}
$$

Their squared unnormalized norms are $2,6,3$. The pairwise unnormalized [dot products](../../inner-product.md#dot-product) are $1-1=0$, $1-1=0$, and $1-2+1=0$, so these unit [eigenvectors](../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvector) are mutually orthogonal. The [eigenvalues](../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) sum to $6$ and have product zero, agreeing with part (a).

<h3 id="11/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [11](#11)

<h4 id="11/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#11/c)

Expand an arbitrary [vector](../../vector-space.md#vector) in the [orthonormal eigenbasis](../../linear-operator-theory.md#orthonormal-eigenbasis):

$$
\mathbf r=r_-\mathbf e_-+r_0\mathbf e_0+r_+\mathbf e_+,
\qquad A\mathbf r=-6r_-\mathbf e_-+12r_+\mathbf e_+.
$$

Its image has no component along $\mathbf e_0$, so

$$
\boxed{(A\mathbf r)\cdot\mathbf e_0=0\quad\text{for every }\mathbf r,
\qquad\mathbf e_0=\frac1{\sqrt6}(1,2,1)^{\mathsf T}\ne0.}
$$

Alternatively, symmetry gives $(A\mathbf r)\cdot\mathbf e_0=\mathbf r\cdot A\mathbf e_0=0$. This is [image-kernel orthogonality for an adjoint](../../hilbert-space.md#image-kernel-orthogonality-for-an-adjoint), with $A^{\mathsf T}=A$.

<h3 id="11/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [11](#11)

<h4 id="11/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#11/d)

Resolve the input along the three perpendicular eigendirections. The [linear map](../../vector-space.md#linear-map) **removes the component along $(1,2,1)$, stretches the component along $(1,-1,1)$ by $12$, and reverses and stretches the component along $(1,0,-1)$ by $6$**. Every output lies in the [plane](../../geometry-and-topology.md#plane) perpendicular to $(1,2,1)$. A nonzero input in the [kernel of a linear map](../../kernel-of-a-linear-map.md) $A$ is sent to zero; other nonzero inputs remain in that output [plane](../../geometry-and-topology.md#plane). Equivalently, take the [orthogonal projection](../../hilbert-space.md#orthogonal-projection) onto this [plane](../../geometry-and-topology.md#plane), then scale by the two signed [eigenvalues](../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) along its perpendicular eigendirections.

The nonparallel direction [unit vectors](../../vector-space.md#unit-vector) have nonzero [cross product](../../vector-space.md#cross-product). Let

$$
\mathbf n=\frac{\widehat{\mathbf b}\times\widehat{\mathbf d}}
{|\widehat{\mathbf b}\times\widehat{\mathbf d}|}.
$$

For any joining [vector](../../vector-space.md#vector) $\mathbf s=\mathbf c-\mathbf a+\mu\widehat{\mathbf d}-\lambda\widehat{\mathbf b}$, its normal component is fixed:

$$
\mathbf s\cdot\mathbf n=(\mathbf c-\mathbf a)\cdot\mathbf n.
$$

Thus its length is at least the absolute value of that component. Since the two line directions span $\mathbf n^\perp$, suitable $\lambda,\mu$ cancel the entire component in that [plane](../../geometry-and-topology.md#plane), attaining the bound. Therefore the [distance between skew lines](../../euclidean-geometry.md#distance-between-skew-lines), including the zero-distance intersecting case, is

$$
\boxed{d_{\min}=\frac{|(\mathbf c-\mathbf a)\cdot
(\widehat{\mathbf b}\times\widehat{\mathbf d})|}
{|\widehat{\mathbf b}\times\widehat{\mathbf d}|}.}
$$

The numerator is a [scalar triple product](../../multilinear-algebra.md#scalar-triple-product). The nonparallel hypothesis is essential to the denominator and to the spanning argument.

Choose any nonzero auxiliary [vector](../../vector-space.md#vector) $\mathbf q$ not collinear with $\mathbf p$. Then $\mathbf p,\mathbf q,\mathbf p\times\mathbf q$ form a [basis](../../vector-space.md#basis), so write

$$
\mathbf x=\alpha\mathbf p+\beta\mathbf q+\gamma(\mathbf p\times\mathbf q).
$$

Taking the [dot product](../../inner-product.md#dot-product) with $\mathbf p$ gives $\alpha|\mathbf p|^2+\beta\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf q=k$. This separates the two cases requested in the hint.

If $\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf q\ne0$, choose $\alpha,\gamma$ freely and solve for $\beta$:

$$
\boxed{\mathbf x=\alpha\mathbf p+
\frac{k-\alpha|\mathbf p|^2}{\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf q}\,\mathbf q
+\gamma(\mathbf p\times\mathbf q),\qquad \alpha,\gamma\in\mathbb R.}
$$

If $\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf q=0$, the coefficient of $\mathbf p$ is fixed and the other two are free:

$$
\boxed{\mathbf x=\frac{k}{|\mathbf p|^2}\mathbf p
+\beta\mathbf q+\gamma(\mathbf p\times\mathbf q),\qquad\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb R.}
$$

These formulas describe the whole [plane](../../geometry-and-topology.md#plane) with normal $\mathbf p$, including $k=0$. Completeness follows from the [basis](../../vector-space.md#basis) expansion, not only from checking that the displayed [vectors](../../vector-space.md#vector) satisfy the equation. The [parametrization of a plane with an auxiliary vector](../../geometry-and-topology.md#parametrization-of-a-plane-with-an-auxiliary-vector) also gives a single formula valid in both cases.

<h3 id="14/c">c</h3>

<h4 id="14/c/solution">Solution</h4>

Use the convention $x=r\sin\theta\cos\phi$, $y=r\sin\theta\sin\phi$, $z=r\cos\theta$, with $\theta$ measured from the positive vertical axis. The squared [radius](../../topology.md#radius) is

$$

Since the horizontal radial distance is $1/\sqrt{1+t^2}>0$ for finite $t$,

$$
\cos\theta=\frac{t}{\sqrt{1+t^2}},\qquad

The horizontal angle advances with $t$. Thus the [spherical coordinates](../../multivariable-calculus.md#spherical-coordinate-system) are

\boxed{r(t)=1,\qquad\theta(t)=\frac\pi2-\arctan t,\qquad
\phi(t)=t\pmod{2\pi}.}
$$

An unwrapped continuous azimuth is simply $\phi=t$; a principal-angle convention introduces harmless jumps by $2\pi$. The polar angle stays in $(0,\pi)$ and approaches the two poles only as $t\to\pm\infty$.

\int_0^\pi h\sin nx\,dx

$$
\boxed{h(x)=\frac8\pi\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\sin((2n+1)x)}{(2n+1)^3},\qquad0\leq x\leq\pi.}
$$

The [Fourier sine series of an odd quadratic arch](../../fourier-series.md#fourier-sine-series-of-an-odd-quadratic-arch) converges to this continuous extension; its coefficients are also absolutely summable. Uniform [sampling without replacement](../../statistical-inference.md#sampling-without-replacement) therefore gives the [hypergeometric distribution](../../discrete-probability-distribution.md#hypergeometric-distribution)

$$
\boxed{p_j=\frac{\binom Kj\binom{N-K}{M-j}}{\binom NM},
\quad\max(0,M-N+K)\leq j\leq\min(M,K).}
$$

Outside this integer range the [probability](../../probability-theory.md#probability) is zero. If ordered catches were counted instead, every haul would have $M!$ orders and that factor would cancel from the [probability](../../probability-theory.md#probability) ratio.

Here equal catchability means equal selection [probability](../../probability-theory.md#probability) for each individual remaining fish. It does not [mean](../../expected-value.md) that the two species have [probability](../../probability-theory.md#probability) $1/2$ on each draw regardless of their populations. This interpretation is the one that yields the stated hypergeometric law.

\boxed{\mathbb P(X=1)=\frac{\binom21\binom81}{45}=\frac{16}{45}.}

<h4 id="18/c/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#18/c)

<h5 id="18/c/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

Only one labelled haul contains both trout, giving

\boxed{\mathbb P(X=2)=\frac{\binom22\binom80}{45}=\frac1{45}.}

<h4 id="18/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#18/c)

The three preceding [probabilities](../../probability-theory.md#probability) satisfy

$$
\boxed{\frac{28}{45}+\frac{16}{45}+\frac1{45}=1.}
$$

Their [expected value](../../expected-value.md) and second [moment](../../expected-value.md#moment) are

$$
\mu=\frac{16+2}{45}=\frac25,\qquad
\mathbb E[X^2]=\frac{16+4}{45}=\frac49.
$$

Subtracting the squared [mean](../../expected-value.md) gives

$$
\boxed{\mu=\frac25,\qquad\sigma^2=\frac49-\frac4{25}=\frac{64}{225}.}
$$

The value $\mu=2(2/10)$ also follows from [linearity of expectation](../../expected-value.md#linearity-of-expectation). The [variance](../../variance.md) includes the finite-population reduction caused by [sampling without replacement](../../statistical-inference.md#sampling-without-replacement).

<h3 id="18/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [18](#18)

<h4 id="18/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#18/d)

When only a tiny fraction of either species is removed, the trout fraction among the remaining fish stays nearly constant at $p=K/N$. Successive catches are then approximately independent trials with that fixed success [probability](../../probability-theory.md#probability). The count therefore approaches the [binomial distribution](../../discrete-probability-distribution.md#binomial-distribution)

$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(X=j)\approx\binom Mj p^j(1-p)^{M-j},\qquad p=K/N.}
$$

The approximation comes from negligible depletion, not from an exact [independence](../../random-variable.md#independent-random-variables) property of [sampling without replacement](../../statistical-inference.md#sampling-without-replacement).

## 19

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="19/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [19](#19)

<h4 id="19/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#19/a)

Write the formal [Maclaurin series](../../calculus.md#maclaurin-series) as $\sum_{n\geq0}a_nx^n$, where $a_n=f^{(n)}(0)/n!$. If the successive nonzero coefficient ratios have a limit, the [ratio test](../../sequence-and-series.md#ratio-test) gives

$$
\left|\frac{a_{n+1}x^{n+1}}{a_nx^n}\right|
=|x|\left|\frac{f^{(n+1)}(0)}{(n+1)f^{(n)}(0)}\right|\longrightarrow\frac{|x|}{R},
$$

so the series converges absolutely for $|x|<R$, where

$$
\boxed{R=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left|\frac{(n+1)f^{(n)}(0)}{f^{(n+1)}(0)}\right|.}
$$

If that ratio does not exist, the [Cauchy-Hadamard theorem](../../sequence-and-series.md#cauchy-hadamard-theorem) gives the general formula $R^{-1}=\limsup|a_n|^{1/n}$. The [radius of a Maclaurin series from successive derivatives](../../sequence-and-series.md#radius-of-a-maclaurin-series-from-successive-derivatives) concerns convergence of the series; equality to the original [function](../../function.md) additionally requires [analyticity](../../sequence-and-series.md#analyticity) or a vanishing [Taylor remainder](../../calculus.md#taylor-remainder). Mere differentiability, or even smoothness, does not ensure that equality.

For the specified logarithm,

$$
\ln(2+x)=\ln2+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}x^n}{n2^n},\qquad |x|<2.
$$

The absolute successive-term ratio tends to $|x|/2$, so **the interval of [absolute convergence](../../sequence-and-series.md#absolute-convergence) is $(-2,2)$**. At $x=2$ the nonconstant terms are the alternating harmonic series, which converges conditionally to $\ln2$, giving the full sum $\ln4$. At $x=-2$ all nonconstant terms are $-1/n$, so the series diverges to $-\infty$; the original logarithm is also undefined there. Thus the full real convergence interval is **$(-2,2]$**.

<h3 id="19/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [19](#19)

<h4 id="19/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#19/b)

Multiply the [Maclaurin series](../../calculus.md#maclaurin-series) $e^{-x}=1-x+x^2/2+O(x^3)$ by the [binomial series](../../sequence-and-series.md#binomial-series) $(1+x)^{-1/2}=1-x/2+3x^2/8+O(x^3)$. The constant, linear and quadratic coefficients are therefore

$$
\boxed{e^{-x}(1+x)^{-1/2}=1-\frac32x+\frac{11}{8}x^2+O(x^3).}
$$

<h3 id="19/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [19](#19)

<h4 id="19/c/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#19/c)

<h5 id="19/c/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#19/c/i)

There is an indexing defect in the original PDF: the term at $n=1$ is undefined because $\ln1=0$. Thus the series as literally indexed is not defined. The natural finite-index repair is to start at $n=2$, and that repaired series still **diverges**.

Indeed, for $n\geq2$ the terms are positive and

$$
\frac{u_{n+1}}{u_n}
=2\frac{n\ln n}{(n+1)\ln(n+1)}\longrightarrow2>1.
$$

They eventually increase at a geometric rate and do not tend to zero. Since the [terms of a convergent series tend to zero](../../sequence-and-series.md#terms-of-a-convergent-series-tend-to-zero), no choice of a finite replacement for the missing first term could make the tail converge.

<h4 id="19/c/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#19/c)

<h5 id="19/c/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#19/c/ii)

Use the bound $|\sin n|\leq1$:

$$
\left|\frac{\sin n}{n^2}\right|\leq\frac1{n^2}.
$$

The comparison [p-series](../../sequence-and-series.md#p-series) has exponent $2>1$ and converges. Hence, by the [comparison test for series](../../sequence-and-series.md#comparison-test-for-series), the original series is **absolutely convergent**. No alternating-sign or monotonicity assumption about $\sin n$ is needed.

<h3 id="19/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [19](#19)

<h4 id="19/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#19/d)

Index the terms from $n=0$:

$$
S(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{n+4}}{(n+3)n!}.
$$

Its absolute successive-term ratio tends to zero for every fixed $x$, so it converges everywhere. Divide by $x$ temporarily and use the [exponential series divided by a shifted index](../../calculus.md#exponential-series-divided-by-a-shifted-index) with $k=3$:

$$
\frac{S(x)}x=\int_0^x t^2e^t\,dt
=\big[e^t(t^2-2t+2)\big]_0^x.
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{S(x)=x\big[(x^2-2x+2)e^x-2\big].}
$$

At $x=0$ both the original series and this formula are zero, so the temporary division loses no value. The termwise [integration](../../integral.md) is justified by uniform convergence of the exponential series on the finite [integration](../../integral.md) interval.

## 20

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="20/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [20](#20)

At a regular constrained [stationary point](../../calculus-of-variations.md#stationary-point), the [Lagrange multiplier](../../mathematical-optimization.md#lagrange-multiplier) equations are $f_x=\lambda g_x$, $f_y=\lambda g_y$. Eliminating the multiplier gives

$$
f_xg_y-f_yg_x=\lambda g_xg_y-\lambda g_yg_x=0.
$$

Thus the required [Lagrange multiplier determinant condition in two variables](../../mathematical-optimization.md#lagrange-multiplier-determinant-condition-in-two-variables) is

$$
\boxed{\det\begin{pmatrix}f_x&g_x\\f_y&g_y\end{pmatrix}_{(a,b)}=0.}
$$

If the constraint [gradient](../../multivariable-calculus.md#gradient) vanishes, this [determinant](../../multilinear-algebra.md#determinant) is zero automatically, although the regular multiplier theorem cannot be applied. The condition is necessary and is not sufficient to classify the constrained point. This addresses the original unnumbered task before part (a).

<h3 id="20/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [20](#20)

<h4 id="20/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#20/a)

Maximizing the squared distance avoids differentiating a square root. The [Lagrange multiplier](../../mathematical-optimization.md#lagrange-multiplier) equations for $f=x^2+y^2$ are

$$
2x=\lambda(2x+y),\qquad2y=\lambda(x+2y),\qquad x^2+y^2+xy=4.
$$

Adding and subtracting the first two gives $(2-3\lambda)(x+y)=0$ and $(2-\lambda)(x-y)=0$. The feasible [stationary points](../../calculus-of-variations.md#stationary-point) therefore lie on $x=y$ or $x=-y$. The first case gives $(x,y)=(\pm2/\sqrt3,\pm2/\sqrt3)$ and squared distance $8/3$. The second gives $(2,-2)$ and $(-2,2)$, with squared distance $8$.

The [ellipse](../../euclidean-geometry.md#ellipse) is compact and its constraint [gradient](../../multivariable-calculus.md#gradient) does not vanish on the curve, so an extremum exists among these points. A direct global check is $4=f+xy\geq f/2$, using $xy\geq-f/2$. Equality occurs when $x=-y$. Consequently

$$
\boxed{d_{\max}=2\sqrt2,\qquad\text{at }(2,-2),\ (-2,2).}
$$

<h3 id="20/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [20](#20)

<h4 id="20/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#20/b)

Assume positive corridor widths $a,b$. Write the line through the two wall-contact endpoints in intercept form. Requiring it to pass through the inner corner gives

$$
\frac a{a+X}+\frac b{b+Y}=1
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad XY=ab,
\qquad X,Y>0.
$$

Its squared length is $F=(a+X)^2+(b+Y)^2$. Apply [Lagrange multipliers](../../mathematical-optimization.md#lagrange-multiplier) to this [function](../../function.md) subject to $G=XY-ab=0$:

$$
2(a+X)=\lambda Y,\qquad2(b+Y)=\lambda X.
$$

Multiplying by $X$ and $Y$ respectively eliminates the multiplier, giving $X(a+X)=Y(b+Y)$. Substitute $Y=ab/X$ and multiply by $X^2$:

$$
X^3(a+X)=ab^2(a+X),\qquad
\boxed{(X^3-ab^2)(X+a)=0.}
$$

The branch $X=-a$ is geometrically inadmissible. Thus $X=a^{1/3}b^{2/3}$ and $Y=a^{2/3}b^{1/3}$. If $A=a^{1/3}$ and $B=b^{1/3}$, the two intercepts become $A(A^2+B^2)$ and $B(A^2+B^2)$, so

$$
F_{\min}=(A^2+B^2)^3,
\qquad\boxed{L=(a^{2/3}+b^{2/3})^{3/2}.}
$$

Along $XY=ab$ in the positive quadrant, $F\to\infty$ as $X\to0^+$ or $X\to\infty$, and this is its only [stationary point](../../calculus-of-variations.md#stationary-point). It is therefore the minimum of the longest sticks that fit at each orientation. A stick being turned must pass through every intermediate orientation, so this narrowest clearance is precisely the maximum carryable length, rather than a maximum of $F$. This is the [ladder around a right-angled corner](../../mathematical-optimization.md#ladder-around-a-right-angled-corner) problem, with a zero-width stick and wall contact allowed as in the ideal geometry.
