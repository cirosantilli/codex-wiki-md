↑ **Parent:** [6](#6)

Set $\xi=x-ct$. The [chain rule](../../calculus.md#chain-rule) gives $u_t=-cf'(\xi)$ and $u_x=f'(\xi)$, so $u_t+cu_x=0$ for any differentiable $f$. The initial profile fixes $f(\xi)=\cos\xi$, giving

$$
\boxed{u(x,t)=\cos(x-ct)}.
$$

This is the translated initial profile, as described by the [transport equation](../../transport-equation.md).

## 11

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 2](paper-2.md)

<h3 id="11/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [11](#11)

<h4 id="11/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#11/a)

Let $C$ [mean](../../expected-value.md) control-group membership and $E$ the adverse event. Equal allocation gives $P(C)=P(C^c)=1/2$, so the [law of total probability](../../probability-space.md#law-of-total-probability) gives $P(E)=(p_0+p_1)/2$. [Bayes' theorem](../../probability-theory.md#bayes-theorem) then gives

$$
\boxed{P(C\mid E)=\frac{p_0P(C)}{p_0P(C)+p_1P(C^c)}
=\frac{p_0}{p_0+p_1}}.
$$

This is defined when $p_0+p_1>0$. If both are zero, the conditioning event has zero [probability](../../probability-theory.md#probability) and the stated [conditional probability](../../probability-theory.md#conditional-probability) is undefined.

<h3 id="11/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [11](#11)

<h4 id="11/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#11/b)

Normalize the [Rayleigh distribution](../../continuous-probability-distribution.md#rayleigh-distribution) by substituting $s=x^2/(2\sigma^2)$:

$$
1=A\int_0^\infty xe^{-x^2/(2\sigma^2)}\,dx=A\sigma^2,
\qquad\boxed{A=\sigma^{-2}}.
$$

For its sketch, the density starts at zero, is positive for $x>0$, and tends to zero at infinity. [Differentiation](../../calculus.md#differentiation) gives

$$
f'(x)=\frac{e^{-x^2/(2\sigma^2)}}{\sigma^2}
\left(1-\frac{x^2}{\sigma^2}\right).
$$

It therefore has a single maximum at $x=\sigma$, with height $e^{-1/2}/\sigma$.

<a id="11/b/image-rayleigh-density-in-units-of-its-scale-parameter-with-its-mode-and-mean"></a>
![](../../past-exam-of-the-mathematics-course-of-the-year-1-of-the-natural-sciences-course-of-the-university-of-cambridge/2014/paper-2-rayleigh-density.png)

**[Figure 1](#11/b/image-rayleigh-density-in-units-of-its-scale-parameter-with-its-mode-and-mean). Rayleigh density in units of its scale parameter, with its mode and mean**.

The [expected value](../../expected-value.md) is

$$
E[X]=\frac1{\sigma^2}\int_0^\infty x^2e^{-x^2/(2\sigma^2)}\,dx.
$$

Integrating by parts with $x\,dx\,e^{-x^2/(2\sigma^2)}=-\sigma^2\,d(e^{-x^2/(2\sigma^2)})$ makes the boundary term vanish and leaves $\int_0^\infty e^{-x^2/(2\sigma^2)}\,dx$. The [Gaussian integral](../../integral.md#gaussian-integral) gives

$$
\boxed{E[X]=\sigma\sqrt{\frac\pi2}}.
$$

<h3 id="11/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [11](#11)

<h4 id="11/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#11/c)

Use the usual model of independent games, each with Sheila-win [probability](../../probability-theory.md#probability) $\theta$. After $m+n$ games, an outcome with $m$ wins and $n$ losses has $\binom{m+n}{m}$ possible orders. Since both totals are below eleven, none of those sequences has already ended the match. Thus

$$
\boxed{P(m\text{ wins},n\text{ losses})=\binom{m+n}{m}\theta^m(1-\theta)^n}.
$$

For a completed Sheila win with $n$ losses, the last game must be her eleventh win. The preceding $10+n$ games contain ten wins and $n$ losses, so their [probability](../../probability-theory.md#probability) is $\binom{10+n}{10}\theta^{10}(1-\theta)^n$, followed by one further factor $\theta$. The allowed loss counts are $0\leq n\leq10$, and these terminal events are disjoint. Hence

$$
\boxed{P(\text{Sheila wins match})=
\sum_{n=0}^{10}\binom{10+n}{10}\theta^{11}(1-\theta)^n}.
$$

This is the [match win probability with a fixed winning score](../../discrete-probability-distribution.md#match-win-probability-with-a-fixed-winning-score), obtained by the [negative binomial stopping argument](../../discrete-probability-distribution.md#negative-binomial-stopping-argument). Constant marginal game-win [probabilities](../../probability-theory.md#probability) alone would not justify the product without the usual independence assumption.

<h3 id="12/a">a</h3>

<h4 id="12/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#12/a)

<h5 id="12/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#12/a/i)

A real square [orthogonal matrix](../../linear-algebra.md#orthogonal-matrix) $Q$ satisfies

$$
\boxed{Q^TQ=I},
$$

equivalently $Q^{-1}=Q^T$. Its columns, and also its rows, form an [orthonormal basis](../../inner-product.md#orthonormal-basis). Consequently it preserves [dot products](../../inner-product.md#dot-product) and [Euclidean norms](../../normed-vector-space.md#euclidean-norm).

<h4 id="12/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#12/a)

<h5 id="12/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#12/a/ii)

To invert the [matrix](../../vector-space.md#matrix), row-reduce the augmented [matrix](../../vector-space.md#matrix) $(M\mid I)$. Subtracting row one from rows two and three gives left rows $(0,-4,1)$ and $(0,0,2)$, so the pivots are nonzero and back-substitution is possible. The resulting [matrix inverse](../../matrix-inverse.md) is

$$
\boxed{M^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}
3/8&1/4&3/8\\1/8&-1/4&1/8\\-1/2&0&1/2
\end{pmatrix}}.
$$

Multiplying by $M$ verifies the identity [matrix](../../vector-space.md#matrix). This inverse is not $M^T$; indeed

$$
M^TM=\begin{pmatrix}3&-1&0\\-1&11&0\\0&0&2\end{pmatrix}\ne I.
$$

Thus **$M$ is not [orthogonal](../../inner-product.md#orthogonal-vectors)**.

<h4 id="12/a/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#12/a)

<h5 id="12/a/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#12/a/iii)

Use the order-reversal rule for the [transpose](../../vector-space.md#transpose) of a product:

$$
(AB)^T(AB)=B^TA^TAB=B^TIB=B^TB=I.
$$

Therefore **the product of two [orthogonal](../../inner-product.md#orthogonal-vectors) [matrices](../../vector-space.md#matrix) is [orthogonal](../../inner-product.md#orthogonal-vectors)**. The argument does not require $A$ and $B$ to commute.

<h3 id="12/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [12](#12)

<h4 id="12/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#12/b)

<h5 id="12/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#12/b/i)

Collect the coefficients of the unknowns into the [matrix](../../vector-space.md#matrix) and the constants into a column. The requested [linear system](../../linear-algebra.md#system-of-linear-equations) is

$$
\boxed{\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1\\2&2&a-1\\a&1&3\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\\z\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}3\\4\\5\end{pmatrix}}.
$$

The unknown coordinate $y$ here is a scalar component; the right-hand column is the [vector](../../vector-space.md#vector) denoted by the question's matrix-form notation.

<h4 id="12/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#12/b)

<h5 id="12/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#12/b/ii)

At $a=2$, eliminate the first column by $R_2\leftarrow R_2-2R_1$ and $R_3\leftarrow R_3-2R_1$ in the augmented [matrix](../../vector-space.md#matrix). The last two rows give $-z=-2$ and $-y+z=-1$. Hence $z=2$, $y=3$, and the first row then gives $x=-2$. Thus

$$
\boxed{(x,y,z)=(-2,3,2)}.
$$

The coefficient [determinant](../../multilinear-algebra.md#determinant) at this parameter is nonzero, so this solution is unique.

<h4 id="12/b/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#12/b)

<h5 id="12/b/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#12/b/iii)

The same elimination at general $a$ gives the rows

$$
\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1&|&3\\0&0&a-3&|&-2\\
0&1-a&3-a&|&5-3a\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The coefficient [determinant](../../multilinear-algebra.md#determinant) is $(a-3)(a-1)$. Only $a=1$ or $a=3$ can therefore fail to have a unique solution. At $a=3$, the second row reads $0=-2$, so **there is no solution**. At $a=1$, the last two rows are proportional and both specify $z=1$, leaving one free unknown; hence **there are infinitely many solutions**. Every other real $a$ gives a unique solution.

<h4 id="12/b/iv">iv</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#12/b)

<h5 id="12/b/iv/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#12/b/iv)

At $a=1$, the reduced system gives $z=1$ and $x+y=2$. Thus all solutions are

$$
\boxed{(x,y,z)=(2-s,s,1),\qquad s\in\mathbb R}.
$$

Geometrically the [solution set](../../set.md#solution-set) is an affine straight line, and substitution verifies all three original equations.

The hyperbolic identity $\cosh^2x-\sinh^2x=1$ and $\cosh x+\sinh x=e^x$ reduce the integrand to $1+e^x$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\int(\cosh^2x+\cosh x-\sinh^2x+\sinh x)\,dx=x+e^x+C}.

<h4 id="13/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#13/a)

<h5 id="13/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#13/a/ii)

Let $D=3x^2+2x+2$. Splitting the numerator into a [derivative](../../calculus.md#derivative) term and a constant gives $x-1=D'/6-4/3$. Thus

$$
\int\frac{x-1}{D}\,dx=\frac16\log D-\frac43\int\frac{dx}{D}.
$$

Complete the square as $D=((3x+1)^2+5)/3$ and substitute $u=3x+1$. The remaining [integral](../../integral.md) is $\int du/(u^2+5)=\arctan(u/\sqrt5)/\sqrt5$. Hence

$$
\boxed{\frac16\log(3x^2+2x+2)
-\frac4{3\sqrt5}\arctan\frac{3x+1}{\sqrt5}+C}.
$$

The quadratic denominator is positive for all real $x$, so no logarithmic absolute value or real-domain exclusion is needed.

<h3 id="13/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [13](#13)

<h4 id="13/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#13/b)

The argument $3x^3+2x$ is odd, so its squared [sine](../../geometry-and-topology.md#sine) is even. Replacing $x$ by $-x$ interchanges the numerator and denominator inside the [logarithm](../../calculus.md#logarithm), changing that factor's sign. Thus the full integrand is an [odd function](../../calculus.md#odd-function). It is continuous on the symmetric interval because $|x|\leq1/\pi<1$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\int_{-1/\pi}^{1/\pi}\sin^2(3x^3+2x)
\log\frac{1-x^5}{1+x^5}\,dx=0}.
$$

<h3 id="13/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [13](#13)

<h4 id="13/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#13/c)

The [fundamental theorem of calculus](../../calculus.md#fundamental-theorem-of-calculus) states that integrating a continuous [function](../../function.md) from a fixed lower endpoint produces an [antiderivative](../../integral.md#antiderivative). More precisely, if $f$ is continuous at the interior point $x$, then

$$
\boxed{\frac{d}{dx}\int_a^x f(y)\,dy=f(x)}.
$$

Indeed, the difference quotient is the average of $f$ over the short interval from $x$ to $x+h$, which tends to $f(x)$ by [continuity](../../calculus.md#continuous-function). The accompanying evaluation rule is $\int_a^b f=F(b)-F(a)$ whenever $F'=f$ on the interval.

<h3 id="13/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [13](#13)

<h4 id="13/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#13/d)

The sum is finite, so [differentiation](../../calculus.md#differentiation) passes through it without a convergence issue. Each lower limit $n$ is constant with respect to $x$, and the [fundamental theorem of calculus](../../calculus.md#fundamental-theorem-of-calculus) gives the same upper-end integrand. Hence the [derivative](../../calculus.md#derivative) is

$$
\sin(x^2+x^6)\sum_{n=0}^N\binom Nn.
$$

The [binomial theorem](../../binomial-coefficient.md#binomial-theorem) at $1+1$ gives the sum $2^N$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\frac{d}{dx}\left[\sum_{n=0}^N\binom Nn
\int_n^x\sin(y^2+y^6)\,dy\right]=2^N\sin(x^2+x^6)}.
$$

\boxed{y(x)=2\sqrt{\frac{3-x^2}{1+x^2}},\qquad-\sqrt3<x<\sqrt3}.

Exactness after multiplication requires $\partial_y(\mu M)=\partial_x(\mu N)$. Since $\mu$ depends only on $x$,

$$

$$

For $\mu=\exp(\int f(x)\,dx)$, the ratio $\mu'/\mu$ is $f(x)$, so

$$
\boxed{f(x)=\frac{M_y-N_x}{N}},\qquad
\boxed{\mu(x)=\exp\int\frac{M_y-N_x}{N}\,dx}.
$$

The assumed existence means this ratio is a [function](../../function.md) only of $x$ wherever the division is valid. For a factor depending only on $y$, the corresponding equation is $\psi'M+\psi M_y=\psi N_x$, giving

$$
\boxed{\psi(y)=\exp\int\frac{N_x-M_y}{M}\,dy}.

<h4 id="15/a/solution">Solution</h4>

With sufficiently many [derivatives](../../calculus.md#derivative), [Taylor's theorem](../../calculus.md#taylor-s-theorem) gives a finite approximation and a controlled remainder. For example, if $f$ is $N+1$ times continuously differentiable between $a$ and $a+\delta$, then

$$
\boxed{f(a+\delta)=\sum_{j=0}^N\frac{f^{(j)}(a)}{j!}\delta^j
+\frac{f^{(N+1)}(a+\theta\delta)}{(N+1)!}\delta^{N+1}},\qquad0<\theta<1.
$$

Thus [derivatives](../../calculus.md#derivative) at the nearby base point determine the [polynomial](../../polynomial.md) approximation; a bound on the next [derivative](../../calculus.md#derivative) bounds its error. The smallness of $\delta$ makes this useful, provided the [derivatives](../../calculus.md#derivative) remain controlled.

The statement's bare assumption of differentiability alone guarantees only $f(a+\delta)=f(a)+f'(a)\delta+o(\delta)$. Higher-order formulas need higher regularity. Even smoothness does not by itself guarantee equality with an infinite [Taylor series](../../calculus.md#taylor-series); that stronger conclusion requires the remainder to tend to zero.

<h3 id="19/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [19](#19)

For a short string element of length $\Delta x$, the small-slope approximation makes the upward components of [tension](../../force.md#tension) at its two ends $T y_x(x+\Delta x,t)$ and $-Ty_x(x,t)$. Its [mass](../../classical-mechanics.md#mass) is $\rho\Delta x$. [Newton's second law](../../force.md#newton-s-second-law) gives

$$
\rho\Delta x\,y_{tt}=T\left[y_x(x+\Delta x,t)-y_x(x,t)\right].
$$

Divide by $\Delta x$ and take the limit to obtain the [wave equation on a string](../../wave-equation.md#wave-equation-on-a-string),

$$
\boxed{y_{tt}=c^2y_{xx},\qquad c=\sqrt{\frac T\rho}}.
$$

Here $\rho$ is [linear mass density](../../fluid-mechanics.md#linear-mass-density), and the derivation assumes small transverse slopes, uniform [tension](../../force.md#tension), and a flexible string.

At the right endpoint the string pulls the ring toward the interior. Its vertical force is therefore $-Ty_x(L,t)$ to first order; the frictionless wire supplies no vertical force. The ring obeys

$$
\boxed{M y_{tt}(L,t)=-Ty_x(L,t)}.
$$

This is the dynamic endpoint law for a [string with an endpoint mass](../../wave-equation.md#string-with-an-endpoint-mass).

For a separated oscillatory solution, write $y=X(x)q(t)$ with $q''=-\omega^2q$ and $X''+(\omega/c)^2X=0$. The fixed left end removes the spatial [cosine](../../geometry-and-topology.md#cosine), so the nonzero [normal modes](../../wave-equation.md#normal-mode) have

$$
y_n(x,t)=\left[A_n\cos(\omega_nt)+B_n\sin(\omega_nt)\right]
\sin\frac{\omega_nx}{c}.
$$

The endpoint law gives $M\omega_n^2\sin(\omega_nL/c)=T(\omega_n/c)\cos(\omega_nL/c)$. Since $\sin(\omega_nL/c)=0$ would also force the [cosine](../../geometry-and-topology.md#cosine) to vanish, it can be divided out for a nonzero [mode](../../probability-and-statistics.md#mode-statistics). Using $T=\rho c^2$ gives

$$
\boxed{\cot\frac{\omega_nL}{c}=\frac{M\omega_n}{\rho c}}.
$$

For physical $M\geq0$, these give all positive modal frequencies. There is no nonzero static linear [mode](../../probability-and-statistics.md#mode-statistics): the left value and right slope conditions would force it to vanish. Positivity also follows by [integration by parts](../../calculus.md#integration-by-parts) from

$$
\omega^2\left(\rho\int_0^L X^2\,dx+MX(L)^2\right)
=T\int_0^L(X')^2\,dx.
$$

For $M=0$, the right endpoint is free, with the [Neumann boundary condition](../../differential-equation.md#neumann-boundary-condition) $y_x(L,t)=0$. At $L=\pi$, the allowed wave numbers are $k_n=n+1/2$, $n\geq0$, and $\omega_n=ck_n$. Zero initial displacement removes every cosine-in-time coefficient. The initial velocity contains only the modes $k=1/2$ and $k=5/2$, each with coefficient one. Dividing those velocity coefficients by their angular frequencies gives

$$
\boxed{y(x,t)=\frac2c\sin\frac{ct}{2}\sin\frac x2
+\frac{2}{5c}\sin\frac{5ct}{2}\sin\frac{5x}{2}}.
$$

At $x=0$ each term vanishes, at $x=\pi$ each spatial [derivative](../../calculus.md#derivative) vanishes, and at $t=0$ the displacement and its time [derivative](../../calculus.md#derivative) reproduce the two [initial conditions](../../differential-equation.md#initial-condition).

<h4 id="20/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#20/c)

The parameter [derivative](../../calculus.md#derivative) of the integrand is $\cos(a\log x)$, whose absolute value is bounded by one on $0<x<1$. This integrable bound justifies [differentiation under the integral sign](../../analysis.md#differentiation-under-the-integral-sign), giving

$$
I'(a)=\int_0^1\cos(a\log x)\,dx.
$$

The original [integral](../../integral.md) also exists: $|\sin(a\log x)/\log x|\leq|a|$, and its apparent singularity at $x=1$ is removable. Set $x=e^{-t}$ to evaluate the [derivative](../../calculus.md#derivative):

$$
I'(a)=\int_0^\infty e^{-t}\cos(at)\,dt
=\operatorname{Re}\int_0^\infty e^{-(1-ia)t}\,dt
=\operatorname{Re}\frac1{1-ia}.
$$

Consequently

$$
\boxed{I'(a)=\frac1{1+a^2}}.
$$

Since $I(0)=0$, integration in the parameter fixes the constant and gives the [logarithmic sine integral](../../analysis.md#logarithmic-sine-integral) identity

$$
\boxed{I(a)=\arctan a,\qquad I(1)=\frac\pi4}.
$$
