<h1 id="17/b/iii/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](../iii.md)

For $\phi=x^2+2y^2$, the [gradient](../../../../../../gradient.md) is $\nabla\phi=(2x,4y)$, and

$$
(2y,-x)\cdot(2x,4y)=0.
$$

Away from the origin, both $\nabla\phi$ and $\nabla u$ lie in the one-dimensional subspace perpendicular to $(2y,-x)$, so they are parallel. More precisely,

$$
\boxed{\nabla u=\lambda(x,y)\nabla\phi}
$$

for some scalar $\lambda$, including $\lambda=0$. If $\nabla u\ne0$, this can also be inverted to express $\nabla\phi$ as a multiple of $\nabla u$. At a [stationary point](../../../../../../stationary-point.md) of $u$ with $\nabla\phi\ne0$, that literal inverse wording is not valid; the geometric assertion is parallelism of the nonzero [gradient](../../../../../../gradient.md) directions.
