The [complex exponential](../../calculus.md#complex-exponential-function) satisfies $e^{iz}=e^{-y}(\cos x+i\sin x)$. Multiplying by $x+iy$ gives

$$
f(z)=e^{-y}\big[(x\cos x-y\sin x)+i(x\sin x+y\cos x)\big].

Thus its [real part](../../complex-analysis.md#real-part) is

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Re}f=e^{-y}(x\cos x-y\sin x)}.

## 3

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 2](paper-2.md)

<h3 id="3/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

The [characteristic polynomial](../../linear-operator-theory.md#characteristic-polynomial) is

$$
\det\begin{pmatrix}-\lambda&a\\a&-\lambda\end{pmatrix}=\lambda^2-a^2.
$$

Its roots give the [eigenvalues](../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue)

$$
\boxed{\lambda=a,\quad\lambda=-a}.
$$

<h3 id="3/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

For $\lambda=a$, the components of an [eigenvector](../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvector) are equal; for $\lambda=-a$, they are opposite. Dividing each [vector](../../vector-space.md#vector) by its [Euclidean norm](../../normed-vector-space.md#euclidean-norm) gives

$$
\boxed{u_+=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}\quad(\lambda=a),\qquad
u_-=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}\quad(\lambda=-a)}.
$$

They are mutually [orthogonal](../../inner-product.md#orthogonal-vectors) and have unit norm. The labels specify their [eigenvalues](../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) even when $a<0$.

## 9

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 2](paper-2.md)

<h3 id="9/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [9](#9)

<h4 id="9/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#9/a)

For real coordinates, work on $y>0$. The [chain rule](../../calculus.md#chain-rule) gives $\xi_y=y^{-1/2}$ and $\xi_{yy}=-\tfrac12y^{-3/2}$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial y^2}
=u''(\xi)(\xi_y)^2+u'(\xi)\xi_{yy}
=\frac{u''(\xi)}y-\frac{u'(\xi)}{2y^{3/2}}}.
$$

The [derivatives](../../calculus.md#derivative) on the right are with respect to $\xi$. Behaviour at $y=0$ requires separate regularity of the composed [function](../../function.md).

<h3 id="9/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [9](#9)

<h4 id="9/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#9/b)

The same [chain rule](../../calculus.md#chain-rule) gives $u_{xx}=u''(\xi)$ and $u_y=u'(\xi)/\sqrt y$. Substituting these and part (a) into the [partial differential equation](../../partial-differential-equation.md) gives

$$
u_{xx}-\frac12u_y-yu_{yy}
=u''-\frac{u'}{2\sqrt y}-y\left(\frac{u''}y-\frac{u'}{2y^{3/2}}\right)=\boxed{0}.
$$

Thus every twice-differentiable profile $u(\xi)$ gives a solution on $y>0$.

Let $S$ denote singing, and $M,F$ denote male and female. Equal population counts give $P(M)=P(F)=1/2$. The [law of total probability](../../probability-space.md#law-of-total-probability) therefore yields

$$
\boxed{P(S)=pP(M)+qP(F)=\frac{p+q}{2}}.
$$

<h3 id="10/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [10](#10)

<h4 id="10/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#10/b)

By [Bayes' theorem](../../probability-theory.md#bayes-theorem), provided $p+q>0$,

$$
\boxed{P(M\mid S)=\frac{P(S\mid M)P(M)}{P(S)}=\frac{p}{p+q}}.
$$

If $p=q=0$, singing has [probability](../../probability-theory.md#probability) zero and the stated observation cannot occur in this model; that [conditional probability](../../probability-theory.md#conditional-probability) is undefined.

## 11

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 2](paper-2.md)

<h3 id="11/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [11](#11)

<h4 id="11/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#11/a)

Expand the [vector triple product](../../multivariable-calculus.md#vector-triple-product) in Cartesian components. Its first component is

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\mathbf a\times(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)]_x
&=a_y(b_xc_y-b_yc_x)-a_z(b_zc_x-b_xc_z)\\
&=b_x(a_yc_y+a_zc_z)-c_x(a_yb_y+a_zb_z)\\
&=b_x(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf c)-c_x(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b),
\end{aligned}
$$

where adding $a_xb_xc_x$ to both products introduces cancelling terms. Likewise the other two components are

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\mathbf a\times(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)]_y
&=a_z(b_yc_z-b_zc_y)-a_x(b_xc_y-b_yc_x)
=b_y(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf c)-c_y(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b),\\
[\mathbf a\times(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)]_z
&=a_x(b_zc_x-b_xc_z)-a_y(b_yc_z-b_zc_y)
=b_z(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf c)-c_z(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b).
\end{aligned}
$$

Together these prove

$$
\boxed{\mathbf a\times(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)
=(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf c)\mathbf b-(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b)\mathbf c}.
$$

Now use the cyclic property of the [scalar triple product](../../multilinear-algebra.md#scalar-triple-product) and the just-proved identity:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)\cdot(\mathbf c\times\mathbf d)
&=\mathbf a\cdot[\mathbf b\times(\mathbf c\times\mathbf d)]\\
&=\mathbf a\cdot[(\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf d)\mathbf c-(\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf c)\mathbf d].
\end{aligned}
$$

This proves [Lagrange's identity](../../multivariable-calculus.md#lagrange-identity-for-the-cross-product) in the requested form,

$$
\boxed{(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)\cdot(\mathbf c\times\mathbf d)
=(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf c)(\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf d)
-(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf d)(\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf c)}.
$$

<h3 id="11/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [11](#11)

<h4 id="11/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#11/b)

The [sphere](../../euclidean-geometry.md#sphere) of centre $\mathbf a$ and radius $p$ has equation

$$
\boxed{|\mathbf r-\mathbf a|^2=p^2}.
$$

Let $d=|\mathbf b-\mathbf a|$ be the distance between the centres. A nondegenerate [circle of intersection of two spheres](../../euclidean-geometry.md#circle-of-intersection-of-two-spheres) exists exactly when

$$
\boxed{|p-q|<d<p+q}.
$$

The upper inequality excludes disjoint or externally tangent [spheres](../../euclidean-geometry.md#sphere). The lower excludes strict containment and internal tangency. It also excludes coincident centres, which give either no intersection or an entire common [sphere](../../euclidean-geometry.md#sphere) rather than one [circle](../../topology.md#circle). These inequalities are sufficient because a triangle with side lengths $p,q,d$ then exists with nonzero area, and rotating its third vertex about the line of centres traces a [circle](../../topology.md#circle).

For a point on both [spheres](../../euclidean-geometry.md#sphere), expand their equations:

$$
\mathbf r^2-2\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf r+a^2=p^2,\qquad
\mathbf r^2-2\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf r+b^2=q^2.
$$

Subtracting cancels $\mathbf r^2$ and gives the [plane](../../geometry-and-topology.md#plane)

$$
\boxed{2(\mathbf b-\mathbf a)\cdot\mathbf r=p^2-q^2+b^2-a^2}.
$$

Its normal is parallel to the line of centres. Under the strict inequalities, the [plane](../../geometry-and-topology.md#plane) cuts either [sphere](../../euclidean-geometry.md#sphere) in a genuine [circle](../../topology.md#circle).

## 12

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 2](paper-2.md)

<h3 id="12/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [12](#12)

<h4 id="12/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#12/a)

<h5 id="12/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#12/a/i)

In [spherical coordinates](../../multivariable-calculus.md#spherical-coordinate-system), $\theta$ is the polar angle from the positive $z$ axis and $\phi$ the azimuthal angle. The [Jacobian determinant](../../multivariable-calculus.md#jacobian-determinant) of the coordinate transformation gives the [volume element](../../integral.md#volume-element)

$$
\boxed{dV=r^2\sin\theta\,dr\,d\theta\,d\phi},\qquad
0\leq\theta\leq\pi,\quad0\leq\phi<2\pi.
$$

<h4 id="12/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#12/a)

<h5 id="12/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#12/a/ii)

Height above the surface is $h=r-R$. Integrate the [density](../../fluid-mechanics.md#density) over all exterior spherical shells:

$$
M=\int_R^\infty\int_0^\pi\int_0^{2\pi}
\rho_0e^{-(r-R)/h_0}r^2\sin\theta\,d\phi\,d\theta\,dr
=4\pi\rho_0\int_0^\infty(R+h)^2e^{-h/h_0}\,dh.
$$

Repeated [integration by parts](../../calculus.md#integration-by-parts), or the substitution $h=h_0s$, gives

$$
\int_0^\infty e^{-h/h_0}\,dh=h_0,\quad
\int_0^\infty he^{-h/h_0}\,dh=h_0^2,\quad
\int_0^\infty h^2e^{-h/h_0}\,dh=2h_0^3.
$$

Expand $(R+h)^2$ and use these moments to obtain the [mass of an exponential spherical atmosphere](../../fluid-mechanics.md#mass-of-an-exponential-spherical-atmosphere):

$$
\boxed{M=4\pi\rho_0h_0(R^2+2Rh_0+2h_0^2)}.
$$

For $h_0\ll R$, the leading term is surface area $4\pi R^2$ times column mass $\rho_0h_0$; the other terms retain the finite-curvature corrections.

<h3 id="12/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [12](#12)

<h4 id="12/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#12/b)

<h5 id="12/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#12/b/i)

The region lies above the [parabola](../../euclidean-geometry.md#parabola) $y=x^2$ and below the upper semicircle $y=\sqrt{1-x^2}$. Their intersections satisfy $x^4+x^2-1=0$, hence $x=\pm a$ with $a^2=(\sqrt5-1)/2$. In particular, $\sqrt{1-a^2}=a^2$. The region is

$$
\boxed{D=\{(x,y):-a\leq x\leq a,\quad x^2\leq y\leq\sqrt{1-x^2}\}}.
$$

It is symmetric about the $y$ axis, with endpoints $(\pm a,a^2)$; at $x=0$ it extends from $y=0$ to $y=1$.

<h4 id="12/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#12/b)

<h5 id="12/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#12/b/ii)

First perform the inner integral, which gives the vertical width of the region:

$$
I=\int_{-a}^{a}\big(\sqrt{1-x^2}-x^2\big)\,dx
=2\int_0^a\big(\sqrt{1-x^2}-x^2\big)\,dx.
$$

Using $\int\sqrt{1-x^2}\,dx=\tfrac12[x\sqrt{1-x^2}+\arcsin x]$ gives

$$
I=a\sqrt{1-a^2}+\arcsin a-\frac23a^3.
$$

At the intersection, $\sqrt{1-a^2}=a^2$, so

$$
\boxed{I=\arcsin a+\frac13a^3,\qquad A=1,\quad B=\frac13}.
$$

<h3 id="13/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [13](#13)

<h4 id="13/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#13/a)

Parametrize the path by $\mathbf r(t)=(x(t),y(t),z(t))$, from $t=a$ to $t=b$. For the [conservative vector field](../../multivariable-calculus.md#conservative-vector-field) $\mathbf F=\nabla\Phi$, the [chain rule](../../calculus.md#chain-rule) gives

$$
\mathbf F\cdot\frac{d\mathbf r}{dt}
=\Phi_x\frac{dx}{dt}+\Phi_y\frac{dy}{dt}+\Phi_z\frac{dz}{dt}
=\frac{d}{dt}\Phi(\mathbf r(t)).
$$

Integrating proves the [fundamental theorem for line integrals](../../multivariable-calculus.md#fundamental-theorem-for-line-integrals):

$$
\boxed{\int_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=\Phi(\mathbf r(b))-\Phi(\mathbf r(a))}.
$$

Only the endpoints matter, so the integral is independent of the connecting path.

<h3 id="13/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [13](#13)

<h4 id="13/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#13/b)

For a twice continuously [differentiable](../../analysis.md#differentiable-function) scalar potential, the [curl](../../multivariable-calculus.md#curl) of its [gradient](../../multivariable-calculus.md#gradient) is

$$
\nabla\times\nabla\Phi
=(\Phi_{zy}-\Phi_{yz},\ \Phi_{xz}-\Phi_{zx},\ \Phi_{yx}-\Phi_{xy}).
$$

Equality of mixed [partial derivatives](../../multivariable-calculus.md#partial-derivative) makes every component zero. Thus

$$
\boxed{\nabla\times\mathbf F=0}.
$$

This is the [curl of a gradient](../../multivariable-calculus.md#curl-of-a-gradient) identity.

<h3 id="13/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [13](#13)

<h4 id="13/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#13/c)

Differentiate the components directly:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(\nabla\times\mathbf F)_x&=\partial_y(-3xz^2-1)-\partial_z(x^2-2y)=0,\\
(\nabla\times\mathbf F)_y&=\partial_z(2xy-z^3)-\partial_x(-3xz^2-1)=-3z^2+3z^2=0,\\
(\nabla\times\mathbf F)_z&=\partial_x(x^2-2y)-\partial_y(2xy-z^3)=2x-2x=0.
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus $\boxed{\nabla\times\mathbf F=0}$. The field is smooth on all of $\mathbb R^3$, a [simply connected domain](../../complex-analysis.md#simply-connected-domain), so the zero-curl condition is sufficient for a [conservative vector field](../../multivariable-calculus.md#conservative-vector-field). The domain condition matters: zero [curl](../../multivariable-calculus.md#curl) alone need not imply a global potential on a region with holes.

<h3 id="13/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [13](#13)

<h4 id="13/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#13/d)

Evaluate the [line integral](../../multivariable-calculus.md#line-integral) along the three coordinate segments, treating coordinates not changing along a segment as constants. On the first segment, $y=z=0$, so $F_x=0$ and the contribution is zero. On the second segment,

$$
\int_0^yF_y(x,\eta,0)\,d\eta=\int_0^y(x^2-2\eta)\,d\eta=x^2y-y^2.
$$

On the final segment,

$$
\int_0^zF_z(x,y,\zeta)\,d\zeta
=\int_0^z(-3x\zeta^2-1)\,d\zeta=-xz^3-z.
$$

The [fundamental theorem for line integrals](../../multivariable-calculus.md#fundamental-theorem-for-line-integrals) therefore gives

$$
\boxed{\Phi(x,y,z)=x^2y-y^2-xz^3-z+C}.
$$

Taking its [gradient](../../multivariable-calculus.md#gradient) verifies each component of the field. The constant is $C=\Phi(0,0,0)$; the field specifies only potential differences because the [gradient](../../multivariable-calculus.md#gradient) of every constant is zero.

<h4 id="15/b/solution">Solution</h4>

Put $p=y'$ and differentiate the general form of [Clairaut's equation](../../differential-equation.md#clairaut-s-equation):

$$
p=p+xp'+f'(p)p'.
$$

Cancellation gives

$$
\boxed{[x+f'(p)]\frac{dp}{dx}=0}.
$$

For $f(p)=1/(p-1)$ the equation is defined only when $p\ne1$. There are two types of smooth branch.

First, constant $p=C\ne1$ gives the straight-line family

$$
\boxed{y=Cx+\frac1{C-1},\qquad C\in\mathbb R\setminus\{1\}}.
$$

Second, $x+f'(p)=0$ gives $x=1/(p-1)^2$, so $x>0$ and $p=1\pm x^{-1/2}$. Substituting in the original equation gives its two singular [envelope](../../geometry-and-topology.md#envelope-mathematics) branches,

$$
\boxed{y=x\pm2\sqrt x\qquad(x>0)}.
$$

Their [derivatives](../../calculus.md#derivative) are indeed $1\pm1/\sqrt x$, verifying the required value of $p$. There is no finite-slope continuation of either [envelope](../../geometry-and-topology.md#envelope-mathematics) through $x=0$, and no real [envelope](../../geometry-and-topology.md#envelope-mathematics) branch for $x<0$.

For completeness, if “solution” requires only a [continuous](../../calculus.md#continuous-function) first [derivative](../../calculus.md#derivative), one can join a line from the family to the corresponding [envelope](../../geometry-and-topology.md#envelope-mathematics) at their tangency point $x=(C-1)^{-2}$. The [function](../../function.md) and its first [derivative](../../calculus.md#derivative) match there, so such piecewise branches also solve the original first-order equation. They are not twice continuously [differentiable](../../analysis.md#differentiable-function) at a nontrivial join. The lines and [envelope](../../geometry-and-topology.md#envelope-mathematics) branches above exhaust the smooth branches; allowing these tangent joins supplies the additional $C^1$ solutions.

<h3 id="18/a">a</h3>

<h4 id="18/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#18/a)

For a real $2\pi$-periodic square-integrable [function](../../function.md), use the [Fourier series](../../fourier-series.md) convention

$$
f(x)\sim\frac{a_0}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^\infty[a_n\cos nx+b_n\sin nx],
$$

with [Fourier coefficients](../../fourier-series.md#fourier-coefficient)

$$
\boxed{a_n=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(x)\cos nx\,dx\quad(n\geq0),\qquad
b_n=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(x)\sin nx\,dx\quad(n\geq1)}.
$$

The trigonometric [orthogonality](../../inner-product.md#orthogonal-vectors) relations are $\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\cos nx\cos mx\,dx=\pi\delta_{nm}$ for positive $n,m$, with the same formula for sines; sine-cosine products integrate to zero. The constant has squared integral $2\pi$ and is [orthogonal](../../inner-product.md#orthogonal-vectors) to every positive harmonic. Thus the squared norm of the $N$th partial sum is

$$
\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f_N(x)^2\,dx
=\frac{a_0^2}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^N(a_n^2+b_n^2).
$$

Taking the limit in the [L2 space](../../measure-theory.md#l2-space-is-a-hilbert-space) gives the Fourier-series [Parseval identity](../../fourier-analysis.md#parseval-identity):

$$
\boxed{\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(x)^2\,dx
=\frac{a_0^2}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^\infty(a_n^2+b_n^2)}.
$$

Square integrability is the natural hypothesis for this finite norm identity; additional smoothness can be used when pointwise convergence is required.

<h4 id="18/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#18/b)

The [function](../../function.md) is odd, so its constant and [cosine](../../geometry-and-topology.md#cosine) coefficients are zero. Its [sine](../../geometry-and-topology.md#sine) coefficient is

$$
b_n=\frac2\pi I_n,\qquad I_n=\int_0^\pi(x^3-\pi^2x)\sin nx\,dx.
$$

The [polynomial](../../polynomial.md) vanishes at both integration endpoints. Two [integrations by parts](../../calculus.md#integration-by-parts) therefore give

$$
I_n=\frac1n\int_0^\pi(3x^2-\pi^2)\cos nx\,dx
=-\frac1{n^2}\int_0^\pi6x\sin nx\,dx.
$$

One more integration yields

$$
\int_0^\pi x\sin nx\,dx
=\left[-\frac{x\cos nx}{n}+\frac{\sin nx}{n^2}\right]_0^\pi
=-\frac{\pi(-1)^n}{n}.
$$

Consequently $I_n=6\pi(-1)^n/n^3$ and $b_n=12(-1)^n/n^3$. Hence

$$
\boxed{g(x)=12\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^n}{n^3}\sin nx,\qquad p=3}.
$$

The periodic continuation is [continuous](../../calculus.md#continuous-function) at $\pm\pi$, where its values are zero, so the series represents it there as well as in the open interval.

<h3 id="18/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [18](#18)

<h4 id="18/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#18/c)

For this odd [function](../../function.md), the [Parseval identity](../../fourier-analysis.md#parseval-identity) from part (a) gives

$$
\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}(x^3-\pi^2x)^2\,dx
=144\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac1{n^6}.
$$

The integrand is even. Expanding and integrating it gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi(x^6-2\pi^2x^4+\pi^4x^2)\,dx
&=2\pi^6\left(\frac17-\frac25+\frac13\right)\\
&=\frac{16\pi^6}{105}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Dividing by $144$ therefore proves

$$
\boxed{\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac1{n^6}=\frac{\pi^6}{945}}.
$$

This evaluates the [Riemann zeta function](../../analytic-number-theory.md#riemann-zeta-function) at six by using the norm of a simple [polynomial](../../polynomial.md).

## 19

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 2](paper-2.md)

<h3 id="19/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [19](#19)

<h4 id="19/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#19/a)

As in the diagram, an inscribed [cylinder](../../geometry-and-topology.md#cylinder-geometry) rests on $z=0$, is coaxial with the paraboloid, and has its top rim on the curved boundary. Thus

$$
h+\frac{Hr^2}{R^2}=H,\qquad V_c=\pi r^2h.
$$

Allowing contact with the boundary is the usual meaning of _inscribed_. Form the [Lagrange multiplier](../../mathematical-optimization.md#lagrange-multiplier) [function](../../function.md)

$$
\mathcal L=\pi r^2h-\lambda\left(h+\frac{Hr^2}{R^2}-H\right).
$$

At a nondegenerate stationary [cylinder](../../geometry-and-topology.md#cylinder-geometry), its two [derivative](../../calculus.md#derivative) equations are

$$
2\pi rh=\lambda\frac{2Hr}{R^2},\qquad \pi r^2=\lambda.
$$

Eliminating $\lambda$ gives $h=Hr^2/R^2$. The [constraint](../../classical-mechanics.md#constraint-mechanics) then gives $h=H/2$ and $r=R/\sqrt2$, so

$$
\boxed{V_{c,\max}=\frac{\pi R^2H}{4}}.
$$

The degenerate endpoints $r=0$ or $h=0$ have zero [volume](../../geometry-and-topology.md#volume), consistent with the positive stationary value being the [maximum](../../set.md#maximum-of-a-subset-of-a-total-order). If the strict inequalities defining the open interior were interpreted as forbidding boundary contact, the same value would instead be a supremum approached by smaller cylinders.

<h3 id="19/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [19](#19)

<h4 id="19/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#19/b)

Use the diagram's labels: $a,b$ are horizontal side lengths and $c$ is the height of the [rectangular parallelepiped](../../euclidean-geometry.md#rectangular-parallelepiped). Its four top vertices are at horizontal distance $\sqrt{a^2+b^2}/2$ from the axis. Thus the contact [constraint](../../classical-mechanics.md#constraint-mechanics) and [volume](../../geometry-and-topology.md#volume) are

$$
c+\frac{H(a^2+b^2)}{4R^2}=H,\qquad V_p=abc.
$$

Apply a [Lagrange multiplier](../../mathematical-optimization.md#lagrange-multiplier):

$$
\mathcal L=abc-\lambda\left(c+\frac{H(a^2+b^2)}{4R^2}-H\right).
$$

For positive dimensions, the [derivative](../../calculus.md#derivative) equations are

$$
bc=\lambda\frac{Ha}{2R^2},\qquad
ac=\lambda\frac{Hb}{2R^2},\qquad ab=\lambda.
$$

Substituting the last equation into the first two gives

$$
c=\frac{Ha^2}{2R^2}=\frac{Hb^2}{2R^2}.
$$

Hence $a=b$, and the [constraint](../../classical-mechanics.md#constraint-mechanics) becomes $2c=H$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{a=b=R,\qquad c=\frac H2,\qquad V_{p,\max}=\frac{R^2H}{2}}.
$$

These are the [maximal cylinder and box in an elliptic paraboloid](../../euclidean-geometry.md#maximal-cylinder-and-box-in-an-elliptic-paraboloid) dimensions for the rectangular case. A horizontal rotation of the square footprint gives the same [volume](../../geometry-and-topology.md#volume) by rotational symmetry.

<h3 id="19/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [19](#19)

<h4 id="19/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#19/c)

The ratio of the two maximal volumes is

$$
\boxed{\frac{V_{c,\max}}{V_{p,\max}}=\frac\pi2>1}.
$$

Thus **the [cylinder](../../geometry-and-topology.md#cylinder-geometry) has the larger [maximum](../../set.md#maximum-of-a-subset-of-a-total-order) [volume](../../geometry-and-topology.md#volume)**, by a factor $\pi/2$. Both optimal shapes have height $H/2$, while their horizontal cross-sections have areas $\pi R^2/2$ and $R^2$, respectively.

## 20

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 2](paper-2.md)

<h3 id="20/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [20](#20)

<h4 id="20/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#20/a)

<h5 id="20/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#20/a/i)

For the solution through $x=0$, work on the connected domain $x>-1$. While $y>0$, [separation of variables](../../partial-differential-equation.md#separation-of-variables) gives

$$
\frac{dy}{\sqrt y}=-\frac{dx}{1+x},\qquad 2\sqrt y=-\ln(1+x)+C.
$$

The [initial condition](../../differential-equation.md#initial-condition) gives $C=2$, so

$$
\boxed{y(x)=\left(1-\frac12\ln(1+x)\right)^2\quad(-1<x\leq e^2-1)}.
$$

The restriction matters: the unsquared equation requires $1-\frac12\ln(1+x)\geq0$. The solution reaches zero at $x_*=e^2-1$. Continuing the squared formula beyond that point would give a positive [derivative](../../calculus.md#derivative), contradicting the original negative square-root right-hand side.

The nonnegative forward continuation is instead

$$
\boxed{y(x)=\begin{cases}
\left(1-\frac12\ln(1+x)\right)^2,&-1<x\leq e^2-1,\\
0,&x\geq e^2-1.
\end{cases}}
$$

The two pieces join with $y'=0$, and both satisfy the original equation. Because the right-hand side is nonpositive for $x>-1$, a nonnegative solution cannot leave zero in the forward direction. This is a finite-time extinction phenomenon for a [separable differential equation](../../differential-equation.md#separable-differential-equation).

<h4 id="20/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#20/a)

<h5 id="20/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#20/a/ii)

This is a [Bernoulli differential equation](../../differential-equation.md#bernoulli-differential-equation) with exponent four. On the nonzero branch selected by $y(0)=1$, put $v=y^{-3}$. Multiplying the original equation by $-3y^{-4}$ gives

$$
v'-v=-3e^x.
$$

Its [integrating factor](../../linear-ordinary-differential-equation.md#integrating-factor) is $e^{-x}$, so $(e^{-x}v)'=-3$ and $e^{-x}v=C-3x$. The [initial condition](../../differential-equation.md#initial-condition) sets $C=1$. Thus

$$
\boxed{y(x)=\frac{e^{-x/3}}{(1-3x)^{1/3}},\qquad -\infty<x<\frac13}.
$$

This is the maximal real solution interval containing zero: the solution diverges as $x\to(1/3)^-$. The real negative branch of the same algebraic formula for $x>1/3$ is disconnected from this initial-value solution. The original equation also admits $y=0$, but it does not satisfy the prescribed [initial condition](../../differential-equation.md#initial-condition).

<h3 id="20/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [20](#20)

<h4 id="20/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#20/b)

For a nonzero separated solution $u=X(x)T(t)$ of the [reaction-diffusion equation](../../diffusion-equation.md#reaction-diffusion-system), substitution and division by $XT$ gives

$$
\frac{T'}T+1=\frac{X''}X.
$$

The two sides depend on different variables, so both equal a separation constant. The homogeneous [Dirichlet boundary conditions](../../differential-equation.md#dirichlet-boundary-condition) select a negative spatial constant, written $-k^2$:

$$
X''+k^2X=0,\qquad T'+(1+k^2)T=0.
$$

For $k>0$, $X=A\sin kx+B\cos kx$. The boundary value at zero makes $B=0$; the boundary value at $L$ then requires $kL=n\pi$, with $n=1,2,\ldots$. A zero spatial constant gives only a linear [function](../../function.md) vanishing at both endpoints, hence the zero [function](../../function.md); a positive spatial constant similarly gives no nonzero admissible hyperbolic solution. Therefore

$$
\boxed{X_n(x)=A_n\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L},\qquad
T_n(t)=B_n\exp\left[-\left(1+\frac{n^2\pi^2}{L^2}\right)t\right]}.
$$

By [superposition](../../vector-space.md#superposition-principle), the [general solution](../../linear-ordinary-differential-equation.md#general-solution) with the stated boundary conditions is

$$
\boxed{u(x,t)=\sum_{n=1}^\infty c_n\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}
\exp\left[-\left(1+\frac{n^2\pi^2}{L^2}\right)t\right]}.
$$

The coefficients are fixed only if initial data are supplied: for $u(x,0)=f(x)$, they are the [Fourier sine series](../../fourier-series.md#fourier-sine-series) coefficients $c_n=(2/L)\int_0^L f(x)\sin(n\pi x/L)\,dx$. Here they remain arbitrary subject to convergence. Every mode has the ordinary diffusive decay rate plus the additional reaction decay rate one.
