The modulus is the [Euclidean norm](../../normed-vector-space.md#euclidean-norm), obtained by taking the square root of the [dot product](../../inner-product.md#dot-product) of the [vector](../../vector-space.md#vector) with itself:

$$
\boxed{\sqrt{1^2+2^2+1^2}=\sqrt6.}
$$

<h3 id="1/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

The [dot product](../../inner-product.md#dot-product) is $1\cdot0+2(-1)+(-1)\cdot1=-3$, while the [Euclidean norms](../../normed-vector-space.md#euclidean-norm) are $\sqrt6$ and $\sqrt2$. Therefore the [angle](../../geometry-and-topology.md#angle) $\theta\in[0,\pi]$ satisfies

$$
\cos\theta=\frac{-3}{\sqrt6\sqrt2}=-\frac{\sqrt3}{2},\qquad \boxed{\theta=\frac{5\pi}{6}=150^\circ.}
$$

## 2

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="2/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

The [derivative](../../calculus.md#derivative) is $y'=2x+1/x$, so the [tangent line](../../calculus.md#tangent-line) has [slope](../../euclidean-geometry.md#slope) $3$ at $x=1$. The corresponding [point](../../geometry-and-topology.md#point-geometry) is $(1,1)$ because $\ln1=0$. Hence the [tangent line](../../calculus.md#tangent-line) is $y-1=3(x-1)$, or $y=3x-2$. Thus

$$
\boxed{\text{slope}=3,\qquad y\text{-intercept}=-2.}
$$

## 3

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="3/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

The [chain rule](../../calculus.md#chain-rule) gives

$$
\boxed{f'(x)=-2x\exp(2-x^2).}
$$

Since $x^2\geq0$, the exponent is at most $2$, with equality precisely at $x=0$. The [exponential function](../../calculus.md#exponential-function) is increasing, so

$$
\boxed{\max_{x\in\mathbb R}f(x)=e^2,\qquad\text{attained at }x=0.}
$$

The sign of the [derivative](../../calculus.md#derivative), positive for $x<0$ and negative for $x>0$, gives the same conclusion.

<h3 id="5/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#5)

Rearrangement gives the [quadratic inequality](../../polynomial.md#quadratic-inequality) $x^2-3x+1\leq0$. The associated [quadratic equation](../../polynomial.md#quadratic-equation) has roots $(3\pm\sqrt5)/2$. Since the coefficient of $x^2$ is positive, the [quadratic inequality](../../polynomial.md#quadratic-inequality) holds between these roots, including the endpoints:

$$
\boxed{\frac{3-\sqrt5}{2}\leq x\leq\frac{3+\sqrt5}{2}.}
$$

## 6

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="6/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#6)

<h4 id="6/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#6/a)

Substitution for $y$ reduces the two [simultaneous equations](../../algebra.md#system-of-equations) to

$$
x^2+(x-1)^4-1=x(x-1)(x^2-3x+4)=0.
$$

The remaining quadratic factor is positive for all real $x$, since $x^2-3x+4=(x-3/2)^2+7/4$. Thus there are exactly $\boxed{2\text{ real solutions}}$.

<h3 id="6/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#6)

<h4 id="6/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#6/b)

From the factorisation in part (a), $x=0$ or $x=1$. Substitution into the [parabola](../../euclidean-geometry.md#parabola) equation gives respectively $y=1$ or $y=0$. Thus the two intersections of the [parabola](../../euclidean-geometry.md#parabola) and the [unit circle](../../complex-analysis.md#complex-unit-circle) are

$$
\boxed{(x,y)=(0,1)\text{ and }(1,0).}
$$

## 7

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="7/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [7](#7)

Since $\cos x>0$ throughout the given [interval](../../real-analysis.md#interval-mathematics), multiplying by $\cos x$ is reversible. The equation simplifies to

$$
2-\sin x=\sqrt3\cos x,\qquad \sin x+\sqrt3\cos x=2\sin(x+\pi/3)=2.
$$

The [sine](../../geometry-and-topology.md#sine) equals $1$ only at $\pi/2+2j\pi$. Here $x+\pi/3\in[\pi/3,5\pi/6)$, so only $x+\pi/3=\pi/2$ is allowed. Hence

$$
\boxed{x=\pi/6.}
$$

Divide by four and use [completing the square](../../polynomial.md#completing-the-square):

$$
x^2-2x+y^2+y=\frac{11}{4},\qquad (x-1)^2+\left(y+\frac12\right)^2=4.

This is the standard equation of a [circle](../../topology.md#circle), so

$$
\boxed{\text{centre}=(1,-1/2),\qquad\text{radius}=2.}
$$

## 9

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="9/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [9](#9)

The [binomial series](../../sequence-and-series.md#binomial-series) begins

$$
(1+ax)^n=1+nax+\frac{n(n-1)}2a^2x^2+O(x^3).
$$

Equating coefficients gives $na=-2$ and $n(n-1)a^2/2=3$. Since $na\ne0$, substitute $a=-2/n$ into the second equation to obtain $2(n-1)/n=3$, so $n=-2$ and $a=1$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{a=1,\qquad n=-2.}
$$

Indeed $(1+x)^{-2}=1-2x+3x^2-\cdots$ for $|x|<1$.

## 11

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="11/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [11](#11)

<h4 id="11/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#11/a)

<h5 id="11/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#11/a/i)

Write $z_1=a+ib$ and $z_2=c+id$ with real $a,b,c,d$. By the definition of [complex conjugation](../../complex-analysis.md#complex-conjugation),

$$
(z_1+z_2)^*=[(a+c)+i(b+d)]^*=(a+c)-i(b+d)=(a-ib)+(c-id)=\boxed{z_1^*+z_2^*.}
$$

<h4 id="11/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#11/a)

<h5 id="11/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#11/a/ii)

With $z_1=a+ib$ and $z_2=c+id$, direct multiplication gives $z_1z_2=(ac-bd)+i(ad+bc)$. Taking the [complex conjugate](../../complex-analysis.md#complex-conjugate) yields

$$
(z_1z_2)^*=(ac-bd)-i(ad+bc)=(a-ib)(c-id)=\boxed{z_1^*z_2^*.}
$$

<h3 id="11/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [11](#11)

<h4 id="11/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#11/b)

The given [complex numbers](../../complex-analysis.md#complex-number) have positive [real parts](../../complex-analysis.md#real-part), so $\arg z_1=\arctan A$ and $\arg z_2=\arctan B$. Their product is

$$
z_1z_2=(1-AB)+i(A+B).
$$

By the assumed [complex argument](../../complex-analysis.md#argument-complex-analysis) ranges, this product also has positive [real part](../../complex-analysis.md#real-part), hence $1-AB>0$. Its [complex argument](../../complex-analysis.md#argument-complex-analysis) is consequently $\arctan[(A+B)/(1-AB)]$, with no extra multiple of $\pi$. Since multiplication adds [complex arguments](../../complex-analysis.md#argument-complex-analysis),

$$
\boxed{\arctan A+\arctan B=\arctan\left(\frac{A+B}{1-AB}\right).}
$$

This proves the [arctangent addition formula](../../geometry-and-topology.md#arctangent-addition-formula) under the stated branch restriction. For arbitrary real $A,B$, the principal [inverse tangent](../../geometry-and-topology.md#inverse-tangent) formula can require adding or subtracting $\pi$ when $AB>1$.

<h3 id="11/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [11](#11)

<h4 id="11/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#11/c)

A counterclockwise [rotation](../../riemannian-geometry.md#rotation-mathematics) through $\alpha$ about the origin in the [complex plane](../../complex-analysis.md#complex-plane) sends $z_j$ to $e^{i\alpha}z_j$. By part (a), and because the [complex conjugate](../../complex-analysis.md#complex-conjugate) of $e^{i\alpha}$ is $e^{-i\alpha}$,

$$
(e^{i\alpha}z_1)(e^{i\alpha}z_2)^*=e^{i\alpha}z_1e^{-i\alpha}z_2^*=\boxed{z_1z_2^*.}
$$

Thus $Q$ is invariant under the common [rotation](../../riemannian-geometry.md#rotation-mathematics).

<h3 id="11/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [11](#11)

<h4 id="11/d/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#11/d)

<h5 id="11/d/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#11/d/i)

Write $z_j=x_j+iy_j$. Multiplication gives

$$
Q=x_1x_2+y_1y_2+i(y_1x_2-x_1y_2).
$$

Therefore the [real part](../../complex-analysis.md#real-part) is the [dot product](../../inner-product.md#dot-product) of the corresponding [vectors](../../vector-space.md#vector):

$$
\boxed{\Re Q=x_1x_2+y_1y_2=\mathbf v_1\cdot\mathbf v_2.}
$$

For nonzero [vectors](../../vector-space.md#vector), this is $|z_1||z_2|\cos(\arg z_1-\arg z_2)$, measuring their alignment.

<h4 id="11/d/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#11/d)

<h5 id="11/d/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#11/d/ii)

From the same multiplication, the [imaginary part](../../complex-analysis.md#imaginary-part) is

$$
\boxed{\Im Q=y_1x_2-x_1y_2=-(\mathbf v_1\times\mathbf v_2)_z.}
$$

Here the planar [vectors](../../vector-space.md#vector) are embedded in three dimensions with zero third coordinate. Its magnitude is the [area](../../differential-geometry.md#surface-area) of their [parallelogram](../../euclidean-geometry.md#parallelogram); its sign is positive when the oriented [angle](../../geometry-and-topology.md#angle) from $\mathbf v_2$ to $\mathbf v_1$ has positive [sine](../../geometry-and-topology.md#sine). The reversed order matters: $\Im(z_1z_2^*)$ is the negative of the usual signed [area](../../differential-geometry.md#surface-area) for the order $(\mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_2)$.

<h3 id="11/e">e</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [11](#11)

<h4 id="11/e/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [E](#11/e)

Since $x=\cos y$ with real $y$, necessarily $-1\leq x\leq1$. Use [Euler's formula](../../complex-analysis.md#euler-s-formula) and set $w=e^{iy}\ne0$. Then

$$
2x=w+w^{-1},\qquad w^2-2xw+1=0,
$$

so the [quadratic equation](../../polynomial.md#quadratic-equation) gives $w=x\pm i\sqrt{1-x^2}$. Let $q=x+i\sqrt{1-x^2}$. It lies on the upper half of the [unit circle](../../complex-analysis.md#complex-unit-circle), so $q=e^{i\theta}$ with $0\leq\theta\leq\pi$. Choose the [complex logarithm](../../analysis.md#complex-logarithm) value $\ln q=i\theta$, taking $\ln(-1)=i\pi$ at $x=-1$. The two roots are $e^{\pm i\theta}$, so $e^{iy}=e^{\pm i\theta}$ gives $y=\pm\theta+2n\pi$.

Because $i\ln q=-\theta$, this set of real solutions is exactly

$$
\boxed{y=\pm i\ln\left(x+i\sqrt{1-x^2}\right)+2n\pi,\qquad n\in\mathbb Z.}
$$

The formula includes $x=\pm1$: the two sign families then coincide after adjusting $n$. No real $y$ exists if $|x|>1$.

<h3 id="11/f">f</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [11](#11)

<h4 id="11/f/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [F](#11/f)

For $\theta\notin2\pi\mathbb Z$, use the [finite geometric series](../../sequence-and-series.md#finite-geometric-series) with ratio $e^{i\theta}$:

$$
\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}e^{in\theta}=\frac{1-e^{iN\theta}}{1-e^{i\theta}}=e^{i(N-1)\theta/2}\frac{\sin(N\theta/2)}{\sin(\theta/2)}.
$$

The second equality follows by factoring $1-e^{it}=-2ie^{it/2}\sin(t/2)$ in numerator and denominator. Taking [real parts](../../complex-analysis.md#real-part) proves the [finite trigonometric sum](../../sequence-and-series.md#finite-trigonometric-sum) identity

$$
\boxed{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\cos(n\theta)=\frac{\cos((N-1)\theta/2)\sin(N\theta/2)}{\sin(\theta/2)}.}
$$

When $\theta=2p\pi$, every [cosine](../../geometry-and-topology.md#cosine) is $1$, so the [sum](../../arithmetic.md#sum) is $\boxed{N}$. This also gives the continuous limiting value at the excluded arguments.

## 12

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="12/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [12](#12)

<h4 id="12/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#12/a)

Take the dumbbell axis to be the $z$ axis, with its midpoint at $z=0$. The right-hand [sphere](../../euclidean-geometry.md#sphere) has centre $z=\alpha a$ and [radius](../../topology.md#radius) $R=\beta a$, while its junction with the [cylinder](../../geometry-and-topology.md#cylinder-geometry) is at $z=\gamma a$. Put $d=(\alpha-\gamma)a$, so $0<d<R$. The [Pythagorean theorem](../../euclidean-geometry.md#pythagorean-theorem) in a meridional cross-section gives the handle [radius](../../topology.md#radius) $H$ from $H^2+d^2=R^2$. Thus

$$
\boxed{H=a\sqrt{\beta^2-(\alpha-\gamma)^2}.}
$$

<h3 id="12/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [12](#12)

<h4 id="12/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#12/b)

Use [cylindrical coordinates](../../multivariable-calculus.md#cylindrical-coordinate-system) $(r,\phi,z)$ about the dumbbell axis. Their [Jacobian determinant](../../multivariable-calculus.md#jacobian-determinant) contributes the volume factor $r$, so the handle's [mass](../../classical-mechanics.md#mass) is the [volume integral](../../multivariable-calculus.md#volume-integral)

$$
M_h=\rho\int_{-\gamma a}^{\gamma a}\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^H r\,dr\,d\phi\,dz=\rho(2\gamma a)(2\pi)\frac{H^2}{2}.
$$

Inserting the [radius](../../topology.md#radius) from part (a) yields

$$
\boxed{M_h=2\pi\rho\gamma a^3\left[\beta^2-(\alpha-\gamma)^2\right].}
$$

<h3 id="12/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [12](#12)

<h4 id="12/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#12/c)

Centre [cylindrical coordinates](../../multivariable-calculus.md#cylindrical-coordinate-system) on the right-hand [sphere](../../euclidean-geometry.md#sphere), with the axial coordinate $s=z-\alpha a$ positive outwards. Using $R=\beta a$ and $d=(\alpha-\gamma)a$, the retained solid satisfies

$$
-d\leq s\leq R,\qquad0\leq r\leq\sqrt{R^2-s^2},\qquad0\leq\phi<2\pi.
$$

In particular, it contains more than a [hemisphere](../../euclidean-geometry.md#hemisphere); it is the small cap on the inward side that has been removed. The [volume integral](../../multivariable-calculus.md#volume-integral) for its [mass](../../classical-mechanics.md#mass), including the [Jacobian determinant](../../multivariable-calculus.md#jacobian-determinant) $r$, is

$$
\begin{aligned}
M_e&=\rho\int_{-d}^{R}\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^{\sqrt{R^2-s^2}}r\,dr\,d\phi\,ds\\
&=\pi\rho\int_{-d}^{R}(R^2-s^2)\,ds\\
&=\pi\rho\left(\frac{2R^3}{3}+R^2d-\frac{d^3}{3}\right).
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus each end piece has [mass](../../classical-mechanics.md#mass)

$$
\boxed{M_e=\pi\rho a^3\left[\frac{2\beta^3}{3}+\beta^2(\alpha-\gamma)-\frac{(\alpha-\gamma)^3}{3}\right].}
$$

Equivalently this is the [volume of a spherical cap](../../euclidean-geometry.md#volume-of-a-spherical-cap) with height $R+d$, multiplied by the uniform [mass density](../../fluid-mechanics.md#density).

<h4 id="12/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#12/d)

Use [spherical coordinates](../../multivariable-calculus.md#spherical-coordinate-system) $(r,\theta,\phi)$ centred on the right-hand [sphere](../../euclidean-geometry.md#sphere), with polar axis pointing outwards along $s$. The [Jacobian determinant](../../multivariable-calculus.md#jacobian-determinant) is $r^2\sin\theta$, and the retained region is $r\leq R$ with $r\cos\theta\geq-d$.

For $0\leq r\leq d$, every polar [angle](../../geometry-and-topology.md#angle) $0\leq\theta\leq\pi$ is allowed. For $d<r\leq R$, the cut requires $0\leq\theta\leq\arccos(-d/r)$. Splitting the [volume integral](../../multivariable-calculus.md#volume-integral) at $r=d$ therefore gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
M_e={}&\rho\int_0^{2\pi}\left[\int_0^d\int_0^\pi r^2\sin\theta\,d\theta\,dr+\int_d^R\int_0^{\arccos(-d/r)}r^2\sin\theta\,d\theta\,dr\right]d\phi\\
={}&2\pi\rho\left[2\int_0^d r^2\,dr+\int_d^R r^2\left(1+\frac dr\right)\,dr\right]\\
={}&2\pi\rho\left[\frac{2d^3}{3}+\frac{R^3-d^3}{3}+\frac d2(R^2-d^2)\right]\\
={}&\boxed{\pi\rho\left(\frac{2R^3}{3}+R^2d-\frac{d^3}{3}\right).}
\end{aligned}
$$

Substitution of $R=\beta a$ and $d=(\alpha-\gamma)a$ reproduces the [mass](../../classical-mechanics.md#mass) in part (c). The radial split avoids integrating over a polar [angle](../../geometry-and-topology.md#angle) range independent of $r$, which would incorrectly include part of the removed [spherical cap](../../euclidean-geometry.md#spherical-cap).

<h3 id="12/e">e</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [12](#12)

<h4 id="12/e/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [E](#12/e)

The handle and the two end pieces have disjoint interiors; their common boundary [circles](../../topology.md#circle) contribute no [volume](../../geometry-and-topology.md#volume). Hence the total [mass](../../classical-mechanics.md#mass) is $M=M_h+2M_e$. With $\delta=\alpha-\gamma$, the results above give

$$
\boxed{M=2\pi\rho a^3\left[\gamma(\beta^2-\delta^2)+\frac{2\beta^3}{3}+\beta^2\delta-\frac{\delta^3}{3}\right].}
$$

Equivalently, since $\gamma+\delta=\alpha$,

$$
\boxed{M=2\pi\rho a^3\left[\alpha\beta^2+\frac{2\beta^3}{3}-\gamma(\alpha-\gamma)^2-\frac{(\alpha-\gamma)^3}{3}\right].}
$$

Taking [antiderivatives](../../integral.md#antiderivative) produces $\ln|\mu|=-\tfrac12\ln(16+x^2)+C$. A convenient nonzero [integrating factor for a differential one-form](../../linear-ordinary-differential-equation.md#integrating-factor-for-a-differential-one-form) is therefore

$$
\boxed{\mu(x)=\frac1{\sqrt{16+x^2}}.}
$$

An explicit potential, which also proves global exactness, is

$$
F(x,y)=y\sqrt{16+x^2}-\frac13(16+x^2)^{3/2}.

Indeed $F_x=P$ and $F_y=Q$ for the chosen [integrating factor](../../linear-ordinary-differential-equation.md#integrating-factor).

The [Pythagorean theorem](../../euclidean-geometry.md#pythagorean-theorem) gives $c^2=a^2+b^2$. Taking a [total differential](../../multivariable-calculus.md#total-differential) gives $2c\,dc=2a\,da+2b\,db$, so

$$
\boxed{\frac{dc}{c}=\frac{a\,da+b\,db}{a^2+b^2}=\frac{a^2}{a^2+b^2}\frac{da}{a}+\frac{b^2}{a^2+b^2}\frac{db}{b}.}
$$

The [area](../../differential-geometry.md#surface-area) of the [right triangle](../../euclidean-geometry.md#right-triangle) is $A=ab/2$, so the [product rule](../../calculus.md#product-rule) gives $dA=(b\,da+a\,db)/2$ and

$$
\boxed{\frac{dA}{A}=\frac{da}{a}+\frac{db}{b}.}
$$

The prescribed small changes therefore give $dA/A\approx0.01-0.02=-0.01$: **the area decreases by approximately $1\%$**. For comparison, retaining the product of the two finite percentage changes gives $(1.01)(0.98)-1=-0.0102$, an exact decrease of $1.02\%$; the [total differential](../../multivariable-calculus.md#total-differential) retains only the first-order terms.

<h3 id="14/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [14](#14)

<h4 id="14/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#14/b)

Put $q=\varphi'(y-\beta z)$. Apply [implicit differentiation](../../multivariable-calculus.md#implicit-differentiation) with respect to $x$ and $y$, using the [chain rule](../../calculus.md#chain-rule):

$$
1-\alpha z_x=-\beta qz_x,\qquad -\alpha z_y=q(1-\beta z_y).
$$

Consequently

$$
(\alpha-\beta q)z_x=1,\qquad (\alpha-\beta q)z_y=-q.
$$

At a [point](../../geometry-and-topology.md#point-geometry) where the assumed [differentiable function](../../analysis.md#differentiable-function) $z(x,y)$ exists, the first equation forces $\alpha-\beta q\ne0$. Combining the equations therefore gives

$$
\boxed{\alpha z_x+\beta z_y=\frac{\alpha-\beta q}{\alpha-\beta q}=1.}
$$

The assertion is understood on such differentiable branches of the [implicit function](../../function.md#implicit-function); arbitrary parameter choices need not produce one everywhere.

<h3 id="14/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [14](#14)

<h4 id="14/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#14/c)

The scaling relation says that $z$ is a [homogeneous function](../../real-analysis.md#homogeneous-function) of degree $n$. Fix $(x,y)$ and differentiate the identity with respect to the positive parameter $\lambda$. The [chain rule](../../calculus.md#chain-rule) gives

$$
xz_x(\lambda x,\lambda y)+yz_y(\lambda x,\lambda y)=n\lambda^{n-1}z(x,y).
$$

Setting $\lambda=1$ proves the [Euler theorem for homogeneous functions](../../real-analysis.md#euler-theorem-for-homogeneous-functions) identity

$$
\boxed{xz_x+yz_y=nz.}
$$

Differentiate once more with respect to $\lambda$, using equality of the mixed [partial derivatives](../../multivariable-calculus.md#partial-derivative) for the assumed twice continuously differentiable [function](../../function.md):

$$
x^2z_{xx}(\lambda x,\lambda y)+2xyz_{xy}(\lambda x,\lambda y)+y^2z_{yy}(\lambda x,\lambda y)=n(n-1)\lambda^{n-2}z(x,y).
$$

At $\lambda=1$ this is the [second-order Euler identity for homogeneous functions](../../real-analysis.md#second-order-euler-identity-for-homogeneous-functions):

$$
\boxed{x^2z_{xx}+2xyz_{xy}+y^2z_{yy}=n(n-1)z.}
$$

The repeated parameter [differentiation](../../calculus.md#differentiation) accounts for the factor $n(n-1)$, rather than $n^2$.

<h3 id="15/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [15](#15)

<h4 id="15/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#15/a)

[Taylor's theorem](../../calculus.md#taylor-s-theorem) gives a finite [Taylor polynomial](../../calculus.md#taylor-polynomial) of degree $n$, consisting of the first $n+1$ terms, and a remainder:

$$
\boxed{f(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\frac{f^{(j)}(a)}{j!}(x-a)^j+R_{n+1}(x).}
$$

In the [Taylor theorem with Lagrange remainder](../../calculus.md#taylor-theorem-with-lagrange-remainder) form, assuming the required [derivatives](../../calculus.md#derivative) exist on an [interval](../../real-analysis.md#interval-mathematics) containing $a$ and $x$,

$$
\boxed{R_{n+1}(x)=\frac{f^{(n+1)}(\xi)}{(n+1)!}(x-a)^{n+1},}
$$

for some $\xi$ strictly between $a$ and $x$ when $x\ne a$; at $x=a$ the remainder is zero. In particular, if $|f^{(n+1)}|\leq M$ on that segment, then $|R_{n+1}(x)|\leq M|x-a|^{n+1}/(n+1)!$. This is a finite expansion; it does not assume equality with an infinite [Taylor series](../../calculus.md#taylor-series).

<h3 id="15/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [15](#15)

<h4 id="15/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#15/b)

For the [natural logarithm](../../calculus.md#natural-logarithm), $f(1)=0$, $f'(1)=1$, $f''(1)=-1$, and $f^{(3)}(x)=2/x^3$. The quadratic [Taylor polynomial](../../calculus.md#taylor-polynomial) therefore gives

$$
\ln(1.1)\approx0.1-\frac{0.1^2}{2}=\boxed{0.095}.
$$

The [Taylor remainder](../../calculus.md#taylor-remainder) is $R_3=2(0.1)^3/(6\xi^3)$ with $1<\xi<1.1$. It is positive, so the approximation is **an underestimate**. Moreover,

$$
\boxed{0<\ln(1.1)-0.095<\frac{(0.1)^3}{3}=\frac1{3000}.}
$$

Thus $1/3000\approx0.0003334$ is a valid upper bound on the [absolute error](../../numerical-analysis.md#absolute-error).

With $N/2$ white balls, uniform [sampling without replacement](../../statistical-inference.md#sampling-without-replacement) gives the [probability](../../probability-theory.md#probability)

$$
p_w=\frac{N/2}{N}\frac{N/2-1}{N-1}=\frac{N-2}{4(N-1)}.
$$

Rearranging, $4p_w(N-1)=N-2$, so

$$
\boxed{N=\frac{2-4p_w}{1-4p_w}=\frac{2(1-2p_w)}{1-4p_w}.}
$$

A finite admissible population has even $N\geq2$ and $0\leq p_w<1/4$, so the denominator is nonzero.

To obtain one continuous [antiderivative](../../integral.md#antiderivative) on the whole real axis, add $j\pi/2$ on the $j$th such [interval](../../real-analysis.md#interval-mathematics) and use the continuous limits at their endpoints.

## 18

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="18/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [18](#18)

<h4 id="18/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#18/a)

<h5 id="18/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#18/a/i)

The first coordinate direction is already an [eigenvector](../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvector) of $A$ with [eigenvalue](../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) $4$. On the last two coordinates, the [matrix](../../vector-space.md#matrix) is $\begin{pmatrix}2&4\\4&2\end{pmatrix}$, with [characteristic polynomial](../../linear-operator-theory.md#characteristic-polynomial)

$$
\det\begin{pmatrix}\lambda-2&-4\\-4&\lambda-2\end{pmatrix}=(\lambda-2)^2-16=(\lambda-6)(\lambda+2).
$$

Thus the three [eigenvalues](../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) are $4,6,-2$. For $\lambda=6$, the last two components are equal; for $\lambda=-2$, they are opposite. Normalising by the [Euclidean norm](../../normed-vector-space.md#euclidean-norm) gives the complete list

$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|c}
\lambda&\text{unit eigenvector}\\\hline
4&(1,0,0)^T\\
6&(0,1,1)^T/\sqrt2\\
-2&(0,1,-1)^T/\sqrt2
\end{array}}
$$

Each sign-reversed [vector](../../vector-space.md#vector) is an equally valid choice. The three displayed [eigenvectors](../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvector) are mutually [orthogonal](../../inner-product.md#orthogonal-vectors), as expected for a real [symmetric matrix](../../linear-algebra.md#symmetric-matrix) with distinct [eigenvalues](../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue).

<h4 id="18/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#18/a)

<h5 id="18/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#18/a/ii)

Subtracting the second row from the third gives a third row $(0,2,0)$, so expansion along it shows $\det B=-2\ne0$. Thus the [matrix inverse](../../matrix-inverse.md) exists and every right-hand side has a unique solution.

To calculate it, temporarily solve $B\mathbf x=\mathbf b$ with general $\mathbf b=(b_1,b_2,b_3)^T$. Subtracting the second equation from the third and from the first gives

$$
2x_2=b_3-b_2,\qquad x_1-x_2=b_1-b_2.
$$

Back-substitution into the second equation gives

$$
x_1=b_1-\frac32b_2+\frac12b_3,\quad x_2=-\frac12b_2+\frac12b_3,\quad x_3=-b_1+\frac72b_2-\frac32b_3.
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{B^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&-3/2&1/2\\0&-1/2&1/2\\-1&7/2&-3/2\end{pmatrix}.}
$$

For the given right-hand side, [matrix multiplication](../../vector-space.md#matrix-multiplication) yields the unique answer

$$
\boxed{\mathbf x=B^{-1}(1,2,3)^T=(-1/2,1/2,3/2)^T.}
$$

<h3 id="18/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [18](#18)

<h4 id="18/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#18/b)

<h5 id="18/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#18/b/i)

The three coordinate [basis vectors](../../vector-space.md#basis-vector) are [eigenvectors](../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvector), so $M$ is diagonal in those coordinates. Exactly one coordinate is multiplied by $-1$, while the other two are fixed. The three possibilities are

$$
\boxed{M=\operatorname{diag}(-1,1,1),\quad\operatorname{diag}(1,-1,1),\quad\operatorname{diag}(1,1,-1).}
$$

They are respectively reflections in the coordinate [planes](../../geometry-and-topology.md#plane) $x=0$, $y=0$ and $z=0$. Each is a [Householder reflection](../../linear-algebra.md#householder-transformation) with [unit normal](../../differential-geometry.md#unit-normal) equal to the coordinate [eigenvector](../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvector) carrying the [eigenvalue](../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) $-1$.

<h4 id="18/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#18/b)

<h5 id="18/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#18/b/ii)

For each possibility in part (i), squaring the diagonal entries gives $M^2=I$. Equivalently, the [linear transformation](../../vector-space.md#linear-map) fixes a [vector space basis](../../vector-space.md#basis) after being applied twice. Therefore

$$
\boxed{M^2\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\\z\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\\z\end{pmatrix}.}
$$

Geometrically, reflecting twice in the same [plane](../../geometry-and-topology.md#plane) returns every [vector](../../vector-space.md#vector) to itself.

## 19

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="19/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [19](#19)

<h4 id="19/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#19/a)

Apply the [Leibniz rule](../../calculus.md#leibniz-rule) for the $n$th [derivative](../../calculus.md#derivative) of a product. Only the first three terms survive, because all [derivatives](../../calculus.md#derivative) of $x^2$ of order greater than two vanish and every [derivative](../../calculus.md#derivative) of $e^x$ is $e^x$:

$$
\frac{d^n}{dx^n}(x^2e^x)=e^x\left[\binom n0x^2+\binom n1(2x)+\binom n2(2)\right]=e^x[x^2+2nx+n(n-1)].
$$

At $n=100$ this gives

$$
\boxed{\frac{d^{100}}{dx^{100}}(x^2e^x)=e^x(x^2+200x+9900).}
$$

<h3 id="19/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [19](#19)

<h4 id="19/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#19/b)

<h5 id="19/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#19/b/i)

The [epsilon-delta definition of continuity](../../calculus.md#epsilon-delta-definition-of-continuity) says that $f$ is continuous at $a$ if

$$
\boxed{\text{for every }\varepsilon>0\text{ there exists }\delta>0\text{ such that }|x-a|<\delta\implies|f(x)-f(a)|<\varepsilon.}
$$

Here $a$ belongs to the domain of $f$, and the implication is required for every $x$ in that domain. The number $\delta$ may depend on $\varepsilon$ and $a$, but must work for all such $x$ simultaneously.

<h4 id="19/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#19/b)

<h5 id="19/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#19/b/ii)

The bound $|\cos(1/x)|\leq1$ gives $|f(x)-f(0)|\leq|x|^p$ when $x\ne0$. If $p>0$, given any $\varepsilon>0$, choose $\delta=\varepsilon^{1/p}$. Then $0<|x|<\delta$ implies

$$
|f(x)-f(0)|\leq|x|^p<\delta^p=\varepsilon;
$$

the inequality also holds at $x=0$. This proves [continuity](../../calculus.md#continuous-function) using the [epsilon-delta definition of continuity](../../calculus.md#epsilon-delta-definition-of-continuity).

For necessity, take the positive [sequence](../../sequence-and-series.md#sequence) $x_n=1/(2\pi n)\to0$. Then $\cos(1/x_n)=1$, so $f(x_n)=(2\pi n)^{-p}$. For $p=0$ this equals $1$; for $p<0$ it tends to $+\infty$. In neither case does it tend to $f(0)=0$, so [continuity](../../calculus.md#continuous-function) fails. Consequently

$$
\boxed{f\text{ is continuous at }0\iff p>0.}
$$

This is an instance of the general [continuity and differentiability thresholds for a damped oscillation](../../analysis.md#continuity-and-differentiability-thresholds-for-a-damped-oscillation).

<h4 id="19/b/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#19/b)

<h5 id="19/b/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#19/b/iii)

The [difference quotient](../../calculus.md#difference-quotient) at zero is

$$
\frac{f(h)-f(0)}h=\frac{|h|^p}{h}\cos(1/h),\qquad h\ne0.
$$

If $p>1$, its [absolute value](../../real-analysis.md#absolute-value) is at most $|h|^{p-1}\to0$, so $f$ is [differentiable](../../analysis.md#differentiable-function) at zero with [derivative](../../calculus.md#derivative) $0$.

To exclude $p\leq1$, use $h_n=1/(2\pi n)$ and $k_n=1/(2\pi n+\pi/2)$. The [difference quotient](../../calculus.md#difference-quotient) equals $(2\pi n)^{1-p}$ along the first [sequence](../../sequence-and-series.md#sequence), and $0$ along the second. For $p=1$ the first value is $1$, while for $p<1$ it is unbounded. Hence a common finite [limit](../../calculus.md#limit-of-a-function) is impossible. Therefore

$$
\boxed{f\text{ is differentiable at }0\iff p>1,\qquad f'(0)=0\text{ when }p>1.}
$$

<h3 id="19/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [19](#19)

<h4 id="19/c/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#19/c)

<h5 id="19/c/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#19/c/i)

For the positive terms $a_n=n^4/3^n$, the consecutive ratio is

$$
\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}=\frac13\left(1+\frac1n\right)^4\longrightarrow\frac13<1.
$$

The [ratio test](../../sequence-and-series.md#ratio-test) therefore proves that **the [series](../../sequence-and-series.md#series-mathematics) converges absolutely**. More explicitly, for all sufficiently large $n$ the ratio is at most a fixed $q<1$, so the tail is bounded by a convergent [geometric series](../../sequence-and-series.md#geometric-series).

<h4 id="19/c/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#19/c)

<h5 id="19/c/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#19/c/ii)

Compare the positive terms with those of the [harmonic series](../../sequence-and-series.md#harmonic-series):

$$
\frac{(n^2+2n)/(n^3+3n^2+1)}{1/n}=\frac{n^3+2n^2}{n^3+3n^2+1}\longrightarrow1.
$$

By the [limit comparison test](../../sequence-and-series.md#limit-comparison-test), the given [series](../../sequence-and-series.md#series-mathematics) has the same convergence behaviour as $\sum1/n$, which diverges. Thus **the [series](../../sequence-and-series.md#series-mathematics) diverges to $+\infty$**.

<h3 id="20/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [20](#20)

<h4 id="20/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#20/a)

The [Leibniz integral rule](../../analysis.md#leibniz-integral-rule) combines contributions from both moving endpoints with [differentiation under the integral sign](../../analysis.md#differentiation-under-the-integral-sign):

$$
\boxed{\frac d{dx}\int_{a(x)}^{b(x)}f(x,t)\,dt=f(x,b(x))b'(x)-f(x,a(x))a'(x)+\int_{a(x)}^{b(x)}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(x,t)\,dt.}
$$

The [partial derivative](../../multivariable-calculus.md#partial-derivative) in the last [integral](../../integral.md) holds $t$ fixed. Suitable regularity and convergence are assumed as stipulated.

<h3 id="20/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [20](#20)

<h4 id="20/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#20/b)

<h5 id="20/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#20/b/i)

For the [Leibniz integral rule](../../analysis.md#leibniz-integral-rule), the upper endpoint has [derivative](../../calculus.md#derivative) $2\pi x$ and the lower endpoint has [derivative](../../calculus.md#derivative) $1$. The integrand has [partial derivative](../../multivariable-calculus.md#partial-derivative) $4xt+\sin t$. Therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
h'(x)={}&\left(2\pi x^4+x\sin(\pi x^2)\right)2\pi x-\left(2x^3+x\sin x\right)\\
&+\int_x^{\pi x^2}(4xt+\sin t)\,dt.
\end{aligned}
$$

The last [integral](../../integral.md) is $[2xt^2-\cos t]_x^{\pi x^2}=2\pi^2x^5-2x^3-\cos(\pi x^2)+\cos x$. Combining terms gives

$$
\boxed{h'(x)=6\pi^2x^5-4x^3+2\pi x^2\sin(\pi x^2)-x\sin x+\cos x-\cos(\pi x^2).}
$$

<h4 id="20/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#20/b)

<h5 id="20/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#20/b/ii)

Holding $x$ fixed when taking the [antiderivative](../../integral.md#antiderivative) with respect to $t$ gives

$$
h(x)=[x^2t^2-x\cos t]_x^{\pi x^2}=\pi^2x^6-x^4-x\cos(\pi x^2)+x\cos x.

Use the [product rule](../../calculus.md#product-rule) and the [chain rule](../../calculus.md#chain-rule) to differentiate this explicit expression:

$$
\boxed{h'(x)=6\pi^2x^5-4x^3-\cos(\pi x^2)+2\pi x^2\sin(\pi x^2)+\cos x-x\sin x.}

This agrees term by term with part (i).

<h3 id="20/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [20](#20)

<h4 id="20/c/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#20/c)

<h5 id="20/c/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#20/c/i)

Write $I(x)=\ln g(x)$. For $x>-1$, [differentiation under the integral sign](../../analysis.md#differentiation-under-the-integral-sign) gives

$$
I'(x)=\int_0^1\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\frac{t^x-1}{\ln t}\right)dt=\int_0^1t^x\,dt=\frac1{x+1}.
$$

Indeed, on any compact parameter [interval](../../real-analysis.md#interval-mathematics) $c\leq x\leq d$ with $c>-1$, the differentiated integrand is bounded by the integrable [function](../../function.md) $t^c$. The [dominated convergence theorem](../../measure-theory.md#dominated-convergence-theorem) therefore justifies the operation despite the improper lower endpoint. The apparent singularity at $t=1$ is removable.

Since $g=e^I$, the [chain rule](../../calculus.md#chain-rule) gives the [first-order linear differential equation](../../linear-ordinary-differential-equation.md#first-order-linear-differential-equation)

$$
\boxed{g'(x)=\frac{g(x)}{x+1},\qquad x>-1.}
$$

<h4 id="20/c/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#20/c)

<h5 id="20/c/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#20/c/ii)

The required range is

$$
\boxed{x>-1.}
$$

Near $t=1$, the ratio $(t^x-1)/\ln t$ tends to $x$, so it causes no convergence problem. Near $t=0$, one useful identity is

$$
\frac{t^x-1}{\ln t}=\int_0^x t^s\,ds.
$$

For $x>-1$, this bounds its [absolute value](../../real-analysis.md#absolute-value) by $|x|t^{\min(x,0)}$, which is integrable on $(0,1)$. It also supplies the domination used in part (i).

For $x=-1$, the integrand is negative and behaves as $-1/[t|\ln t|]$ near zero; its [improper integral](../../real-analysis.md#improper-integral) diverges. For $x<-1$ its negative singularity is even stronger, and comparison with that same divergent [integral](../../integral.md) again rules out convergence. Thus the original finite real-valued definition of $g$ has precisely the stated domain.

<h4 id="20/c/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#20/c)

<h5 id="20/c/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#20/c/iii)

At $x=0$, the integrand vanishes for $0<t<1$, so $I(0)=0$ and

$$
\boxed{g(0)=1.}
$$

Solving the [first-order linear differential equation](../../linear-ordinary-differential-equation.md#first-order-linear-differential-equation) from part (i) on $(-1,\infty)$ gives $g=C(x+1)$. The [initial condition](../../differential-equation.md#initial-condition) fixes $C=1$, so

$$
\boxed{g(x)=x+1,\qquad x>-1.}
$$

Equivalently, the defining exponent is the [logarithmic integral representation of the natural logarithm](../../calculus.md#logarithmic-integral-representation-of-the-natural-logarithm), $I(x)=\ln(x+1)$.

<h4 id="20/c/iv">iv</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#20/c)

<h5 id="20/c/iv/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#20/c/iv)

For $x>-1$, interpret the endpoint $-1$ by a one-sided limit. Part (iii) shows that $g$ extends continuously there with value zero, so both nested [integrals](../../integral.md) converge. Let $F(x)$ denote the nested expression inside the [derivative](../../calculus.md#derivative). The [fundamental theorem of calculus](../../calculus.md#fundamental-theorem-of-calculus), applied twice with moving lower endpoints, gives

$$
F'(x)=-\int_x^{-1}g(t)\,dt,\qquad F''(x)=g(x),\qquad F^{(3)}(x)=g'(x).
$$

By part (iii), $g'(x)=1$, hence

$$
\boxed{F^{(3)}(x)=1\qquad(x>-1).}
$$

A direct check is $\int_s^{-1}(t+1)\,dt=-(s+1)^2/2$, so $F(x)=(x+1)^3/6$, with the same third [derivative](../../calculus.md#derivative).
