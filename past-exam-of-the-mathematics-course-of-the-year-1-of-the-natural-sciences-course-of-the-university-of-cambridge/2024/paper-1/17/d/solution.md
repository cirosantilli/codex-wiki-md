<h1 id="17/d/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [D](../d.md)

On an [interval](../../../../../interval-mathematics.md) where $\tan x$ is defined, use the [substitution rule](../../../../../integration-by-substitution.md) $t=\tan x$. Then $dx=dt/(1+t^2)$ and $\cos^2x=1/(1+t^2)$, giving

$$
\int\frac{dx}{1+3\cos^2x}=\int\frac{dt}{t^2+4}=\boxed{\frac12\arctan\left(\frac{\tan x}{2}\right)+C.}
$$

The [inverse tangent](../../../../../inverse-tangent.md) expression is valid on each [interval](../../../../../interval-mathematics.md) $(j\pi-\pi/2,j\pi+\pi/2)$ separately. The original integrand is continuous for all real $x$, since its denominator is at least $1$. To obtain one continuous [antiderivative](../../../../../antiderivative.md) on the whole real axis, add $j\pi/2$ on the $j$th such [interval](../../../../../interval-mathematics.md) and use the continuous limits at their endpoints.
