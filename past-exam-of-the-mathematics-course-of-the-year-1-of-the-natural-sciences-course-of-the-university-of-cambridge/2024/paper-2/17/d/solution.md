<h1 id="17/d/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [D](../d.md)

Call the three [planes](../../../../../plane.md) $P_1,P_2,P_3$ in their given order.

For $\alpha\ne3,-1/2$, the nonzero [determinant](../../../../../determinant.md) makes their [normal vectors](../../../../../normal-vector.md) [linearly independent](../../../../../linear-independence.md). The three [planes](../../../../../plane.md) meet at the single point found in part (c)(i), for every $\beta$.

For $\alpha=3$, $P_2$ and $P_3$ coincide. Their common [plane](../../../../../plane.md) and $P_1$ have nonparallel [normal vectors](../../../../../normal-vector.md), since their first two coefficients are $(1,-1)$ and $(1,6)$. Thus they intersect in the [straight line](../../../../../straight-line.md) from part (c)(ii), for every $\beta$.

For $\alpha=-1/2$, write the three [plane](../../../../../plane.md) equations as

$$
P_1:x-y+\beta z=1,\qquad P_2:x-y+3z=-1,\qquad P_3:x-y-\tfrac12z=-1.
$$

The last two meet on $z=0$, $x-y=-1$, which never lies on $P_1$. If $\beta=3$, $P_1$ and $P_2$ are distinct parallel [planes](../../../../../plane.md), while $P_3$ cuts both. If $\beta=-1/2$, $P_1$ and $P_3$ are distinct parallel [planes](../../../../../plane.md), while $P_2$ cuts both. For every other $\beta$, each pair of [planes](../../../../../plane.md) intersects in a [straight line](../../../../../straight-line.md); these three pairwise intersection lines are distinct and parallel to $(1,1,0)^T$, with no common point. These exhaust the geometric cases.
