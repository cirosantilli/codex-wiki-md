<h3 id="1/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

Bring all terms to the left and factor the [quadratic polynomial](../../polynomial.md#quadratic-polynomial):

$$
2x^2+x-6=(2x-3)(x+2).
$$

Its leading coefficient is positive, so it is nonpositive between its two [polynomial roots](../../polynomial.md#root-of-a-polynomial), including the endpoints. Therefore

$$
\boxed{-2\le x\le\frac32.}
$$

## 2

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="2/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

Complete the squares in the two coordinates:

$$
(x-2)^2-4+4(y-1)^2-4=-4,
\qquad\frac{(x-2)^2}{4}+(y-1)^2=1.
$$

This standard equation of an [ellipse](../../euclidean-geometry.md#ellipse) is symmetric about $x=2$ and $y=1$. Its centre is therefore

$$
\boxed{(2,1).}
$$

<h4 id="3/a/solution">Solution</h4>

At a point of tangency, the [derivative](../../calculus.md#derivative) of the curve equals the [slope](../../euclidean-geometry.md#slope) one of the straight line. The [chain rule](../../calculus.md#chain-rule) gives $2\cos2x=1$. Since $0\le2x\le\pi$, the unique solution is $x=\pi/6$. The corresponding height is $\sin(\pi/3)=\sqrt3/2$. Substitution in the [tangent line](../../calculus.md#tangent-line) gives

$$
\boxed{c=\frac{\sqrt3}{2}-\frac\pi6.}
$$

<h3 id="3/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

For finite nonzero [slopes](../../euclidean-geometry.md#slope), their product is $-1$. The tangent has [slope](../../euclidean-geometry.md#slope) one, so the normal line has

\boxed{\text{slope}=-1.}

The two equal legs meet at the [right angle](../../euclidean-geometry.md#right-angle), so the [Pythagorean theorem](../../euclidean-geometry.md#pythagorean-theorem) gives $AB^2+AC^2=BC^2=4$, with $AB=AC$. Thus $AC=\sqrt2$. The midpoint condition then yields

<h3 id="4/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#4)

<h4 id="4/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#4/b)

The triangle $ADB$ is right-angled at $A$, with $AB=\sqrt2$ and $AD=1/\sqrt2$. Another application of the [Pythagorean theorem](../../euclidean-geometry.md#pythagorean-theorem) gives $DB=\sqrt{2+1/2}=\sqrt{5/2}$. At the [angle](../../geometry-and-topology.md#angle) with vertex $D$, the adjacent side is $AD$ and the hypotenuse is $DB$. Hence

$$
\boxed{\cos\angle ADB=\frac{AD}{DB}=\frac1{\sqrt5}.}
$$

The [double-angle formula](../../geometry-and-topology.md#double-angle-formula) gives $\cos2x=1-2\sin^2x$. Setting $s=\sin x$ reduces the equation to $2s^2+s-1=(2s-1)(s+1)=0$. On $0\le x\le\pi$, the [sine](../../geometry-and-topology.md#sine) is nonnegative, so only $s=1/2$ is allowed. Therefore

$$
\boxed{x=\frac\pi6\quad\text{or}\quad x=\frac{5\pi}6.}
$$

↑ **Parent:** [6](#6)

Use [integration by parts](../../calculus.md#integration-by-parts) with $u=\ln x$ and $dv=x^2\,dx$. This gives the [antiderivative](../../integral.md#antiderivative)

$$
\int x^2\ln x\,dx=\frac{x^3}{3}\ln x-\frac13\int x^2\,dx
=\frac{x^3}{3}\ln x-\frac{x^3}{9}+C.
$$

Evaluating at the two bounds, with $\ln1=0$, yields

$$
\boxed{\int_1^y x^2\ln x\,dx=\frac{y^3}{3}\ln y-\frac{y^3-1}{9}.}
$$

## 8

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="8/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [8](#8)

Multiply the [partial fraction decomposition](../../isolated-singularity.md#partial-fraction-decomposition) by $(x+1)(x+2)^2$ to obtain a [polynomial identity](../../polynomial.md#polynomial-identity):

$$
x^2+1=a(x+2)^2+b(x+1)(x+2)-5(x+1).
$$

At $x=-1$, this gives $a=2$. Comparing the coefficients of $x^2$ then gives $a+b=1$, so

$$
\boxed{a=2,\qquad b=-1.}
$$

The remaining coefficients agree: $4a+3b-5=0$ and $4a+2b-5=1$. The original rational identity is understood away from its [poles](../../isolated-singularity.md#pole) $-1$ and $-2$.

## 9

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="9/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [9](#9)

<h4 id="9/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#9/a)

By [Thales's theorem](../../euclidean-geometry.md#thales-s-theorem), the two sides meeting on the circular arc are [orthogonal](../../inner-product.md#orthogonal-vectors), because the opposite side is a diameter. Both [vectors](../../vector-space.md#vector) point towards that meeting point; reversing both does not change their [dot product](../../inner-product.md#dot-product). Consequently

$$
\boxed{\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=0.}
$$

<h3 id="9/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [9](#9)

<h4 id="9/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#9/b)

Let the semicircle have radius $R$, and write $A=|\mathbf a|$, $B=|\mathbf b|$. The [right triangle](../../euclidean-geometry.md#right-triangle) has hypotenuse $2R$, so the [Pythagorean theorem](../../euclidean-geometry.md#pythagorean-theorem) gives $A^2+B^2=4R^2$. Its [area](../../differential-geometry.md#surface-area) is $AB/2$, whereas half the [area](../../differential-geometry.md#surface-area) of the semicircle is $\pi R^2/4$. Equating these and eliminating $R$ gives

$$
\boxed{AB=\frac\pi8(A^2+B^2).}
$$

Equivalently, the relation between the two [norms](../../normed-vector-space.md#norm) can be written

$$
\boxed{\frac AB=\frac{4\pm\sqrt{16-\pi^2}}{\pi}.}
$$

The two possible ratios are reciprocals, corresponding to interchanging the two sides.

<h3 id="11/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [11](#11)

<h4 id="11/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#11/a)

Put $c=\cos\theta$ and $s=\sin\theta$. Taking the [real part](../../complex-analysis.md#real-part) of $(c+is)^6$ in [De Moivre's theorem](../../analysis.md#de-moivre-s-theorem) gives

$$
\cos6\theta=c^6-15c^4s^2+15c^2s^4-s^6.
$$

Taking the [imaginary parts](../../complex-analysis.md#imaginary-part) of the second and fourth powers gives $\sin2\theta=2cs$ and $\sin4\theta=4cs(c^2-s^2)$. Hence

$$
\begin{aligned}
8\cos6\theta+15\sin4\theta\sin2\theta
&=8(c^6-15c^4s^2+15c^2s^4-s^6)+120c^2s^2(c^2-s^2)\\
&=\boxed{8(\cos^6\theta-\sin^6\theta).}
\end{aligned}
$$

The mixed powers cancel exactly.

<h3 id="11/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [11](#11)

<h4 id="11/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#11/b)

The [complex conjugate](../../complex-analysis.md#complex-conjugate) is $z^*=x-iy$. From the complex [sine](../../geometry-and-topology.md#sine) and [cosine](../../geometry-and-topology.md#cosine) identities,

$$
\sin(x-iy)=\sin x\cosh y-i\cos x\sinh y,\qquad
\cos(x-iy)=\cos x\cosh y+i\sin x\sinh y.
$$

Divide these expressions and multiply by the [complex conjugate](../../complex-analysis.md#complex-conjugate) of the denominator. Its squared [modulus](../../complex-analysis.md#modulus) is $\cos^2x\cosh^2y+\sin^2x\sinh^2y=(\cos2x+\cosh2y)/2$. The numerator simplifies using $\cosh^2y-\sinh^2y=1$ and the [double-angle formulas](../../geometry-and-topology.md#double-angle-formula). Thus

$$
\tan(z^*)=\frac{\sin2x-i\sinh2y}{\cos2x+\cosh2y},
\qquad\boxed{\Re\tan(z^*)=\frac{\sin2x}{\cos2x+\cosh2y},\quad\Im\tan(z^*)=-\frac{\sinh2y}{\cos2x+\cosh2y}.}
$$

These expressions apply except at the [poles](../../isolated-singularity.md#pole) $y=0$, $x=\pi/2+k\pi$ with integer $k$, where the original [tangent function](../../geometry-and-topology.md#tangent-function) is undefined.

<h3 id="11/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [11](#11)

<h4 id="11/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#11/d)

Take $r>0$ and select a [complex argument](../../complex-analysis.md#argument-complex-analysis) $\theta$ for $z$. To specify the [complex exponentiation](../../analysis.md#complex-exponentiation), use $z^{1+i}=\exp((1+i)(\ln r+i\theta))$. Expanding its exponent gives

$$
z^{1+i}=e^{\ln r-\theta}e^{i(\theta+\ln r)}.
$$

Its [complex logarithm](../../analysis.md#complex-logarithm) therefore has values

$$
\boxed{\Re f=\ln r-\theta,\qquad\Im f=\theta+\ln r+2\pi k,\quad k\in\mathbb Z.}
$$

The continuous logarithm obtained by retaining the displayed exponent uses $k=0$. If the outer logarithm is the [principal logarithm](../../analysis.md#principal-complex-logarithm), choose $k$ to put the [imaginary part](../../complex-analysis.md#imaginary-part) in $(-\pi,\pi]$ instead; the [real part](../../complex-analysis.md#real-part) is unchanged. In particular one cannot generally assert $\operatorname{Log}(e^w)=w$ without checking its [imaginary part](../../complex-analysis.md#imaginary-part).

The condition on the [real part](../../complex-analysis.md#real-part) gives $\ln r=\theta$, so the required [logarithmic spiral](../../topology.md#logarithmic-spiral) has [polar coordinates](../../multivariable-calculus.md#polar-coordinates)

$$
\boxed{r=e^\theta,\qquad z(\theta)=e^\theta(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta).}
$$

As $\theta$ increases, it runs from the negative [real axis](../../complex-analysis.md#real-axis) near radius $e^{-\pi}$ through $-ie^{-\pi/2}$, $1$ and $ie^{\pi/2}$ to the negative [real axis](../../complex-analysis.md#real-axis) at radius $e^\pi$. The [angle](../../geometry-and-topology.md#angle) increases by one complete turn. Using the conventional [principal argument](../../complex-analysis.md#principal-argument) $-\pi<\theta\le\pi$, the inner negative-axis endpoint is excluded and the outer endpoint is included. The convention $-\pi\le\theta<\pi$ reverses those endpoint choices. If both limiting arguments are admitted by the paper's wording, both limiting endpoints are drawn; the interior curve is the same. The origin is never included because the [complex logarithm](../../analysis.md#complex-logarithm) is undefined there.

## 12

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="12/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [12](#12)

<h4 id="12/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#12/a)

The height is constant along each circular ring, whose [area element](../../differential-geometry.md#area-element-of-a-surface) after angular integration is $2\pi r\,dr$. The [volume integral](../../multivariable-calculus.md#volume-integral) in [cylindrical coordinates](../../multivariable-calculus.md#cylindrical-coordinate-system) is therefore

$$
V=2\pi\int_0^R rR e^{-r/R}\,dr=2\pi R^3\int_0^1ue^{-u}\,du.
$$

Here $u=r/R$. Using [integration by parts](../../calculus.md#integration-by-parts), $\int_0^1ue^{-u}\,du=[-(u+1)e^{-u}]_0^1=1-2/e$. Hence

$$
\boxed{R=\left[\frac{V}{2\pi(1-2/e)}\right]^{1/3}.}
$$

The dimensionless coefficient is positive, and the cube root of a [volume](../../geometry-and-topology.md#volume) has the required [physical dimension](../../mathematical-physics.md#physical-dimension) of [length](../../physics.md#length).

<h3 id="12/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [12](#12)

<h4 id="12/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#12/b)

Integrate by horizontal slices. At a fixed height $0\le z\le H$, the parabolic boundary gives $|x|\le L\sqrt{z/H}$; the other inequality gives $|y|\le b(2-z/H)$. Thus the cross-section is a rectangle of [area](../../differential-geometry.md#surface-area)

$$
A(z)=4Lb\sqrt{\frac zH}\left(2-\frac zH\right).
$$

The [volume integral](../../multivariable-calculus.md#volume-integral), followed by [integration by substitution](../../integral.md#integration-by-substitution) $u=z/H$, is

$$
\begin{aligned}
V_{\rm bank}&=\int_0^H A(z)\,dz
=4LbH\int_0^1(2u^{1/2}-u^{3/2})\,du\\
&=4LbH\left(\frac43-\frac25\right)
=\boxed{\frac{56}{15}LbH.}
\end{aligned}
$$

This has units of [volume](../../geometry-and-topology.md#volume). As a check on the geometry, the width decreases with height while the length of the horizontal slice increases from zero to $2L$.

<h3 id="12/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [12](#12)

<h4 id="12/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#12/c)

In the $x,z$ plane, the embankment lies above the [parabola](../../euclidean-geometry.md#parabola) $z=Hx^2/L^2$ and below $z=H$. The bore has circular cross-section

$$
\mathcal D=\left\{(x,z):x^2+(z-D/2)^2\le D^2/4\right\}.
$$

It touches the valley floor at the origin. To check that this entire [disk](../../topology.md#disk-mathematics) lies inside the projected embankment, its equation gives $x^2\le Dz-z^2\le Dz$. Since $HD/L^2<1$, it follows that $Hx^2/L^2\le z$, with strict inequality away from the bottom contact. Also $z\le D<H$. Thus no circular segment needs to be subtracted from the tunnel section.

For each $(x,z)$ in the [disk](../../topology.md#disk-mathematics), the length of bore inside the material is the local width $2b(2-z/H)$. The required [volume integral](../../multivariable-calculus.md#volume-integral) is therefore

$$
V_{\rm cut}=\iint_{\mathcal D}2b\left(2-\frac zH\right)\,dx\,dz.
$$

The [disk](../../topology.md#disk-mathematics) has [area](../../differential-geometry.md#surface-area) $A=\pi D^2/4$ and [centroid](../../euclidean-geometry.md#centroid) height $D/2$, so $\iint_{\mathcal D}z\,dx\,dz=AD/2$. Applying the [volume of a solid with affine thickness](../../multivariable-calculus.md#volume-of-a-solid-with-affine-thickness) formula gives

$$

Write the [scalar field](../../classical-field-theory.md#scalar-field) as $u=R(r)Y(\theta,\phi)$, where $R=r+r^{-2}$ and $Y=\sin\theta\cos\phi$. Its radial contribution to the [Laplacian](../../multivariable-calculus.md#laplacian) in [spherical coordinates](../../multivariable-calculus.md#spherical-coordinate-system) is

Work on an open domain with $xy>0$ and $x\ne0$, where the displayed square root is real and differentiable. Split $u=v+h$ with $v=\phi(xy)$ and $h=\sqrt{xy}\,\psi(y/x)$. For the first term, the [chain rule](../../calculus.md#chain-rule) gives

$$
v_{xx}=y^2\phi''(xy),\qquad v_{yy}=x^2\phi''(xy),
$$

so $x^2v_{xx}-y^2v_{yy}=0$.

For the second term, set $t=y/x$. Its first [partial derivatives](../../multivariable-calculus.md#partial-derivative) are

$$
h_x=\frac{\sqrt{xy}}x\left(\frac12\psi(t)-t\psi'(t)\right),\qquad
h_y=\frac{\sqrt{xy}}y\left(\frac12\psi(t)+t\psi'(t)\right).
$$

They show $xh_x+yh_y=h$, the degree-one case of [Euler theorem for homogeneous functions](../../real-analysis.md#euler-theorem-for-homogeneous-functions). Differentiating that identity with respect to each variable yields

$$
xh_{xx}+yh_{xy}=0,\qquad xh_{xy}+yh_{yy}=0.
$$

The mixed [partial derivatives](../../multivariable-calculus.md#partial-derivative) agree here by the [chain rule](../../calculus.md#chain-rule). Multiplying these equations by $x$ and $y$, respectively, proves the [second derivative identity for a degree-one homogeneous function](../../real-analysis.md#second-derivative-identity-for-a-degree-one-homogeneous-function):

$$
x^2h_{xx}=-xyh_{xy}=y^2h_{yy}.
$$

Adding the results for $v$ and $h$ verifies the [partial differential equation](../../partial-differential-equation.md)

$$
\boxed{x^2u_{xx}-y^2u_{yy}=0.}
$$

Equivalently, $h(\lambda x,\lambda y)=\lambda h(x,y)$ for positive $\lambda$, so the same proof follows directly from its [homogeneous function](../../real-analysis.md#homogeneous-function) property. The statement is local to a domain where the given expression is defined; it does not require assigning values on $x=0$.

The finite form of [Taylor's theorem](../../calculus.md#taylor-s-theorem) is

$$
\boxed{f(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^k+R_n(x),\qquad R_n(x)=\frac{f^{(n)}(\xi)}{n!}(x-a)^n,}

where $\xi$ lies between $a$ and $x$ when $x\ne a$. Sufficient hypotheses are continuous [derivatives](../../calculus.md#derivative) up to order $n-1$ on the interval joining the endpoints and an $n$th [derivative](../../calculus.md#derivative) in its interior. The first $n$ terms end at degree $n-1$; the [Taylor remainder](../../calculus.md#taylor-remainder) uses the $n$th [derivative](../../calculus.md#derivative). At $x=a$ the remainder is zero. This finite [Taylor polynomial](../../calculus.md#taylor-polynomial) identity does not require convergence of an infinite [Taylor series](../../calculus.md#taylor-series).

$$

$$

The interpretation is that removing a non-blue ball increases the chance of blue on the second draw; seeing the two different colours therefore favours the no-replacement explanation.

<h5 id="18/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#18/a/i)

Since $A^2=-I$, the [matrix inverse](../../matrix-inverse.md) is $A^{-1}=-A$. The [orthogonal matrix](../../linear-algebra.md#orthogonal-matrix) condition also gives $A^{-1}=A^{\mathsf T}$, so $A^{\mathsf T}=-A$. Thus $A$ is [skew-symmetric](../../linear-algebra.md#skew-symmetric-matrix), forcing $a=0$ and $c=-b$. Its columns must be [unit vectors](../../vector-space.md#unit-vector), giving $b^2=1$. Therefore the two possibilities are

$$
\boxed{A=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{or}\quad A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}.}
$$

Both satisfy the original conditions by direct multiplication. They are the two [orthogonal skew-symmetric matrices](../../linear-algebra.md#orthogonal-skew-symmetric-matrix) in two dimensions, representing opposite quarter-turn [rotations](../../riemannian-geometry.md#rotation-mathematics).

<h4 id="18/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#18/a)

<h5 id="18/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#18/a/ii)

For either $A$ from part (i), choose

$$
\boxed{B=-\frac{I+A}{\sqrt2}.}
$$

Indeed, using $A^2=-I$ and $A^{\mathsf T}=-A$,

$$
B^2=\frac{I+2A+A^2}{2}=A,\qquad
B^{\mathsf T}B=\frac{(I-A)(I+A)}2=I.
$$

Its [matrix trace](../../linear-algebra.md#matrix-trace) is $-2/\sqrt2=-\sqrt2<0$. Explicitly, for the two $A$ matrices in the order displayed above, the corresponding [orthogonal matrices](../../linear-algebra.md#orthogonal-matrix) are

$$
\boxed{B=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}-1&1\\-1&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
B=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}-1&-1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}.}
$$

Their negatives have positive [matrix trace](../../linear-algebra.md#matrix-trace) and would not meet the additional sign condition.

<h3 id="18/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [18](#18)

<h4 id="18/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#18/b)

<h5 id="18/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#18/b/i)

Each prediction is a linear combination of the constant parameter and the coefficient of $x_i$. Thus the [design matrix](../../linear-regression.md#design-matrix) is

$$
\boxed{M=\begin{pmatrix}1&x_1\\1&x_2\\\vdots&\vdots\\1&x_n\end{pmatrix}.}
$$

Its $i$th row multiplied by the parameter [vector](../../vector-space.md#vector) $(\alpha,\beta)^{\mathsf T}$ gives the required prediction $\alpha+\beta x_i$.

<h4 id="18/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#18/b)

<h5 id="18/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#18/b/ii)

Compute the two sides of the [normal equations for linear least squares](../../linear-regression.md#normal-equations-for-linear-least-squares) and divide by $n$:

$$
\begin{pmatrix}1&\overline x\\\overline x&\overline{x^2}\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\\beta\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}\overline y\\\overline{xy}\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The first equation gives $\alpha=\overline y-\beta\overline x$. Substitute this into the second and solve:

$$
\boxed{\beta=\frac{\overline{xy}-\overline x\,\overline y}{\overline{x^2}-\overline x^{\,2}},\qquad
\alpha=\frac{\overline{x^2}\,\overline y-\overline x\,\overline{xy}}{\overline{x^2}-\overline x^{\,2}}.}
$$

These [simple linear regression](../../linear-regression.md#simple-linear-regression) coefficients are unique provided the observed distances are not all equal, since

$$
\overline{x^2}-\overline x^{\,2}=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\overline x)^2>0.
$$

The condition $n\ge2$ alone does not guarantee this. If all $x_i=x_0$, the [design matrix](../../linear-regression.md#design-matrix) has [matrix rank](../../vector-space.md#matrix-rank) one and the valid alternative is the family

$$
\boxed{\alpha+x_0\beta=\overline y,\qquad\beta\text{ arbitrary}.}
$$

In this [rank-deficient ordinary least squares](../../normal-linear-model.md#rank-deficient-ordinary-least-squares) case, the fitted predictions are uniquely $\overline y$ but the two parameters cannot be separately determined.

## 19

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="19/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [19](#19)

<h4 id="19/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#19/a)

<h5 id="19/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#19/a/i)

For every integer $n$, $\cos((2n-1)\pi)=-1$. The [series](../../sequence-and-series.md#series-mathematics) is therefore the negative of the [harmonic series](../../sequence-and-series.md#harmonic-series):

$$
\sum_{n=1}^N\frac{\cos((2n-1)\pi)}n=-\sum_{n=1}^N\frac1n.
$$

For a direct divergence argument, each block $2^{j-1}<n\le2^j$ contributes at least $2^{j-1}/2^j=1/2$ to the positive sum. Its [partial sums](../../sequence-and-series.md#partial-sum) grow without bound, so

$$
\boxed{\text{the given series diverges to }-\infty.}
$$

Despite the odd multiples of $\pi$, the signs do not alternate.

<h4 id="19/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#19/a)

<h5 id="19/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#19/a/ii)

For each fixed $n$, the inner [geometric series](../../sequence-and-series.md#geometric-series) sums to $2^{-n}/(1-1/2)=2^{1-n}$. Summing this second [geometric series](../../sequence-and-series.md#geometric-series) gives

$$
\boxed{\sum_{n=0}^\infty\sum_{m=n}^\infty2^{-m}=\sum_{n=0}^\infty2^{1-n}=4.}
$$

All terms are nonnegative, so there is no conditional-rearrangement issue; the iterated sum is finite and the [double series](../../sequence-and-series.md#double-series) converges. Equivalently, collecting equal values of $m$ gives $\sum_{m=0}^\infty(m+1)2^{-m}=4$.

<h3 id="19/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [19](#19)

<h4 id="19/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#19/b)

<h5 id="19/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#19/b/i)

The positive [polynomial root](../../polynomial.md#root-of-a-polynomial) is $x_*=\varepsilon+\sqrt{1+\varepsilon^2}$. Its [Taylor expansion](../../calculus.md#taylor-expansion) near zero is

$$
x_*=1+\varepsilon+\frac12\varepsilon^2-\frac18\varepsilon^4+O(\varepsilon^6).
$$

The starting error is therefore $x_0-x_*=-\varepsilon-\tfrac12\varepsilon^2+O(\varepsilon^4)$. Consequently

$$
\boxed{|x_0-x_*|\sim|\varepsilon|,\qquad n_0=1.}
$$

For signed $\varepsilon$, the magnitude scaling should be written with $|\varepsilon|$; this is the precise interpretation of the proportionality in the question.

<h4 id="19/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#19/b)

<h5 id="19/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#19/b/ii)

The [Newton root-finding iteration](../../numerical-analysis.md#newton-root-finding-iteration) for this [quadratic polynomial](../../polynomial.md#quadratic-polynomial) is

$$
x_{k+1}=x_k-\frac{x_k^2-2\varepsilon x_k-1}{2(x_k-\varepsilon)}
=\frac{x_k^2+1}{2(x_k-\varepsilon)}.
$$

At $x_0=1$, it gives $x_1=1/(1-\varepsilon)$. Its [geometric series](../../sequence-and-series.md#geometric-series) expansion is $1+\varepsilon+\varepsilon^2+\varepsilon^3+O(\varepsilon^4)$. Subtracting the expansion of the [polynomial root](../../polynomial.md#root-of-a-polynomial) yields

$$
x_1-x_*=\frac12\varepsilon^2+\varepsilon^3+O(\varepsilon^4),
\qquad\boxed{|x_1-x_*|\sim\frac12|\varepsilon|^2,\qquad n_1=2.}
$$

<h4 id="19/b/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#19/b)

<h5 id="19/b/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#19/b/iii)

Use the [Exact Newton error identity for a quadratic](../../numerical-analysis.md#exact-newton-error-identity-for-a-quadratic) to expose the cancellation without a long quotient expansion. Put $e_k=x_k-x_*$. Since $f(x_*)=0$, direct expansion gives

$$
f(x_k)=2(x_*-\varepsilon)e_k+e_k^2,\qquad
e_{k+1}=e_k-\frac{2(x_*-\varepsilon)e_k+e_k^2}{2(x_*-\varepsilon+e_k)}
=\frac{e_k^2}{2(x_k-\varepsilon)}.
$$

From part (ii), $e_1=\tfrac12\varepsilon^2+O(\varepsilon^3)$ and $x_1-\varepsilon\to1$. It follows that

$$
e_2=\frac{\frac14\varepsilon^4+O(\varepsilon^5)}{2(1+O(\varepsilon^2))}
=\frac18\varepsilon^4+O(\varepsilon^5).
$$

Thus the stronger result is

$$
\boxed{|x_2-x_*|\sim\frac18|\varepsilon|^4,\qquad n_2=4>3.}
$$

The vanishing lower-order terms are a concrete instance of [quadratic convergence](../../numerical-analysis.md#quadratic-convergence): to leading order, this step squares the previous error.

## 20

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="20/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [20](#20)

<h4 id="20/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#20/a)

Write $d=b-a$ and $h=d/n$. The required [Riemann sum](../../real-analysis.md#riemann-sum) is

$$
S_n=h\sum_{k=1}^n(a+kh)^2
=h\left[na^2+2ah\sum_{k=1}^nk+h^2\sum_{k=1}^nk^2\right].
$$

Using $\sum k=n(n+1)/2$ and the given sum of squares,

$$
\boxed{S_n=a^2d+ad^2\frac{n+1}{n}+d^3\frac{(n+1)(2n+1)}{6n^2}.}
$$

An equivalent expression separates its [limit](../../calculus.md#limit-of-a-function) and finite-$n$ corrections:

$$
S_n=\frac{b^3-a^3}{3}+\frac{(b-a)(b^2-a^2)}{2n}+\frac{(b-a)^3}{6n^2}.
$$

Consequently

$$
\boxed{\lim_{n\to\infty}S_n=\frac{b^3-a^3}{3}=\int_a^bx^2\,dx.}
$$

The function is continuous, so the [Riemann sums](../../real-analysis.md#riemann-sum) converge to its [definite integral](../../integral.md#definite-integral), the desired [area](../../differential-geometry.md#surface-area) under the [parabola](../../euclidean-geometry.md#parabola). Both corrections are positive when $0<a<b$, agreeing with the geometric fact that these right-endpoint rectangles overestimate an increasing curve.

<h3 id="20/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [20](#20)

<h4 id="20/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#20/b)

Apply the [Leibniz integral rule](../../analysis.md#leibniz-integral-rule), including both moving boundaries and the explicit parameter in the integrand. The upper bound has [derivative](../../calculus.md#derivative) $-\sin x$, the lower bound has [derivative](../../calculus.md#derivative) $\cos x$, and the integrand has [partial derivative](../../multivariable-calculus.md#partial-derivative) $-t^4e^{-xt^4}$ with respect to $x$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{f'(x)=-\sin x\,e^{-x\cos^4x}-\cos x\,e^{-x\sin^4x}-\int_{\sin x}^{\cos x}t^4e^{-xt^4}\,dt.}
$$

The formula remains valid when the bounds coincide or occur in reversed order, by the oriented [definite integral](../../integral.md#definite-integral) convention. The integrand and its [partial derivative](../../multivariable-calculus.md#partial-derivative) are continuous on every finite integration interval.

<h3 id="20/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [20](#20)

<h4 id="20/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#20/c)

For the first [definite integral](../../integral.md#definite-integral), use [integration by substitution](../../integral.md#integration-by-substitution) $t=\tan x$, with $dx=dt/(1+t^2)$, $\cos^2x=1/(1+t^2)$ and $\sin^2x=t^2/(1+t^2)$. This gives

$$
I=\int_0^\infty\frac{dt}{p+qt^2}
=\frac1{\sqrt{pq}}\left[\arctan\left(t\sqrt{\frac qp}\right)\right]_0^\infty
=\boxed{\frac\pi{2\sqrt{pq}}.}
$$

For the second calculation, let $D=p\cos^2x+q\sin^2x$. Since $p,q>0$, this denominator is bounded away from zero; on compact positive parameter ranges the differentiated integrands have uniform bounds. Thus [differentiation under the integral sign](../../analysis.md#differentiation-under-the-integral-sign) is justified and gives

$$
I_p=-\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{\cos^2x}{D^2}\,dx=-\frac\pi{4p^{3/2}q^{1/2}},\qquad
I_q=-\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{\sin^2x}{D^2}\,dx=-\frac\pi{4p^{1/2}q^{3/2}}.
$$

Adding the two equations and using the [Pythagorean trigonometric identity](../../geometry-and-topology.md#pythagorean-trigonometric-identity) in the numerators yields $J=-I_p-I_q$, hence

$$
\boxed{J=\frac{\pi(p+q)}{4(pq)^{3/2}}.}
$$

For $p=q$, this becomes $\pi/(2p^2)$, agreeing with direct integration of the constant integrand.
