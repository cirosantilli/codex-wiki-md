<h1 id="14/b/ii/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](../ii.md)

Take [determinants](../../../../../../determinant.md) of $(CB)^{\mathsf T}=-B^{-1}C$. In order three, multiplication of a [matrix](../../../../../../matrix.md) by minus one changes its [determinant](../../../../../../determinant.md)'s sign. Hence

$$
\det C\,\det B=-\frac{\det C}{\det B}.
$$

The assumption $\det(B^{\mathsf T})\ne0$ is the same as $\det B\ne0$, so multiplication by $\det B$ gives

$$
\bigl((\det B)^2+1\bigr)\det C=0.
$$

Since the [matrices](../../../../../../matrix.md) are real, the first factor is strictly positive. Thus $\boxed{\det C=0}$, proving that $C$ is a [singular matrix](../../../../../../singular-matrix.md). This is [singularity forced by an odd-dimensional transpose-inverse identity](../../../../../../singularity-forced-by-an-odd-dimensional-transpose-inverse-identity.md); no [orthogonality](../../../../../../orthogonal-vectors.md) of $B$ was used.
