## 1

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="1/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

Set $t=2^x>0$. The [exponential function](../../calculus.md#exponential-function) identity $4^x=(2^x)^2$ reduces the problem to the [quadratic equation](../../polynomial.md#quadratic-equation)

$$
t^2-t-2=(t-2)(t+1)=0.
$$

Positivity excludes $t=-1$, so $2^x=2$. Since the [exponential function](../../calculus.md#exponential-function) $2^x$ is strictly increasing, the unique answer is $\boxed{x=1}$.

## 2

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="2/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

Join the centre of the [unit circle](../../complex-analysis.md#complex-unit-circle) to adjacent vertices of the [regular polygon](../../euclidean-geometry.md#regular-polygon). Each of the $n$ resulting [triangles](../../euclidean-geometry.md#triangle) has two sides of [length](../../physics.md#length) $1$ and included [angle](../../geometry-and-topology.md#angle) $2\pi/n$. Bisecting one gives a [right triangle](../../euclidean-geometry.md#right-triangle), so each polygon side, a [chord of a circle](../../topology.md#chord-of-a-circle), has [length](../../physics.md#length) $2\sin(\pi/n)$. Thus the perimeter is $2n\sin(\pi/n)$.

Each of these [triangles](../../euclidean-geometry.md#triangle) has [area](../../differential-geometry.md#surface-area) $\tfrac12\sin(2\pi/n)$, so the polygon's [area](../../differential-geometry.md#surface-area) is $\tfrac n2\sin(2\pi/n)$. Comparing the two expressions gives

$$
\boxed{m=2,\qquad k=2.}
$$

<h3 id="3/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

Hence their [angle](../../geometry-and-topology.md#angle) is $\boxed{\theta=\pi\text{ radians}=180^\circ}$.

↑ **Parent:** [A](#4/a)

The [derivative](../../calculus.md#derivative) is

$$
f'(x)=\frac{2x}{a^2}-\frac{2a^2}{x^3}.
$$

A [stationary point](../../calculus-of-variations.md#stationary-point) at $x=1$ requires $2/a^2-2a^2=0$, or $a^4=1$. Since $a>0$, $\boxed{a=1}$.

<h3 id="4/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#4)

<h4 id="4/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#4/b)

For the requested parameter value $a=2$, the [function graph](../../function.md#graph-of-a-function) has two branches, symmetric about the vertical axis because $f$ is an [even function](../../calculus.md#even-function). Its [derivatives](../../calculus.md#derivative) are

$$
f'(x)=\frac x2-\frac8{x^3}=\frac{x^4-16}{2x^3},\qquad f''(x)=\frac12+\frac{24}{x^4}>0.
$$

The [stationary points](../../calculus-of-variations.md#stationary-point) are $\boxed{(-2,2)\text{ and }(2,2)}$, both [local minima](../../analysis.md#local-minimum) and also the lowest points of their respective branches. The [function](../../function.md) tends to $+\infty$ as $x\to0^\pm$, so $x=0$ is a vertical [asymptote](../../topology.md#asymptote); it also tends to $+\infty$ as $|x|\to\infty$. The negative branch decreases to $(-2,2)$ and then rises towards the vertical [asymptote](../../topology.md#asymptote); the positive branch falls from the vertical [asymptote](../../topology.md#asymptote) to $(2,2)$ and then rises.

The double-angle identity for the [cosine](../../geometry-and-topology.md#cosine) gives $\cos^2x-1=(\cos2x-1)/2$. Termwise [integration](../../integral.md) therefore gives

$$
\boxed{\int(\cos^2x-1)\,dx=\frac14\sin2x-\frac x2+C.}
$$

Here $C$ is an arbitrary constant in the [antiderivative](../../integral.md#antiderivative).

## 7

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="7/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [7](#7)

<h4 id="7/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#7/a)

Writing $v=y-1$ turns the [first-order linear differential equation](../../linear-ordinary-differential-equation.md#first-order-linear-differential-equation) into $v'=v$. Its general solution is $v=Ce^x$, so $y=1+Ce^x$. The [initial condition](../../differential-equation.md#initial-condition) gives $C=-1$, hence

$$
y(x)=1-e^x,\qquad \boxed{y(1)=1-e}.
$$

<h3 id="7/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [7](#7)

<h4 id="7/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#7/b)

The general solution of the [first-order linear differential equation](../../linear-ordinary-differential-equation.md#first-order-linear-differential-equation) is $y=1+Ce^x$. The [initial condition](../../differential-equation.md#initial-condition) $y(0)=1$ gives $C=0$, so the solution of this [initial value problem](../../differential-equation.md#initial-value-problem) is the constant [function](../../function.md) $y(x)=1$. Thus $\boxed{y(1)=1}$.

## 8

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="8/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [8](#8)

For the [partial fraction decomposition](../../isolated-singularity.md#partial-fraction-decomposition), multiply by $(x-1)(x-2)$ to obtain the [polynomial identity](../../polynomial.md#polynomial-identity)

$$
x^2+1=a(x-1)(x-2)+b(x-2)+c(x-1).
$$

Comparing the $x^2$ coefficients gives $a=1$. Substitution of $x=1$ gives $2=-b$, while $x=2$ gives $5=c$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{a=1,\qquad b=-2,\qquad c=5.}
$$

The original identity holds for $x\ne1,2$; evaluating the multiplied [polynomial identity](../../polynomial.md#polynomial-identity) at those values is valid.

## 11

↑ **Parent:** [Paper 1](paper-1.md)

<h3 id="11/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [11](#11)

<h4 id="11/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#11/a)

<h5 id="11/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#11/a/i)

This is a [plane](../../geometry-and-topology.md#plane) through the origin in three-dimensional [Euclidean space](../../normed-vector-space.md#euclidean-norm), with nonzero [normal vector](../../differential-geometry.md#normal-vector) $(1,2,-3)^T$:

$$
\boxed{\{(x,y,z):x+2y-3z=0\}\text{ is a plane through }\mathbf0.}
$$

<h4 id="11/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#11/a)

<h5 id="11/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#11/a/ii)

For example, choose

$$
\boxed{\mathbf u=(-2,1,0)^T,\qquad \mathbf v=(3,0,1)^T.}
$$

Both [vectors](../../vector-space.md#vector) satisfy the [plane equation](../../geometry-and-topology.md#cartesian-equation-of-a-plane). They are [linearly independent vectors](../../vector-space.md#linear-independence): in $\alpha\mathbf u+\beta\mathbf v=\mathbf0$, the second coordinate gives $\alpha=0$ and the third gives $\beta=0$.

<h4 id="11/a/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#11/a)

<h5 id="11/a/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#11/a/iii)

**No.** Every [vector](../../vector-space.md#vector) in the [plane](../../geometry-and-topology.md#plane) can be written as

$$
(x,y,z)^T=(-2y+3z,y,z)^T=y(-2,1,0)^T+z(3,0,1)^T.
$$

The two [linearly independent vectors](../../vector-space.md#linear-independence) in part (ii) therefore form a [vector space basis](../../vector-space.md#basis) for this [vector subspace](../../vector-space.md#vector-subspace). Its [dimension](../../vector-space.md#dimension-vector-space) is $2$, so any three [vectors](../../vector-space.md#vector) in it have [linear dependence](../../vector-space.md#linear-dependence).

<h3 id="11/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [11](#11)

<h4 id="11/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#11/b)

<h5 id="11/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#11/b/i)

The [dot product](../../inner-product.md#dot-product) with the given [unit vector](../../vector-space.md#unit-vector) is $\mathbf r\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}=(x+z)/\sqrt2$. Subtracting the corresponding [orthogonal projection](../../hilbert-space.md#orthogonal-projection) gives

$$
\mathbf r-\hat{\mathbf n}(\mathbf r\cdot\hat{\mathbf n})=\left(\frac{x-z}{2},y,\frac{z-x}{2}\right)^T.
$$

Taking its squared [Euclidean norm](../../normed-vector-space.md#euclidean-norm) yields the equivalent [Cartesian](../../linear-algebra.md#cartesian-coordinate-system) equation

$$
\boxed{\frac{(x-z)^2}{2}+y^2=4}.
$$

<h4 id="11/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#11/b)

<h5 id="11/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#11/b/ii)

The removed [vector](../../vector-space.md#vector) is the [orthogonal projection](../../hilbert-space.md#orthogonal-projection) of $\mathbf r$ onto the [line](../../euclidean-geometry.md#straight-line) through the origin in direction $\hat{\mathbf n}$. The remaining [vector](../../vector-space.md#vector) measures the perpendicular displacement from that [line](../../euclidean-geometry.md#straight-line). Fixing its [Euclidean norm](../../normed-vector-space.md#euclidean-norm) at $2$ therefore describes **an infinite circular [cylinder](../../geometry-and-topology.md#cylinder-geometry) of radius $2$**, with axis $x=z$, $y=0$.

<h4 id="11/b/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#11/b)

<h5 id="11/b/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#11/b/iii)

The [plane equation](../../geometry-and-topology.md#cartesian-equation-of-a-plane) $x+z=0$ has [normal vector](../../differential-geometry.md#normal-vector) $(1,0,1)^T=\sqrt2\hat{\mathbf n}$. Thus its [normal vector](../../differential-geometry.md#normal-vector) is parallel to the [cylinder](../../geometry-and-topology.md#cylinder-geometry)'s axis and the [plane](../../geometry-and-topology.md#plane) is perpendicular to that axis. It passes through the origin, so the intersection is **a [circle](../../topology.md#circle) of radius $2$ centred at the origin in that [plane](../../geometry-and-topology.md#plane)**.

Indeed, with the [orthonormal basis](../../inner-product.md#orthonormal-basis) $\mathbf e=(1,0,-1)^T/\sqrt2$ and $\mathbf f=(0,1,0)^T$ of the [plane](../../geometry-and-topology.md#plane), write $\mathbf r=s\mathbf e+t\mathbf f$. The [cylinder](../../geometry-and-topology.md#cylinder-geometry) equation becomes $s^2+t^2=4$. Equivalently, the intersection has the [parametric equation](../../geometry-and-topology.md#parametric-equation)

$$
\boxed{\mathbf r(\theta)=2\cos\theta\,\mathbf e+2\sin\theta\,\mathbf f,\quad0\leq\theta<2\pi.}
$$

<h4 id="11/b/iv">iv</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#11/b)

<h5 id="11/b/iv/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#11/b/iv)

Use the [distance from a point to a plane](../../geometry-and-topology.md#distance-from-a-point-to-a-plane) formula with the [unit normal](../../differential-geometry.md#unit-normal) $\hat{\mathbf n}$. Since the [plane](../../geometry-and-topology.md#plane) passes through the origin, the distance is

$$
\boxed{d=|\mathbf p\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}|=\frac{|2+0|}{\sqrt2}=\sqrt2}.
$$

For a direct check, the [closest point on a plane](../../geometry-and-topology.md#closest-point-on-a-plane) is $\mathbf p-(\mathbf p\cdot\hat{\mathbf n})\hat{\mathbf n}=(1,\sqrt2,-1)^T$, whose displacement from $\mathbf p$ has [Euclidean norm](../../normed-vector-space.md#euclidean-norm) $\sqrt2$.

<h3 id="11/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [11](#11)

<h4 id="11/c/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#11/c)

<h5 id="11/c/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#11/c/i)

Let $\mathbf d=\mathbf a_2-\mathbf a_1$ and $\mathbf w=\mathbf v_1\times\mathbf v_2$. The assumed [linear independence](../../vector-space.md#linear-independence) gives $\mathbf w\ne\mathbf0$. Every displacement joining one [line](../../euclidean-geometry.md#straight-line) to the other is

$$
\mathbf s=\mathbf d+\lambda_2\mathbf v_2-\lambda_1\mathbf v_1.
$$

Its [dot product](../../inner-product.md#dot-product) with $\mathbf w$ is always $\mathbf d\cdot\mathbf w$. The component in that direction is therefore fixed, so [orthogonal decomposition](../../hilbert-space.md#orthogonal-decomposition-by-a-closed-subspace) gives $\|\mathbf s\|\geq|\mathbf d\cdot\mathbf w|/\|\mathbf w\|$.

The two [linearly independent vectors](../../vector-space.md#linear-independence) $\mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_2$ span the [plane](../../geometry-and-topology.md#plane) perpendicular to $\mathbf w$. Suitable $\lambda_1,\lambda_2$ cancel the component of $\mathbf d$ in that [plane](../../geometry-and-topology.md#plane), attaining the lower bound. Thus the [distance between skew lines](../../euclidean-geometry.md#distance-between-skew-lines) formula, also valid when the [lines](../../euclidean-geometry.md#straight-line) intersect, gives

$$
\boxed{d(L_1,L_2)=\frac{|(\mathbf a_2-\mathbf a_1)\cdot(\mathbf v_1\times\mathbf v_2)|}{\|\mathbf v_1\times\mathbf v_2\|}.}
$$

<h4 id="11/c/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#11/c)

<h5 id="11/c/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#11/c/ii)

The nonzero direction [vectors](../../vector-space.md#vector) now have [linear dependence](../../vector-space.md#linear-dependence), so the [lines](../../euclidean-geometry.md#straight-line) are parallel or coincident. Subtract from $\mathbf d=\mathbf a_2-\mathbf a_1$ its [orthogonal projection](../../hilbert-space.md#orthogonal-projection) along $\mathbf v_1$; the remaining component is the shortest joining displacement. The [distance between parallel lines](../../euclidean-geometry.md#distance-between-parallel-lines) is

$$
\boxed{d(L_1,L_2)=\left\|\mathbf d-\frac{\mathbf d\cdot\mathbf v_1}{\mathbf v_1\cdot\mathbf v_1}\mathbf v_1\right\|=\frac{\|\mathbf d\times\mathbf v_1\|}{\|\mathbf v_1\|}.}
$$

The second expression follows from the relation between the [cross product](../../vector-space.md#cross-product) and the perpendicular component. It includes distance zero for coincident [lines](../../euclidean-geometry.md#straight-line).

<h4 id="11/c/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#11/c)

<h5 id="11/c/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#11/c/iii)

For the stipulated [linearly independent vectors](../../vector-space.md#linear-independence) $\mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_2$, the denominator in part (i) is nonzero. Thus

$$
\boxed{d(L_1,L_2)=0\iff(\mathbf a_2-\mathbf a_1)\cdot(\mathbf v_1\times\mathbf v_2)=0.}
$$

Equivalently, $\mathbf a_2-\mathbf a_1$ belongs to the [linear span](../../vector-space.md#linear-span) of $\mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_2$. Geometrically the [lines](../../euclidean-geometry.md#straight-line) lie in one [plane](../../geometry-and-topology.md#plane), and, since their direction [vectors](../../vector-space.md#vector) are not parallel, **they intersect at a unique [point](../../geometry-and-topology.md#point-geometry)**. The [linear combination](../../vector-space.md#linear-combination) expressing $\mathbf a_2-\mathbf a_1$ gives the two parameters of that intersection.

Write $F(x)=\ln(e^{ax}-b)$. Direct [differentiation](../../calculus.md#differentiation), using the [chain rule](../../calculus.md#chain-rule), gives

No further case is needed, since the linear and quadratic coefficients cannot vanish under the given restrictions. All remainders use [Big O notation](../../real-analysis.md#big-o-notation).

With $b=1$, nonnegativity of the [probability density function](../../continuous-probability-distribution.md#probability-density-function) at both $t$ and $-t$ requires

$$
\boxed{-\frac{3\sqrt3}{\pi}\leq a\leq\frac{3\sqrt3}{\pi}.}

Sufficiency follows from the same decreasing-ratio argument at every interior [point](../../geometry-and-topology.md#point-geometry). Where $\cos x\ne0$, the [separable differential equation](../../differential-equation.md#separable-differential-equation) is $y'=y\tan x$. Separating and taking [antiderivatives](../../integral.md#antiderivative) gives $\ln|y|=-\ln|\cos x|+C$, for nonzero $y$. Including the zero solution, the explicit general solution is

$$
\boxed{y(x)=C\sec x,\qquad C\in\mathbb R,}
$$

on any [interval](../../real-analysis.md#interval-mathematics) with $\cos x\ne0$. It can also be obtained without dividing by $y$: the [product rule](../../calculus.md#product-rule) gives $(y\cos x)'=0$.

A nonzero solution cannot extend through a zero of $\cos x$. If a differentiable solution is required on an [interval](../../real-analysis.md#interval-mathematics) containing such a zero, the original equation there requires $y=0$, and the only such solution across that zero is $y\equiv0$.

<h4 id="15/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#15/a)

<h5 id="15/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#15/a/ii)

For $x\ne0$ and $y\ne0$, put $v=y/x$. This is the standard substitution for a [homogeneous first-order differential equation](../../differential-equation.md#homogeneous-first-order-differential-equation). Since $y'=v+xv'$, the equation becomes

$$
1+v^2-v(v+xv')=0,\qquad vv'=\frac1x.
$$

Taking [antiderivatives](../../integral.md#antiderivative) yields $v^2=2\ln|x|+C$. The explicit general solution is therefore

$$
\boxed{y(x)=\pm x\sqrt{2\ln|x|+C},}
$$

on any [interval](../../real-analysis.md#interval-mathematics) where $x\ne0$ and $2\ln|x|+C>0$, with a fixed choice of sign. For a check that does not divide by $y$, set $w=y^2$ to get $w'-2w/x=2x$ and hence $w=x^2(2\ln|x|+C)$.

There is no missing zero solution: at $y=0$ with $x\ne0$, the original equation would require $x^2=0$. Also no real solution on an [interval](../../real-analysis.md#interval-mathematics) about $x=0$ can arise from this family, since $2\ln|x|+C$ becomes negative near $0$.

<h3 id="15/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [15](#15)

<h4 id="15/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#15/b)

For the given [differential form](../../differential-form.md), set $P=x(y^4+1)$ and $Q=y(2x^2y^2+1)$. Their [partial derivatives](../../multivariable-calculus.md#partial-derivative) satisfy

$$
P_y=4xy^3=Q_x.
$$

In fact an explicit potential is

$$
F(x,y)=\frac12x^2(y^4+1)+\frac12y^2,
$$

since $F_x=P$ and $F_y=Q$. Thus the [differential form](../../differential-form.md) is an [exact differential form](../../differential-form.md#exact-differential-form) on all of $\mathbb R^2$.

Replacing $y^2$ by $u$ suggests the potential

$$
H(x,u)=\frac12\left[x^2(u^2+1)+u\right].
$$

Direct [differentiation](../../calculus.md#differentiation) verifies that $dH/dx$ is exactly the left-hand side of the required [ordinary differential equation](../../differential-equation.md#ordinary-differential-equation). This direct verification remains valid even for negative $u$; it does not require a real square root $y=\sqrt u$. Therefore

$$
x^2u^2+u+x^2=C.
$$

The [initial condition](../../differential-equation.md#initial-condition) gives $C=1$. Solving this [quadratic equation](../../polynomial.md#quadratic-equation) in $u$ and selecting the branch that has $u(1)=0$ gives

$$
\boxed{u(x)=\frac{-1+\sqrt{1+4x^2-4x^4}}{2x^2}=\frac{2(1-x^2)}{1+\sqrt{1+4x^2-4x^4}}.}
$$

The second expression also defines the removable value $u(0)=1$. The maximal open [interval](../../real-analysis.md#interval-mathematics) containing $1$ on which this is a differentiable real solution is

$$
\boxed{-\sqrt{\frac{1+\sqrt2}{2}}<x<\sqrt{\frac{1+\sqrt2}{2}}.}
$$

At either endpoint the square root vanishes and $2x^2u+1=0$, while $x(u^2+1)\ne0$, so the original [ordinary differential equation](../../differential-equation.md#ordinary-differential-equation) cannot hold there with a finite [derivative](../../calculus.md#derivative).

At the central [stationary point](../../calculus-of-variations.md#stationary-point), $u=v=0$, so $f=0$. At each of the other [stationary points](../../calculus-of-variations.md#stationary-point), $u^2+v^2=2$ and hence $f=uv/e$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
f(1,-1)&=0,\\
f(2,0)=f(0,-2)&=e^{-1},\\
f(2,-2)=f(0,0)&=-e^{-1}.
\end{aligned}}
$$

<h3 id="16/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [16](#16)

<h4 id="16/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#16/c)

In the translated coordinates $u=x-1$, $v=y+1$, the zero [level set](../../topology.md#level-set) is the pair of [lines](../../euclidean-geometry.md#straight-line) $u=0$ and $v=0$. The [function](../../function.md) is positive in the quadrants $uv>0$ and negative in those with $uv<0$, and tends to zero as $u^2+v^2\to\infty$. Thus its nonzero [contours](../../topology.md#contour-plot) form closed curves around the four off-centre [stationary points](../../calculus-of-variations.md#stationary-point).

For an explicit description of the [contour plot](../../topology.md#contour-plot), use [polar coordinates](../../multivariable-calculus.md#polar-coordinates) $u=r\cos\theta$, $v=r\sin\theta$:

$$
f=\frac{r^2}{2}e^{-r^2/2}\sin2\theta.
$$

At fixed direction within a quadrant, the magnitude increases until $r=\sqrt2$ and then decreases towards zero. Also $|u|e^{-u^2/2}\leq e^{-1/2}$, with equality exactly when $|u|=1$, and similarly for $v$. Consequently $|f|\leq e^{-1}$; each positive level below $e^{-1}$ encloses a positive peak in each positive quadrant, with the analogous negative contours in the negative quadrants.

To classify the [stationary points](../../calculus-of-variations.md#stationary-point), the [Hessian matrix](../../multivariable-calculus.md#hessian-matrix) is

$$
\nabla^2f=E\begin{pmatrix}uv(u^2-3)&(1-u^2)(1-v^2)\\(1-u^2)(1-v^2)&uv(v^2-3)\end{pmatrix}.
$$

At $u=v=0$ it is $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$, with [eigenvalues](../../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) $1$ and $-1$, so $(1,-1)$ is a [saddle point](../../analysis.md#saddle-point). At $u,v\in\{-1,1\}$ it is $-2fI$. Therefore **$(2,0)$ and $(0,-2)$ are strict [local maxima](../../analysis.md#local-maximum), and $(2,-2)$ and $(0,0)$ are strict [local minima](../../analysis.md#local-minimum)**. These are also global maxima and minima by the bound just proved.

↑ **Parent:** [D](#16/d)

The [gradient](../../multivariable-calculus.md#gradient) is

$$
\boxed{\nabla f=E\bigl(v(1-u^2),u(1-v^2)\bigr)^T.}
$$

It is perpendicular to each nonzero [level set](../../topology.md#level-set) and points towards increasing $f$. Near the two positive [local maxima](../../analysis.md#local-maximum), the [Hessian matrix](../../multivariable-calculus.md#hessian-matrix) is $-2e^{-1}I$, so the [gradient](../../multivariable-calculus.md#gradient) arrows point inward. Near the two negative [local minima](../../analysis.md#local-minimum), the [Hessian matrix](../../multivariable-calculus.md#hessian-matrix) is $2e^{-1}I$, so they point outward. These arrows are shown on the figure in part (c).

At the [saddle point](../../analysis.md#saddle-point), $\nabla f\approx(v,u)^T$. Along $v=u$, the arrows point away from the centre on both sides; along $v=-u$, they point towards the centre on both sides. On the positive $u$ axis they point up, on the negative $u$ axis down, on the positive $v$ axis right, and on the negative $v$ axis left. This specifies the four intervening sectors of the [gradient](../../multivariable-calculus.md#gradient) pattern.

By the definition of [matrix multiplication](../../vector-space.md#matrix-multiplication) and [trace](../../linear-algebra.md#matrix-trace), with all indices summed from $1$ to $D$,

<h3 id="18/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [18](#18)

<h4 id="18/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#18/b)

A [Fourier series](../../fourier-series.md) represents a periodic [function](../../function.md) as a constant plus [sine](../../geometry-and-topology.md#sine) and [cosine](../../geometry-and-topology.md#cosine) waves whose frequencies are integer multiples of its fundamental frequency. For period $2L$, use the convention

$$
f(x)\sim\frac{a_0}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[a_n\cos\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)+b_n\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)\right],
$$

where the [Fourier coefficients](../../fourier-series.md#fourier-coefficient) are

$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
a_0&=\frac1L\int_{-L}^{L}f(x)\,dx,\\
a_n&=\frac1L\int_{-L}^{L}f(x)\cos\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)\,dx,\quad n\geq1,\\
b_n&=\frac1L\int_{-L}^{L}f(x)\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)\,dx,\quad n\geq1.
\end{aligned}}
$$

The coefficient formulas follow by multiplying by a basis [sine](../../geometry-and-topology.md#sine) or [cosine](../../geometry-and-topology.md#cosine) and using their [orthogonality](../../inner-product.md#orthogonal-vectors) over a full period. A [Fourier series](../../fourier-series.md) need not equal an arbitrary [function](../../function.md) at every [point](../../geometry-and-topology.md#point-geometry). The [pointwise convergence of a piecewise smooth Fourier series](../../fourier-series.md#pointwise-convergence-of-a-piecewise-smooth-fourier-series) theorem says that for a piecewise continuously differentiable [function](../../function.md), the [Fourier series](../../fourier-series.md) converges at each [point](../../geometry-and-topology.md#point-geometry) to the average of the two one-sided limits, and hence to $f(x)$ wherever $f$ is continuous.

<h3 id="18/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [18](#18)

<h4 id="18/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#18/c)

The [function](../../function.md) is even, so each product with a [sine](../../geometry-and-topology.md#sine) is an [odd function](../../calculus.md#odd-function) and all the [Fourier coefficients](../../fourier-series.md#fourier-coefficient) $b_n$ vanish. Its constant coefficient is

$$
a_0=\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi e^x\,dx=\frac{2(e^\pi-1)}\pi.
$$

For $n\geq1$, part (a) gives the remaining [Fourier coefficients](../../fourier-series.md#fourier-coefficient):

$$
a_n=\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi e^x\cos(nx)\,dx=\frac{2\left[(-1)^ne^\pi-1\right]}{\pi(1+n^2)}.
$$

Consequently the [Fourier cosine series](../../fourier-series.md#fourier-cosine-series) is

$$
\boxed{f(x)=\frac{e^\pi-1}{\pi}+\frac2\pi\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^ne^\pi-1}{1+n^2}\cos(nx).}
$$

The periodic extension is continuous, including at $\pm\pi$, and piecewise continuously differentiable. Thus the [Fourier series](../../fourier-series.md) equals $f$ everywhere. Its coefficients are also bounded in magnitude by a constant times $1/n^2$, which gives [absolute convergence](../../sequence-and-series.md#absolute-convergence).

<h4 id="19/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#19/a)

All $a_n$ are positive, and $a_1=e^{-1}$. The [logarithm](../../calculus.md#logarithm) turns consecutive ratios into differences, producing the finite [telescoping sum](../../arithmetic.md#telescoping-sum)

$$
\sum_{k=1}^{n}u_k=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\bigl(\ln a_{k+1}-\ln a_k\bigr)=\ln a_{n+1}+1.
$$

Thus, already for every finite $n$,

$$
a_{n+1}=\exp\left(-1+\sum_{k=1}^{n}u_k\right).
$$

If the [partial sums](../../sequence-and-series.md#partial-sum) converge to $S$, the [continuity](../../calculus.md#continuous-function) of the [exponential function](../../calculus.md#exponential-function) gives

$$
\boxed{\lim_{n\to\infty}a_n=\exp\left(-1+\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{n}u_k\right)=e^{-1+S}.}
$$

The shift from $a_{n+1}$ to $a_n$ preserves the [limit of a sequence](../../sequence-and-series.md#limit-of-a-sequence). Convergence of the [partial sums](../../sequence-and-series.md#partial-sum) is established in part (f), so no convergence assumption is left unsupported.

<h3 id="19/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [19](#19)

<h4 id="19/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#19/b)

Cancellation of the [factorials](../../combinatorics.md#factorial) in the consecutive ratio gives

$$
\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}=e^{-1}\left(1+\frac1n\right)^{n+1/2},\qquad u_n=\left(n+\frac12\right)\ln\left(1+\frac1n\right)-1.
$$

Set $t=1/n$. The [derivative](../../calculus.md#derivative) of the [natural logarithm](../../calculus.md#natural-logarithm) at $1$ gives $\ln(1+t)/t\to1$. Therefore

$$
u_n=\left(1+\frac t2\right)\frac{\ln(1+t)}t-1\longrightarrow\boxed{0}.
$$

Thus the necessary condition from the [term test for divergence](../../sequence-and-series.md#term-test-for-divergence) is satisfied. On its own it does not prove that $\sum u_n$ converges.

<h3 id="19/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [19](#19)

<h4 id="19/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#19/c)

For $n>1$, the [Taylor series](../../calculus.md#taylor-series) of the [natural logarithm](../../calculus.md#natural-logarithm) converges absolutely at $1/n$:

$$
\ln\left(1+\frac1n\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{j+1}}{jn^j}.
$$

Multiply this [power series](../../sequence-and-series.md#power-series) by $n+1/2$ and subtract $1$. The constant term cancels the subtracted $1$, and the coefficient of $1/n$ is $-1/2+1/2=0$. For $k\geq2$, the coefficient of $n^{-k}$ is

$$
\frac{(-1)^k}{k+1}+\frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{2k}=(-1)^k\left(\frac1{k+1}-\frac1{2k}\right).
$$

Hence

$$
\boxed{u_n=\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^k}{n^k}\left(\frac1{k+1}-\frac1{2k}\right).}
$$

In particular $u_n=1/(12n^2)-1/(12n^3)+O(n^{-4})$, in [Big O notation](../../real-analysis.md#big-o-notation).

A [rational lower bound for the natural logarithm](../../calculus.md#rational-lower-bound-for-the-natural-logarithm) proves positivity for every $n$, including $n=1$. Define

Then $h(0)=0$, while direct [differentiation](../../calculus.md#differentiation) gives

<h3 id="20/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [20](#20)

<h4 id="20/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#20/a)

Let $G=x^2/a^2+y^2/a^2+z^2/b^2-1$. Its [gradient](../../multivariable-calculus.md#gradient) cannot vanish on the [ellipsoid](../../euclidean-geometry.md#ellipsoid), since the origin is not on the surface. Thus the [Lagrange multiplier](../../mathematical-optimization.md#lagrange-multiplier) equations capture every constrained [stationary point](../../calculus-of-variations.md#stationary-point):

$$
\boxed{4x^3=\frac{2\lambda x}{a^2},\qquad 2yz^2=\frac{2\lambda y}{a^2},\qquad 2y^2z=\frac{2\lambda z}{b^2},\qquad \frac{x^2+y^2}{a^2}+\frac{z^2}{b^2}=1.}
$$

The [temperature](../../temperature.md) is a [sum](../../arithmetic.md#sum) of squares, so $T\geq0$. Equality requires $x=0$ and $yz=0$. The surface constraint then gives the four coldest [points](../../geometry-and-topology.md#point-geometry):

$$
\boxed{T_{\min}=0,\qquad (x,y,z)=(0,\pm a,0)\text{ or }(0,0,\pm b).}
$$

These are all such [points](../../geometry-and-topology.md#point-geometry), because a [sum](../../arithmetic.md#sum) of nonnegative terms can vanish only when each term vanishes.

<h3 id="20/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [20](#20)

<h4 id="20/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#20/b)

Solve the [Lagrange multiplier](../../mathematical-optimization.md#lagrange-multiplier) equations by separating cases according to which coordinates vanish, so that division by a coordinate loses no [stationary points](../../calculus-of-variations.md#stationary-point).

If $x\ne0$ and $y=z=0$, the surface constraint gives

$$
\boxed{(x,y,z)=(\pm a,0,0),\qquad T=a^4.}
$$

If $x\ne0$ and exactly one of $y,z$ is nonzero, the equation for that nonzero coordinate forces $\lambda=0$, while the $x$ equation gives $\lambda=2a^2x^2>0$. Hence there are no [stationary points](../../calculus-of-variations.md#stationary-point) in that case.

If $x=0$ and $y,z\ne0$, the two remaining equations give $\lambda=a^2z^2=b^2y^2$. The surface constraint therefore has equal contributions from $y$ and $z$, giving four [stationary points](../../calculus-of-variations.md#stationary-point):

$$
\boxed{(x,y,z)=\left(0,\varepsilon\frac a{\sqrt2},\eta\frac b{\sqrt2}\right),\qquad T=\frac{a^2b^2}{4},\quad\varepsilon,\eta\in\{-1,1\}.}
$$

If all three coordinates are nonzero, the equations imply

$$
\lambda=2a^2x^2=a^2z^2=b^2y^2,
$$

so $z^2=2x^2$ and $y^2=2a^2x^2/b^2$. Substitution in the surface constraint gives, with $D=4a^2+b^2$,

$$
x^2=\frac{a^2b^2}{D},\qquad y^2=\frac{2a^4}{D},\qquad z^2=\frac{2a^2b^2}{D}.
$$

Thus the remaining eight [stationary points](../../calculus-of-variations.md#stationary-point) and their [temperatures](../../temperature.md) are

$$
\boxed{(x,y,z)=\left(\varepsilon_1\frac{ab}{\sqrt D},\varepsilon_2\frac{\sqrt2a^2}{\sqrt D},\varepsilon_3\frac{\sqrt2ab}{\sqrt D}\right),\qquad T=\frac{a^4b^2}{4a^2+b^2},\quad\varepsilon_1,\varepsilon_2,\varepsilon_3\in\{-1,1\}.}
$$

For example, their common [temperature](../../temperature.md) follows from $x^4+y^2z^2=a^4b^4/D^2+4a^6b^2/D^2=a^4b^2/D$. The cases with $x=0$ and $yz=0$ are exactly the coldest [points](../../geometry-and-topology.md#point-geometry) already listed. This exhausts all possibilities, giving fourteen other [stationary points](../../calculus-of-variations.md#stationary-point) in addition to those four.

<h3 id="20/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [20](#20)

<h4 id="20/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#20/c)

For $a=b=1$, the [stationary points](../../calculus-of-variations.md#stationary-point) found in parts (a) and (b) have [temperatures](../../temperature.md) $0$, $1$, $1/4$ and $1/5$. The [unit sphere](../../topology.md#unit-sphere) is compact and $T$ is a [continuous function](../../calculus.md#continuous-function), so the [extreme value theorem](../../real-analysis.md#extreme-value-theorem) ensures that a hottest [point](../../geometry-and-topology.md#point-geometry) exists; it must be one of those [stationary points](../../calculus-of-variations.md#stationary-point). Thus

$$
\boxed{T_{\max}=1,\qquad (x,y,z)=(1,0,0)\text{ or }(-1,0,0).}
$$

For a direct bound, put $s=x^2\in[0,1]$. The identity $(y^2-z^2)^2\geq0$ gives $y^2z^2\leq(y^2+z^2)^2/4=(1-s)^2/4$, and

$$
T\leq s^2+\frac{(1-s)^2}{4}\leq1,
$$

since $1-s^2-(1-s)^2/4=(1-s)(5s+3)/4\geq0$. Equality forces $s=1$, giving exactly the two [points](../../geometry-and-topology.md#point-geometry) above.
