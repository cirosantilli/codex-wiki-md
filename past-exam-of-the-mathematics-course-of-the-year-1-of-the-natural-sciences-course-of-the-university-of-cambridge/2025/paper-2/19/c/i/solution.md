<h1 id="19/c/i/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [I](../i.md)

For $a>0$, the fact that [sine](../../../../../../sine.md) is a [bounded function](../../../../../../bounded-function.md) gives $|f_a(x)-f_a(0)|\leq |x|^a$. Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose $\delta=\varepsilon^{1/a}$. If $|x|<\delta$, then $|f_a(x)-f_a(0)|<\varepsilon$. This proves [continuity](../../../../../../continuous-function.md) directly from its definition.

For $a\leq0$, use the first [sequence](../../../../../../sequence.md) from part (b), for which $f_a(x_n)=x_n^a$. When $a=0$ these values are one; when $a<0$ they tend to $+\infty$. In either case, for $\varepsilon=1/2$ every neighbourhood of zero contains a sufficiently late $x_n$ with $|f_a(x_n)-f_a(0)|\geq1/2$. This contradicts the definition of [continuity](../../../../../../continuous-function.md). Hence

$$
\boxed{f_a\text{ is continuous at }0\iff a>0}.
$$
