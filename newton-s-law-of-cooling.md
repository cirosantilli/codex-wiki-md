<h1 id="newton-s-law-of-cooling">Newton's law of cooling</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [Linear ordinary differential equation](linear-ordinary-differential-equation-split.md)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton's_law_of_cooling)

[Newton's law of cooling](newton-s-law-of-cooling.md) models an object's [temperature](temperature-split.md) $y$ using a positive constant rate $k$ and ambient [temperature](temperature-split.md) $T_a$. The displayed [ordinary differential equation](ordinary-differential-equation.md) assumes one [temperature](temperature-split.md) represents the object, the environment remains at $T_a$, and the proportional rate stays fixed over the modeled range. It also describes warming when $y<T_a$. These are modeling assumptions: [MIT's notes on modeling by first-order linear equations](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-03sc-differential-equations-fall-2011/1ce2cf783d5f1d15ee411ec2e5f928ec_MIT18_03SCF11_s1_6text.pdf) explain the proportional law through a [tangent line](tangent-line.md) approximation and caution about large [temperature](temperature-split.md) differences.

[Dimensional analysis](dimensional-analysis.md) requires $k$ to have units of inverse time. The product $k(t-t_0)$ in the [exponential function](exponential-function.md) solution is therefore dimensionless. The [equilibrium point](equilibrium-point-of-a-dynamical-system.md) $y=T_a$ attracts both warmer and colder starting states: the excess [temperature](temperature-split.md) undergoes [exponential decay](exponential-decay.md). See [initial value problem](initial-value-problem.md) for computed cooling times, [direction field](direction-field.md) for the family of trajectories, and [separable differential equation](separable-differential-equation.md) for a derivation.
