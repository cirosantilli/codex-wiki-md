# Bragg angle

↑ **Parent:** [Bragg's law](bragg-s-law.md)

The [Bragg angle](bragg-angle.md) is the glancing angle from the incident beam to the selected [lattice planes](lattice-plane.md), with $0<\theta\leq90^\circ$. If $\alpha$ is instead the angle from the beam to the plane normal, $\alpha=90^\circ-\theta$ and [Bragg's law](bragg-s-law.md) becomes $n\lambda=2d\cos\alpha$. Defining the angle explicitly prevents a [sine](sine.md)-versus-[cosine](cosine.md) error as well as a factor-of-two error in the detector deflection.

Choose $\lambda=0.154\,\mathrm{nm}$ and $d=0.200\,\mathrm{nm}$ as an illustrative model, not historical measurements. For the first [diffraction order](diffraction-order.md),

$$
\sin\theta=\frac{0.154}{2(0.200)}=0.385,\qquad
\theta\simeq22.643740^\circ,\qquad
2\theta\simeq45.287481^\circ.
$$

The second [diffraction order](diffraction-order.md) has $\sin\theta=0.770$ and $2\theta\simeq100.707778^\circ$; the third would require $\sin\theta=1.155$ and is impossible. These are different [diffraction orders](diffraction-order.md) of the same [interplanar spacing](interplanar-spacing.md) at the same [wavelength](wavelength.md).

Conversely, a first-order reflection at a measured deflection $2\theta$ gives the [interplanar spacing](interplanar-spacing.md)

$$
d=\frac{\lambda}{2\sin\theta}.
$$

Using $2\theta\simeq45.287481^\circ$ recovers $d\simeq0.200\,\mathrm{nm}$. This is the useful reversal: a known [wavelength](wavelength.md) turns an angular measurement into an atomic length. An unassigned [diffraction order](diffraction-order.md) or an unknown [wavelength](wavelength.md) prevents that single measurement from determining $d$ uniquely.
