# Nucleic acid double helix

↑ **Parent:** [Nucleic acid](nucleic-acid.md)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nucleic_acid_double_helix)

A [nucleic acid double helix](nucleic-acid-double-helix.md) consists of two [nucleic acid](nucleic-acid.md) strands winding around a common axis. In the familiar [DNA](deoxyribonucleic-acid.md) arrangement, [sugar](sugar.md)–[phosphate](phosphate.md) backbones lie outside and paired [nucleobases](nucleotide-base.md) lie inside, stacked along the axis. The strands are [antiparallel nucleic acid strands](antiparallel-nucleic-acid-strands.md). The flattened [base pairing](base-pairing.md) ladder above displays partners and direction; winding that ladder into a [helix](helix.md) adds spatial organization. [RNA](ribonucleic-acid.md) can also form double-helical paired regions, so the geometry is not exclusive to [DNA](deoxyribonucleic-acid.md). See [Alberts et al., Molecular Biology of the Cell: DNA structure](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26821/).

The useful surprise is that a structure explains a copying principle. Complementary [base pairs](base-pair.md) allow each [DNA](deoxyribonucleic-acid.md) strand to specify the other's [nucleic acid sequence](nucleic-acid-sequence.md); separate strands can each act as templates in [DNA replication](dna-replication.md). This is a structural constraint, while the [enzymes](enzyme.md) and stages of [DNA replication](dna-replication.md) supply the actual process. A [nucleic acid sequence](nucleic-acid-sequence.md) specifies letter order without, by itself, giving every detail of the three-dimensional [helix](helix.md).

On 25 April 1953, James Watson and Francis Crick published a model with two oppositely directed chains, outside [phosphate](phosphate.md) groups, and specific [base pairs](base-pair.md). Their short paper explicitly connected this pairing to a possible copying mechanism, while describing the model as requiring further experimental tests. See the original [Molecular Structure of Nucleic Acids: A Structure for Deoxyribose Nucleic Acid, Nature 171, 737–738](https://doi.org/10.1038/171737a0), with an accessible [reprint of the original paper](https://inters.org/files/watsoncrick1953.pdf).

Rosalind Franklin and Raymond Gosling's paper in the same issue had a different role: it presented [X-ray](x-ray.md) [diffraction](diffraction.md) evidence from [DNA](deoxyribonucleic-acid.md) fibres and its structural interpretation, including the helical character of the wetter B form and evidence for [phosphate](phosphate.md) groups on the outside. They distinguished forms produced at different water contents and assessed compatibility with the Watson–Crick model. Read their original [Molecular Configuration in Sodium Thymonucleate, Nature 171, 740–741 (1953)](https://doi.org/10.1038/171740a0). The model and the experimental analysis are distinct contributions; a [diffraction](diffraction.md) pattern is evidence constraining a [molecular geometry](molecular-geometry.md), rather than a direct picture of letter-by-letter [base pairing](base-pairing.md).
