<h1 id="newton-s-first-law">Newton's first law</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [Newton's laws of motion](newton-s-laws-of-motion.md)

In an [inertial frame](inertial-frame.md), a body with zero net [external force](external-force.md) remains at rest or moves with constant [velocity](velocity.md). For a [point mass](point-mass.md) of constant positive [mass](mass.md), write the net [external force](external-force.md) as the [vector](vector.md) sum of all [external forces](external-force.md) on that body:

$$
\mathbf F_{\mathrm{net}}=\sum_i\mathbf F_i=\mathbf0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\mathbf a=\frac{d\mathbf v}{dt}=\mathbf0,\qquad
\mathbf v(t)=\mathbf v_0,\qquad
\mathbf r(t)=\mathbf r_0+\mathbf v_0(t-t_0).
$$

Here $\mathbf r_0$ and $\mathbf v_0$ are the [position](position.md) and [velocity](velocity.md) at [time](time-in-physics.md) $t_0$. The zero net [external force](external-force.md) must hold throughout the interval, not merely at one instant. Both the magnitude and direction of the [velocity](velocity.md) stay fixed: constant [speed](speed.md) along a curved [trajectory](trajectory.md) still involves [acceleration](acceleration.md). Rest is the case $\mathbf v_0=\mathbf0$. For an extended closed system, the analogous statement concerns its [center of mass](center-of-mass.md); zero net [external force](external-force.md) need not prevent its parts from moving relative to one another.

The short mathematical argument is to use [Newton's second law](newton-s-second-law.md) with $m>0$, then take the [integral](integral-split.md) of $d\mathbf v/dt=\mathbf0$ and of $d\mathbf r/dt=\mathbf v_0$. This gives the two constants fixed by the initial [position](position.md) and [velocity](velocity.md). The physical content also includes the existence of [inertial frames](inertial-frame.md) in which these relations apply; an arbitrary accelerating [reference frame](reference-frame.md) need not show constant [velocity](velocity.md) for a body with zero actual net [external force](external-force.md).

The useful question is therefore which [forces](force-split.md) change the motion, rather than which [force](force-split.md) keeps it going. A [free-body diagram](free-body-diagram.md) may show several nonzero [forces](force-split.md) whose [vector](vector.md) sum is zero. Equal opposing [forces](force-split.md) on the same body can balance; a [third-law interaction pair](third-law-interaction-pair.md) acts on different bodies and cannot be cancelled within that one body's diagram.

In 1687, [Isaac Newton](isaac-newton.md) stated Law I in _Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica_: a body's rest or uniform straight motion persists unless impressed [forces](force-split.md) compel a change. The following paragraph identifies air resistance and [gravity](gravity.md) as influences on the [trajectory](trajectory.md) of a thrown body. The [vector](vector.md) equations above are a modern expression of the law, not a quotation of his notation.[Isaac Newton, Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica (1687), Axiomata sive leges motus, printed p. 12: Lex I and the following projectile paragraph; normalized transcription](philosophiae-naturalis-principia-mathematica-by-isaac-newton/axiomata-sive-leges-motus.md)
