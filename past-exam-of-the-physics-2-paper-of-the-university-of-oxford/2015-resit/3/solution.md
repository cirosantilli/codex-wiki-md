<h1 id="3/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [3](../3.md)

For a fixed number $N$ of identical bulbs, first approximate each as a [resistor](../../../resistor.md) of [electrical resistance](../../../electrical-resistance.md) $R$ connected to a fixed ideal battery [electric potential difference](../../../electric-potential-difference.md) $V$. A [series circuit](../../../series-circuit.md) has [electrical resistance](../../../electrical-resistance.md) $NR$, while a [parallel circuit](../../../parallel-circuit.md) has [electrical resistance](../../../electrical-resistance.md) $R/N$. Thus

$$
P_{\rm series}=\frac{V^2}{NR},\qquad P_{\rm parallel}=\frac{NV^2}{R},\qquad\boxed{\frac{P_{\rm parallel}}{P_{\rm series}}=N^2}.
$$

Parallel connection therefore maximizes dissipation in this model: every bulb receives the full source [electric potential difference](../../../electric-potential-difference.md) instead of sharing it. For $N=1$ the two arrangements coincide.

An actual [incandescent light bulb](../../../incandescent-light-bulb.md) changes [electrical resistance](../../../electrical-resistance.md) as its filament heats, so a single constant $R$ need not describe both operating points. The same qualitative conclusion holds for identical stable bulbs with increasing current-voltage function $i(U)$: parallel operation gives $P_p=NV i(V)$, whereas symmetric series operation gives $P_s=V i(V/N)$. The former is larger for $N>1$. This comparison assumes a fixed collection and an ideal source as specified; an unlimited number of parallel bulbs on a zero-internal-resistance ideal battery would have no finite total-power maximum.
