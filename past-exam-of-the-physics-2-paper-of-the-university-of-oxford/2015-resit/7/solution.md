<h1 id="7/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [7](../7.md)

[Fermat principle](../../../fermat-principle.md) states that the [optical path length](../../../optical-path-length.md) $\int n\,ds$, equivalently the travel time in the nondispersive stationary isotropic ray model, is stationary for the actual [light ray](../../../light-ray.md) between fixed endpoints. It need not be a minimum in every optical system.

For two homogeneous media separated by $y=0$, place the fixed endpoints at $(0,h_1)$ and $(D,-h_2)$, with positive heights. If the ray crosses at $(x,0)$, its optical path is

$$
\mathcal L(x)=n_1\sqrt{x^2+h_1^2}+n_2\sqrt{(D-x)^2+h_2^2}.
$$

Stationarity with respect to the crossing point gives

$$
\frac{d\mathcal L}{dx}=n_1\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+h_1^2}}-n_2\frac{D-x}{\sqrt{(D-x)^2+h_2^2}}=0.
$$

The two fractions are $\sin\theta_1$ and $\sin\theta_2$, with angles measured from the interface normal. Thus

$$
\boxed{n_1\sin\theta_1=n_2\sin\theta_2},
$$

which is [Snell's law](../../../snell-s-law.md). Dividing $\mathcal L$ by the vacuum light [speed](../../../speed.md) gives the corresponding travel time; minimizing geometric distance without refractive-index weighting would incorrectly predict a straight ray through unlike media.
