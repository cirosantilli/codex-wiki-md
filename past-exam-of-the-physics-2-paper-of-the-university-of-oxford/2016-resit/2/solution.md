<h1 id="2/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [2](../2.md)

Introduce cylinder length $\ell$ and line charge density $\lambda=Q/\ell$; only charge per length matters in the long-cylinder approximation. A coaxial cylindrical [Gaussian surface](../../../gaussian-surface.md) gives $E_r2\pi r\ell=Q/\epsilon_0$. Choosing the outer cylinder's [electric potential](../../../electric-potential.md) to be zero,

$$
\boxed{\mathbf E(r)=\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\epsilon_0r}\hat{\mathbf r},\quad V(r)=\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\epsilon_0}\ln\frac br,\quad a<r<b.}
$$

The [capacitance per unit length](../../../capacitance-per-unit-length.md) follows from $C=Q/[V(a)-V(b)]$:

$$
\boxed{\frac C\ell=\frac{2\pi\epsilon_0}{\ln(b/a)}.}
$$

If $Q$ is intended as a line charge rather than a finite total charge, replace $\lambda$ by that $Q$. Neglecting end effects, equal opposite cylinder charges make the exterior [electric field](../../../electric-field-split.md) vanish; the potential difference between the conductors remains the quantity defining their [capacitance](../../../capacitance.md).
