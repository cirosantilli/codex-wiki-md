<h1 id="2/d/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [D](../d.md)

Use a converging [thin lens](../../../../thin-lens.md) as a [magnifying glass](../../../../magnifying-glass.md), with the screen at or just inside its front [focal plane](../../../../focal-plane.md). It presents an upright [virtual image](../../../../virtual-image.md) at infinity or at the eye's [near point](../../../../near-point.md), respectively. Let the [near point](../../../../near-point.md) distance be $D$; the conventional numerical assumption is $D=250\,\mathrm{mm}$, which is not specified in the paper.

Under the usual approximation that the eye is close to the eyepiece, interpreting $50\,\mathrm{mm}$ as the [focal length](../../../../focal-length.md) $f$ gives the familiar [angular magnifications](../../../../angular-magnification.md)

$$
M_\infty=\frac Df=5,\qquad
\boxed{M_{\rm near}=1+\frac Df=6\quad(f=50\,\mathrm{mm},\ D=250\,\mathrm{mm}).}
$$

The first is for a relaxed eye with the screen at the front [focal plane](../../../../focal-plane.md). For the second, the [virtual image](../../../../virtual-image.md) is at signed distance $v=-D$ and the [thin lens](../../../../thin-lens.md) equation gives screen distance $u=fD/(D+f)=41.7\,\mathrm{mm}$. Thus the often intended maximum $6$ requires a screen-to-eye distance of approximately $41.7\,\mathrm{mm}$, rather than exactly $50\,\mathrm{mm}$.

If $50\,\mathrm{mm}$ instead fixes the physical screen-to-eye separation $s$, an eye adjacent to the [optical lens](../../../../lens-optics.md) sees the [angular magnification](../../../../angular-magnification.md)

$$
\boxed{M=\frac Ds=5\quad(s=50\,\mathrm{mm},\ D=250\,\mathrm{mm}).}
$$

This is achieved by $f=s$ for relaxed viewing, or by $f=sD/(D-s)=62.5\,\mathrm{mm}$ for a [virtual image](../../../../virtual-image.md) at the [near point](../../../../near-point.md). The latter follows from $1/f=1/s-1/D$.

More generally, the source does not specify the eyepiece-to-eye distance either. If that distance is $e$, then $u=s-e$ and a [virtual image](../../../../virtual-image.md) at the [near point](../../../../near-point.md) has [transverse magnification](../../../../transverse-magnification.md) $(D-e)/u$. Its [angular magnification](../../../../angular-magnification.md) relative to unaided viewing at $D$ is

$$
M=\frac{D-e}{s-e}.
$$

For example, $s=50\,\mathrm{mm}$ and $e=25\,\mathrm{mm}$ give $M=9$, with $u=25\,\mathrm{mm}$ and $f=28.125\,\mathrm{mm}$. This is a concrete counterexample to a universal maximum of $5$ at fixed screen-to-eye separation. In ideal [geometrical optics](../../../../geometrical-optics-split.md), $e\to s^-$ allows arbitrarily large $M$ with an arbitrarily short [focal length](../../../../focal-length.md); practical [aperture](../../../../aperture.md) and [optical aberrations](../../../../optical-aberration.md) constrain this. A unique numerical maximum therefore needs the conventional near-eye eyepiece geometry and [near point](../../../../near-point.md) assumption. The conventional $f=50\,\mathrm{mm}$ answer is $6$, with those assumptions stated explicitly.
