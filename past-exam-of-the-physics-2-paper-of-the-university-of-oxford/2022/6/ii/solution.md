<h1 id="6/ii/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](../ii.md)

Approximate the atmosphere by concentric spherical layers of [refractive index](../../../../refractive-index.md). At every boundary, [Snell's law](../../../../snell-s-law.md) conserves $nr\sin\psi$, where $\psi$ is the angle between the [light ray](../../../../light-ray.md) and the radius. Straight propagation between boundaries conserves the same quantity because $r\sin\psi$ is the distance of that line from the origin. In the smooth limit this is the [spherical ray invariant](../../../../spherical-ray-invariant.md)

$$
p=n(r)r\sin\psi.
$$

At the surface, a [light ray](../../../../light-ray.md) apparently on the horizon is tangent, so $p=n_sR$. In vacuum far from the Earth, $n=1$, and the straight incident line has distance $p$ from the centre. Removing the atmosphere while retaining that incident line gives minimum altitude

$$
\boxed{H=p-R=(n_s-1)R=0.0003(6400\,\mathrm{km})=1.92\,\mathrm{km}.}
$$

The detailed variation of [refractive index](../../../../refractive-index.md) with altitude changes the local curvature of the [light ray](../../../../light-ray.md), but not the conserved $p$. This conclusion assumes a spherically stratified atmosphere and a [light ray](../../../../light-ray.md) which actually connects the surface to space; nonspherical structure or a trapping layer would require separate analysis.
