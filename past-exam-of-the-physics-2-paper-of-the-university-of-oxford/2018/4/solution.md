<h1 id="4/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [4](../4.md)

The [intensity of an isotropic radiator](../../../intensity-of-an-isotropic-radiator.md) at distance $r$ is the radiated [power](../../../power.md) divided by spherical area: $\mathcal I=P/(4\pi r^2)$. For a locally plane far-field [electromagnetic wave](../../../electromagnetic-wave-split.md) in air, its mean [Poynting flux](../../../poynting-flux.md) is $\mathcal I=E_{\mathrm{rms}}^2/Z_0=E_{\mathrm{peak}}^2/(2Z_0)$, where the vacuum [wave impedance](../../../wave-impedance.md) is $Z_0\simeq376.7\ \Omega$. Thus

$$
\mathcal I=\frac{10^5}{4\pi(10^5)^2}\simeq7.96\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}},
$$

and

$$
\boxed{E_{\mathrm{rms}}\simeq1.73\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{V\,m^{-1}},\qquad E_{\mathrm{peak}}\simeq2.45\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}.}
$$

Both conventions are given because “field strength” alone does not specify RMS or peak. This estimate treats the stated transmitter [power](../../../power.md) as radiated power and ignores ground reflection and absorption.
