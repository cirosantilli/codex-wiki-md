<h1 id="3/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [3](../3.md)

Assemble the [spherically symmetric charge distribution](../../../spherically-symmetric-charge-distribution.md) by adding thin concentric shells, with the final [charge density](../../../charge-density.md) kept uniform. When the assembled radius is $s$, the enclosed [electric charge](../../../electric-charge-split.md) and the next increment are

$$
q(s)=Q\frac{s^3}{r^3},\qquad dq=\frac{3Qs^2}{r^3}\,ds.
$$

The [electric potential](../../../electric-potential.md) of the already assembled material at that shell is $k_eq(s)/s$. Consequently its assembly work is $dU=k_eq(s)\,dq/s$, and the [electrostatic energy of a uniformly charged solid sphere](../../../electrostatic-energy-of-a-uniformly-charged-solid-sphere.md) is

$$
U=\frac{3k_eQ^2}{r^6}\int_0^r s^4\,ds,
\qquad
\boxed{U=\frac{3k_eQ^2}{5r}=\frac{3Q^2}{20\pi\epsilon_0r}.}
$$

There is no extra factor of one half in this assembly [integral](../../../integral-split.md): each pair of charge elements has already been counted exactly once, when the later element was brought in. A solid uniformly charged insulator has this value; a conducting sphere has its [electric charge](../../../electric-charge-split.md) on the surface and instead has [electrostatic energy](../../../electrostatic-energy.md) $k_eQ^2/(2r)$.
