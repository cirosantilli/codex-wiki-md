<h1 id="8/a/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [A](../a.md)

The [method of images](../../../../method-of-images.md) replaces a conducting boundary by fictitious [point charges](../../../../point-charge.md) placed outside the physical region being solved. Choose their positions and strengths so that the resulting [electric potential](../../../../electric-potential.md) has the required constant value on each conductor. The [uniqueness theorem for the electrostatic potential](../../../../uniqueness-theorem-for-the-electrostatic-potential.md) then guarantees that this solution equals the physical solution in the allowed region, provided the other boundary conditions are also satisfied.

For a plane held at zero [electric potential](../../../../electric-potential.md), a real charge $q$ a height $d$ above the plane has an image $-q$ the same distance below it. The pair's [electric potential](../../../../electric-potential.md) vanishes everywhere on the plane. Its tangential [electric field](../../../../electric-field-split.md) consequently vanishes there, as required in [electrostatic equilibrium](../../../../electrostatic-equilibrium.md). The image is a mathematical representation of the induced [surface charge](../../../../surface-charge.md), not a physical charge placed inside the conductor. Forces on the real charge can be computed from the image field, but total physical [electrostatic energy](../../../../electrostatic-energy.md) must account for the conductor's induced response rather than simply counting a full real-plus-image pair energy.
