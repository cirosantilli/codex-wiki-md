## 1

↑ **Parent:** [2013](2013.md)

<h3 id="1/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1/a)

The [minimal polynomial](../linear-operator-theory.md#minimal-polynomial) of a [linear transformation](../vector-space.md#linear-map) $T$ is the unique [monic polynomial](../polynomial.md#monic-polynomial) of least [degree](../polynomial.md#degree-of-a-polynomial) among the nonzero [polynomials](../polynomial.md) $p\in F[x]$ satisfying $p(T)=0$:

$$
\boxed{m_T\text{ monic},\quad m_T(T)=0,\quad\deg m_T\text{ least possible}.}
$$

Such a [polynomial](../polynomial.md) exists because the [endomorphism](../algebra.md#endomorphism) space is finite-dimensional, so sufficiently many [powers](../energy.md#power) $I,T,T^2,\ldots$ are [linearly dependent](../vector-space.md#linear-dependence). Monic normalization is possible over the [field](../algebra.md#field) $F$. Two [monic polynomials](../polynomial.md#monic-polynomial) annihilating $T$ of the same least [degree](../polynomial.md#degree-of-a-polynomial) have a difference of smaller [degree](../polynomial.md#degree-of-a-polynomial) annihilating $T$, so their difference is zero. For the zero [vector space](../vector-space.md) the convention is $m_T=1$.

<h3 id="1/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

Apply [polynomial division](../polynomial.md#polynomial-division) in $F[x]$:

$$
f=q m_T+r,\qquad r=0\text{ or }\deg r<\deg m_T.
$$

Evaluation at $T$ gives $r(T)=f(T)-q(T)m_T(T)=0$. A nonzero remainder could be rescaled to a monic [polynomial](../polynomial.md) annihilating $T$ of smaller [degree](../polynomial.md#degree-of-a-polynomial), contradicting the defining minimality. Hence $r=0$ and

$$
\boxed{m_T\mid f.}
$$

In particular, the annihilating [polynomials](../polynomial.md) form the [principal ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal) $(m_T)$.

<h3 id="1/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#1/c)

Since $p$ and $q$ are coprime, [Bézout's identity for polynomials](../polynomial.md#bezout-s-identity-for-polynomials) gives $a,b\in F[x]$ with $ap+bq=1$. For every $v\in V$,

$$
v=b(T)q(T)v+a(T)p(T)v.
$$

The first summand belongs to $U$: applying $p(T)$ gives $b(T)p(T)q(T)v=0$. The second belongs to $W$ by the same calculation. Here all [polynomials](../polynomial.md) in $T$ commute, and $p(T)q(T)=f(T)=0$. Thus $V=U+W$.

If $v\in U\cap W$, both terms on the right of the identity vanish, so $v=0$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{V=U\oplus W.}
$$

Finally, $p(T)Tv=Tp(T)v=0$ whenever $v\in U$; hence $T(U)\subseteq U$. Replacing $p$ by $q$ proves $T(W)\subseteq W$. These are [invariant subspaces](../representation-theory.md#invariant-subspace); this is the coprime-factor case of the [primary decomposition theorem for linear maps](../linear-operator-theory.md#primary-decomposition-theorem-for-linear-maps).

<h3 id="1/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#1/d)

Write $m_1,m_2$ for the [minimal polynomials](../linear-operator-theory.md#minimal-polynomial) of the two [restrictions](../function.md#restriction-mathematics). Since $p(T)$ vanishes on $U$ and $q(T)$ vanishes on $W$, part (b) gives

$$
m_1\mid p,\qquad m_2\mid q.
$$

Hence $m_1,m_2$ are coprime. By the direct-sum decomposition, a [polynomial](../polynomial.md) annihilates $T$ exactly when it annihilates both [restrictions](../function.md#restriction-mathematics), which happens exactly when it is divisible by both $m_1$ and $m_2$. Consequently

$$
m_T=\operatorname{lcm}(m_1,m_2)=m_1m_2.
$$

If $m_1=p$ and $m_2=q$, this immediately gives $m_T=pq=f$. Conversely, if $m_T=pq$, the [monic polynomials](../polynomial.md#monic-polynomial) $p/m_1$ and $q/m_2$ have product one, so each is one. Thus

$$
\boxed{m_T=pq\iff m_{T|_U}=p\text{ and }m_{T|_W}=q.}
$$

This also handles a zero summand, whose [minimal polynomial](../linear-operator-theory.md#minimal-polynomial) is one; a nonconstant factor cannot be its [minimal polynomial](../linear-operator-theory.md#minimal-polynomial).

<h3 id="1/e">e</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/e/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [E](#1/e)

Part (b) requires $m_T$ to be a monic [divisor](../divisibility.md#divisor) of $x^2(x-1)$. The constant [divisor](../divisibility.md#divisor) one cannot annihilate a nonzero [vector space](../vector-space.md). Every remaining [divisor](../divisibility.md#divisor) can occur in dimension three:

$$
\boxed{m_T\in\{x,\ x^2,\ x-1,\ x(x-1),\ x^2(x-1)\}.}
$$

Here are corresponding [matrices](../vector-space.md#matrix), all satisfying $T^3-T^2=0$:

$$
\begin{array}{c|c}
m_T&\text{matrix of }T\\\hline
x&0_{3\times3}\\
x^2&\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\0&0&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\\
x-1&I_3\\
x(x-1)&\operatorname{diag}(0,0,1)\\
x^2(x-1)&\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\0&0&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}
\end{array}
$$

The last [matrix](../vector-space.md#matrix) contains a size-two [nilpotent](../commutative-algebra.md#nilpotent) [Jordan block](../linear-operator-theory.md#jordan-block), requiring the factor $x^2$, and an [eigenvector](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvector) with [eigenvalue](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) one, requiring $x-1$. Thus its [minimal polynomial](../linear-operator-theory.md#minimal-polynomial) is exactly $f$. The [zero matrix](../vector-space.md#zero-matrix) has [minimal polynomial](../linear-operator-theory.md#minimal-polynomial) $x\neq f$, providing the other requested example.

<h3 id="2/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#2/a)

Let $p=\sum_{j=1}^m\langle u,v_j\rangle v_j$. [Orthonormality](../inner-product.md#orthonormal-set) gives $\langle u-p,v_k\rangle=0$ for every $k$, so $u-p$ is [orthogonal](../inner-product.md#orthogonal-vectors) to $p$. The [Pythagorean theorem for inner product spaces](../inner-product.md#pythagorean-theorem-in-an-inner-product-space) gives

$$
\|u\|^2=\|u-p\|^2+\|p\|^2
=\|u-p\|^2+\sum_{j=1}^m\langle u,v_j\rangle^2.
$$

Since a squared [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm) is nonnegative,

$$
\boxed{\langle u,u\rangle\geq\sum_{j=1}^m\langle u,v_j\rangle^2.}
$$

This is [Bessel's inequality](../hilbert-space.md#bessel-s-inequality), with equality exactly when $u$ belongs to the [span](../vector-space.md#linear-span) of the [orthonormal set](../inner-product.md#orthonormal-set).

<h3 id="2/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

<h5 id="2/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#2/b/i)

Use the [Euclidean inner product](../inner-product.md#dot-product) on $\mathbb R^n$, with $a=(a_1,\ldots,a_n)$ and $b=(b_1,\ldots,b_n)$. If $b=0$, both sides of the desired inequality are zero. Otherwise take the single [unit vector](../vector-space.md#unit-vector) $v=b/\|b\|$ in part (a). Then

$$
\|a\|^2\geq\frac{\langle a,b\rangle^2}{\|b\|^2},
$$

so

$$
\boxed{\left(\sum_{i=1}^n a_i^2\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^n b_i^2\right)\geq\left(\sum_{i=1}^n a_ib_i\right)^2.}
$$

This is the real [Cauchy-Schwarz inequality](../probability-and-statistics.md#cauchy-schwarz-inequality). Equality holds when the two [vectors](../vector-space.md#vector) are [linearly dependent](../vector-space.md#linear-dependence).

<h4 id="2/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

<h5 id="2/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#2/b/ii)

In part (i), set every $b_i=1$. Its squared [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm) is $n$, giving

$$
\boxed{n\sum_{i=1}^n a_i^2\geq\left(\sum_{i=1}^n a_i\right)^2.}
$$

The equality condition is $a_1=\cdots=a_n$, because $a$ must be a [scalar multiple](../vector-space.md#scalar-multiple) of $(1,\ldots,1)$.

<h4 id="2/b/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

<h5 id="2/b/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#2/b/iii)

In $\mathbb R^{2n}$, take the two [unit vectors](../vector-space.md#unit-vector)

$$
v_{\mathrm{even}}=\frac1{\sqrt n}(0,1,0,1,\ldots,0,1),\qquad
v_{\mathrm{odd}}=\frac1{\sqrt n}(1,0,1,0,\ldots,1,0).
$$

Their supports are disjoint, so they are [orthonormal](../inner-product.md#orthonormal-set). Apply part (a) to $a=(a_1,\ldots,a_{2n})$:

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{2n}a_i^2\geq\frac1n\left(\sum_{i=1}^n a_{2i}\right)^2+\frac1n\left(\sum_{i=1}^n a_{2i-1}\right)^2.
$$

Multiplying by $n$ gives

$$
\boxed{n\sum_{i=1}^{2n}a_i^2\geq\left(\sum_{i=1}^n a_{2i}\right)^2+\left(\sum_{i=1}^n a_{2i-1}\right)^2.}
$$

<h3 id="2/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#2/c)

Equality in part (a) requires $a$ to lie in the [span](../vector-space.md#linear-span) of the two chosen [unit vectors](../vector-space.md#unit-vector). Equivalently, there are real constants $r,s$ such that

$$
\boxed{a_{2i-1}=r,\qquad a_{2i}=s\qquad(1\leq i\leq n).}
$$

One can also see the necessity directly by writing the nonnegative deficit as

$$
n\sum_{i=1}^{2n}a_i^2-\left(\sum_{i=1}^na_{2i}\right)^2-\left(\sum_{i=1}^na_{2i-1}\right)^2
=n\sum_{i=1}^n(a_{2i}-\bar a_e)^2+n\sum_{i=1}^n(a_{2i-1}-\bar a_o)^2,
$$

where $\bar a_e$ and $\bar a_o$ are the means of the even and odd entries. The deficit vanishes exactly when each group is constant; the two group constants need not be equal.

## 3

↑ **Parent:** [2013](2013.md)

<h3 id="3/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

The [first isomorphism theorem for rings](../commutative-algebra.md#first-isomorphism-theorem-for-rings) states that the map

$$
\boxed{R/\ker f\longrightarrow\operatorname{im}f,\qquad r+\ker f\longmapsto f(r)}
$$

is a [ring isomorphism](../algebra.md#ring-isomorphism). It is well defined because two representatives differ by an element of the [kernel](../kernel-of-a-linear-map.md) exactly when their images agree. It preserves addition and multiplication, is onto its stated codomain, and has trivial [kernel](../kernel-of-a-linear-map.md). In particular, if $f$ is [surjective](../algebra.md#surjective-function) then $R/\ker f\cong S$.

<h3 id="3/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

Define $\Phi:R\to R/I\times R/J$ by $\Phi(r)=(r+I,r+J)$. It is a [ring homomorphism](../commutative-algebra.md#ring-homomorphism) with

$$
\ker\Phi=I\cap J.
$$

Since $I+J=R$, choose $i\in I,j\in J$ such that $i+j=1$. For arbitrary $a,b\in R$, the element $r=aj+bi$ satisfies $r-a=i(b-a)\in I$ and $r-b=j(a-b)\in J$. Hence $\Phi(r)=(a+I,b+J)$, proving [surjectivity](../algebra.md#surjective-function). The first [isomorphism](../algebra.md#isomorphism) theorem now gives

$$
\boxed{R/(I\cap J)\cong R/I\times R/J.}
$$

This is the two-ideal [Chinese remainder theorem for ideals](../mathematics.md#chinese-remainder-theorem-for-ideals). The hypothesis is comaximality of the two [ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal); no assertion that every pair of [ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) has this property is needed.

<h3 id="3/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#3/c)

[Coprimality](../divisibility.md#coprime-integers) gives [polynomials](../polynomial.md) $a,b\in\mathbb Q[x]$ with $ag+bh=1$, so the [ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) $(g)$ and $(h)$ are comaximal. Also

$$
(g)\cap(h)=(gh).
$$

Indeed, if $u=gr=hs$, multiplying $ag+bh=1$ by $u$ writes $u=aghs+bhgr$, a multiple of $gh$; the reverse inclusion is immediate. Part (b), with $f=gh$, gives

$$
\boxed{\mathbb Q[x]/(f)\cong\mathbb Q[x]/(g)\times\mathbb Q[x]/(h).}
$$

The explicit [isomorphism](../algebra.md#isomorphism) takes the class of a [polynomial](../polynomial.md) $r$ to its classes modulo $g$ and $h$.

<h3 id="3/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#3/d)

Because $g,h$ are nonconstant, their classes in $\mathbb Q[x]/(gh)$ are nonzero: each has smaller [degree](../polynomial.md#degree-of-a-polynomial) than $gh$, so neither is a multiple of $gh$. Their product is zero. Thus **the [quotient](../arithmetic.md#quotient) has [zero divisors](../mathematics.md#zero-divisor)**.

If both factors have [degree](../polynomial.md#degree-of-a-polynomial) one, each [quotient](../arithmetic.md#quotient) by one factor is [isomorphic](../algebra.md#isomorphism) to $\mathbb Q$, by evaluation at its rational [root](../semisimple-lie-algebra.md#root-of-a-root-system). [Coprimality](../divisibility.md#coprime-integers) ensures the two roots are distinct. Part (c) therefore identifies the ring with $\mathbb Q\times\mathbb Q$. If $(r,s)^2=(0,0)$, then $r^2=s^2=0$ in a [field](../algebra.md#field), so $r=s=0$.

$$
\boxed{\text{There is no nonzero square-zero element when both factors have degree one.}}
$$

A ring may therefore have [zero divisors](../mathematics.md#zero-divisor) without having nonzero [nilpotent](../commutative-algebra.md#nilpotent) elements.

<h3 id="3/e">e</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/e/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [E](#3/e)

Factorization over $\mathbb Q$ gives

$$
R_1\cong\mathbb Q\times\mathbb Q,\qquad
R_2\cong\mathbb Q(i),\qquad
R_3\cong\mathbb Q[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2),\quad\varepsilon=x-1.
$$

For $R_1$, the factors $x-1$ and $x+1$ are coprime. The nonzero elements $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ multiply to zero, so the ring is neither an [integral domain](../commutative-algebra.md#integral-domain) nor a [field](../algebra.md#field). Squaring coordinatewise shows it has no nonzero square-zero element.

For $R_2$, the [polynomial](../polynomial.md) $x^2+1$ has no rational [root](../semisimple-lie-algebra.md#root-of-a-root-system) and is [irreducible](../representation-theory.md#irreducible-representation) of [degree](../polynomial.md#degree-of-a-polynomial) two over $\mathbb Q$. The [quotient](../arithmetic.md#quotient) is consequently a [field](../algebra.md#field), hence an [integral domain](../commutative-algebra.md#integral-domain), and has no nonzero square-zero element.

For $R_3$, the class $\varepsilon$ is nonzero by its [degree](../polynomial.md#degree-of-a-polynomial), but $\varepsilon^2=0$. Thus this ring is neither an [integral domain](../commutative-algebra.md#integral-domain) nor a [field](../algebra.md#field), and does have a nonzero square-zero element.

$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|ccc}&\text{integral domain}&\text{field}&\exists u\neq0:\ u^2=0\\\hline
R_1&\text{no}&\text{no}&\text{no}\\
R_2&\text{yes}&\text{yes}&\text{no}\\
R_3&\text{no}&\text{no}&\text{yes}
\end{array}}
$$

**No two are [isomorphic](../algebra.md#isomorphism).** Being a [field](../algebra.md#field) separates $R_2$ from the other two; existence of a nonzero square-zero element separates $R_1$ from $R_3$. Both properties are preserved by [ring isomorphisms](../algebra.md#ring-isomorphism).

## 4

↑ **Parent:** [2013](2013.md)

<h3 id="4/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#4)

<h4 id="4/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#4/a)

A function is [holomorphic](../holomorphic-function.md) on an [open set](../topology.md#open-set) when it is [complex differentiable](../holomorphic-function.md#complex-differentiability) at every point of that set. Explicitly, for every $z\in U$, the limit

$$
\boxed{f'(z)=\lim_{h\to0,\ h\in\mathbb C\setminus\{0\}}\frac{f(z+h)-f(z)}h}
$$

exists as a finite [complex number](../complex-analysis.md#complex-number). The limit is independent of the direction in which the complex increment approaches zero; existence of [derivatives](../calculus.md#derivative) only along the real axis would not suffice.

<h3 id="4/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#4)

<h4 id="4/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#4/b)

[Cauchy's integral theorem](../complex-analysis.md#cauchy-s-integral-theorem) says that if $f$ is [holomorphic](../holomorphic-function.md) on an [open set](../topology.md#open-set) $U$ and a closed, [piecewise smooth](../complex-analysis.md#piecewise-smooth-curve) [contour](../complex-analysis.md#complex-integration-contour) $\gamma$ is [null-homotopic](../homotopy.md#null-homotopic-map) in $U$, then

$$
\boxed{\int_\gamma f(z)\,dz=0.}
$$

In particular, this holds for every closed [contour](../complex-analysis.md#complex-integration-contour) when $U$ is [simply connected](../algebraic-topology.md#simply-connected-space), and for a simple closed [contour](../complex-analysis.md#complex-integration-contour) whose boundary and enclosed region lie in $U$. For the later arguments it suffices to use the triangular version: the [integral](../integral.md) around a [triangle](../euclidean-geometry.md#triangle) is zero if the [triangle](../euclidean-geometry.md#triangle) and its interior lie in $U$ and $f$ is [holomorphic](../holomorphic-function.md) there.

Both the holomorphicity condition and a suitable condition excluding holes inside the [contour](../complex-analysis.md#complex-integration-contour) matter. [Continuity](../calculus.md#continuous-function) alone, which is the starting hypothesis for this question, does not imply the theorem's conclusion; nor does holomorphicity on an arbitrary punctured [open set](../topology.md#open-set) imply zero [integral](../integral.md) around every loop.

<h3 id="4/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#4)

<h4 id="4/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#4/c)

[Morera's theorem](../complex-analysis.md#morera-s-theorem) states that a [continuous](../calculus.md#continuous-function) function on an [open subset](../topology.md#open-set) of $\mathbb C$ is [holomorphic](../holomorphic-function.md) if its [integral](../integral.md) around every triangular [contour](../complex-analysis.md#complex-integration-contour), with [triangle](../euclidean-geometry.md#triangle) and interior contained in that [open set](../topology.md#open-set), is zero.

To prove this, work in any open disk $D$ whose closure lies in $U$, and fix $z_0\in D$. Define

$$
F(z)=\int_{[z_0,z]}f(w)\,dw,
$$

where the segment lies in $D$ by [convexity](../real-analysis.md#convex-function). The zero [integral](../integral.md) on the [triangle](../euclidean-geometry.md#triangle) with vertices $z_0,z,z+h$ gives

$$
F(z+h)-F(z)=\int_{[z,z+h]}f(w)\,dw
=h\int_0^1f(z+th)\,dt.
$$

[Continuity](../calculus.md#continuous-function) at $z$ implies that the last [integral](../integral.md) tends to $f(z)$ as $h\to0$: its difference from $f(z)$ is [bounded](../topological-analysis.md#boundedness) by $\sup_{0\leq t\leq1}|f(z+th)-f(z)|$. Therefore $F'=f$ on $D$. The function $F$ is [holomorphic](../holomorphic-function.md), and the allowed theorem that a [holomorphic](../holomorphic-function.md) [derivative](../calculus.md#derivative) is [holomorphic](../holomorphic-function.md) implies that $f=F'$ is [holomorphic](../holomorphic-function.md) on $D$.

Every point of $U$ lies in such a disk, proving the result throughout $U$:

$$
\boxed{\text{continuity plus vanishing triangle integrals implies holomorphicity}.}
$$

The proof is local and does not require $U$ to be [simply connected](../algebraic-topology.md#simply-connected-space).

<h3 id="4/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#4)

<h4 id="4/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#4/d)

For two moving vertices $p(s),q(s)$, the [integral](../integral.md) along their joining segment is

$$
J_{p,q}(s)=\bigl(q(s)-p(s)\bigr)\int_0^1 f\bigl((1-t)p(s)+tq(s)\bigr)\,dt.
$$

The [integral](../integral.md) factor is [continuous](../calculus.md#continuous-function) in $s$ by the allowed fact, and the other factor is [continuous](../calculus.md#continuous-function) because the vertices are [continuous](../calculus.md#continuous-function). Thus $J_{p,q}$ is [continuous](../calculus.md#continuous-function), including when its two endpoints coincide.

The [integral](../integral.md) around the [triangle](../euclidean-geometry.md#triangle) is exactly

$$
I(s)=J_{a,b}(s)+J_{b,c}(s)+J_{c,a}(s).
$$

Each term is [continuous](../calculus.md#continuous-function), so their sum is [continuous](../calculus.md#continuous-function) at zero and

$$
\boxed{\lim_{s\to0}\int_{\Delta_s}f(z)\,dz=\int_{\Delta_0}f(z)\,dz.}
$$

No holomorphicity is used here. [Compactness](../topology.md#compact-space) of the fixed containing [triangle](../euclidean-geometry.md#triangle) also gives a direct proof of the allowed fact through [uniform continuity](../topological-analysis.md#uniform-continuity) of $f$.

<h3 id="4/e">e</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#4)

<h4 id="4/e/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [E](#4/e)

First take $z\in U_-$ and put $w=\bar z\in U_+$. Reflection symmetry of the function gives

$$
\frac{f(z+h)-f(z)}h
=\overline{\frac{f(w+\bar h)-f(w)}{\bar h}}
\longrightarrow\overline{f'(w)}.
$$

Hence $f$ is [holomorphic](../holomorphic-function.md) on $U_-$, with $f'(z)=\overline{f'(\bar z)}$ there.

It remains to cross the real axis. Fix a real point $p\in U$ and a small disk about $p$ with closure in $U$. Consider a [triangle](../euclidean-geometry.md#triangle) in a still smaller disk. Intersect its filled interior with the closed upper and lower half-planes and triangulate the resulting [convex](../real-analysis.md#convex-function) polygons. This gives finitely many [triangles](../euclidean-geometry.md#triangle) lying on one side of the real axis, possibly with edges on that axis. Degenerate pieces contribute zero.

For an upper piece, translate its three vertices by $i\varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon>0$. For sufficiently small $\varepsilon$ the translated [triangle](../euclidean-geometry.md#triangle) lies entirely in $U_+$ and remains in $U$, so its boundary [integral](../integral.md) is zero by Cauchy's theorem. Part (d), or its segment-integral proof, shows that the [integral](../integral.md) converges to that around the untranslated piece as $\varepsilon\downarrow0$. Thus the original piece has zero [integral](../integral.md). Translate lower pieces by $-i\varepsilon$ to obtain the same conclusion in $U_-$.

When all the oriented [triangle](../euclidean-geometry.md#triangle) integrals are added, their internal edges cancel, including the segments on the real axis. The original [triangle](../euclidean-geometry.md#triangle) therefore has zero boundary [integral](../integral.md). [Continuity](../calculus.md#continuous-function) of $f$ on $U$ now allows [Morera's theorem](../complex-analysis.md#morera-s-theorem) in the disk, proving holomorphicity near $p$. Together with the two half-plane arguments this gives

$$
\boxed{f\text{ is holomorphic throughout }U.}
$$

This is a [Morera gluing lemma across a straight line](../complex-analysis.md#morera-gluing-lemma-across-a-straight-line); the original [continuity](../calculus.md#continuous-function) hypothesis is essential to the limiting step.

<h3 id="5/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#5)

<h4 id="5/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#5/a)

For an [isolated singularity](../isolated-singularity.md) at $a$, write the [Laurent expansion](../analysis.md#laurent-series) in a punctured disk as $f(z)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}c_k(z-a)^k$. Its [residue](../analysis.md#residue) is

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{res}(f;a)=c_{-1}.}
$$

The [sine](../geometry-and-topology.md#sine) in the denominator has simple zeros precisely at the integers, and the numerator is nonzero there. Consequently $g(z)=\pi\cot(\pi z)$ has [simple poles](../isolated-singularity.md#simple-pole) exactly at $n\in\mathbb Z$, with

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{res}(g;n)=\frac{\pi\cos(\pi n)}{\pi\cos(\pi n)}=1.}
$$

There are no other finite [singularities](../complex-analysis.md#complex-singularity).

<h3 id="5/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#5)

<h4 id="5/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#5/b)

The [residue theorem](../analysis.md#residue-theorem) states that if a function is [holomorphic](../holomorphic-function.md) on and inside a positively oriented simple closed [contour](../complex-analysis.md#complex-integration-contour) except at finitely many isolated [singularities](../complex-analysis.md#complex-singularity) $a_1,\ldots,a_N$ in its interior, then

$$
\boxed{\int_\gamma f(z)\,dz=2\pi i\sum_{j=1}^N\operatorname{res}(f;a_j).}
$$

No [singularity](../complex-analysis.md#complex-singularity) may lie on the [contour](../complex-analysis.md#complex-integration-contour). More generally, a closed [contour](../complex-analysis.md#complex-integration-contour) has a winding-number-weighted sum of [residues](../analysis.md#residue), provided it and its enclosed winding regions lie in the domain on which the isolated [singularities](../complex-analysis.md#complex-singularity) are specified.

<h3 id="5/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#5)

<h4 id="5/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#5/c)

A subset $B\subseteq\mathbb C$, with its [subspace topology](../topology.md#subspace-topology), is [compact](../topology.md#compact-space) when every [open cover](../topology.md#open-cover) of $B$ has a [finite subcover](../topology.md#finite-subcover). The [Heine-Borel theorem](../topology.md#heine-borel-theorem) in the [complex plane](../complex-analysis.md#complex-plane), identified with $\mathbb R^2$, says

$$
\boxed{B\text{ compact}\iff B\text{ closed and bounded}.}
$$

Closedness is in the ambient [complex plane](../complex-analysis.md#complex-plane). For a [continuous](../calculus.md#continuous-function) real-valued function on a nonempty [compact](../topology.md#compact-space) set, the [supremum](../real-analysis.md#supremum) and [infimum](../real-analysis.md#infimum) are finite and attained.

<h3 id="5/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#5)

<h4 id="5/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#5/d)

[Periodicity](../function.md#periodicity) reduces all the indicated vertical segments to the [compact](../topology.md#compact-space) segment $1/2+i[-1,1]$, which misses every pole. [Continuity](../calculus.md#continuous-function) therefore gives the first uniform bound. In fact an explicit identity gives a stronger estimate for every real $y$:

$$
g(m+1/2+iy)=-i\pi\tanh(\pi y),\qquad |g(m+1/2+iy)|\leq\pi.
$$

Thus we can take $D_1=\pi$.

Write $L=m+1/2$. On the vertical sides of the square the same bound holds, since both real coordinates are half-integers. On either horizontal side $|\operatorname{Im}z|=L\geq1/2$. For $\operatorname{Im}z=y>0$, put $w=e^{2\pi iz}$, so $|w|=e^{-2\pi y}$. Using

$$
\cot(\pi z)=i\frac{w+1}{w-1}
$$

gives $|g(z)|\leq\pi(1+e^{-2\pi y})/(1-e^{-2\pi y})\leq\pi\coth(\pi/2)$. [Conjugation](../group-action.md#conjugation) gives the same estimate below the real axis. Hence an explicit bound valid on every square is

$$
\boxed{D_2=\pi\coth(\pi/2).}
$$

For $L>|b|$, the [contour](../complex-analysis.md#complex-integration-contour) has length $8L$ and every point on it has $|z|\geq L$. The [reverse triangle inequality](../topological-analysis.md#reverse-triangle-inequality) yields $|z^2-b^2|\geq L^2-b^2$, so the [ML inequality](../complex-analysis.md#estimation-lemma) gives

$$
\left|\int_{\gamma_m}\frac{g(z)}{z^2-b^2}\,dz\right|
\leq\frac{8LD_2}{L^2-b^2}\longrightarrow0.
$$

Orient the square positively. Its enclosed poles at the integers $-m,\ldots,m$ have [residues](../analysis.md#residue) $1/(n^2-b^2)$. Since $b$ is nonintegral, the additional poles $b$ and $-b$ are distinct from those integers and from one another. Their [residues](../analysis.md#residue) are $g(b)/(2b)$ and $g(-b)/(-2b)$, both equal to $g(b)/(2b)$ because $g$ is odd. Their combined contribution is therefore $\pi\cot(\pi b)/b$.

The [residue theorem](../analysis.md#residue-theorem) gives

$$
\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma_m}\frac{g(z)}{z^2-b^2}\,dz
=-\frac1{b^2}+2\sum_{n=1}^m\frac1{n^2-b^2}+\frac{\pi\cot(\pi b)}b.
$$

The series is [absolutely convergent](../sequence-and-series.md#absolute-convergence), since its terms are $O(n^{-2})$. Taking the limit, then including the $n=0$ term, yields

$$
\boxed{\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac1{n^2-b^2}=-\frac1{2b^2}-\frac{\pi\cot(\pi b)}{2b},\qquad b\in\mathbb R\setminus\mathbb Z.}
$$

This is [cotangent contour summation](../analysis.md#cotangent-contour-summation). Keeping the $n=0$ term separate until the last step determines the sign of the $1/(2b^2)$ contribution.

## 6

↑ **Parent:** [2013](2013.md)

<h3 id="6/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#6)

<h4 id="6/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#6/a)

A [conformal map](../geometry-and-topology.md#conformal-map) preserves oriented [angles](../geometry-and-topology.md#angle) between regular smooth curves through each point. For a [holomorphic](../holomorphic-function.md) map with nonzero [derivative](../calculus.md#derivative), the [derivative](../calculus.md#derivative) also describes the local scale and [rotation](../riemannian-geometry.md#rotation-mathematics).

Indeed, if two curves $\gamma_1,\gamma_2$ meet at $z$ and have nonzero tangent [vectors](../vector-space.md#vector) $v_1,v_2$, the real [chain rule](../calculus.md#chain-rule) and [complex differentiability](../holomorphic-function.md#complex-differentiability) give

$$
(f\circ\gamma_j)'=f'(z)v_j.
$$

Because $f'(z)\neq0$, the [image](../set-theory.md#image-of-a-function) [tangents](../geometry-and-topology.md#tangent) are nonzero and

$$
\boxed{\frac{(f\circ\gamma_2)'}{(f\circ\gamma_1)'}=\frac{v_2}{v_1}.}
$$

Taking arguments proves preservation of oriented [angles](../geometry-and-topology.md#angle). Multiplication by $f'(z)$ rotates every tangent by $\arg f'(z)$ and scales every length by $|f'(z)|$. Thus the hypothesis gives [conformality](../geometry-and-topology.md#conformality) at every point of $U$; global [injectivity](../algebra.md#injective-function) is not needed for this local conclusion.

<h3 id="6/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#6)

<h4 id="6/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#6/b)

A [Möbius transformation](../group-theory.md#mobius-transformation) maps generalized [circles](../topology.md#circle) to generalized [circles](../topology.md#circle) on the [Riemann sphere](../riemann-surfaces.md#riemann-sphere). A [generalized circle](../group-theory.md#generalized-circle-under-a-mobius-transformation) is an ordinary [circle](../topology.md#circle) or a [straight line](../euclidean-geometry.md#straight-line) together with the point at infinity. Its [image](../set-theory.md#image-of-a-function) is a [straight line](../euclidean-geometry.md#straight-line) precisely when it contains infinity, equivalently when the [preimage](../set-theory.md#preimage) of infinity lies on the extended real axis.

If $c=0$, the pole is infinity itself and the transformation is the [affine map](../geometry-and-topology.md#affine-map) $(a/d)z+b/d$, taking the extended real axis to a line. If $c\neq0$, the pole is $-d/c$, which is real exactly when $d/c$ is real. Thus the full condition is

$$
\boxed{c=0\quad\text{or}\quad c\neq0\text{ and }d/c\in\mathbb R.}
$$

Equivalently, it is $\operatorname{Im}(d\bar c)=0$, a condition also valid when $c=0$. If it fails, the [image](../set-theory.md#image-of-a-function) misses infinity and is therefore **an ordinary [circle](../topology.md#circle)**. The nonzero [determinant](../multilinear-algebra.md#determinant) ensures that none of these generalized [circles](../topology.md#circle) collapses to a point.

<h3 id="6/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#6)

<h4 id="6/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#6/c)

[Liouville's theorem](../complex-analysis.md#liouville-theorem) states that every [bounded](../topological-analysis.md#boundedness) [entire function](../holomorphic-function.md#entire-function) is constant:

$$
\boxed{f\text{ holomorphic on }\mathbb C,\quad\sup_{z\in\mathbb C}|f(z)|<\infty\quad\Longrightarrow\quad f\text{ constant}.}
$$

For example, the Cauchy [derivative](../calculus.md#derivative) estimate on a [circle](../topology.md#circle) of radius $R$ gives $|f'(z)|\leq M/R$. Letting $R\to\infty$ gives $f'=0$ everywhere, proving the conclusion.

<h3 id="6/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#6)

<h4 id="6/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#6/d)

It suffices to construct an [injective](../algebra.md#injective-function) [holomorphic](../holomorphic-function.md) map $\Phi_j:U_j\to\mathbb D$ for each domain. Then $\Phi_j\circ f$ is a [bounded](../topological-analysis.md#boundedness) [entire function](../holomorphic-function.md#entire-function) and is constant by [Liouville's theorem](../complex-analysis.md#liouville-theorem); [injectivity](../algebra.md#injective-function) of $\Phi_j$ forces $f$ to be constant.

For the [upper half-plane](../complex-analysis.md#upper-half-plane-complex-analysis), use the [Cayley transform between the half-plane and disk](../complex-analysis.md#cayley-transform-between-the-half-plane-and-disk)

$$
\Phi_1(z)=\frac{z-i}{z+i}.
$$

Its denominator is nonzero there, and $|z+i|^2-|z-i|^2=4\operatorname{Im}z>0$, so its [image](../set-theory.md#image-of-a-function) lies in the [unit disk](../euclidean-geometry.md#unit-disk). The [determinant](../multilinear-algebra.md#determinant) is nonzero, hence it is [injective](../algebra.md#injective-function) and [conformal](../geometry-and-topology.md#conformal-map).

On the plane slit along the nonnegative real axis, choose the branch

$$
S(z)=\sqrt{|z|}\,e^{i\operatorname{Arg}z/2},\qquad0<\operatorname{Arg}z<2\pi.
$$

This is the [holomorphic square root](../complex-analysis.md#holomorphic-square-root) obtained from the corresponding [logarithm branch](../analysis.md#branch-of-the-complex-logarithm). Its [argument](../complex-analysis.md#argument-complex-analysis) lies in $(0,\pi)$, so it maps $U_2$ bijectively onto the [upper half-plane](../complex-analysis.md#upper-half-plane-complex-analysis), with inverse $w\mapsto w^2$. Its [derivative](../calculus.md#derivative) is $1/(2S(z))\neq0$. Therefore

$$
\Phi_2(z)=\frac{S(z)-i}{S(z)+i}
$$

is an [injective](../algebra.md#injective-function) [conformal map](../geometry-and-topology.md#conformal-map) of $U_2$ onto the [unit disk](../euclidean-geometry.md#unit-disk).

For the complement of the closed interval, first use

$$
M(z)=\frac{z}{1-z}.
$$

Its only finite pole is the excluded point one, and it is [injective](../algebra.md#injective-function) with [derivative](../calculus.md#derivative) $1/(1-z)^2\neq0$. If $M(z)=t\geq0$, solving gives $z=t/(1+t)\in[0,1)$, which is excluded. Hence $M(U_3)\subset U_2$, and

$$
\Phi_3(z)=\Phi_2\left(\frac{z}{1-z}\right)
$$

is the required [injective](../algebra.md#injective-function) [conformal map](../geometry-and-topology.md#conformal-map) into the disk. More precisely, $M(U_3)=U_2\setminus\{-1\}$ and $\Phi_3(U_3)=\mathbb D\setminus\{0\}$, since $S(-1)=i$. No claim that the interval complement is conformally equivalent to the whole disk is required.

Applying Liouville to the three [bounded](../topological-analysis.md#boundedness) compositions gives

$$
\boxed{f:\mathbb C\to U_j\text{ holomorphic}\Longrightarrow f\text{ constant},\qquad j=1,2,3.}
$$

This illustrates [Liouville rigidity from a bounded holomorphic embedding](../complex-analysis.md#liouville-rigidity-from-a-bounded-holomorphic-embedding): an explicit [injective](../algebra.md#injective-function) [bounded](../topological-analysis.md#boundedness) coordinate is enough.
