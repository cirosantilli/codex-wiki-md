↑ **Parent:** [A](../a.md)

The [history of science](../../../../history-of-science-split.md) is relevant wherever a philosophical claim concerns how inquiry actually develops, what its methods accomplish or what successful theories warrant us in believing. It cannot by itself determine every normative question about rationality. A historical event supplies evidence to an argument; it is not a rule that whatever scientists did must have been rational.

[Thomas Kuhn](../../../../thomas-kuhn.md)'s [The Structure of Scientific Revolutions by Thomas Kuhn](../../../../the-structure-of-scientific-revolutions-by-thomas-kuhn.md) makes this relevance explicit. Its historical approach challenges the picture of development as the straightforward accumulation of facts and theories under an unchanging method. Investigations conducted within a shared framework can concentrate on puzzle-solving rather than on constant attempts to overthrow their foundations. Episodes of larger change can alter the relevant problems and standards. This is evidence that a philosophical account purporting to describe all successful inquiry must accommodate more than an isolated [hypothesis](../../../../hypothesis.md) confronted by an isolated [observation](../../../../observation.md).[Introduction and the discussion of normal science and revolutionary change](../../../../the-structure-of-scientific-revolutions-by-thomas-kuhn.md)

The force of a historical example depends on the claim being tested. If a methodology says that rational scientists must reject a theory at its first anomalous reading, a well-documented case of productive work through anomalies is a serious objection to its descriptive adequacy and possibly its practical advice. It does not refute the validity of a deductive rule. From a failed prediction one still infers that some element of the predictive package is wrong; history helps show why identifying that element is not automatic. The [Duhem problem](../../../../duhem-problem.md) supplies the logical distinction, while historical investigation identifies how calibration, alternative explanations and further [experiments](../../../../experiment.md) were actually handled.

History is also central to the [pessimistic induction](../../../../pessimistic-induction.md). If some once-successful theories contained rejected posits, predictive success does not guarantee that every theoretical term refers. The replacement of [phlogiston theory](../../../../phlogiston-theory.md) by an [oxygen theory of combustion](../../../../oxygen-theory-of-combustion.md) is philosophically relevant precisely because it changed explanatory roles and material accounting, rather than simply replacing an unpopular name. A responsible comparison examines what each version predicted, how it was modified and which experimental findings were decisive. It should not attribute one conveniently absurd doctrine to every earlier investigator.[Success, reference and the historical counterexamples used against convergent realism](../../../../a-confutation-of-convergent-realism-by-larry-laudan.md)[Preliminary Discourse and Part I, chapters III–V: the organization of inquiry and combination with oxygen](../../../../elements-of-chemistry-by-antoine-lavoisier.md)

A realist can respond by distinguishing the parts responsible for success from other commitments or by identifying structure retained across change. This response itself has historical content. It must show the connection between the retained relations and actual achievements, rather than drawing a retrospective line through whatever resembles the present theory. History can constrain both the objection and the defence. It does not uniquely choose between realism, [structural realism](../../../../structural-realism.md) and empiricism without further epistemic premises.

There are limits to the evidential role. A logical objection to an invalid inference does not require counting how many famous investigators used it. Nor does a prevalence of a practice establish its justification. One must distinguish an account of why a community accepted a view from an argument that the view was supported by the evidence. The [context of discovery](../../../../context-of-discovery.md) and the [context of justification](../../../../context-of-justification.md) can be distinguished analytically without assuming that the circumstances of discovery never supply information relevant to justification.

Historical work also requires interpretive care. Sources written for patrons, students or critics can present an ideal of method rather than a complete record of practice. Later terminology can conceal changes in a term's role. Selecting only the eventual winners encourages [historical presentism](../../../../historical-presentism.md) and an exaggerated appearance of inevitability. These are reasons to improve the source-based inquiry, not reasons to ignore it.

The qualified answer is therefore affirmative. History provides counterexamples, identifies live alternatives and tests sweeping pictures of progress, method and theoretical commitment. Philosophy helps formulate what would count as a counterexample and which conclusions the evidence supports. Neither discipline replaces the other. Their interaction is most productive when an explicit general claim is confronted with a carefully reconstructed case, leaving the normative or logical steps visible rather than treating a historical narrative as a self-sufficient verdict.
