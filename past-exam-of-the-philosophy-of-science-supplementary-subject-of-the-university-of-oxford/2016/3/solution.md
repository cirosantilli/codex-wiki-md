<h1 id="3/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [3](../3.md)

The [problem of induction](../../../problem-of-induction.md) concerns the justification of inferences which go beyond what their premises deductively entail. Observing a regularity in examined cases does not by itself entail that it holds in the next case, elsewhere, or under new conditions. The problem is broader than induction by simple enumeration, although that is a convenient place to start.

[David Hume](../../../david-hume.md) separates demonstrative reasoning from reasoning about matters of fact. Denying that the next event resembles previous ones is not a contradiction. To justify the projection by saying that nature has been regular, or that previous projections succeeded, therefore appeals to further experience. Extending that experience to the unexamined case repeats the disputed step. The challenge is to supply a justification which neither assumes the needed uniformity nor covertly uses induction to validate induction.[Section IV, part II: whether an inference from experience can be founded on reasoning without circularity](../../../an-enquiry-concerning-human-understanding-by-david-hume.md)

The scientific content is easy to expose with a finite data set. Suppose measured pairs are $(x_i,y_i)$ and $f(x_i)=y_i$. Every function

$$
g(x)=f(x)+\lambda\prod_{i=1}^{n}(x-x_i)
$$

agrees with those measured values, while generally differing elsewhere. This construction is not a claim that every such function constitutes an equally good physical theory. Smoothness, dimensional constraints, known mechanisms and other [experiments](../../../experiment.md) can restrict the candidates. It shows only that the finite [observations](../../../observation.md) alone do not deductively fix a unique continuation. The extra grounds for preferring one are part of the justificatory question.

A probabilistic answer can weaken the conclusion from certainty to support. For [Bernoulli trials](../../../bernoulli-trial.md) independent conditional on a common unknown success chance $\theta$, a uniform [prior distribution](../../../prior-probability.md) gives the [posterior density](../../../posterior-density.md) $(n+1)\theta^n$ after $n$ successes. The [posterior predictive probability](../../../posterior-predictive-probability.md) of another success is

$$
\boxed{\frac{n+1}{n+2}.}
$$

The model represents rationally increasing confidence conditional on its sampling assumptions and prior. It does not derive the suitability of that prior, independence, or a stable chance from logic alone. It also distinguishes prediction from a universal generalization: under this continuous prior, $P(\theta=1)=0$ both before and after any finite number of successes. A separate positive [probability mass](../../../probability-mass.md) at one is needed if that exact possibility is to receive positive posterior [probability mass](../../../probability-mass.md). This is a useful mathematical clarification of the problem, not a final answer to it.

[Karl Popper](../../../karl-popper.md)'s [falsificationism](../../../falsificationism.md) offers a different response. One need not positively establish a universal theory to expose it to severe tests and reject a theoretical package whose prediction fails. The deductive inference is valid. Nevertheless, deciding that a surviving theory is a better guide to a future application still demands an account of why its past survival matters, and deciding which assumption failed raises the [Duhem problem](../../../duhem-problem.md). Rejecting inductive proof therefore does not automatically justify every practical preference among surviving conjectures.[Chapter 1: conjectures, attempts at refutation and the difference between support and conclusive verification](../../../science-conjectures-and-refutations-by-karl-popper.md)

A pragmatic defence instead asks what procedure is sensible when predictions must be made. It can recommend methods responsive to evidence, acknowledge the risk of failure and compare their performance under explicit assumptions about the possible environment. Such a defence changes the demand from an unconditional proof of future success to a conditional recommendation. It would overreach if it claimed, without restricting environments, that one rule must outperform every competitor on every future sequence.

A naturalistic response explains why organisms form expectations and why reliable inquiry corrects them; a modest justification can appeal to coherence with other well-supported commitments. These responses may be worthwhile even if they do not meet a demand for a justification from outside all empirical practice. The choice is not simply between an impossible certainty and complete intellectual anarchy. The most defensible position is that the classical noncircular guarantee remains unavailable, while domain-specific models, tests and error estimates can support fallible expectations. Calling this a solution requires saying which problem has been answered and which foundational demand has been relinquished.
