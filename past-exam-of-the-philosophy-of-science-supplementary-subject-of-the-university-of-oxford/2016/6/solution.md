<h1 id="6/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [6](../6.md)

The claim is defensible if it means that whether an object is observable does not change merely because an investigator adopts a different theory. It is indefensible if it means that one can identify every observable object without empirical knowledge or theoretical assumptions. [Bas van Fraassen](../../../bas-van-fraassen.md)'s distinction between those two kinds of dependence is essential to assessing his position.

For [constructive empiricism](../../../constructive-empiricism.md), [observability](../../../observability-philosophy-of-science.md) is a relation to an epistemic community and its perceptual capacities. An object need not actually have been seen to be observable: there may be circumstances in which members of that community would perceive it. This differs from the claim that everything which exists is observable, and from the claim that something exists merely because it would be observable if present. A hypothetical macroscopic animal can satisfy the latter conditional without existing. [Empirical adequacy](../../../empirical-adequacy.md) consequently concerns more than agreement with the [observations](../../../observation.md) already recorded.[Chapter 2, pp. 12–19, and chapter 3, pp. 57–59: observable phenomena, human capacities and the independence claim](../../../the-scientific-image-by-bas-van-fraassen.md)

There is a plausible objective component. Human eyes and nervous systems have physical capacities and limitations; changing one's account of them does not immediately change those capacities. Two theories may disagree about whether a particular object could be perceived under specified conditions, just as two theories can disagree about its [temperature](../../../temperature-split.md). The fact at issue need not be relative to the theories merely because the descriptions are theoretical. Confusing knowledge of a fact with the constitution of that fact would make almost every scientific question theory-dependent in an unhelpfully sweeping sense.

Nevertheless, inquiry into perceptual limits uses [optics](../../../optics-split.md), [physiology](../../../physiology-split.md) and [psychophysics](../../../psychophysics.md). It may involve assumptions about illumination, contrast, visual resolution and the relation between stimulus and response. An investigator cannot infer that every such assumption is harmless simply by declaring the underlying fact objective. Judgments about unperformed or impossible viewing situations are particularly demanding. [Theory-ladenness](../../../theory-ladenness.md) of the evidence is therefore compatible with the modest independence claim, while creating a problem for any stronger promise of a wholly theory-free boundary.

Instruments sharpen the issue. A visible detector spot can be an observable event even when the particle inferred to have caused it is not itself perceived. [Bas van Fraassen](../../../bas-van-fraassen.md) treats instruments as arrangements producing new phenomena to be represented, rather than automatically as windows making every inferred microscopic entity observable. His later discussion explicitly distinguishes detection by instruments from perception. Accepting that distinction does not require denying the reliability or scientific importance of the detection.[Section II: instruments as experimental arrangements producing observable effects, and detection distinguished from observation](../../../constructive-empiricism-now-by-bas-van-fraassen.md)

A realist may object that the resulting boundary gives an undue epistemic privilege to unaided perception. Calibration and convergence among independent instruments can support a microscopic explanation as powerfully as some ordinary perceptual claims. That objection challenges the role of the distinction in governing belief, rather than showing that there are no objective facts about human perception. It is possible to accept the physiological distinction while denying that it marks the proper stopping point of scientific commitment.

There is also a reflexive question. If scientific theories disclose the boundary, how can an empiricist use those theories without already believing all their claims about unobservable mechanisms? A possible answer is to accept and use models while restricting belief to their adequate representation of observable capacities and effects. Whether that answer suffices depends on how much unobservable structure is indispensable to the particular boundary judgment. The issue cannot be resolved by either assuming all theory true or claiming that using a theory invariably amounts to believing every part of it.

The strongest conclusion distinguishes three propositions. Observability can be an objective but human-relative physical matter; our knowledge of its limits can remain theory-mediated and revisable; and the epistemic privilege assigned to that distinction can still be contested. [Bas van Fraassen](../../../bas-van-fraassen.md) is right to reject the idea that a change of descriptive theory itself alters what our perceptual organs can do. He is not thereby entitled to a theory-free classification of every case or to a demonstration that belief about unobservables is always unwarranted. The disputed quotation supports the first distinction most plausibly, not those stronger conclusions.
