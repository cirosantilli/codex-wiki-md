**The [history of science](../../../history-of-science-split.md) can constrain philosophical claims and support conditional methodological lessons. It cannot, without further premises, turn a record of what scientists did into a rule about what everyone ought to believe.** Its strongest contribution is often to reveal that an apparently obvious account of science was too simple.

Consider the claim that scientific progress always adds new facts while preserving every central accepted proposition. The transition from [Newtonian mechanics](../../../newtonian-mechanics-split.md) to [special relativity](../../../special-relativity-split.md) supplies a counterexample. Under a [Galilean transformation](../../../galilean-transformation.md), temporal separation is absolute; a [Lorentz transformation](../../../lorentz-transformation.md) mixes time and space coordinates. These are incompatible accounts at unrestricted scope. Yet [Newtonian mechanics](../../../newtonian-mechanics-split.md) retains great predictive value at low [velocity](../../../velocity.md). Historical change therefore supports a distinction between wholesale accumulation of truths and more limited retention of successful relations. It neither shows that all past knowledge was worthless nor guarantees that current fundamental theories are final.

[Thomas Kuhn](../../../thomas-kuhn.md) makes the replacement of central commitments, problems and standards central to his account of scientific revolutions. His introductory examples concern changes that reorganize inquiry, rather than merely adding one item to a fixed inventory.[Introduction: revolutionary changes in commitments, accepted problems and scientific practice](../../../the-structure-of-scientific-revolutions-by-thomas-kuhn.md) This supports a warning against reading present categories unaltered into the past. It does not establish that different scientific communities can never compare evidence or understand one another. Semantic or methodological change is weaker than the claim that rational comparison is impossible.

A second lesson concerns anomalies. If actual successful inquiry sometimes retains a theory while examining instruments or [auxiliary hypotheses](../../../auxiliary-hypothesis.md), a methodology demanding abandonment at the first discrepancy misdescribes that inquiry. This matters because experimental predictions concern a package, not a lone sentence. [Lakatos](../../../imre-lakatos.md)'s comparison of progressive and degenerating programmes turns attention to whether retaining a framework produces independently corroborated developments.[transcript: scientific theories' tenacity and the need to distinguish successful development from retrospective adjustment](../../../science-and-pseudoscience-by-imre-lakatos.md) The lesson is conditional: persistence is defensible when it opens productive tests and alternatives, not simply because famous scientists once persisted.

The [history of science](../../../history-of-science-split.md) also puts pressure on [scientific realism](../../../scientific-realism.md). If empirically successful theories have posited entities later rejected, success alone cannot guarantee literal truth. [Larry Laudan](../../../larry-laudan.md)'s historical challenge targets a proposed explanatory connection between success, reference and approximate truth.[section 3, historical examples used to challenge success as a guarantee of reference](../../../a-confutation-of-convergent-realism-by-larry-laudan.md) But a list of failures cannot establish the fraction of successful theories that fail unless the population and selection process are specified. Cases chosen precisely because they were overturned are an especially poor basis for an unqualified numerical forecast about present theories.

There is also a distinction between a historical cause of belief and a justification for belief. Training, instrumentation and institutions affect which theories scientists can develop and test. This makes science a human activity without showing that its results are merely expressions of local preference. A historically informed evaluation should compare the evidence available at the time, the competing proposals, and the costs of different mistakes. Otherwise, hindsight converts the eventual winner's assumptions into premises that earlier participants supposedly should already have known.

These conclusions are philosophically important because they change how we allocate trust. They favour confidence differentiated by claim and domain, explicit attention to [auxiliary hypotheses](../../../auxiliary-hypothesis.md), and humility about extrapolation. They also favour institutions that preserve criticism and [scientific replication](../../../scientific-replication.md), because individual ingenuity does not ensure reliable correction. The further normative premise is explicit: methods deserve support insofar as they help inquiry detect error and achieve its evidential aims.

History consequently offers counterexamples to universal philosophical generalizations and evidence for competing explanations of scientific change. It does not choose a unique epistemology automatically. Its value lies in making accounts of rationality and progress answerable to what careful investigation actually reveals, while keeping the passage from description to evaluation open to argument.
