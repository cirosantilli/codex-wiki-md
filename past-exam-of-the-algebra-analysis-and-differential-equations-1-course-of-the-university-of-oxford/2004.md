## Algebra

↑ **Parent:** [2004](2004.md)

<h3 id="algebra/1">1</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [Algebra](#algebra)

<h4 id="algebra/1/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#algebra/1)

**$\dim_{\mathbb R}V=4$.** For arbitrary matrices,

$$
\langle A\mid B\rangle=\operatorname{tr}(A^tB)=\sum_{i,j=1}^2a_{ij}b_{ij}.
$$

This is [bilinear](../multilinear-algebra.md#bilinear-map) and [symmetric](../set-theory.md#symmetric-relation). Also $\langle A\mid A\rangle=\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}^2\geq0$, with equality exactly when every entry vanishes. Thus it is an [inner product](../inner-product.md), the [Frobenius inner product](../inner-product.md#frobenius-inner-product).

If $S^t=S$ and $K^t=-K$, invariance of [trace](../linear-algebra.md#matrix-trace) under transposition and cyclicity give

$$
\langle S\mid K\rangle=\operatorname{tr}(SK)=\operatorname{tr}((SK)^t)=-\operatorname{tr}(KS)=-\operatorname{tr}(SK)=0.
$$

Furthermore $\langle I\mid A\rangle=\operatorname{tr}A$, so every traceless [matrix](../vector-space.md#matrix) is [orthogonal](../inner-product.md#orthogonal-vectors) to the identity. One convenient [orthonormal basis](../inner-product.md#orthonormal-basis) adapted to these observations is

$$
\boxed{\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix},\quad\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}.}
$$

Their entrywise inner products are zero in distinct pairs and one on the diagonal; four orthonormal vectors in this four-dimensional space form a [basis](../vector-space.md#basis).

<h3 id="algebra/2">2</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [Algebra](#algebra)

<h4 id="algebra/2/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#algebra/2)

[Self-adjointness](../linear-operator-theory.md#self-adjoint-operator) means $\langle Tv,w\rangle=\langle v,Tw\rangle$ for all $v,w\in V$, or $T=T^*$. Take the [inner product](../inner-product.md) linear in its first variable. If $Tv=\lambda v$ with $v\ne0$, then

$$
\lambda\|v\|^2=\langle Tv,v\rangle=\langle v,Tv\rangle=\overline\lambda\|v\|^2.
$$

Therefore **every [eigenvalue](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) is real**.

If $U$ is invariant and $w\in U^\perp$, then for every $u\in U$, $Tu\in U$ and $\langle Tw,u\rangle=\langle w,Tu\rangle=0$. Hence $Tw\in U^\perp$, proving invariance of the [orthogonal complement](../hilbert-space.md#orthogonal-complement).

Induct on $d=\dim V$. The zero-dimensional case is empty. For $d>0$, choose an [eigenvector](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvector) and normalize it to a unit vector $e$. Its span is invariant, so its [orthogonal complement](../hilbert-space.md#orthogonal-complement) is invariant as just proved. The restriction of $T$ to that complement is again [self-adjoint](../linear-operator-theory.md#self-adjoint-operator). Its [dimension](../vector-space.md#dimension-vector-space) is $d-1$, so [induction](../foundations-of-mathematics.md#mathematical-induction) supplies an [orthonormal basis](../inner-product.md#orthonormal-basis) of [eigenvectors](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvector) there. Adjoining $e$ gives **an orthonormal [eigenbasis](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenbasis) of $V$**. This proves the [finite-dimensional spectral theorem](../linear-operator-theory.md#finite-dimensional-spectral-theorem) without assuming it in the argument.

<h3 id="algebra/3">3</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [Algebra](#algebra)

<h4 id="algebra/3/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#algebra/3)

<h5 id="algebra/3/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#algebra/3/i)

A [ring](../commutative-algebra.md#ring) is a set with an abelian-group [addition](../arithmetic.md#addition), associative [multiplication](../arithmetic.md#multiplication) and both distributive laws; here we use [rings](../commutative-algebra.md#ring) with multiplicative identity. A [field](../algebra.md#field) is a nonzero [commutative ring](../commutative-algebra.md#commutative-ring) in which every nonzero element has a multiplicative inverse.

**The [positive integers](../integer.md#positive-integer) form neither a [ring](../commutative-algebra.md#ring) nor a [field](../algebra.md#field).** They do not contain an [additive identity](../algebraic-operation.md#additive-identity) zero, and they also lack [additive inverses](../arithmetic.md#additive-inverse).

<h4 id="algebra/3/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#algebra/3)

<h5 id="algebra/3/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#algebra/3/ii)

**$\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z$ is a [ring](../commutative-algebra.md#ring) but not a [field](../algebra.md#field).** Quotient operations inherit associativity and distributivity from the integers, and residues have [additive inverses](../arithmetic.md#additive-inverse). However the nonzero residue $\overline2$ satisfies $\overline2\,\overline2=\overline0$, so it cannot be invertible. A [field](../algebra.md#field) has no nonzero [zero divisors](../mathematics.md#zero-divisor).

<h4 id="algebra/3/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#algebra/3)

<h5 id="algebra/3/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#algebra/3/iii)

**$\mathbb Z/3\mathbb Z$ is a [field](../algebra.md#field), and consequently also a [ring](../commutative-algebra.md#ring).** Its nonzero elements are $\overline1$ and $\overline2$, with inverses $\overline1$ and $\overline2$ respectively. The [quotient ring](../commutative-algebra.md#quotient-ring) axioms are inherited as in the preceding part.

<h4 id="algebra/3/iv">iv</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#algebra/3)

<h5 id="algebra/3/iv/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#algebra/3/iv)

This is the [Gaussian integer](../commutative-algebra.md#gaussian-integer) [ring](../commutative-algebra.md#ring) $\mathbb Z[i]$. It contains zero and one and is closed under [addition](../arithmetic.md#addition), [additive inverses](../arithmetic.md#additive-inverse) and [multiplication](../arithmetic.md#multiplication), since

$$
(a+bi)(c+di)=(ac-bd)+(ad+bc)i.
$$

Its operations inherit the [ring](../commutative-algebra.md#ring) axioms from $\mathbb C$. It is **a [ring](../commutative-algebra.md#ring) but not a [field](../algebra.md#field)**: the nonzero element 2 has complex inverse $1/2$, which is not a [Gaussian integer](../commutative-algebra.md#gaussian-integer).

## Analysis

↑ **Parent:** [2004](2004.md)

<h3 id="analysis/4">4</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [Analysis](#analysis)

<h4 id="analysis/4/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#analysis/4)

Writing $z=x+iy$ and $f=u+iv$, the [Cauchy-Riemann equations](../analysis.md#cauchy-riemann-equations) are

$$
\boxed{u_x=v_y,\qquad u_y=-v_x.}
$$

With continuous second derivatives, mixed derivatives commute. Thus

$$
u_{xx}+u_{yy}=v_{yx}-v_{xy}=0,\qquad v_{xx}+v_{yy}=-u_{yx}+u_{xy}=0.
$$

Both real and imaginary parts are [harmonic](../partial-differential-equation.md#harmonic-function).

For $g(z)=\operatorname{Re}z$, the real and imaginary parts are $x$ and zero, so $u_x=1\ne0=v_y$. For $h(z)=\bar z$, they are $x$ and $-y$, so $u_x=1\ne-1=v_y$. The necessary [Cauchy-Riemann equations](../analysis.md#cauchy-riemann-equations) fail at every point. Hence **both functions are nowhere [holomorphic](../holomorphic-function.md)**, indeed nowhere [complex differentiable](../holomorphic-function.md#complex-differentiability).

<h3 id="analysis/5">5</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [Analysis](#analysis)

<h4 id="analysis/5/a">a</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#analysis/5)

<h5 id="analysis/5/a/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#analysis/5/a)

A [removable singularity](../isolated-singularity.md#removable-singularity) at $a$ means that there is a [holomorphic function](../holomorphic-function.md) on the whole disc which agrees with $f$ away from $a$. Equivalently, the [Laurent series](../analysis.md#laurent-series) has no negative powers. The value at $a$ is filled in by its finite limit.

<h4 id="analysis/5/b">b</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#analysis/5)

<h5 id="analysis/5/b/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#analysis/5/b)

A [pole](../isolated-singularity.md#pole) of order $m\geq1$ means that $(z-a)^mf(z)$ extends holomorphically to $a$ and has a nonzero value there. Equivalently, the lowest nonzero power in the [Laurent expansion](../analysis.md#laurent-series) is $(z-a)^{-m}$.

<h4 id="analysis/5/c">c</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#analysis/5)

<h5 id="analysis/5/c/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#analysis/5/c)

An [essential singularity](../isolated-singularity.md#essential-singularity) is an [isolated singularity](../isolated-singularity.md) which is neither removable nor a [pole](../isolated-singularity.md#pole); equivalently its [Laurent expansion](../analysis.md#laurent-series) has infinitely many nonzero negative-power terms.

For the final unnumbered classification, the only possible finite singularities are the zeros of the denominator, namely $z=\pm i$ and $z=k\pi$ for integers $k$. At zero,

$$
f(z)=\frac1{(1+z^2)(\sin z/z)^2}\longrightarrow1,
$$

so **zero is removable, with extension value one**. At each nonzero $k\pi$, $\sin z$ has a simple zero, the other denominator factor is nonzero and the numerator is nonzero. These points are **[poles](../isolated-singularity.md#pole) of order two**, with leading coefficient $(k\pi)^2/((k\pi)^2+1)$.

At $z=\pm i$, the [polynomial](../polynomial.md) denominator has a simple zero. Since $\sin(\pm i)=\pm i\sinh1\ne0$ and the numerator is $-1$, these are **simple [poles](../isolated-singularity.md#pole)**. There are no essential singularities on $\mathbb C$ and no other finite singularities.

<h3 id="analysis/6">6</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [Analysis](#analysis)

<h4 id="analysis/6/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#analysis/6)

After division by $z(z+2)$, the coefficients are

$$
p(z)=\frac{z+1}{z(z+2)},\qquad q(z)=-\frac{\alpha^2}{z(z+2)}.
$$

Their only finite singularities are **$z=0$ and $z=-2$**. At either point $z_0$, the products $(z-z_0)p$ and $(z-z_0)^2q$ are analytic, so both points are regular singular points. All other finite points are ordinary.

The lowest power after substituting the [Frobenius series](../complex-analysis.md#frobenius-solution) gives $\sigma(2\sigma-1)=0$. The coefficient of $z^{n+\sigma}$ then gives

$$
\boxed{\sigma=0\text{ or }\frac12,\qquad a_{n+1}=\frac{\alpha^2-(n+\sigma)^2}{(n+\sigma+1)(2n+2\sigma+1)}a_n.}
$$

For both exponents the denominator is nonzero for every $n\geq0$, so each arbitrary nonzero $a_0$ determines a series. Unless it terminates, its coefficient ratio tends to $-1/2$, proving convergence for $|z|<2$. For the half-integer exponent choose a square-root branch. Their leading powers $1$ and $z^{1/2}$ show that the two local solutions are [linearly independent](../vector-space.md#linear-independence).

If $\alpha=m$ is a [positive integer](../integer.md#positive-integer), choose $\sigma=0$. The recurrence numerator is nonzero for $0\leq n<m$ and vanishes at $n=m$. Thus $a_m\ne0$, $a_{m+1}=0$, and all later coefficients vanish: **a [polynomial](../polynomial.md) solution of exact degree $m$ exists**. It is proportional to the [Chebyshev polynomial](../numerical-analysis.md#chebyshev-polynomial) $T_m(z+1)$, since the substitution $t=z+1$ transforms the equation into $(t^2-1)y''+ty'-m^2y=0$.

## Differential equations

↑ **Parent:** [2004](2004.md)

<h3 id="differential-equations/7">7</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [Differential equations](#differential-equations)

<h4 id="differential-equations/7/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [7](#differential-equations/7)

The first iterate is

$$
\boxed{y_1(x)=1+2x+\frac{x^3}{3}.}
$$

On the prescribed box, $F(x,y)=1+x^2+y^2$ is continuous, $|F|\leq102$, and $|F(x,y)-F(x,v)|\leq20|y-v|$. The initial value is distance nine from the nearer horizontal edge. Thus on $|x|\leq h=9/102=3/34$, the Picard map keeps functions within the box: its values differ from one by at most $102h=9$.

Successive differences satisfy, by [induction](../foundations-of-mathematics.md#mathematical-induction),

$$
|y_{n+1}(x)-y_n(x)|\leq102\frac{20^n|x|^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}.
$$

The [Weierstrass M-test](../probability-and-statistics.md#weierstrass-m-test) proves [uniform convergence](../sequence-and-series.md#uniform-convergence) on this local interval. The [uniform limit](../sequence-and-series.md#uniform-limit) is continuous, and the Lipschitz estimate allows passage to the limit inside the integral, giving the displayed integral equation. Differentiation gives $y'=1+x^2+y^2$, $y(0)=1$. The same local Lipschitz bound proves local [uniqueness](../mathematical-logic.md#uniqueness).

Direct differentiation gives $u'=\sec^2(x+\pi/4)=1+u^2$, $u(0)=1$, and $u\to+\infty$ as $x\uparrow\pi/4$. On the common positive existence interval put $w=y-u$. It satisfies $w'=(y+u)w+x^2$, $w(0)=0$, so the integrating-factor formula gives

$$
w(x)=e^{\int_0^x(y+u)\,ds}\int_0^x t^2e^{-\int_0^t(y+u)\,ds}\,dt\geq0.
$$

Thus $y\geq u$. Let $x_*>0$ be the maximal forward existence endpoint. The solution is strictly increasing because $y'\geq1$. If $x_*$ were finite and $y$ remained bounded, its finite limit and the smooth right side would allow local continuation, contradicting maximality. Comparison with $u$ prevents existence through $\pi/4$. Consequently

$$
\boxed{0<x_*\leq\frac\pi4,\qquad\lim_{x\uparrow x_*}y(x)=+\infty.}
$$

There is **no contradiction with Picard's theorem**, which gives local existence and [uniqueness](../mathematical-logic.md#uniqueness). Its box estimate gives only a short guaranteed interval, and the Lipschitz constant for $y^2$ is not globally bounded in $y$.

<h3 id="differential-equations/8">8</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [Differential equations](#differential-equations)

<h4 id="differential-equations/8/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [8](#differential-equations/8)

First solve for $v=y'$. [Multiplication](../arithmetic.md#multiplication) by $e^x$ and use of $v(1)=0$ give

$$
v(x)=-e^{-x}\int_x^1e^tf(t)\,dt.
$$

Integrating from zero to $x$, and using $y(0)=0$, yields $y(x)=-\int_0^xe^{-s}\int_s^1e^tf(t)\,dt\,ds$. Reversing the order of integration gives

$$
\boxed{G_1(x,t)=1-e^t\quad(0\leq t\leq x),\qquad G_2(x,t)=e^{t-x}-e^t\quad(x\leq t\leq1).}
$$

Thus $G(x,t)$ equals $G_1$ for $t<x$ and $G_2$ for $x<t$. It is continuous at $x=t$, vanishes at $x=0$, and has $G_x(1,t)=0$. Its derivative jumps from $-1$ to zero across $x=t$, so the jump is $+1$, matching the coefficient of $y''$. Both pieces satisfy $G_{xx}+G_x=0$ away from the jump. These facts also verify the Green representation directly. The homogeneous solutions are $A+Be^{-x}$, and the two [boundary conditions](../differential-equation.md#boundary-condition) force $A=B=0$, proving [uniqueness](../mathematical-logic.md#uniqueness).

For $f(t)=e^{-t}$, the formula gives

$$
\int_0^x(e^{-t}-1)\,dt+\int_x^1(e^{-x}-1)\,dt=-xe^{-x}.
$$

Hence **$y(x)=-xe^{-x}$**. Indeed $y'=(x-1)e^{-x}$ and $y''=(2-x)e^{-x}$, which verify the equation and both [boundary conditions](../differential-equation.md#boundary-condition).

<h3 id="differential-equations/9">9</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [Differential equations](#differential-equations)

<h4 id="differential-equations/9/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [9](#differential-equations/9)

<h5 id="differential-equations/9/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#differential-equations/9/i)

This is the **[heat equation](../diffusion-equation.md#heat-equation)**, with $y$ as time. The stated zero solution is **unique**. Define $E(y)=\frac12\int_0^1u(x,y)^2\,dx$. For $0<y<1$, [integration by parts](../calculus.md#integration-by-parts) and $u_y=u_{xx}$ give

$$
E'(y)=\int_0^1u\,u_{xx}\,dx=[uu_x]_0^1-\int_0^1u_x^2\,dx=-\int_0^1u_x^2\,dx\leq0.
$$

The boundary term is zero because $u(0,y)=0$ and $u_x(1,y)=0$. Also $E(0)=0$. Nonnegativity forces $E(y)=0$, and [continuity](../calculus.md#continuous-function) gives $u=0$ throughout the square. The unknown final-time boundary values are determined by evolution and need not be prescribed. Under the stated regularity the integrations can first be made on an interior interval and then passed to the endpoints using [continuity](../calculus.md#continuous-function) of $u,u_x,u_y$.

<h4 id="differential-equations/9/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [9](#differential-equations/9)

<h5 id="differential-equations/9/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#differential-equations/9/ii)

This is **[Laplace's equation](../partial-differential-equation.md#laplace-equation)**. Zero is **not unique**: with $k=\pi/2$,

$$
\boxed{u(x,y)=\sin(kx)\sinh(ky)}
$$

is nonzero and [harmonic](../partial-differential-equation.md#harmonic-function). It vanishes on $y=0$ and $x=0$, while $u_x(1,y)=k\cos(k)\sinh(ky)=0$.

A sufficient additional condition is **$u(x,1)=0$** on the top edge. [Green's first identity](../partial-differential-equation.md#green-s-first-identity) then gives

$$
\int_D|\nabla u|^2\,dx\,dy=\int_{\partial D}u\,\partial_nu\,ds-\int_Du\Delta u\,dx\,dy=0.
$$

On the bottom, left and top edges $u=0$; on the right edge the normal derivative is zero. Thus the [gradient](../multivariable-calculus.md#gradient) vanishes and $u$ is constant; the bottom value makes that constant zero. For $C^2(D)\cap C^1(\overline D)$, apply the identity on inset rectangles and pass to the boundary using [continuity](../calculus.md#continuous-function) of the function and its [gradient](../multivariable-calculus.md#gradient).

<h4 id="differential-equations/9/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [9](#differential-equations/9)

<h5 id="differential-equations/9/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#differential-equations/9/iii)

This is the **[wave equation](../wave-equation.md)**, again taking $y$ as time. Zero is **not unique**: for $k=\pi/2$,

$$
\boxed{u(x,y)=\sin(kx)\sin(ky)}
$$

satisfies $u_{xx}=u_{yy}$ and all the stated [boundary conditions](../differential-equation.md#boundary-condition). The [initial displacement](../differential-equation.md#initial-displacement) is zero but the [initial velocity](../differential-equation.md#initial-velocity) is not prescribed.

A sufficient extra condition is **$u_y(x,0)=0$**. For the resulting homogeneous data use wave energy

$$
E(y)=\frac12\int_0^1(u_y^2+u_x^2)\,dx.
$$

The equation and [integration by parts](../calculus.md#integration-by-parts) give $E'(y)=[u_xu_y]_0^1=0$. At $x=1$ the factor $u_x$ vanishes, and at $x=0$ the factor $u_y$ vanishes because $u(0,y)=0$. The [initial displacement](../differential-equation.md#initial-displacement) implies $u_x(x,0)=0$, so the extra [initial velocity](../differential-equation.md#initial-velocity) condition gives $E(0)=0$. Consequently $u_x=u_y=0$ and $u=0$. With the stated boundary regularity, integrate the local [energy conservation](../mathematical-physics.md#conservation-of-energy) law over inset rectangles and let their edges approach the boundary; the energy and flux terms converge by [continuity](../calculus.md#continuous-function) of the first derivatives.
