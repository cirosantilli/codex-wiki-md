# Buoyancy

↑ **Parent:** [Fluid mechanics](fluid-mechanics-split.md)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buoyancy)

[Buoyancy](buoyancy.md) is the resultant upward [force](force.md) exerted by a resting [fluid](fluid.md) on an immersed body in a downward [gravitational field](gravitational-field.md). The [fluid](fluid.md) pushes inward, perpendicular to each part of the body's surface. Because [hydrostatic pressure](hydrostatic-pressure.md) increases with depth, the upward push from below exceeds the downward push from above. Opposite horizontal [forces](force.md) cancel; [buoyancy](buoyancy.md) is the remaining vertical [force](force.md). A sinking body still receives this upward support. [OpenStax, University Physics, §14.4: origin of buoyancy](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/14-4-archimedes-principle-and-buoyancy)

More precisely, [pressure](pressure.md) $p$ on a small surface of [area](surface-area.md) $dA$ with outward unit [normal vector](normal-vector.md) $\mathbf n$ exerts

$$
d\mathbf F=-p\mathbf n\,dA.
$$

The minus sign says the [force](force.md) points into the body. On a [cube](cube.md), the top [force](force.md) points down, the bottom [force](force.md) points up, and the side [forces](force.md) cancel in pairs because opposite sides have the same [pressure](pressure.md) at each depth. This is a surface [traction](traction.md); [gravity](gravity.md) on the body itself is a separate [body force](body-force.md). [MIT, A. Techet, Hydrodynamics reading 1, p. 5: pressure traction](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/2-016-hydrodynamics-13-012-fall-2005/dc299d2ff05553b3515dccc23fc0f7d2_2005reading1.pdf)

For a computed example, stipulate resting [water](water.md) with constant [mass density](density.md) $\rho_f=1000\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ and uniform [gravitational acceleration](gravitational-acceleration.md) $g=9.81\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$. A fully immersed [cube](cube.md) has side $L=0.10\ \mathrm m$, top depth $h_t=0.20\ \mathrm m$ and bottom depth $h_b=0.30\ \mathrm m$. Each horizontal face has [area](surface-area.md) $A=L^2=0.010\ \mathrm{m^2}$, and its [volume](volume.md) is $V=L^3=0.001\ \mathrm{m^3}$. Its top and bottom [gauge pressures](gauge-pressure.md) are

$$
p_{g,t}=\rho_fgh_t=1962\ \mathrm{Pa},\qquad
p_{g,b}=\rho_fgh_b=2943\ \mathrm{Pa}.
$$

The corresponding [forces](force.md) relative to the common surface [pressure](pressure.md) are $p_{g,t}A=19.62\ \mathrm N$ downward and $p_{g,b}A=29.43\ \mathrm N$ upward. Therefore the net [buoyancy](buoyancy.md) is

$$
F_b=(p_{g,b}-p_{g,t})A=9.81\ \mathrm N.
$$

Using absolute [pressures](pressure.md) adds the same $p_{\mathrm{surface}}A$ to both face [forces](force.md), leaving their difference unchanged. Zero [gauge pressure](gauge-pressure.md) at the surface does not mean zero absolute [pressure](pressure.md). Increasing both depths by the same amount also leaves [buoyancy](buoyancy.md) unchanged in this constant-[mass density](density.md) model.

![](hydrostatic-pressure-and-buoyancy.png)

**[Figure 9](#image-how-a-hydrostatic-pressure-difference-supports-an-immersed-cube). How a hydrostatic pressure difference supports an immersed cube**. Left: a fully immersed [cube](cube.md) of side $0.10\ \mathrm m$ has its top at depth $0.20\ \mathrm m$ and bottom at $0.30\ \mathrm m$. The inward [pressure](pressure.md) [forces](force.md) on its sides cancel; its horizontal faces have [area](surface-area.md) $0.010\ \mathrm{m^2}$. The displayed face [forces](force.md) use [gauge pressure](gauge-pressure.md), whose common surface offset cancels in the net $9.81\ \mathrm N$ upward [buoyancy](buoyancy.md). Right: [gauge pressure](gauge-pressure.md) increases linearly with depth, marking $1962\ \mathrm{Pa}$ at the top and $2943\ \mathrm{Pa}$ at the bottom.

[Archimedes' principle](archimedes-principle.md) turns this calculation into a rule for bodies of any shape. It explains [floating equilibrium](floating-equilibrium.md) and lets us infer an immersed body's [volume](volume.md) from the difference between its [force](force.md) from [gravity](gravity.md) and the [force](force.md) needed to support it. In particular, [buoyancy](buoyancy.md) depends on the displaced [volume](volume.md) and the surrounding [mass density](density.md), whereas the body's own [mass density](density.md) decides how its [force](force.md) from [gravity](gravity.md) compares with that support.
