<h1 id="1/c/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [C](../c.md)

During the time light crosses a [telescope](../../../../../telescope.md) of [length](../../../../../length.md) $L$, [Earth](../../../../../earth.md) moves sideways by approximately $vL/c$. The [telescope](../../../../../telescope.md) must tilt towards [Earth](../../../../../earth.md)'s motion so that both ends intercept the ray. Thus the [annual stellar aberration amplitude](../../../../../annual-stellar-aberration-amplitude.md) at a transverse sightline is $\delta\simeq v/c$.

Taking one orbital year as $365.25$ days and treating the orbit as circular,

$$
v=\frac{2\pi(1.5\times10^{11}\,\mathrm m)}{365.25(86400\,\mathrm s)}=2.99\times10^4\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}},
\qquad
\boxed{\delta\simeq9.96\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{rad}=20.5\text{ arcseconds}.}
$$

This is the instantaneous shift relative to a stationary heliocentric observer, not the doubled peak-to-peak annual excursion. The exact [Lorentz transformation](../../../../../lorentz-transformation.md) for a ray transverse in that frame gives $\sin\delta=v/c$, with the same first-order result.
