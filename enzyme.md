# Enzyme

↑ **Parent:** [Biology](biology-split.md)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enzyme)

An [enzyme](enzyme.md) is a biological [catalyst](catalyst.md): it accelerates a [chemical reaction](chemical-reaction.md) and is regenerated after a successful catalytic cycle. Most [enzymes](enzyme.md) are [proteins](protein.md), but catalytic [RNA](ribonucleic-acid.md) molecules, called [ribozymes](ribozyme.md), also exist. Many reactions needed by a [biological cell](biological-cell.md) would proceed too slowly under its ordinary conditions without [enzymes](enzyme.md). See [Cooper, The Cell: the central role of enzymes as biological catalysts](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK9921/).

The biological beauty is selective speed. A [biological cell](biological-cell.md) contains many possible [reactants](reactant.md); its [enzymes](enzyme.md) help determine which transformations happen on a useful timescale. In [metabolism](metabolism.md), one step's [reaction product](product-chemistry.md) can become another step's [substrate](substrate-biochemistry.md), so selective catalysts support an organized flow of material.

The [ribosome](ribosome.md) is a striking biological example: the machinery that builds [proteins](protein.md) has [RNA](ribonucleic-acid.md) at its catalytic centre. The complete [ribosome](ribosome.md) contains both [RNA](ribonucleic-acid.md) and [proteins](protein.md), but its bond-forming [active site](active-site.md) is built from [RNA](ribonucleic-acid.md). Structural evidence established this distinction in [Nissen et al., The structural basis of ribosome activity in peptide bond synthesis, Science 289, 920–930 (2000)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10937990/).

Selection occurs at an [active site](active-site.md), the region where [substrate](substrate-biochemistry.md) binding and catalytic chemistry take place. Shape and chemical interactions favour some [substrates](substrate-biochemistry.md) over others; binding can also reshape the [enzyme](enzyme.md), an [induced fit](induced-fit.md). Recognition alone is insufficient: holding a [substrate](substrate-biochemistry.md) extremely tightly need not make it react quickly. Catalysis requires a favourable route through the [transition state](transition-state.md), the high-barrier configuration between [reactants](reactant.md) and [reaction products](product-chemistry.md). Positioning reactive groups and stabilizing this configuration relative to the bound [substrate](substrate-biochemistry.md) can lower that barrier. Specificity is often strong, but need not mean exactly one acceptable [substrate](substrate-biochemistry.md). See [Cooper, The Cell: mechanisms of enzymatic catalysis](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK9921/).

**[Figure 56](#image-enzymes-lower-reaction-barriers-while-leaving-the-overall-gibbs-free-energy-difference-unchanged). Enzymes lower reaction barriers while leaving the overall Gibbs free energy difference unchanged**. Original illustrative profiles, with the free [enzyme](enzyme.md) included in both endpoint states. The horizontal coordinate orders configurations along each route; it is neither elapsed time nor a measured distance. Actual catalytic mechanisms can have several intermediates and barriers.

Read the diagram numerically. Its chosen [Gibbs free energies](gibbs-free-energy.md) are $0$ for the [reactants](reactant.md) and $-10\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ for the [reaction products](product-chemistry.md). The uncatalysed peak is $60\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$, whereas the catalysed peak is $40\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$. The forward free-energy barriers are therefore $60$ and $40\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$; viewed from the [reaction products](product-chemistry.md), the reverse barriers are $70$ and $50\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$. Both decrease by $20\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$, while the endpoint difference stays $-10\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$. These are constructed values, not measurements of a particular [enzyme](enzyme.md).

This separates [chemical kinetics](chemical-kinetics.md) from [chemical equilibrium](chemical-equilibrium.md). An [enzyme](enzyme.md) changes how rapidly the system approaches [chemical equilibrium](chemical-equilibrium.md); it does not change the [equilibrium constant](equilibrium-constant.md) for the same overall [chemical reaction](chemical-reaction.md) under the same conditions. Faster catalysis therefore supplies no extra driving [energy](energy.md). In a [biological cell](biological-cell.md), continuing supply of [reactants](reactant.md) and removal of [reaction products](product-chemistry.md) can nevertheless sustain a net flow: the surroundings keep that system away from [chemical equilibrium](chemical-equilibrium.md). See [IUBMB, Symbolism and terminology in enzyme kinetics: basic definitions](https://iubmb.qmul.ac.uk/kinetics/ek1t3.html).

Some [enzymes](enzyme.md) require a [cofactor](cofactor-biochemistry.md), an additional nonprotein component such as a metal [ion](ion.md) or a small organic molecule. Organic helpers are often called [coenzymes](coenzyme.md); they can assist transfers that the [protein](protein.md) alone cannot readily perform. The [cofactor](cofactor-biochemistry.md) may remain bound or exchange with the surroundings, depending on the system. A [coenzyme](coenzyme.md) can be chemically changed in one reaction and regenerated by another: regeneration of the [enzyme](enzyme.md) does not mean every helper is unchanged in that individual reaction. Some [enzymes](enzyme.md) need no additional [cofactor](cofactor-biochemistry.md). See [Alberts et al., Molecular Biology of the Cell: small molecules associated with proteins](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26911/) and [Alberts et al., activated carriers and their regeneration](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26838/).

How much faster does adding [substrate](substrate-biochemistry.md) make a reaction? [Enzyme kinetics](enzyme-kinetics.md) gives a quantitative answer when a model's assumptions fit. For an illustrative reaction obeying [Michaelis-Menten kinetics](michaelis-menten-kinetics.md), use the [Michaelis-Menten equation](michaelis-menten-equation.md) with [substrate](substrate-biochemistry.md) [concentration](concentration.md) $s$, limiting [reaction rate](reaction-rate.md) $V_{\max}$ and [Michaelis constant](michaelis-constant.md) $K_M$. At $s/K_M=0.1$, substitution gives $v/V_{\max}=0.1/1.1=1/11\approx0.0909$. At $s/K_M=1$, it gives $1/2$; at $s/K_M=10$, it gives $10/11\approx0.9091$. Thus a hundredfold increase in [substrate](substrate-biochemistry.md) between the first and last examples yields only a tenfold increase in [reaction rate](reaction-rate.md).

<a id="image-illustrative-michaelis-menten-kinetics-s-k-m-0-1-1-10-gives-v-v-max-1-11-1-2-10-11"></a>
![](enzyme-substrate-saturation.png)

**[Figure 57](#image-illustrative-michaelis-menten-kinetics-s-k-m-0-1-1-10-gives-v-v-max-1-11-1-2-10-11). Illustrative Michaelis-Menten kinetics: $s/K_M=0.1,1,10$ gives $v/V_{\max}=1/11,1/2,10/11$**. Original normalized curve and computed examples, with a logarithmic concentration axis to separate the three values. The dashed upper line is a limiting rate approached at high [substrate](substrate-biochemistry.md) [concentration](concentration.md), not reached at a finite concentration in this model.

For a chosen $V_{\max}=110\,\mathrm{\mu M\,min^{-1}}$, those fractions mean [reaction rates](reaction-rate.md) of $10$, $55$ and $100\,\mathrm{\mu M\,min^{-1}}$. Doubling [substrate](substrate-biochemistry.md) from $10K_M$ to $20K_M$ raises the normalized rate only from $10/11$ to $20/21\approx0.9524$, a relative increase of about $4.76\%$. In this simplest model, the catalytic machinery is already nearly fully occupied. Doubling the amount of active [enzyme](enzyme.md) instead doubles $V_{\max}$ and the predicted [reaction rates](reaction-rate.md) at fixed free [substrate](substrate-biochemistry.md) [concentration](concentration.md), provided the other conditions are unchanged. This distinction helps decide whether additional [substrate](substrate-biochemistry.md) or additional catalytic capacity could improve throughput.

The interpretation has limits. The simplest [Michaelis-Menten kinetics](michaelis-menten-kinetics.md) assumes a single [substrate](substrate-biochemistry.md), fixed active [enzyme](enzyme.md) amount, a suitable [quasi-steady-state approximation](quasi-steady-state-approximation.md), and initial measurements with negligible reverse reaction from accumulated [reaction product](product-chemistry.md). Substantial depletion, interacting binding sites, or inhibition by excess [substrate](substrate-biochemistry.md) can invalidate this model. Within it, $K_M$ is the [substrate](substrate-biochemistry.md) [concentration](concentration.md) giving half the limiting [reaction rate](reaction-rate.md); it is not generally the binding [dissociation constant](dissociation-constant.md) $K_d$. See [IUBMB, enzyme kinetics: steady-state approximation and substrate dissociation](https://iubmb.qmul.ac.uk/kinetics/ek1t3.html) and [IUBMB, Michaelis-Menten and non-Michaelis-Menten kinetics](https://iubmb.qmul.ac.uk/kinetics/ek4t6.html). Measuring activity also requires specified [temperature](temperature.md), [pH](ph.md) and necessary [cofactor](cofactor-biochemistry.md) availability: a weak signal can reflect unsuitable conditions rather than little [enzyme](enzyme.md). See [Brooks et al., Assay Guidance Manual: basics of enzymatic assays for HTS](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK92007/).
