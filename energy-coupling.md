# Energy coupling

↑ **Parent:** [Chemical reaction](chemical-reaction.md)

[Energy coupling](energy-coupling.md) links an [endergonic reaction](endergonic-reaction.md) to an [exergonic reaction](exergonic-reaction.md) through a [reaction mechanism](reaction-mechanism.md), so that the combined transformation has a favourable change in [Gibbs free energy](gibbs-free-energy.md). [ATP](adenosine-triphosphate.md) [hydrolysis](hydrolysis.md) is a common driving [chemical reaction](chemical-reaction.md). A shared [reaction intermediate](reaction-intermediate.md), produced in one step and consumed in another, can physically connect the transformations; merely placing two independent [chemical reactions](chemical-reaction.md) in the same [chemical solution](solution-chemistry.md) does not establish [energy coupling](energy-coupling.md). See [Alberts et al., Molecular Biology of the Cell: coupled reactions and activated intermediates](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26838/).

Energy coupling: $+20-30.5=-10.5\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ under the same stipulated conditions**. Original diagram of cumulative changes in [Gibbs free energy](gibbs-free-energy.md) as two contributions are added. The levels are bookkeeping steps, not a [reaction mechanism](reaction-mechanism.md), actual [reaction intermediates](reaction-intermediate.md), elapsed time, or [activation energy](activation-energy.md) barriers. A [reaction mechanism](reaction-mechanism.md) must enforce the joint transformation for the net favourable value to drive the desired conversion.

For a computed example, stipulate an [endergonic reaction](endergonic-reaction.md) $\mathrm A\longrightarrow\mathrm B$ with $\Delta G_1^{\circ\prime}=+20\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ under precisely the same biochemical [standard state](standard-state.md) conditions as the quoted [ATP](adenosine-triphosphate.md) [hydrolysis](hydrolysis.md) value. Assume a [reaction mechanism](reaction-mechanism.md) couples one [ATP](adenosine-triphosphate.md) [hydrolysis](hydrolysis.md) to each A-to-B conversion. The formal sum is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm A&\longrightarrow\mathrm B,
&\Delta G_1^{\circ\prime}&=+20\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}},\\
\mathrm{ATP}+\mathrm{H_2O}&\longrightarrow\mathrm{ADP}+\mathrm P_i,
&\Delta G_2^{\circ\prime}&=-30.5\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}},\\
\mathrm A+\mathrm{ATP}+\mathrm{H_2O}&\longrightarrow\mathrm B+\mathrm{ADP}+\mathrm P_i,
&\Delta G_{\mathrm{net}}^{\circ\prime}&=-10.5\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The sum $+20+(-30.5)=-10.5$ gives an [exergonic reaction](exergonic-reaction.md). The $+20$ is a chosen teaching value, not a measured property of an identified substance. Outside these reference conditions, add the actual $\Delta G_1$ and $\Delta G_2$, each evaluated using consistent [thermodynamic activities](thermodynamic-activity.md); mixing a standard value for one [chemical reaction](chemical-reaction.md) with an unrelated cellular value for the other gives no justified prediction.

Why do the changes in [Gibbs free energy](gibbs-free-energy.md) add? [Gibbs free energy](gibbs-free-energy.md) is a [state function](state-function.md). Adding consistent reaction changes cancels any shared [reaction intermediate](reaction-intermediate.md) and leaves the difference between the combined [reactants](reactant.md) and combined [reaction products](product-chemistry.md). This explains the thermodynamic accounting; it does not construct the [reaction mechanism](reaction-mechanism.md). In many [ATP](adenosine-triphosphate.md)-dependent routes, [phosphorylation](phosphorylation.md) transfers a [phosphate](phosphate.md) group to a [substrate](substrate-biochemistry.md) or an [enzyme](enzyme.md), and subsequent transformation consumes that modified material. The shared material is what connects the steps. Other [reaction mechanisms](reaction-mechanism.md) use [ATP](adenosine-triphosphate.md) differently. See [Alberts et al., Molecular Biology of the Cell: ATP-driven biosynthesis](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26838/).

The useful achievement is that a common regenerated [nucleotide](nucleotide.md) can connect many different processes in [metabolism](metabolism.md). The net negative change in [Gibbs free energy](gibbs-free-energy.md) permits the coupled conversion, but supplies no [reaction rate](reaction-rate.md): [enzymes](enzyme.md) can lower [activation energy](activation-energy.md) barriers without changing the endpoint difference. [Energy coupling](energy-coupling.md) changes which overall [chemical reaction](chemical-reaction.md) is being considered; [catalysis](catalysis.md) accelerates an available route. See [OpenStax Biology 2e, 6.2: free-energy changes and reaction barriers](https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/6-2-potential-kinetic-free-and-activation-energy).

A historical landmark is Fritz Lipmann's [1941 essay, Metabolic Generation and Utilization of Phosphate Bond Energy](https://doi.org/10.1002/9780470122464.ch4). Its sections on the [phosphate](phosphate.md) cycle, generation and utilization of phosphate-bond [energy](energy.md), and group transfer organized these processes around a reusable chemical carrier. The historical terminology should be read with the modern distinction between net [hydrolysis](hydrolysis.md) and the cost of breaking a [chemical bond](chemical-bond.md).
