<h1 id="1/a/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [A](../a.md)

Use the [angular-diameter ratio under a collinear boost](../../../../../angular-diameter-ratio-under-a-collinear-boost.md), with motion along the common sightline and small angular radius $\vartheta$. For a boost toward the source, [relativistic aberration](../../../../../relativistic-aberration.md) of the incoming light gives $\tan(\vartheta'/2)=e^{-\eta}\tan(\vartheta/2)$, where $\eta$ is the relative [rapidity](../../../../../rapidity.md). Thus the observer seeing the smaller disk has [angular diameter](../../../../../angular-diameter.md) reduced by $e^{-\eta}$. The ratio two implies $e^\eta=2$, so

$$
\boxed{\frac{v_{\rm rel}}c=\tanh(\ln2)=\frac{2^2-1}{2^2+1}=\frac35}.
$$

The answer is **$0.60c$**. Both observations occur at one event, so this is a [relativistic aberration](../../../../../relativistic-aberration.md) comparison rather than a change of geometrical distance during observation. Arbitrary transverse relative motion would require additional directional information; the stated motion toward the same distant target selects the collinear interpretation.
