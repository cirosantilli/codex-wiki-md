# Laplacian

↑ **Parent:** [Vector calculus](vector-calculus.md)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplacian)

The [Laplacian](laplacian.md) takes the [divergence](divergence.md) of a [scalar field](scalar-field.md)'s [gradient](gradient.md). For a [real-valued function](real-valued-function.md) $f$ on an open region of [Euclidean space](euclidean-norm.md), with continuous [partial derivatives](partial-derivative.md) through order two, its expression in a [Cartesian coordinate system](cartesian-coordinate-system.md) is

$$
\Delta f=\nabla\cdot\nabla f=\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i^2}=\operatorname{tr}(D^2f).
$$

Thus the [Laplacian](laplacian.md), also called the [Laplace operator](laplace-operator.md), adds the coordinate [second derivatives](second-derivative.md); equivalently it is the [matrix trace](matrix-trace.md) of the [Hessian matrix](hessian-matrix.md). Its output is another [scalar field](scalar-field.md).[Volume II, §2–7, equations 2.52–2.54: divergence of a gradient and the Cartesian Laplacian](the-feynman-lectures-on-physics-volume-ii-by-richard-feynman-and-others.md)

Three original examples distinguish shape from this sum. The bowl $f(x,y)=x^2+y^2$ has $\Delta f=2+2=4$. The saddle $g(x,y)=x^2-y^2$ instead has $\Delta g=2-2=0$: opposite [second derivatives](second-derivative.md) cancel. It is a [harmonic function](harmonic-function.md), although its [graph of a function](graph-of-a-function.md) is not a [plane](plane.md). For the [Gaussian function](gaussian-function.md) $h(x,y)=e^{-r^2}$, with $r^2=x^2+y^2$, differentiation gives

$$
\Delta h=4(r^2-1)e^{-r^2}.
$$

Its [Laplacian](laplacian.md) is negative inside the [circle](circle.md) of [radius](radius.md) one, zero on that [circle](circle.md), and positive outside it. The [Gaussian function](gaussian-function.md) itself is positive throughout: its value and its [Laplacian](laplacian.md) describe different properties.

![](laplacian-bowl-saddle-and-gaussian.png)

**[Figure 7](#image-the-same-laplacian-measures-a-bowl-a-saddle-and-a-gaussian-function). The same Laplacian measures a bowl, a saddle and a Gaussian function**. Original analytic surfaces. Height shows $f$; the common color scale shows $\Delta f$, with blue negative, neutral gray zero and red positive. Vertical scales differ. The black [circle](circle.md) on the [Gaussian function](gaussian-function.md) is $r=1$, where its [Laplacian](laplacian.md) changes sign.

The sum does not favor a particular orientation. Under a change of [Cartesian coordinate system](cartesian-coordinate-system.md) represented by an [orthogonal matrix](orthogonal-matrix.md) $Q$, the [Hessian matrix](hessian-matrix.md) becomes $Q^{\mathsf T}(D^2f)Q$. The [cyclic property of the trace](cyclic-property-of-the-trace.md) and $QQ^{\mathsf T}=I$ preserve its [matrix trace](matrix-trace.md). This makes the [Laplacian](laplacian.md) useful when the behavior depends on spatial differences equally in every direction, rather than on the arbitrary choice of axes.

For a [vector field](vector-field.md), the Euclidean [Laplacian](laplacian.md) acts separately on components expressed in a fixed [Cartesian coordinate system](cartesian-coordinate-system.md). Components in a position-dependent basis require differentiating that basis too; applying a scalar formula separately to polar components omits those terms.[Volume II, component formula following equation 2.54 and final paragraph of §2–8: fixed rectangular components](the-feynman-lectures-on-physics-volume-ii-by-richard-feynman-and-others.md) The scalar formula in [Laplacian in polar coordinates](laplacian-in-polar-coordinates.md) is valid away from $r=0$; smoothness at the origin must be handled in coordinates that remain regular there.
