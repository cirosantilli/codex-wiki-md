# Contraction mapping theorem

↑ **Parent:** [Fixed-point theorem](fixed-point-theorem.md)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banach_fixed-point_theorem)

A [contraction mapping](contraction-mapping.md) $T:X\to X$ on a nonempty [complete metric space](complete-metric-space.md) has exactly one [fixed point](fixed-point.md) $x_*$, and the [sequence](sequence.md) $x_{n+1}=T(x_n)$ converges to $x_*$ from every $x_0\in X$. The same constant $0\leq L<1$ must satisfy $d(Tx,Ty)\leq Ld(x,y)$ for all $x,y\in X$. The statement applies to [metric spaces](metric-space.md), including closed subsets of a [Banach space](banach-space-split.md); neither linearity nor a [vector space](vector-space-split.md) structure is required.[Dirk Schütz, Analysis III/IV, page 13: contraction mapping theorem](https://maths.dur.ac.uk/users/norbert.peyerimhoff/analysis0708/dirknotes.pdf)

The force of the result is that existence, uniqueness, and a practical approximation procedure arrive together. Repeated application produces corrections bounded by a [geometric series](geometric-series.md), hence a [Cauchy sequence](cauchy-sequence.md); [completeness](completeness.md) supplies its limit. The [Lipschitz continuity](lipschitz-continuity.md) of $T$ makes that limit a [fixed point](fixed-point.md). Two different [fixed points](fixed-point.md) would have their positive distance multiplied by at most $L<1$ while remaining unchanged, which is impossible.
