<h3 id="1/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1/a)

<h5 id="1/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#1/a/i)

A [weak local minimizer of a variational integral](../calculus-of-variations.md#weak-local-minimizer-of-a-variational-integral) is an admissible $\bar u$ for which there is $\rho>0$ such that

$$
\boxed{I(v)\geq I(\bar u)\quad\text{whenever }v\in\mathcal A,\ \|v-\bar u\|_{W^{1,\infty}}<\rho.}
$$

The [Sobolev norm](../sobolev-space.md#sobolev-norm) controls both the function and its first [weak derivative](../distribution-theory.md#weak-derivative). The boundary values remain fixed because both functions are admissible.

<h4 id="1/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1/a)

<h5 id="1/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#1/a/ii)

A [strong local minimizer of a variational integral](../calculus-of-variations.md#strong-local-minimizer-of-a-variational-integral) is an admissible $\bar u$ for which some $\rho>0$ satisfies

$$
\boxed{I(v)\geq I(\bar u)\quad\text{for }v\in\mathcal A\text{ with }\|v-\bar u\|_\infty<\rho.}
$$

Only the function values must be close; admissible derivatives may be large. Every strong local minimizer is therefore also a [weak local minimizer of a variational integral](../calculus-of-variations.md#weak-local-minimizer-of-a-variational-integral).

A [point of convexity](../real-analysis.md#point-of-convexity) of $g\in C^1(\mathbb R)$ is a point $v_0$ where its tangent line supports the entire graph from below:

$$
g(v)\geq g(v_0)+g'(v_0)(v-v_0)\quad\text{for every }v\in\mathbb R.
$$

The [Weierstrass necessary condition](../calculus-of-variations.md#weierstrass-necessary-condition) says that, at almost every $x$, $\bar u'(x)$ is a [point of convexity](../real-analysis.md#point-of-convexity) of $v\mapsto f(x,\bar u(x),v)$. In terms of the [Weierstrass excess function](../calculus-of-variations.md#weierstrass-excess-function),

$$
E(x,\bar u,\bar u',v)=f(x,\bar u,v)-f(x,\bar u,\bar u')-f_v(x,\bar u,\bar u')(v-\bar u')\geq0\quad(v\in\mathbb R).
$$

Now use the stated [derivative](../calculus.md#derivative) [coercivity](../real-analysis.md#coercive-function). If $\bar u$ were not a local minimizer in $L^1$, there would be $v_n\in\mathcal A$ with $\|v_n-\bar u\|_1\to0$ and $I(v_n)<I(\bar u)$. The energy bound gives

$$
c\int_a^b|v_n'|^{2010}\,dx\leq I(\bar u)+d(b-a).
$$

The common value $v_n(a)=\alpha$ controls their function norms as well, by $|v_n(x)|\leq|\alpha|+\int_a^b|v_n'|$. Thus $(v_n)$ is bounded in $W^{1,2010}(a,b)$. The one-dimensional compact [Sobolev embedding](../sobolev-space.md#failure-of-first-order-sobolev-embedding-into-linfinity-in-two-dimensions) into $C([a,b])$ supplies a uniformly convergent subsequence. Its limit must be $\bar u$, since the same subsequence converges to $\bar u$ in $L^1$. Eventually $\|v_n-\bar u\|_\infty<\rho$, contradicting strong local minimality. Hence **$\bar u$ is also a local minimizer in $L^1$**. This is [coercivity transfers strong minimality to L1 minimality](../calculus-of-variations.md#coercivity-transfers-strong-minimality-to-l1-minimality).

<h3 id="1/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

<h5 id="1/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#1/b/i)

**Zero is a strict weak local minimizer, but is not a strong local minimizer.** Put $D=\int_0^1|u'|^2\,dx$. Since $u(0)=0$, the [Cauchy-Schwarz inequality](../probability-and-statistics.md#cauchy-schwarz-inequality) gives $|u(x)|^2\leq xD$, and therefore

$$
\int_0^1x^{2010}u(x)^2\,dx\leq\frac{D}{2012}.
$$

For sufficiently small $\|u'\|_\infty$, $\sin(u'^2)\geq\tfrac12u'^2$ [pointwise](../sequence-and-series.md#pointwise-convergence). Hence

$$
I(u)\geq\left(\frac12-\frac1{2012}\right)D\geq0,
$$

with equality only when $u=0$. Thus zero is a strict [weak local minimizer of a variational integral](../calculus-of-variations.md#weak-local-minimizer-of-a-variational-integral).

For the strong claim, define a [Lipschitz](../real-analysis.md#lipschitz-continuity) triangular wave

$$
u_N(x)=\sqrt\pi\,\operatorname{dist}(x,N^{-1}\mathbb Z).
$$

It has zero endpoint values, $\|u_N\|_\infty=\sqrt\pi/(2N)\to0$, and $u_N'=\pm\sqrt\pi$ almost everywhere. Thus $\sin(u_N'^2)=\sin\pi=0$, while $u_N$ is nonzero on a set of positive measure. Consequently

$$
\boxed{I(u_N)=-\int_0^1x^{2010}u_N^2\,dx<0=I(0).}
$$

These uniformly small competitors disprove the [strong local minimizer of a variational integral](../calculus-of-variations.md#strong-local-minimizer-of-a-variational-integral) property.

<h4 id="1/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

<h5 id="1/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#1/b/ii)

**Zero is a strict strong local minimizer, and therefore also a strict weak local minimizer.** Here $I(0)=-2/2010$, and

$$
I(u)-I(0)=\int_0^1u'^2\,dx-\frac2{2010}\int_0^1(\cosh u-1)\,dx.
$$

For $|u|\leq1$, [Taylor's theorem](../calculus.md#taylor-s-theorem) gives $\cosh u-1\leq\tfrac12\cosh(1)u^2$. Also $u(0)=0$ and the [Cauchy-Schwarz inequality](../probability-and-statistics.md#cauchy-schwarz-inequality) give $\int_0^1u^2\,dx\leq\tfrac12\int_0^1u'^2\,dx$. Therefore

$$
I(u)-I(0)\geq\left(1-\frac{\cosh1}{4020}\right)\int_0^1u'^2\,dx.
$$

The coefficient is positive. Thus every nonzero admissible $u$ with $\|u\|_\infty\leq1$ has strictly greater energy, proving that zero is a strict [strong local minimizer of a variational integral](../calculus-of-variations.md#strong-local-minimizer-of-a-variational-integral).

## 2

↑ **Parent:** [2010](2010.md)

<h3 id="2/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#2/a)

For a fixed-endpoint variation $\varphi\in W^{1,\infty}_0(a,b)$, the [second variation](../calculus-of-variations.md#second-variation) is

$$
\delta^2I(\bar u)(\varphi,\varphi)=\left.\frac{d^2}{dt^2}I(\bar u+t\varphi)\right|_{t=0}=\int_a^b\left(f_{vv}\varphi'^2+2f_{uv}\varphi\varphi'+f_{uu}\varphi^2\right)dx,
$$

where all coefficients are evaluated at $(x,\bar u(x),\bar u'(x))$. The notation $\delta^2I(\bar u)>0$ means this is strictly positive for every nonzero admissible variation. This is [pointwise](../sequence-and-series.md#pointwise-convergence) positive definiteness of the [quadratic form](../quadratic-form.md), not an assertion of a prescribed uniform lower bound.

Put

$$
P=f_{vv},\qquad Q=f_{uu}-\frac d{dx}f_{uv}.
$$

After [integration by parts](../calculus.md#integration-by-parts), the form is $q(\varphi)=\int_a^b(P\varphi'^2+Q\varphi^2)\,dx$. Its [Jacobi equation](../calculus-of-variations.md#jacobi-equation) is

$$
\boxed{-(Pw')'+Qw=0.}
$$

In the regular case $P>0$ on $[a,b]$, a [conjugate point](../calculus-of-variations.md#conjugate-point) $c\in(a,b]$ to $a$ is a point for which a nonzero solution satisfies $w(a)=w(c)=0$. Equivalently it is a zero of the normalized solution with $w(a)=0$, $P(a)w'(a)=1$.

Under this regular [Strengthened Legendre condition](../calculus-of-variations.md#strengthened-legendre-condition), the [Jacobi positivity criterion](../calculus-of-variations.md#jacobi-positivity-criterion) is

$$
\boxed{q>0\iff\text{there is no conjugate point to }a\text{ in }(a,b].}
$$

The endpoint $b$ must be excluded as a [conjugate point](../calculus-of-variations.md#conjugate-point) too. To prove necessity, suppose a Jacobi solution vanishes at $a$ and $c$. Define $\varphi=w$ on $[a,c]$ and zero on $[c,b]$. It is a nonzero [Lipschitz](../real-analysis.md#lipschitz-continuity) admissible variation, and

$$
q(\varphi)=\int_a^c(Pw'^2+Qw^2)dx=[Pww']_a^c=0,
$$

a contradiction. For completeness, the converse follows from the [Jacobi identity for the second variation](../calculus-of-variations.md#jacobi-identity-for-the-second-variation): when the normalized $w$ has no further zero,

$$
q(\varphi)=\int_a^bP\left(\varphi'-\frac{w'}w\varphi\right)^2dx.
$$

The boundary contribution $[P(w'/w)\varphi^2]_a^b$ vanishes, including the limit at $a$ since $w$ has a simple zero and $\varphi=O(x-a)$. Equality forces $\varphi=Cw$, and $\varphi(b)=0$ with $w(b)\ne0$ forces $C=0$.

Independently of regularity, positivity necessarily implies the ordinary [Legendre necessary condition](../calculus-of-variations.md#legendre-necessary-condition) $P\geq0$. If $P<0$ on an interval, localized rapidly oscillating test functions have a negative [derivative](../calculus.md#derivative) contribution of order frequency squared, dominating the bounded zero-order term. This contradicts positivity.

The assumptions stated here do not themselves impose $P>0$. That strengthened condition is a regularity hypothesis for the familiar Jacobi criterion, and is not necessary for strict positivity: the integrand $f=u^2$ gives $q(\varphi)=2\int\varphi^2>0$ with $P=0$. For a criterion covering the full possibly degenerate class, use [Regularized Jacobi criterion for a degenerate second variation](../calculus-of-variations.md#regularized-jacobi-criterion-for-a-degenerate-second-variation). The following conditions are necessary and sufficient:

- $P\geq0$;
- for every $\eta>0$, the regular equation $-((P+\eta)w')'+Qw=0$ has no [conjugate point](../calculus-of-variations.md#conjugate-point) to $a$ in $(a,b]$;
- the original weak Jacobi boundary problem has no nonzero [Lipschitz](../real-analysis.md#lipschitz-continuity) solution with zero endpoint values.

These conditions are necessary because $q>0$ gives $q_\eta(\varphi)=q(\varphi)+\eta\int\varphi'^2>0$, so the regular criterion just proved applies; a weak Jacobi solution tested against itself would give $q=0$. They are sufficient as well: the regular criterion gives $q_\eta>0$ for every $\eta>0$, hence $q\geq0$ by letting $\eta\downarrow0$. If $q(\varphi)=0$, nonnegativity of $q(\varphi+t\psi)$ for every real $t$ forces its mixed bilinear term to vanish for every test $\psi$. This is precisely the weak [Jacobi equation](../calculus-of-variations.md#jacobi-equation). The third condition then forces $\varphi=0$. Thus the criterion treats degeneracy without silently assuming the strengthened condition.

<h3 id="2/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

<h5 id="2/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#2/b/i)

Here $P=2$, $Q=-2\lambda^2$ and the [Jacobi equation](../calculus-of-variations.md#jacobi-equation) is $w''+\lambda^2w=0$. With $w(0)=0$ and $w'(0)=1$, its solution is $w(x)=\sin(|\lambda|x)/|\lambda|$ for $\lambda\ne0$, and $w(x)=x$ for $\lambda=0$. Its first positive zero is $\pi/|\lambda|$ when $\lambda\ne0$.

If $\lambda^2<\pi^2/b^2$, no [conjugate point](../calculus-of-variations.md#conjugate-point) occurs in $(0,b]$. The [Jacobi positivity criterion](../calculus-of-variations.md#jacobi-positivity-criterion) gives

$$
\boxed{\delta^2I_\lambda(0)(\varphi,\varphi)=2\int_0^b(\varphi'^2-\lambda^2\varphi^2)dx>0\quad(\varphi\ne0).}
$$

Since $I_\lambda$ itself is this [quadratic form](../quadratic-form.md) divided by two, $I_\lambda(u)>0=I_\lambda(0)$ for every nonzero admissible $u$. Thus **zero is the unique [global minimizer](../analysis.md#global-minimizer)**. This argument establishes the result before using the sharp [Poincaré inequality](../sobolev-space.md#poincare-inequality).

<h4 id="2/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

<h5 id="2/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#2/b/ii)

Fix an admissible $u$. The preceding result gives $\int_0^bu'^2\,dx\geq\lambda^2\int_0^bu^2\,dx$ for every $\lambda^2<\pi^2/b^2$. Letting $\lambda^2$ increase to $\pi^2/b^2$ proves

$$
\boxed{\int_0^bu'^2dx\geq\frac{\pi^2}{b^2}\int_0^bu^2dx.}
$$

Equality is attained by

$$
\boxed{u(x)=A\sin(\pi x/b),\qquad A\in\mathbb R.}
$$

Indeed the [derivative](../calculus.md#derivative) and function squared integrals are $A^2\pi^2/(2b)$ and $A^2b/2$. These are all equality cases: an equality function minimizes the nonnegative threshold [quadratic form](../quadratic-form.md), so its [first variation](../calculus-of-variations.md#first-variation) vanishes. The resulting weak equation $u''+(\pi/b)^2u=0$ makes $u$ smooth and, with the endpoint conditions, yields precisely these multiples. This is the [Wirtinger inequality](../calculus-of-variations.md#wirtinger-inequality).

<h4 id="2/b/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

<h5 id="2/b/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#2/b/iii)

Use $u_A(x)=A\sin(\pi x/b)$, which is admissible for every real $A$. Direct integration gives

$$
I_\lambda(u_A)=\frac{A^2b}{2}\left(\frac{\pi^2}{b^2}-\lambda^2\right).
$$

The coefficient is strictly negative, so the energy tends to minus infinity as $|A|\to\infty$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\inf_{u\in\mathcal A}I_\lambda(u)=-\infty.}
$$

↑ **Parent:** [2010](2010.md)

<h3 id="3/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

<h5 id="3/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#3/a/i)

The function $u_*(x)=x^{1/4}$ has [weak derivative](../distribution-theory.md#weak-derivative) $u_*'(x)=\tfrac14x^{-3/4}\in L^1(0,1)$ and the required endpoint values, so it belongs to the admissible [Sobolev space](../sobolev-space.md). Its fourth power is $x$, and hence

$$
\boxed{I_0(u_*)=0.}
$$

Since the integrand $(u^4-x)^2$ is nonnegative, zero is a global lower bound. Thus **$u_*$ is a [global minimizer](../analysis.md#global-minimizer)**. Its [derivative](../calculus.md#derivative) does not belong to $L^{4/3}$, since its $4/3$ power is proportional to $x^{-1}$; positive regularization therefore needs different competitors.

<h4 id="3/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

<h5 id="3/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#3/a/ii)

A convenient form of the [Tonelli existence theorem](../calculus-of-variations.md#tonelli-existence-theorem) is: on a finite interval with prescribed endpoint values, if a continuous integrand $F(x,z,v)$ is [convex](../real-analysis.md#convex-function) in $v$ and satisfies $F(x,z,v)\geq c|v|^p-C$ for some $p>1$, then, provided a finite-energy admissible competitor exists, its integral attains a minimum in the corresponding $W^{1,p}$ class. The lower bound may more generally subtract an integrable function of $x$. Compact one-dimensional [Sobolev embedding](../sobolev-space.md#failure-of-first-order-sobolev-embedding-into-linfinity-in-two-dimensions) controls $z$, while [convexity](../real-analysis.md#convex-function) gives [weak lower semicontinuity](../functional-analysis.md#weak-lower-semicontinuity) in the [derivative](../calculus.md#derivative).

Here $p=4/3$ and $F_\varepsilon=(z^4-x)^2+\varepsilon|v|^{4/3}$ is continuous, [convex](../real-analysis.md#convex-function) in $v$, and bounded below by $\varepsilon|v|^{4/3}$. The competitor $u(x)=x$ has finite energy. Thus for every $\varepsilon>0$ the [Tonelli existence theorem](../calculus-of-variations.md#tonelli-existence-theorem) gives an admissible minimizer in $W^{1,4/3}(0,1)$.

Any finite-energy member of the larger stated $W^{1,1}$ class necessarily has [derivative](../calculus.md#derivative) in $L^{4/3}$ and bounded function values by [absolute continuity](../sobolev-space.md#absolutely-continuous-function); it therefore belongs to $W^{1,4/3}$. All other competitors have infinite energy. Hence **the same function $u_\varepsilon$ is a [global minimizer](../analysis.md#global-minimizer) in the full stated class**.

<h4 id="3/a/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

<h5 id="3/a/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#3/a/iii)

The finite-energy comparison $I_\varepsilon(u_\varepsilon)\leq I_\varepsilon(x)<\infty$ gives $\varepsilon\int|u_\varepsilon'|^{4/3}<\infty$. Together with the endpoint value and [absolute continuity](../sobolev-space.md#absolutely-continuous-function), this proves $u_\varepsilon\in W^{1,4/3}(0,1)$.

For $\alpha>1/4$, the power competitor $u_\alpha=x^\alpha$ is admissible and has finite [derivative](../calculus.md#derivative) energy. Direct integration gives

$$
I_\varepsilon(u_\alpha)=\frac1{8\alpha+1}-\frac2{4\alpha+2}+\frac13+\frac{3\varepsilon\alpha^{4/3}}{4\alpha-1}.
$$

Choose $\delta=\varepsilon^{1/3}$ and $\alpha=1/4+\delta$. The first three terms tend to zero as $\alpha\downarrow1/4$, while the last equals $\tfrac34\varepsilon^{2/3}(1/4+\varepsilon^{1/3})^{4/3}\to0$. By minimality and nonnegativity,

$$
\boxed{0\leq I_\varepsilon(u_\varepsilon)\leq I_\varepsilon(u_{1/4+\varepsilon^{1/3}})\longrightarrow0.}
$$

One also obtains the quantitative bound $I_\varepsilon(u_\varepsilon)=O(\varepsilon^{2/3})$: for $\alpha=1/4+\delta$, the [mean value theorem](../calculus.md#mean-value-theorem) gives $|x^{4\alpha}-x|\leq4\delta x|\log x|$, and $\int_0^1x^2\log^2x\,dx=2/27$. Thus the squared mismatch is at most $32\delta^2/27$, matching the order of the [derivative](../calculus.md#derivative) penalty with this choice. This is a [power-law comparison for vanishing derivative regularization](../calculus-of-variations.md#power-law-comparison-for-vanishing-derivative-regularization).

<h3 id="3/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

Use the [direct method in the calculus of variations](../calculus-of-variations.md#direct-method-in-the-calculus-of-variations). The affine function $1-x$ is admissible with finite energy, and the energy is nonnegative since $2010$ is even. Thus $m=\inf I$ is finite. Choose a [minimizing sequence](../calculus-of-variations.md#minimizing-sequence) $u_n$ with uniformly bounded energy. Then

$$
\frac12\int_0^1|u_n'|^2dx\leq I(u_n)\leq C.
$$

The fixed value $u_n(0)=1$ and the [Cauchy-Schwarz inequality](../probability-and-statistics.md#cauchy-schwarz-inequality) give $\|u_n\|_\infty\leq1+\|u_n'\|_2$ and $|u_n(x)-u_n(y)|\leq\|u_n'\|_2|x-y|^{1/2}$. Hence the sequence is bounded in $W^{1,2}$ and uniformly bounded and equicontinuous.

By [reflexivity](../set-theory.md#reflexive-relation) and the [Arzelà-Ascoli theorem](../topological-analysis.md#arzela-ascoli-theorem), pass to a subsequence with $u_n\rightharpoonup u$ in $W^{1,2}$ and $u_n\to u$ uniformly. The two limits coincide; [uniform convergence](../sequence-and-series.md#uniform-convergence) also preserves $u(0)=1$, $u(1)=0$. Since all functions stay in a common bounded interval, [uniform convergence](../sequence-and-series.md#uniform-convergence) gives $u_n^{2010}\to u^{2010}$ uniformly, and therefore convergence of the potential integrals. The [derivative](../calculus.md#derivative) term is lower semicontinuous for [weak convergence](../weak-topology.md#weak-convergence), so

$$
I(u)\leq\liminf_n\frac12\int|u_n'|^2+\lim_n\int u_n^{2010}=\liminf_nI(u_n)=m.
$$

Admissibility gives $I(u)\geq m$. Thus

$$
\boxed{I(u)=m,\quad\text{so the global minimum is attained}.}
$$
