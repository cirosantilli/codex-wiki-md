<h1 id="2/c/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [C](../c.md)

**Several mechanisms could explain an observed association without a causal effect of power on friendliness.** No exposure was assigned, so this is an [observational study](../../../../observational-study.md). Age, organizational role, wealth, gender, industry, public prominence or other common causes could produce [confounding](../../../../confounding.md). Reverse direction is also possible: interpersonal behavior might influence advancement and hence power. Adjusting for age alone would not remove every alternative explanation.

The measured outcome is _perceived friendliness from a photograph_. Facial expression, pose, lighting, clothing, attractiveness and familiarity could affect those ratings independently of everyday behavior. If raters recognize an executive or know their status, expectations could alter the outcome; financial experts' power ratings may themselves involve judgment and [measurement error](../../../../measurement-error.md). These are [construct validity](../../../../construct-validity.md) issues, since a photographic impression need not measure friendliness toward colleagues.

The preselected thirty executives are not a probability sample of all workers or even necessarily of all executives. [Selection bias](../../../../selection-bias.md), narrow representation and [restriction of range](../../../../restriction-of-range.md) could alter associations and limit [external validity](../../../../external-validity.md). Multiple ratings of the same executive are also not independent executive observations; ignoring that structure would be [pseudoreplication](../../../../pseudoreplication.md). These possibilities apply whether the reported association is positive, negative or inconclusive; the unavailable numerical findings are not assumed.
