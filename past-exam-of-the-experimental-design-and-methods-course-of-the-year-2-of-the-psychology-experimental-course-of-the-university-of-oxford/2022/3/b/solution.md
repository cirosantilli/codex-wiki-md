Use a **bivariate [correlation](../../../../pearson-correlation-coefficient.md)** to describe the direction and strength of association between two measured variables, such as study time and test score. The [Pearson correlation coefficient](../../../../pearson-correlation-coefficient.md) describes linear association between two quantitative variables: its sign gives direction, and its magnitude reflects linear association on a standardized scale. A [confidence interval](../../../../confidence-interval.md) describes estimation uncertainty; a corresponding [hypothesis test](../../../../statistical-hypothesis-test-split.md) can assess a specified null association under its sampling assumptions.

Inspect a [scatter plot](../../../../scatter-plot.md) for curvature, clusters, influential observations and [restriction of range](../../../../restriction-of-range.md). A coefficient near zero does not exclude a strong nonlinear relationship. For ordered or markedly nonnormal measurements, a [Spearman rank correlation](../../../../spearman-s-rank-correlation-coefficient.md) can describe monotone association, using ranks; it does not require a linear relation in the original units. Ordinary inferential procedures require independent observational units and appropriate assumptions for their chosen method. **[Correlation](../../../../pearson-correlation-coefficient.md) alone does not identify a causal direction or exclude [confounding](../../../../confounding.md).**
