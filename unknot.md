# Unknot

↑ **Parent:** [Knot](knot.md)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unknot)

The unknot $U$ is the [knot](knot.md) type represented by the planar [circle](circle.md)

$$
U(t)=(\cos t,\sin t,0),\qquad t\in\mathbb R/(2\pi\mathbb Z).
$$

Equivalently, an [unknot](unknot.md) is a [knot](knot.md) that bounds a smoothly embedded [disk](disk-mathematics.md) in the [three-sphere](three-sphere.md). The [disk](disk-mathematics.md) must itself be embedded; merely filling a loop with a map that is allowed to intersect itself does not establish that the [knot](knot.md) is an [unknot](unknot.md). Flattening a neighborhood of an embedded spanning [disk](disk-mathematics.md) explains why this criterion recovers the planar [circle](circle.md).

A complicated drawing can still represent an [unknot](unknot.md). Starting with a crossing-free [knot diagram](knot-diagram.md) of $U$, insert a small kink by a type I [Reidemeister move](reidemeister-move.md). The resulting [knot diagram](knot-diagram.md) has one crossing, but removing the kink exhibits the same [unknot](unknot.md). Repeating in separate small regions gives [knot diagrams](knot-diagram.md) of the [unknot](unknot.md) with arbitrarily many crossings, although the [crossing number of a knot](crossing-number-of-a-knot.md) is $c(U)=0$. A drawing's apparent complexity is therefore not a certificate of knotting.

The normalized [Alexander polynomial of a knot](alexander-polynomial.md) and [Jones polynomial](jones-polynomial.md) of the [unknot](unknot.md) are both $1$, and its [knot group](knot-group.md) is the [infinite cyclic group](infinite-cyclic-group.md) $\mathbb Z$, because the [knot exterior](knot-exterior.md) is a [solid torus](solid-torus.md). These are useful comparison values. In particular, a nonconstant [Alexander polynomial of a knot](alexander-polynomial.md) certifies that a [knot](knot.md) is not an [unknot](unknot.md), but the converse fails: nontrivial untwisted [Whitehead doubles](whitehead-double.md) also have [Alexander polynomial of a knot](alexander-polynomial.md) $1$.
