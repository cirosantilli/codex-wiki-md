<h4 id="1/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1/a)

<h5 id="1/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#1/a/i)

Put $A=\{|X|\geq Z\}$. Both $A$ and its [indicator function](../measure-theory.md#indicator-function) are [measurable](../measure-theory.md#measurability), and $0\leq Z\mathbf1_A\leq |X|$. Thus $Z\mathbf1_A$ is an [integrable random variable](../expected-value.md#integrable-random-variable). Monotonicity of [conditional expectation](../measure-theory.md#conditional-expectation) gives

$$
\mathbb E[Z\mathbf1_A\mid\mathcal G]\leq\mathbb E[|X|\mid\mathcal G]\quad\text{a.s.}
$$

The measurable-factor property of [conditional expectation](../measure-theory.md#conditional-expectation) says that a bounded $\mathcal G$-[measurable function](../measure-theory.md#measurable-function) $H$ satisfies $\mathbb E[HW\mid\mathcal G]=H\mathbb E[W\mid\mathcal G]$ for every [integrable random variable](../expected-value.md#integrable-random-variable) $W$. Here the factor $Z$ need not be bounded. Apply that property first to $Z\wedge m$ and $W=\mathbf1_A$, then use [monotone convergence](../measure-theory.md#monotone-convergence-theorem) on the nonnegative products, including the conditional version, to obtain

$$
\mathbb E[Z\mathbf1_A\mid\mathcal G]
=\lim_{m\to\infty}(Z\wedge m)\mathbb E[\mathbf1_A\mid\mathcal G]
=Z\mathbb E[\mathbf1_A\mid\mathcal G].
$$

All the left-hand [conditional expectations](../measure-theory.md#conditional-expectation) are well defined; the final one is finite [almost surely](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#almost-sure-convergence) because $Z\mathbf1_A$ is an [integrable random variable](../expected-value.md#integrable-random-variable). Since $Z>0$ [almost surely](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#almost-sure-convergence), division now yields

$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[\mathbf1_{\{|X|\geq Z\}}\mid\mathcal G]
\leq Z^{-1}\mathbb E[|X|\mid\mathcal G]\quad\text{a.s.}}
$$

This proves the [conditional Markov inequality with a measurable threshold](../probability-inequality.md#conditional-markov-inequality-with-a-measurable-threshold) without assuming that $Z^{-1}|X|$ is an [integrable random variable](../expected-value.md#integrable-random-variable). The measurable-factor rule, with its product-integrability condition, is also stated in the standard [conditional expectation](../measure-theory.md#conditional-expectation) theory.[§4.1.2, Theorem 4.1.14, printed pp. 212–213: bringing out a measurable factor](../mathematics-bibliography.md#probability-theory-and-examples-fifth-edition-by-rick-durrett)

<h4 id="1/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1/a)

<h5 id="1/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#1/a/ii)

Write $A=g(X)$ and $B=g(Y)$. The two [conditional expectation](../measure-theory.md#conditional-expectation) identities supply a $\sigma(Y)$-[measurable](../measure-theory.md#measurability) version of $B$ and a $\sigma(X)$-[measurable](../measure-theory.md#measurability) version of $A$. Work with those versions, which do not change any [almost sure equality](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#almost-sure-equality). Both lie in [L2 space](../measure-theory.md#l2-space-is-a-hilbert-space), and the [Cauchy-Schwarz inequality](../probability-and-statistics.md#cauchy-schwarz-inequality) makes their product an [integrable random variable](../expected-value.md#integrable-random-variable).

Testing the first [conditional expectation](../measure-theory.md#conditional-expectation) identity against $B$, and the second against $A$, gives

$$
\mathbb E[AB]=\mathbb E[B^2],\qquad
\mathbb E[AB]=\mathbb E[A^2].
$$

For completeness, these tests with unbounded multipliers are valid by first using the bounded truncations $B_m=(-m)\vee(B\wedge m)$ and $A_m=(-m)\vee(A\wedge m)$, then passing to the limit with the [Cauchy-Schwarz inequality](../probability-and-statistics.md#cauchy-schwarz-inequality) and [convergence in L2](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#convergence-in-l2). Consequently,

$$
\mathbb E[(A-B)^2]=\mathbb E[A^2]+\mathbb E[B^2]-2\mathbb E[AB]=0.
$$

A nonnegative [random variable](../random-variable.md) with zero [expected value](../expected-value.md) vanishes [almost surely](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#almost-sure-convergence). Therefore **$g(X)=g(Y)$ almost surely**. This is [mutual conditional expectation rigidity in L2](../measure-theory.md#mutual-conditional-expectation-rigidity-in-l2); it also follows from the [equality case for conditional second moments](../measure-theory.md#equality-case-for-conditional-second-moments).

For a counterexample to equality of the original [random variables](../random-variable.md), let $X$ give [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability) $1/4$ to each of $-2,-1,1,2$, set $Y=-X$, and use the nonconstant [function](../function.md) $g(t)=t^2$. Then $g(X)=g(Y)=X^2$ takes both values $1$ and $4$. It is [measurable](../measure-theory.md#measurability) with respect to both generated [sigma-algebras](../measure-theory.md#sigma-algebra), so both required [conditional expectation](../measure-theory.md#conditional-expectation) identities hold. Symmetry gives $\mathbb EX=\mathbb EY=0$, while neither [random variable](../random-variable.md) can be zero. Thus

$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(X=\mathbb EX)=\mathbb P(Y=\mathbb EY)=0,\qquad\mathbb P(X=Y)=0.}
$$

<h3 id="1/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

<h5 id="1/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#1/b/i)

For every $n$, $\mathcal F_n$ is a [sigma-algebra](../measure-theory.md#sigma-algebra) contained in $\mathcal F$, and including one more [random variable](../random-variable.md) among its generators can only enlarge it. Hence $\mathcal F_n\subseteq\mathcal F_{n+1}$; the trivial $\mathcal F_0$ is contained in every [sigma-algebra](../measure-theory.md#sigma-algebra). These are exactly the requirements for a [filtration](../stochastic-process.md#filtration-probability-theory).

The constant $X_0=x$ is $\mathcal F_0$-[measurable](../measure-theory.md#measurability). Suppose $X_n$ is $\mathcal F_n$-[measurable](../measure-theory.md#measurability). The pair $(X_n,U_{n+1})$ is then $\mathcal F_{n+1}$-[measurable](../measure-theory.md#measurability), and the [event](../probability-space.md#event) $\{U_{n+1}\leq X_n\}$ is $\mathcal F_{n+1}$-[measurable](../measure-theory.md#measurability) because comparison of two real coordinates defines a [Borel set](../measure-theory.md#borel-set). Define its [indicator function](../measure-theory.md#indicator-function) by $B_{n+1}$. The recursion becomes

$$
X_{n+1}=\frac{X_n+B_{n+1}}2.
$$

It is a [measurable function](../measure-theory.md#measurable-function) of that pair. [Mathematical induction](../foundations-of-mathematics.md#mathematical-induction) proves $X_n$ is $\mathcal F_n$-[measurable](../measure-theory.md#measurability) for every $n$, so **$(X_n)$ is an adapted process**. The same recursion also proves $0<X_n<1$ at every finite time, a useful bound for the later [integrability](../measure-theory.md#integrability) arguments.

<h4 id="1/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

<h5 id="1/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#1/b/ii)

Retain the binary [random variables](../random-variable.md) $B_j=\mathbf1_{\{U_j\leq X_{j-1}\}}$. Iterating the recursion gives

$$
2^nX_n=x+\sum_{j=1}^n2^{j-1}B_j.
$$

Each binary history determines the full vector $(X_0,\ldots,X_n)$, so its [sigma-algebra](../measure-theory.md#sigma-algebra) is finite by the [sigma-algebra of a finite-valued random vector](../measure-theory.md#sigma-algebra-of-a-finite-valued-random-vector). Conversely, because $0<x<1$, the integer $2^nX_n-x$ uniquely determines the digits $B_1,\ldots,B_n$; also $B_j=2X_j-X_{j-1}$. Thus

$$
\mathcal F_n^X=\sigma(B_1,\ldots,B_n)=\sigma(X_n).
$$

All $2^n$ binary histories have positive [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability). By [independence](../random-variable.md#independent-random-variables) of the driving [uniform random variables](../continuous-probability-distribution.md#uniform-random-variable), along a fixed finite history every intermediate state is in $(0,1)$, so each subsequent conditional branch [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability), either $X_{j-1}$ or $1-X_{j-1}$, is strictly positive. It follows that $\mathcal F_n^X$ has $2^n$ nonempty fibres, each an [atom of a sigma-algebra](../measure-theory.md#atom-of-a-sigma-algebra), and exactly $2^{2^n}$ elements.

[Adaptedness](../stochastic-process.md#adapted-process) from the preceding part gives $\mathcal F_n^X\subseteq\mathcal F_n$. For $n\geq1$, however, $\mathcal F_n$ contains the [events](../probability-space.md#event) $\{U_1\leq t\}$ for every $t\in[0,1]$. They have distinct [probabilities](../probability-theory.md#probability) $t$, so there are infinitely many distinct such [events](../probability-space.md#event). Consequently the finite [sigma-algebra](../measure-theory.md#sigma-algebra) $\mathcal F_n^X$ cannot equal $\mathcal F_n$. **The inclusion is strict for every $n\geq1$.**

<h4 id="1/b/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

<h5 id="1/b/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#1/b/iii)

The [adapted process](../stochastic-process.md#adapted-process) is bounded by $1$, so every $X_n$ is an [integrable random variable](../expected-value.md#integrable-random-variable), indeed a [square-integrable](../measure-theory.md#square-integrable-function) [random variable](../random-variable.md). [Independence](../random-variable.md#independent-random-variables) of $U_{n+1}$ from $\mathcal F_n$ and its [continuous uniform distribution](../continuous-probability-distribution.md#continuous-uniform-distribution) give

$$
\mathbb E[B_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n]=X_n.
$$

This calculation with a random threshold is justified by conditioning on the $\mathcal F_n$-[measurable](../measure-theory.md#measurability) value of $X_n$: for a fixed $t\in[0,1]$, the [Lebesgue integral](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-integral) $\int_0^1\mathbf1_{\{u\leq t\}}\,du$ equals $t$. More formally, [independence](../random-variable.md#independent-random-variables) integrates the bounded [Borel measurable function](../measure-theory.md#borel-measurable-function) $(t,u)\mapsto\mathbf1_{\{u\leq t\}}$ over its second coordinate. Therefore

$$
\mathbb E[X_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n]
=\tfrac12X_n+\tfrac12\mathbb E[B_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n]=X_n,
$$

which verifies the [martingale](../martingale.md) property for this [filtration](../stochastic-process.md#filtration-probability-theory).

Since $B_{n+1}^2=B_{n+1}$, the [conditional expectation](../measure-theory.md#conditional-expectation) of the squared [martingale difference](../martingale.md#martingale-difference) is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E[(X_{n+1}-X_n)^2\mid\mathcal F_n]
&=\tfrac14\mathbb E[(B_{n+1}-X_n)^2\mid\mathcal F_n]\\
&=\tfrac14(X_n-2X_n^2+X_n^2)
=\tfrac14X_n(1-X_n).
\end{aligned}
$$

The [law of total expectation](../measure-theory.md#law-of-total-expectation) yields **$\mathbb E[(X_{n+1}-X_n)^2]=\frac14\mathbb E[X_n(1-X_n)]$**. In particular, $\mathbb EX_n=x$ for every $n$.

<h4 id="1/b/iv">iv</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

<h5 id="1/b/iv/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#1/b/iv)

The [L2-bounded martingale convergence theorem](../martingale.md#l2-bounded-martingale-convergence-theorem) states that a [martingale](../martingale.md) with $\sup_n\mathbb E|X_n|^2<\infty$ converges both [almost surely](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#almost-sure-convergence) and with [convergence in L2](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#convergence-in-l2) to a [square-integrable](../measure-theory.md#square-integrable-function) [random variable](../random-variable.md). Its hypotheses hold because $0<X_n<1$. Thus there is $X_\infty\in[0,1]$ [almost surely](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#almost-sure-convergence) with both kinds of convergence. Alternatively, the [martingale convergence theorem](../martingale.md#martingale-convergence-theorem) gives the [almost sure convergence](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#almost-sure-convergence), and the bound $|X_n-X_\infty|^2\leq1$ gives [convergence in L2](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#convergence-in-l2) by the [dominated convergence theorem](../measure-theory.md#dominated-convergence-theorem).[§4.4, Theorem 4.4.6, printed p. 237: the Lp martingale convergence theorem](../mathematics-bibliography.md#probability-theory-and-examples-fifth-edition-by-rick-durrett)

The calculation of the [conditional expectation](../measure-theory.md#conditional-expectation) of the square is especially informative. The [martingale](../martingale.md) property makes the cross term vanish in the square expansion, so

$$
\mathbb E[X_{n+1}^2\mid\mathcal F_n]
=X_n^2+\tfrac14X_n(1-X_n).
$$

Subtracting this from $\mathbb E[X_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n]=X_n$ gives

$$
\mathbb E[X_{n+1}(1-X_{n+1})\mid\mathcal F_n]
=\tfrac34X_n(1-X_n).
$$

Taking [expected values](../expected-value.md) and iterating proves

$$
\mathbb E[X_n(1-X_n)]=x(1-x)(3/4)^n.
$$

By [bounded convergence theorem](../measure-theory.md#bounded-convergence-theorem), $\mathbb E[X_\infty(1-X_\infty)]=0$. The [random variable](../random-variable.md) inside this [expected value](../expected-value.md) is nonnegative, so it is zero [almost surely](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#almost-sure-convergence); on $[0,1]$ this forces $X_\infty\in\{0,1\}$. Again by [bounded convergence theorem](../measure-theory.md#bounded-convergence-theorem), $\mathbb EX_\infty=\lim_n\mathbb EX_n=x$. Hence

$$
\boxed{X_\infty\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(x),\qquad
\mathbb P(X_\infty=1)=x,\quad\mathbb P(X_\infty=0)=1-x.}
$$

This is the [Bernoulli polarization martingale](../martingale.md#bernoulli-polarization-martingale) with update size $1/2$. It also has an exact [mean squared error](../statistical-modelling.md#mean-squared-error): the terminal representation $X_n=\mathbb E[X_\infty\mid\mathcal F_n]$ and the [equality case for conditional second moments](../measure-theory.md#equality-case-for-conditional-second-moments) give

$$
\mathbb E[(X_\infty-X_n)^2]
=\mathbb E[X_\infty^2]-\mathbb E[X_n^2]
=x(1-x)(3/4)^n.
$$

<h3 id="2/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#2/a)

Let $m=\mathbb EX$. First, $m$ lies in the open [interval](../real-analysis.md#interval-mathematics) $I$. For example, if $I$ has a finite lower endpoint $\ell$, then $X-\ell>0$ [almost surely](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#almost-sure-convergence), and its [expected value](../expected-value.md) is strictly positive; hence $m>\ell$. The corresponding argument at a finite upper endpoint gives the other strict inequality. [Integrability](../measure-theory.md#integrability) ensures $m$ is finite when either endpoint is infinite.

The tangent [affine function](../vector-space.md#affine-function) at $m$ gives the [supporting inequality for a differentiable convex function](../real-analysis.md#supporting-inequality-for-a-differentiable-convex-function). Under the stipulated condition $f''>0$, contact with that tangent occurs only at $m$: define

$$
h(t)=f(t)-f(m)-f'(m)(t-m).
$$

Then $h(m)=0$, and $h(t)>0$ for every $t\ne m$. One direct proof uses $h'(t)=f'(t)-f'(m)$: $f'$ is strictly increasing, so $h$ decreases towards $m$ on the left and increases away from $m$ on the right. Therefore

$$
\mathbb E[f(X)]=f(m)+\mathbb E[h(X)]\geq f(m),
$$

with equality exactly when $h(X)=0$ [almost surely](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#almost-sure-convergence), equivalently when $X=m$ [almost surely](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#almost-sure-convergence). Conversely, an [almost surely](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#almost-sure-convergence) constant $X=m$ plainly gives equality. This proves the [strict Jensen equality criterion](../real-analysis.md#strict-jensen-equality-criterion).

No extra assumption that $f(X)$ is an [integrable random variable](../expected-value.md#integrable-random-variable) is needed to interpret this argument. Its [negative part](../function.md#negative-part-of-a-real-valued-function) is bounded by the absolute value of the [integrable random variable](../expected-value.md#integrable-random-variable) $f(m)+f'(m)(X-m)$, since $h(X)\geq0$. Thus its [expected value](../expected-value.md) is defined in $(-\infty,+\infty]$; if it equals the finite number $f(m)$, then $\mathbb E[h(X)]=0$ and the required [integrability](../measure-theory.md#integrability) follows automatically. **Equality holds if and only if $X=\mathbb EX$ almost surely.**

<h3 id="2/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

<h5 id="2/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#2/b/i)

For each $N\geq1$, put $C_N=\bigcup_{n\geq N}A_n$. Closure of a [sigma-algebra](../measure-theory.md#sigma-algebra) under [countable unions](../set.md#countable-union) and [countable intersections](../set.md#countable-intersection) gives

$$
A=\limsup_{n\to\infty}A_n=\bigcap_{N\geq1}C_N\in\mathcal F.
$$

The [events](../probability-space.md#event) $C_N$ decrease to $A$. [Continuity of probability](../probability-space.md#continuity-of-probability) from above applies because $\mathbb P(C_1)\leq1<\infty$. Also, $A_n\subseteq C_N$ whenever $n\geq N$, so

$$
\mathbb P(C_N)\geq\sup_{n\geq N}\mathbb P(A_n).
$$

Letting $N\to\infty$ proves

$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(A)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb P(C_N)
\geq\lim_{N\to\infty}\sup_{n\geq N}\mathbb P(A_n)
=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb P(A_n).}
$$

This [reverse Fatou inequality for events](../probability-space.md#reverse-fatou-inequality-for-events) requires no [independence](../random-variable.md#independent-random-variables).

<h4 id="2/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

<h5 id="2/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#2/b/ii)

Write $B_n=\{X_n>a_n\}$. If $B_n$ occurs, the running maximum at time $n$ exceeds $a_n$, so $B_n\subseteq A_n$ and $\limsup_nB_n\subseteq A$.

For the reverse inclusion, fix an outcome for which only finitely many $B_n$ occur. Choose $N$ such that $X_m\leq a_m$ for every $m\geq N$. The finite prefix has a finite maximum $K=\max_{1\leq m<N}X_m$ when $N>1$; there is no prefix to check when $N=1$. Since $a_n\to\infty$, for every sufficiently large $n$ that prefix satisfies $K\leq a_n$. For the remaining indices $N\leq m\leq n$, the nondecreasing thresholds give

$$
X_m\leq a_m\leq a_n.
$$

Thus $\max_{m\leq n}X_m\leq a_n$ for every sufficiently large $n$, so only finitely many $A_n$ occur. Taking the contrapositive proves the reverse inclusion, and hence the pointwise [event](../probability-space.md#event) equality

$$
\boxed{\limsup_n\{\max_{m\leq n}X_m>a_n\}
=\limsup_n\{X_n>a_n\}.}
$$

This [exceedance equivalence for increasing thresholds](../probability-space.md#exceedance-equivalence-for-increasing-thresholds) is deterministic. Its proof uses both monotonicity of $a_n$ and divergence to $+\infty$; it does not require [independence](../random-variable.md#independent-random-variables) or identical [probability distributions](../probability-distribution.md).

<h4 id="2/b/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

<h5 id="2/b/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#2/b/iii)

The [tail sigma-algebra](../probability-theory.md#tail-sigma-algebra) of this [sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#sequence) is

$$
\mathcal T=\bigcap_{r\geq1}\sigma(X_r,X_{r+1},\ldots).
$$

For each fixed $r$, the preceding [event](../probability-space.md#event) equality gives

$$
A=\bigcap_{N\geq r}\bigcup_{n\geq N}\{X_n>a_n\}.
$$

Every [event](../probability-space.md#event) on the right is determined by coordinates with index at least $r$. Therefore $A\in\sigma(X_r,X_{r+1},\ldots)$ for every $r$, and $A\in\mathcal T$. The [random variables](../random-variable.md) $X_n$ are [independent](../random-variable.md#independent-random-variables), so [Kolmogorov's zero–one law](../probability-theory.md#kolmogorov-s-zero-one-law) applies: every [tail event](../probability-theory.md#tail-event) has [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability) zero or one. Hence **$\mathbb P(A)\in\{0,1\}$**.

The role of [independence](../random-variable.md#independent-random-variables) can also be seen directly. A [tail event](../probability-theory.md#tail-event) is independent of each finite initial [sigma-algebra](../measure-theory.md#sigma-algebra) $\sigma(X_1,\ldots,X_m)$. The [Monotone class theorem](../measure-theory.md#monotone-class-theorem) extends this [independence](../random-variable.md#independent-random-variables) to $\sigma(X_1,X_2,\ldots)$; since $A$ itself lies there, it is independent of itself. Thus $\mathbb P(A)=\mathbb P(A)^2$.[§2.5, Theorem 2.5.3, printed pp. 81–82: the tail sigma-field zero–one law](../mathematics-bibliography.md#probability-theory-and-examples-fifth-edition-by-rick-durrett)

<h4 id="2/b/iv">iv</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

<h5 id="2/b/iv/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#2/b/iv)

For $B_n=\{X_n>a_n\}$, the definition of the common [cumulative distribution function](../probability-distribution.md#cumulative-distribution-function) gives

$$
\mathbb P(B_n)=1-F(a_n).
$$

The strict exceedance matters here: an [atom of a measure](../measure.md#atom-measure-theory) at $a_n$ belongs to $\{X_n\leq a_n\}$, so it is already included in $F(a_n)$. The [events](../probability-space.md#event) $B_n$ are mutually [independent events](../probability-theory.md#independent-events), since each depends on a separate member of the [independent random variables](../random-variable.md#independent-random-variables) $X_n$.

If $\sum_n(1-F(a_n))<\infty$, [countable subadditivity](../measure.md#countable-subadditivity-of-a-measure) gives, for every $N$,

$$
\mathbb P\left(\bigcup_{n\geq N}B_n\right)
\leq\sum_{n\geq N}(1-F(a_n))\longrightarrow0.
$$

[Continuity of probability](../probability-space.md#continuity-of-probability) then gives $\mathbb P(\limsup_nB_n)=0$. This is the [First Borel-Cantelli lemma](../probability-theory.md#borel-cantelli-first-lemma) and does not need [independence](../random-variable.md#independent-random-variables).

If the [series](../sequence-and-series.md#series-mathematics) diverges, [independence](../random-variable.md#independent-random-variables) and $1-u\leq e^{-u}$ give, for every fixed $N$,

$$
\mathbb P\left(\bigcap_{n=N}^{M}B_n^c\right)
=\prod_{n=N}^{M}F(a_n)
\leq\exp\left(-\sum_{n=N}^{M}(1-F(a_n))\right)\longrightarrow0.
$$

Taking $M\to\infty$ shows that the [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability) of no exceedance after $N$ is zero. A [countable union](../set.md#countable-union) over $N$ shows that finitely many exceedances has [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability) zero. This is the [Second Borel-Cantelli lemma](../probability-theory.md#borel-cantelli-second-lemma). Together with the preceding [event](../probability-space.md#event) equality, it proves

$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(A)=
\begin{cases}
0,&\sum_{n\geq1}(1-F(a_n))<\infty,\\
1,&\sum_{n\geq1}(1-F(a_n))=\infty.
\end{cases}}
$$

<h3 id="2/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/c/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#2/c)

<h5 id="2/c/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#2/c/i)

The conditions on the [cumulative distribution function](../probability-distribution.md#cumulative-distribution-function) imply $0<X_n\leq2$ [almost surely](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#almost-sure-convergence). With $\mathcal H_n=\sigma(X_1,\ldots,X_n)$, adjoin $Y_0=1$ and the trivial $\mathcal H_0$. The [random variables](../random-variable.md) $Y_n$ are nonnegative, [adapted](../stochastic-process.md#adapted-process), and bounded by $2^n$ at each fixed time. [Independence](../random-variable.md#independent-random-variables) and $\mathbb EX_{n+1}=1$ give

$$
\mathbb E[Y_{n+1}\mid\mathcal H_n]=Y_n\mathbb EX_{n+1}=Y_n.
$$

Thus this is the [product martingale from independent mean-one factors](../martingale.md#product-martingale-from-independent-mean-one-factors), with $\mathbb EY_n=1$. The [almost sure supermartingale convergence theorem](../martingale.md#almost-sure-supermartingale-convergence-theorem) states that a nonnegative [supermartingale](../martingale.md#supermartingale) converges [almost surely](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#almost-sure-convergence) to a finite [integrable random variable](../expected-value.md#integrable-random-variable). Applied to this nonnegative [martingale](../martingale.md), it shows $Y_n\to Y_\infty<\infty$ [almost surely](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#almost-sure-convergence). Every convergent real [sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#sequence) is bounded, so **$\sup_{n\geq1}Y_n<\infty$ almost surely**.

The printed maximum can in fact be taken literally here. If $X_1=1$ [almost surely](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#almost-sure-convergence), all products equal $1$. Otherwise the [strict Jensen equality criterion](../real-analysis.md#strict-jensen-equality-criterion), applied to the [strictly convex function](../real-analysis.md#strictly-convex-function) $t\mapsto-\sqrt t$ on $(0,\infty)$, gives $q=\mathbb E\sqrt{X_1}<1$. [Independence](../random-variable.md#independent-random-variables) yields $\mathbb E\sqrt{Y_n}=q^n$. By [Markov's inequality](../probability-inequality.md#markov-s-inequality), $\sum_n\mathbb P(Y_n>\varepsilon)\leq\varepsilon^{-1/2}\sum_nq^n<\infty$ for every $\varepsilon>0$. The [First Borel-Cantelli lemma](../probability-theory.md#borel-cantelli-first-lemma) and the countable choices $\varepsilon=1/m$ give $Y_n\to0$ [almost surely](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#almost-sure-convergence). Since $Y_1>0$, a [sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#sequence) with this limit attains its positive supremum among finitely many initial terms. This is an instance of [fractional moment decay of independent mean-one products](../martingale.md#fractional-moment-decay-of-independent-mean-one-products).

<h4 id="2/c/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#2/c)

<h5 id="2/c/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#2/c/ii)

Use the nonnegative [martingale](../martingale.md) $(Y_n)$ and [filtration](../stochastic-process.md#filtration-probability-theory) $(\mathcal H_n)$ established above. Fix a finite $n$ and let

$$
H=\{\max_{1\leq k\leq n}Y_k\geq\lambda\},\qquad
T=\min\bigl(\{1\leq k\leq n:Y_k\geq\lambda\}\cup\{n\}\bigr).
$$

For $j<n$, $\{T\leq j\}$ is the [union](../set.md#set-union) of the threshold-crossing [events](../probability-space.md#event) up to $j$, so $T$ is a [bounded stopping time](../martingale.md#bounded-stopping-time). The [optional stopping theorem](../martingale.md#optional-sampling-theorem-for-a-supermartingale) for a [martingale](../martingale.md) at a [bounded stopping time](../martingale.md#bounded-stopping-time) gives $\mathbb EY_T=\mathbb EY_0=1$. On $H$ one has $Y_T\geq\lambda$; on its [complement](../set.md#complement-of-a-set) one still has $Y_T\geq0$. Therefore

$$
\lambda\mathbb P(H)\leq\mathbb EY_T=1,
\qquad
\boxed{\mathbb P(H)\leq\lambda^{-1}.}
$$

Only a [bounded stopping time](../martingale.md#bounded-stopping-time) has been used, so no extra [uniform integrability](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#uniform-integrability) assumption at an unbounded hitting time is implicit. This is also the [Doob maximal inequality for a nonnegative submartingale](../martingale.md#doob-maximal-inequality-for-a-nonnegative-submartingale) specialized to a mean-one [martingale](../martingale.md).

<h4 id="2/c/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#2/c)

<h5 id="2/c/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#2/c/iii)

For $p>1$, the bound $X_k\leq2$ ensures the finite [moment](../expected-value.md#moment) $m_p=\mathbb EX_1^p\leq2^p$. By [Jensen's inequality](../real-analysis.md#jensen-s-inequality), $m_p\geq(\mathbb EX_1)^p=1$. The process $Z_k=Y_k^p$ is an [adapted](../stochastic-process.md#adapted-process), nonnegative [integrable random variable](../expected-value.md#integrable-random-variable) at each time, and [independence](../random-variable.md#independent-random-variables) gives

$$
\mathbb E[Z_{k+1}\mid\mathcal H_k]
=Y_k^p\mathbb EX_{k+1}^p=m_pZ_k\geq Z_k.
$$

Thus $(Z_k)$ is a [submartingale](../martingale.md#submartingale), and $\mathbb EZ_n=m_p^n$. The [Doob maximal inequality for a nonnegative submartingale](../martingale.md#doob-maximal-inequality-for-a-nonnegative-submartingale) states that $c\mathbb P(\max_{k\leq n}Z_k\geq c)\leq\mathbb EZ_n$ for $c>0$. Apply it with $c=\lambda^p$. Since the power [function](../function.md) is increasing on $[0,\infty)$,

$$
\mathbb P\left(\max_{1\leq k\leq n}Y_k\geq\lambda\right)
\leq\frac{m_p^n}{\lambda^p}.
$$

Combining this with the preceding bound gives

$$
\boxed{\mathbb P\left(\max_{1\leq k\leq n}Y_k\geq\lambda\right)
\leq\min\left\{\lambda^{-1},\ m_p^n\lambda^{-p}\right\}.}
$$

The result is a bound on the running maximum, which is why a maximal [inequality](../arithmetic.md#inequality-mathematics) for a [submartingale](../martingale.md#submartingale), rather than an estimate of the single terminal [random variable](../random-variable.md), is needed.[§4.4, Theorem 4.4.2, printed p. 235: the submartingale maximal inequality](../mathematics-bibliography.md#probability-theory-and-examples-fifth-edition-by-rick-durrett)

<h4 id="3/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

<h5 id="3/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#3/a/i)

For each integer $m\geq1$, let

$$
D_m=\{x\in\mathbb R:\mu(\{x\})\geq1/m\}.
$$

If $D_m$ had $m+1$ distinct members, [finite additivity](../function.md#finite-additivity-of-a-set-function) on their disjoint singleton [Borel sets](../measure-theory.md#borel-set) would give total [measure](../measure.md) at least $(m+1)/m>1$, contradicting $\mu(\mathbb R)=1$. Thus $|D_m|\leq m$.

Every positive singleton [measure](../measure.md) is at least $1/m$ for some sufficiently large $m$. Consequently the set of [point atoms of a measure](../measure.md#point-atom-of-a-measure) is

$$
\{x:\mu(\{x\})>0\}=\bigcup_{m\geq1}D_m.
$$

A [countable union](../set.md#countable-union) of finite [sets](../set.md) is at most [countable](../cardinal-number.md#countable-set). This proves the claim, including the possibility that the set is empty. The same argument gives the [countability of point atoms of a finite measure](../measure.md#countability-of-point-atoms-of-a-finite-measure), with $|D_m|\leq m\mu(\mathbb R)$ in place of $m$.

<h4 id="3/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

<h5 id="3/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#3/a/ii)

The [product measure](../probability-theory.md#product-measure) $\mu\otimes\nu$ is the unique [probability measure](../probability-space.md#probability-measure) on the [sigma-algebra](../measure-theory.md#sigma-algebra) generated by measurable rectangles for which

$$
\boxed{(\mu\otimes\nu)(C\times D)=\mu(C)\nu(D)
\quad\text{for all }C,D\in\mathcal B(\mathbb R).}
$$

Here the printed product notation $\mathcal B(\mathbb R)\times\mathcal B(\mathbb R)$ denotes the generated [product sigma-algebra](../probability-theory.md#product-sigma-algebra), conventionally written $\mathcal B(\mathbb R)\otimes\mathcal B(\mathbb R)$; it is not just the collection of rectangles. On $\mathbb R^2$ it equals $\mathcal B(\mathbb R^2)$: coordinate projections show that rectangles are [Borel sets](../measure-theory.md#borel-set), while open rectangles with rational endpoints give every open [set](../set.md) as a [countable union](../set.md#countable-union) and hence generate the [Borel sigma-algebra](../measure-theory.md#borel-sigma-algebra).

Existence is the [product measure](../probability-theory.md#product-measure) theorem for finite [measures](../measure.md), which are in particular [sigma-finite measures](../measure.md#sigma-finite-measure). Uniqueness follows because the rectangles form a [pi-system](../measure-theory.md#pi-system) generating the [product sigma-algebra](../probability-theory.md#product-sigma-algebra), and two finite [measures](../measure.md) agreeing there agree everywhere by the [Dynkin lemma](../measure-theory.md#dynkin-lemma).[§1.7, Theorem 1.7.1, printed p. 38: existence and characterization on rectangles](../mathematics-bibliography.md#probability-theory-and-examples-fifth-edition-by-rick-durrett)

<h4 id="3/a/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

<h5 id="3/a/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#3/a/iii)

Consider the [Borel set](../measure-theory.md#borel-set)

$$
D=\{(x,y):a<x\leq y\leq b\}.
$$

Its [indicator function](../measure-theory.md#indicator-function) is nonnegative and [measurable](../measure-theory.md#measurability), so the [Tonelli theorem](../measure-theory.md#tonelli-theorem) applies to the [product measure](../probability-theory.md#product-measure), without needing any separate absolute-[integrability](../measure-theory.md#integrability) argument. At a fixed $y\in(a,b]$, its section in the first coordinate is $(a,y]$; outside $(a,b]$ that section is empty. Therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
(\mu\otimes\nu)(D)
&=\int_{\mathbb R}\left(\int_{\mathbb R}\mathbf1_D(x,y)\,\mu(dx)\right)\nu(dy)\\
&=\int_{(a,b]}\mu((a,y])\,\nu(dy)\\
&=\int_{(a,b]}\bigl(F_\mu(y)-F_\mu(a)\bigr)\,\nu(dy).
\end{aligned}
$$

The last equality uses the definition of a [cumulative distribution function](../probability-distribution.md#cumulative-distribution-function) on the half-closed [interval](../real-analysis.md#interval-mathematics). It retains all [point atoms of a measure](../measure.md#point-atom-of-a-measure) on the diagonal $x=y$, excludes a possible [point atom of a measure](../measure.md#point-atom-of-a-measure) at $x=a$, and includes $y=b$, exactly as required by the [set](../set.md) $D$. If $a=b$, both sides vanish. This verifies the identity even when the [probability measures](../probability-space.md#probability-measure) are not [non-atomic measures](../measure.md#non-atomic-measure).[§1.7, Theorem 1.7.2, printed pp. 38–40: integration of a nonnegative measurable function on a product space](../mathematics-bibliography.md#probability-theory-and-examples-fifth-edition-by-rick-durrett)

<h3 id="3/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

<h5 id="3/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#3/b/i)

The [filtration](../stochastic-process.md#filtration-probability-theory) is generated by the successive [independent random variables](../random-variable.md#independent-random-variables) $X_k$. Each $S_n$ is [adapted](../stochastic-process.md#adapted-process), and $|S_n|\leq n$ makes it an [integrable random variable](../expected-value.md#integrable-random-variable). Since [independence](../random-variable.md#independent-random-variables) gives $\mathbb E[X_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n]=0$,

$$
\mathbb E[S_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n]=S_n.
$$

Thus $(S_n)$ is a [martingale](../martingale.md).

Write the accumulated number of visits to zero as

$$
L_n=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\mathbf1_{\{S_k=0\}},\qquad L_0=0.
$$

It is [adapted](../stochastic-process.md#adapted-process), and $0\leq L_n\leq n$. Thus $M_n=|S_n|-L_n$ is also [adapted](../stochastic-process.md#adapted-process) and an [integrable random variable](../expected-value.md#integrable-random-variable). For every integer $s$,

$$
\frac{|s+1|+|s-1|}{2}-|s|
=\begin{cases}1,&s=0,\\0,&s\ne0.\end{cases}
$$

Conditioning on $S_n$ and using [independence](../random-variable.md#independent-random-variables) of the next step therefore gives

$$
\mathbb E[|S_{n+1}|-|S_n|\mid\mathcal F_n]
=\mathbf1_{\{S_n=0\}}
=L_{n+1}-L_n.
$$

The drift of the absolute value is cancelled exactly by the visit count, so $\mathbb E[M_{n+1}-M_n\mid\mathcal F_n]=0$. Hence **both $(S_n)$ and $(M_n)$ are martingales**. The latter is the [absolute value martingale of a simple symmetric random walk](../stochastic-calculus.md#absolute-value-martingale-of-a-simple-symmetric-random-walk), a [discrete Tanaka formula](../stochastic-calculus.md#discrete-tanaka-formula) in which visits to zero supply the correction at the corner of the absolute-value [function](../function.md).

<h4 id="3/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

<h5 id="3/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#3/b/ii)

For each $n$,

$$
\{\tau\leq n\}=\bigcup_{k=0}^n\{S_k\in\{a,b\}\}\in\mathcal F_n,
$$

since $S_k$ is [adapted](../stochastic-process.md#adapted-process). This verifies directly that $\tau$ is a [stopping time](../martingale.md#stopping-time).

Set $d=b-a$. On $\{\tau>md\}$, the [random walk](../random-walk.md) is at some integer strictly between $a$ and $b$. If its next $d$ steps are all $+1$, it must hit $b$ within that block. These future steps are [independent](../random-variable.md#independent-random-variables) of $\mathcal F_{md}$ and have [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability) $2^{-d}$ of being all $+1$. Hence

$$
\mathbb P(\tau>(m+1)d\mid\mathcal F_{md})
\leq(1-2^{-d})\mathbf1_{\{\tau>md\}}.
$$

The [law of total expectation](../measure-theory.md#law-of-total-expectation) and [mathematical induction](../foundations-of-mathematics.md#mathematical-induction) give the [geometric tail bound from a uniform escape probability](../martingale.md#geometric-tail-bound-from-a-uniform-escape-probability):

$$
\mathbb P(\tau>md)\leq(1-2^{-d})^m\longrightarrow0.
$$

Therefore **$\mathbb P(\tau<\infty)=1$**. In fact this also proves $\mathbb E\tau\leq d\,2^d<\infty$ by grouping the [tail-sum formula](../expected-value.md#tail-sum-formula) into blocks of length $d$. No assertion of [independence](../random-variable.md#independent-random-variables) between successive survival [events](../probability-space.md#event) is needed.

<h4 id="3/b/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

<h5 id="3/b/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#3/b/iii)

The [optional stopping theorem](../martingale.md#optional-sampling-theorem-for-a-supermartingale) at a [bounded stopping time](../martingale.md#bounded-stopping-time) states that an [integrable](../measure-theory.md#integrability) [martingale](../martingale.md) $Z_n$ satisfies $\mathbb EZ_T=\mathbb EZ_0$ whenever $T\leq n$ for a fixed finite $n$. Apply this first to $S$ at $T=n\wedge\tau$. The [simple symmetric random walk](../random-walk.md#simple-symmetric-random-walk) cannot cross an integer endpoint without hitting it, so

$$
a\leq S_{n\wedge\tau}\leq b,\qquad
\mathbb E S_{n\wedge\tau}=0.
$$

The preceding part gives $\tau<\infty$ [almost surely](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#almost-sure-convergence), hence $S_{n\wedge\tau}\to S_\tau$ [almost surely](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#almost-sure-convergence). The constant bound $C=\max\{-a,b\}$ permits the [dominated convergence theorem](../measure-theory.md#dominated-convergence-theorem), yielding $\mathbb ES_\tau=0$. Since $S_\tau$ is either $a$ or $b$, solving $ap+b(1-p)=0$ gives its full [probability distribution](../probability-distribution.md):

$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(S_\tau=a)=\frac{b}{b-a},\qquad
\mathbb P(S_\tau=b)=\frac{-a}{b-a}.}
$$

Now let

$$
L_\tau=\sum_{k=0}^{\tau-1}\mathbf1_{\{S_k=0\}}.
$$

This counts the visit at time $0$, and excludes the exit time. Apply the same bounded-time [optional stopping theorem](../martingale.md#optional-sampling-theorem-for-a-supermartingale) to $M_n=|S_n|-L_n$. For each $n$ it gives

$$
\mathbb E L_{n\wedge\tau}=\mathbb E|S_{n\wedge\tau}|.
$$

The visit counts increase to $L_\tau$, so [monotone convergence](../measure-theory.md#monotone-convergence-theorem) applies to the left. [Dominated convergence](../measure-theory.md#dominated-convergence-theorem) applies to the right using $|S_{n\wedge\tau}|\leq C$. Consequently $L_\tau$ is an [integrable random variable](../expected-value.md#integrable-random-variable) and

$$
\boxed{\mathbb E L_\tau=\mathbb E|S_\tau|
=(-a)\frac{b}{b-a}+b\frac{-a}{b-a}
=\frac{-2ab}{b-a}.}
$$

This is the origin case of [expected occupation before interval exit for a simple symmetric random walk](../random-walk.md#expected-occupation-before-interval-exit-for-a-simple-symmetric-random-walk). The argument obtains [integrability](../measure-theory.md#integrability) of the total occupation count before using any unbounded-time [optional stopping](../martingale.md#optional-sampling-theorem-for-a-supermartingale) for $M$, so it does not presuppose the [uniform integrability](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#uniform-integrability) asked about next. The endpoint [probabilities](../probability-theory.md#probability) also agree with the standard [gambler's ruin](../markov-chain.md#gambler-s-ruin) calculation.[§4.8.1, Theorem 4.8.7(a), printed pp. 257–258: symmetric random-walk interval-exit probabilities](../mathematics-bibliography.md#probability-theory-and-examples-fifth-edition-by-rick-durrett)

<h4 id="3/b/iv">iv</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

<h5 id="3/b/iv/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#3/b/iv)

**Yes.** The preceding calculation established $\mathbb E L_\tau=-2ab/(b-a)<\infty$. For every $n$, the stopped [martingale](../martingale.md) satisfies

$$
|M_{n\wedge\tau}|
\leq |S_{n\wedge\tau}|+L_{n\wedge\tau}
\leq C+L_\tau=:H,
\qquad C=\max\{-a,b\}.
$$

Here $H$ is a single nonnegative [integrable random variable](../expected-value.md#integrable-random-variable) that bounds the whole family. Thus, for any $K>0$,

$$
\sup_n\mathbb E\!\left[|M_{n\wedge\tau}|\mathbf1_{\{|M_{n\wedge\tau}|>K\}}\right]
\leq\mathbb E[H\mathbf1_{\{H>K\}}]\longrightarrow0.
$$

The last limit follows from the [dominated convergence theorem](../measure-theory.md#dominated-convergence-theorem). This is precisely [uniform integrability](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#uniform-integrability). It also gives $M_{n\wedge\tau}\to M_\tau=|S_\tau|-L_\tau$ with [convergence in L1](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#convergence-in-l1), and therefore $\mathbb EM_\tau=0$, consistently with the preceding answer. A common bound on the individual [expected values](../expected-value.md) alone would not have sufficed; the [integrable](../measure-theory.md#integrability) dominating [random variable](../random-variable.md) is what supplies control of every tail.
