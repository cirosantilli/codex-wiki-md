## 1

↑ **Parent:** [2023](2023.md)

<h3 id="1/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1/a)

An [irrotational flow](../vorticity.md#irrotational-flow) has $\nabla\times\mathbf u=0$. Hence a [velocity potential](../fluid-mechanics.md#velocity-potential) $\phi$ exists locally, and globally on a [simply connected domain](../complex-analysis.md#simply-connected-domain), with $\mathbf u=\nabla\phi$. The exterior of a sphere in three dimensions has this property. Constant [mass density](../fluid-mechanics.md#density) reduces the [continuity equation](../mathematical-physics.md#continuity-equation) to $\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0$, so

$$
\boxed{\nabla^2\phi=0}.
$$

Thus the [velocity potential](../fluid-mechanics.md#velocity-potential) is a [harmonic function](../partial-differential-equation.md#harmonic-function). For the [Euler equations](../euler-equations-for-an-inviscid-fluid.md), use the [vorticity cross-product identity](../vorticity.md#vorticity-cross-product-identity)

$$
(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u
=\nabla\!\left(\frac12|\mathbf u|^2\right)
-\mathbf u\times(\nabla\times\mathbf u).
$$

The last term vanishes. Since $\partial_t\mathbf u=\nabla\phi_t$, the [Euler momentum equation](../euler-equations-for-an-inviscid-fluid.md) becomes

$$
\nabla\!\left(\phi_t+\frac12|\nabla\phi|^2+\frac p\rho\right)=0.
$$

On each connected fluid region, a function with zero [gradient](../multivariable-calculus.md#gradient) is spatially constant. Therefore the [Unsteady Bernoulli equation](../euler-equations-for-an-inviscid-fluid.md#unsteady-bernoulli-equation) is

$$
\boxed{\frac p\rho+\phi_t+\frac12|\nabla\phi|^2=F(t)}.
$$

The freedom to add a function of time to the [velocity potential](../fluid-mechanics.md#velocity-potential) changes $F(t)$ but leaves the [velocity field](../fluid-mechanics.md#velocity-field) unchanged. On a multiply connected region, zero [vorticity](../vorticity.md) alone guarantees only local potentials; a global single-valued [velocity potential](../fluid-mechanics.md#velocity-potential) additionally requires zero [circulation](../vorticity.md#circulation-physics) around every closed path.

<h3 id="1/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

<h5 id="1/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#1/b/i)

Use the [spherical symmetry](../geometry-and-topology.md#spherical-symmetry) of the expansion, with fluid at rest at infinity and no superposed imposed flow. The radial [Laplace equation](../partial-differential-equation.md#laplace-equation) gives

$$
\partial_r(r^2\phi_r)=0,
\qquad \phi_r=\frac{A(t)}{r^2},
\qquad \phi=G(t)-\frac{A(t)}r.
$$

The moving sphere is a material boundary: its [kinematic boundary condition](../fluid-mechanics.md#kinematic-boundary-condition) is $u_r(R,t)=\dot R$. Consequently $A(t)=R^2\dot R$, and the [spherically symmetric incompressible radial flow](../fluid-mechanics.md#spherically-symmetric-incompressible-radial-flow) has

$$
\boxed{\phi(r,t)=G(t)-\frac{R^2\dot R}{r},\qquad
\mathbf u=\frac{R^2\dot R}{r^2}\,\widehat{\mathbf r}}.
$$

Equivalently, its [volumetric flow rate](../fluid-mechanics.md#volumetric-flow-rate) through any concentric sphere is $4\pi r^2u_r=4\pi R^2\dot R$, the rate of change of the enclosed [volume](../geometry-and-topology.md#volume).

<h4 id="1/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

<h5 id="1/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#1/b/ii)

Put $A=R^2\dot R$. The [partial derivative](../multivariable-calculus.md#partial-derivative) in the [Unsteady Bernoulli equation](../euler-equations-for-an-inviscid-fluid.md#unsteady-bernoulli-equation) is taken at fixed spatial position, so

$$
\phi_t=\dot G-\frac{\dot A}{r},\qquad
|\nabla\phi|^2=\frac{A^2}{r^4}.
$$

At infinity the [velocity](../classical-mechanics.md#velocity) vanishes and $p\to p_\infty$, giving $F(t)=\dot G+p_\infty/\rho$. Substitution cancels the arbitrary $G$ and yields the [pressure field of a pulsating sphere](../fluid-mechanics.md#pressure-field-of-a-pulsating-sphere):

$$
\boxed{\frac{p(r,t)}\rho
=\frac1r\frac d{dt}(R^2\dot R)
-\frac{R^4\dot R^2}{2r^4}+\frac{p_\infty}\rho}.
$$

The distinction between $\partial_t\phi$ at fixed $r$ and the [material derivative](../continuum-mechanics.md#material-derivative) along the moving boundary is essential here.

<h3 id="1/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/c/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#1/c)

<h5 id="1/c/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#1/c/i)

With zero internal [pressure](../pressure.md) and negligible [surface tension](../surface-tension.md), [pressure continuity](../fluid-mechanics.md#pressure-continuity) sets the liquid-side boundary value to $p(R,t)=0$. Evaluating the [pressure field of a pulsating sphere](../fluid-mechanics.md#pressure-field-of-a-pulsating-sphere) there gives

$$
0=\frac1R(R^2\ddot R+2R\dot R^2)-\frac12\dot R^2+\frac{p_\infty}\rho.
$$

Thus the [Rayleigh equation for an inviscid spherical bubble](../fluid-mechanics.md#rayleigh-equation-for-an-inviscid-spherical-bubble), together with its [initial conditions](../differential-equation.md#initial-condition), is

$$
\boxed{R\ddot R+\frac32\dot R^2=-\frac{p_\infty}\rho,
\qquad R(0)=a,\quad\dot R(0)=0}.
$$

For $p_\infty>0$, initially $\ddot R(0)=-p_\infty/(\rho a)<0$, so the physically relevant branch describes [Rayleigh collapse of a spherical cavity](../fluid-mechanics.md#rayleigh-collapse-of-a-spherical-cavity).

<h4 id="1/c/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#1/c)

<h5 id="1/c/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#1/c/ii)

Write $P=p_\infty/\rho>0$. Multiplying the radius [ordinary differential equation](../differential-equation.md#ordinary-differential-equation) by $2R^2\dot R$ gives an exact derivative:

$$
\frac d{dt}(R^3\dot R^2)
=2R^2\dot R\left(R\ddot R+\frac32\dot R^2\right)
=-2PR^2\dot R=-\frac{2P}{3}\frac d{dt}(R^3).
$$

The [initial conditions](../differential-equation.md#initial-condition) therefore give the [first integral](../differential-equation.md#first-integral)

$$
R^3\dot R^2=\frac{2P}{3}(a^3-R^3),\qquad
\boxed{\dot R=-\sqrt{\frac{2p_\infty}{3\rho}
\left(\frac{a^3}{R^3}-1\right)}}.
$$

Choose the negative square root because the cavity starts accelerating inward. It cannot turn around while $0<R<a$, since the squared speed is then strictly positive. Separating variables, with $s=R/a$, proves the [collapse time of a spherical cavity](../fluid-mechanics.md#collapse-time-of-a-spherical-cavity) is

$$
t_{\rm collapse}
=\sqrt{\frac{3\rho}{2p_\infty}}
\int_0^a\frac{R^{3/2}}{\sqrt{a^3-R^3}}\,dR
=a\sqrt{\frac\rho{p_\infty}}
\sqrt{\frac32}\int_0^1\frac{s^{3/2}}{\sqrt{1-s^3}}\,ds.
$$

Hence

$$
\boxed{t_{\rm collapse}=k\sqrt{\frac{\rho a^2}{p_\infty}},\qquad
k=\sqrt{\frac32}\int_0^1\frac{s^{3/2}}{\sqrt{1-s^3}}\,ds}.
$$

The [improper integral](../real-analysis.md#improper-integral) is finite: its integrand behaves as $s^{3/2}$ near zero and as $[3(1-s)]^{-1/2}$ near one. The divergent inward speed as $R\to0$ signals a singular endpoint of this ideal [incompressible flow](../fluid-mechanics.md#incompressible-flow) model; the finite collapse time follows within that model.

## 2

↑ **Parent:** [2023](2023.md)

<h3 id="2/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#2/a)

Use $W=\phi+i\psi$ for the [complex potential of a two-dimensional potential flow](../fluid-mechanics.md#complex-potential-of-a-two-dimensional-potential-flow), so $W'(z)=u-iv$. The [Milne-Thomson circle theorem](../fluid-mechanics.md#milne-thomson-circle-theorem) says that if $f(z)$ is an incident [complex potential of a two-dimensional potential flow](../fluid-mechanics.md#complex-potential-of-a-two-dimensional-potential-flow) whose singularities lie outside $|z|=a$, then

$$
W(z)=f(z)+\overline{f\!\left(\frac{a^2}{\bar z}\right)}
$$

makes $|z|=a$ a [streamline](../fluid-mechanics.md#streamline). Indeed, on that circle $a^2/\bar z=z$, so $W=f+\bar f$ is real and the [stream function](../fluid-mechanics.md#stream-function) is constant. This imposes the [no-penetration boundary condition](../viscous-fluid-flow.md#no-penetration-boundary-condition).

For the [two-dimensional potential doublet](../fluid-mechanics.md#two-dimensional-potential-doublet), put $M=me^{i\alpha}$ and $f=M/(z-b)$, where $b>a$ is real. The theorem gives

$$
\boxed{W(z)=\frac{me^{i\alpha}}{z-b}
+\frac{me^{-i\alpha}z}{a^2-bz}}.
$$

Up to an additive constant, this is equivalently

$$
W(z)=\frac{me^{i\alpha}}{z-b}
-\frac{ma^2e^{-i\alpha}}{b^2(z-a^2/b)}.
$$

The [method of images](../mathematics.md#method-of-images) has placed the image [two-dimensional potential doublet](../fluid-mechanics.md#two-dimensional-potential-doublet) at $a^2/b$, inside the obstacle. We use the solution with zero added [circulation](../vorticity.md#circulation-physics) and no imposed far-field flow; the original doublet is the only singularity in the liquid.

<h3 id="2/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

The [complex potential of a two-dimensional potential flow](../fluid-mechanics.md#complex-potential-of-a-two-dimensional-potential-flow) gives

$$
q(z):=W'(z)
=-\frac{me^{i\alpha}}{(z-b)^2}
+\frac{ma^2e^{-i\alpha}}{(bz-a^2)^2}.
$$

At a [stagnation point](../fluid-mechanics.md#stagnation-point), $q=0$. For $m\ne0$, taking the two square-root alternatives gives

$$
bz-a^2=\sigma a e^{-i\alpha}(z-b),\qquad \sigma\in\{+1,-1\}.
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{z_\sigma
=a\,\frac{a-\sigma b e^{-i\alpha}}{b-\sigma a e^{-i\alpha}},
\qquad \sigma=\pm1}.
$$

The denominators are nonzero because $b>a$. Moreover

$$
|a-\sigma b e^{-i\alpha}|^2
=a^2+b^2-2\sigma ab\cos\alpha
=|b-\sigma a e^{-i\alpha}|^2,
$$

so both [stagnation points](../fluid-mechanics.md#stagnation-point) satisfy $|z_\sigma|=a$. They are distinct: equality would require $2e^{-i\alpha}(a^2-b^2)=0$. Neither is a singularity of $q$. Conversely, the numerator of $q$ is a quadratic polynomial with nonzero leading coefficient, so these are all its finite zeros. Thus there are exactly two boundary [stagnation points](../fluid-mechanics.md#stagnation-point) and none in the open fluid region. If $m=0$, the flow instead vanishes everywhere, which is the degenerate exception to the assertion.

<h3 id="2/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#2/c)

For $q=W'=u-iv$, the [Blasius force theorem](../fluid-mechanics.md#blasius-force-theorem) states that the [force](../force.md) per unit length exerted by a steady [incompressible flow](../fluid-mechanics.md#incompressible-flow) and [irrotational flow](../vorticity.md#irrotational-flow) on an impermeable body is

$$
X-iY=\frac{i\rho}{2}\oint_C q(z)^2\,dz,
$$

where $C$ runs counterclockwise around the body and excludes external flow singularities. The sign corresponds to the [force](../force.md) on the body.

Let $c=a^2/b$ and $A=ma^2e^{-i\alpha}/b^2$. The [complex velocity](../fluid-mechanics.md#complex-velocity) may be written

$$
q(z)=\frac{A}{(z-c)^2}+H(z),\qquad
H(z)=-\frac{me^{i\alpha}}{(z-b)^2}.
$$

Only the image point $c$ lies inside the circular contour. The term $A^2/(z-c)^4$ has zero [residue](../analysis.md#residue); $H^2$ is regular there. In the cross term, the linear term of the [Taylor expansion](../calculus.md#taylor-expansion) of $H$ supplies

$$
\operatorname{Res}_{z=c}q^2
=2AH'(c)
=\frac{4m^2a^2}{b^2(c-b)^3}
=-\frac{4m^2a^2b}{(b^2-a^2)^3}.
$$

The orientation factors have canceled. The [residue theorem](../analysis.md#residue-theorem) now gives

$$
X-iY=-\pi\rho\operatorname{Res}_{z=c}q^2,
\qquad
\boxed{X=\frac{4\pi\rho m^2a^2b}{(b^2-a^2)^3},\qquad Y=0}.
$$

Since $b>a$, $X>0$: the obstacle is pulled toward the doublet, independently of $\alpha$. This is a [force](../force.md) per unit length perpendicular to the plane of flow.

<h3 id="2/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#2/d)

Keep the prescribed orientation $\alpha$ and strength $m$ fixed, and now let the doublet position $b(t)$ be complex. The [Milne-Thomson circle theorem](../fluid-mechanics.md#milne-thomson-circle-theorem) applies instantaneously with

$$
W(z,t)=\frac{M}{z-b}+\frac{\bar M z}{a^2-\bar b z},\qquad M=me^{i\alpha}.
$$

When taking its spatial derivative, $b(t)$ is a parameter. Removing the doublet's own singular term leaves the regular [complex velocity](../fluid-mechanics.md#complex-velocity) at its position:

$$
q_{\rm reg}(b)
=\left.\frac{\bar M a^2}{(a^2-\bar b z)^2}\right|_{z=b}
=\frac{\bar M a^2}{(|b|^2-a^2)^2}.
$$

Because $q=u-iv$, the stipulated [image-induced motion of a planar doublet](../fluid-mechanics.md#image-induced-motion-of-a-planar-doublet) obeys $\dot b=\overline{q_{\rm reg}(b)}$, not $q_{\rm reg}(b)$ itself. Hence

$$
\boxed{\dot b=\frac{ma^2e^{i\alpha}}{(|b|^2-a^2)^2}}.
$$

Its direction is constant. Indeed $\operatorname{Im}(be^{-i\alpha})$ is constant and, since $b(0)=b_0>a$ is real, equals $-b_0\sin\alpha$. Writing $b=re^{i\theta}$ in [polar coordinates](../multivariable-calculus.md#polar-coordinates) gives the path equation

$$
\boxed{r\sin(\theta-\alpha)=-b_0\sin\alpha},
\qquad
\boxed{r(\theta)=\frac{b_0\sin\alpha}{\sin(\alpha-\theta)}}
$$

where the quotient is used when $\sin\alpha\ne0$ and the branch through $(r,\theta)=(b_0,0)$ with $r>a$ is selected. It is the straight line through $b_0$ parallel to $e^{i\alpha}$. An unambiguous parametrization, also covering the degenerate polar cases, is

$$
b=b_0+s e^{i\alpha},\qquad
\dot s=\frac{ma^2}{(|b_0+s e^{i\alpha}|^2-a^2)^2},\qquad s(0)=0.
$$

If $\sin\alpha=0$, the path is radial along the positive real axis, with $\theta=0$ and changing $r$; it cannot be represented as a single-valued $r(\theta)$. The sign of $m$ selects which direction along the line is followed. These paths apply only while the singularity remains outside the obstacle; the model prescribes no orientation evolution.

## 3

↑ **Parent:** [2023](2023.md)

<h3 id="3/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

For a [plane wave](../wave-function.md#plane-wave) with [amplitude](../wave.md#wave-amplitude) $\alpha$, [wavenumber](../wave-equation.md#wavenumber) $k>0$ and [angular frequency](../lagrangian-mechanics.md#angular-frequency) $\omega>0$, the [dispersion relation](../dispersion-relation.md) $\omega=\omega(k)$ specifies which frequencies the governing equations permit. A point of fixed [wave phase](../wave.md#phase-waves) obeys $kx-\omega t=\text{constant}$, so the [phase speed](../dispersion-relation.md#phase-speed) is

$$
\boxed{c_p=\frac\omega k}.
$$

It is the speed of individual crests. For a narrow [wave packet](../dispersion-relation.md#wave-packet), two nearby [plane waves](../wave-function.md#plane-wave) with differences $\Delta k$ and $\Delta\omega$ have a slowly varying [wave envelope](../dispersion-relation.md#envelope-waves) depending on $\Delta k\,x-\Delta\omega\,t$. Taking the limit gives the [group velocity](../dispersion-relation.md#group-velocity)

$$
\boxed{c_g=\frac{d\omega}{dk}}.
$$

This is the envelope's propagation speed and, in the present linear lossless wave model, the [energy](../energy.md) transport speed for a narrow packet.

A [dispersive wave](../dispersion-relation.md#dispersive-wave) has [phase speed](../dispersion-relation.md#phase-speed) dependent on [wavenumber](../wave-equation.md#wavenumber), so different components can change their relative phases and alter the packet's shape. A [nondispersive wave](../dispersion-relation.md#nondispersive-wave) on the chosen propagation branch has constant [phase speed](../dispersion-relation.md#phase-speed) over the relevant band:

$$
\boxed{\omega(k)=ck,\qquad c_p=c_g=c}.
$$

Indeed $dc_p/dk=(c_g-c_p)/k$, so equality throughout a band is equivalent to constant [phase speed](../dispersion-relation.md#phase-speed) there. Equality at an isolated [wavenumber](../wave-equation.md#wavenumber) does not make the entire wave family nondispersive.

<h3 id="3/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

<h5 id="3/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#3/b/i)

The [irrotational flow](../vorticity.md#irrotational-flow) has a [velocity potential](../fluid-mechanics.md#velocity-potential) $\phi$ with $(u,w)=(\phi_x,\phi_z)$. The [incompressible flow](../fluid-mechanics.md#incompressible-flow) satisfies $u_x+w_z=0$, hence the interior [Laplace equation](../partial-differential-equation.md#laplace-equation) is

$$
\boxed{\phi_{xx}+\phi_{zz}=0,\qquad -h<z<\eta(x,t)}.
$$

The [ice](../chemical-substance.md#ice) supplies a boundary load through its [bending stiffness](../elasticity-physics.md#bending-stiffness); it does not alter this interior consequence of [mass conservation](../continuum-mechanics.md#mass-conservation).

<h4 id="3/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

<h5 id="3/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#3/b/ii)

At the rigid bed, the [no-penetration boundary condition](../viscous-fluid-flow.md#no-penetration-boundary-condition) gives

$$
\boxed{\phi_z=0\quad\text{at }z=-h}.
$$

The displaced surface is material, so taking the [material derivative](../continuum-mechanics.md#material-derivative) of $z-\eta(x,t)$ gives the exact [kinematic boundary condition for a free-surface graph](../fluid-mechanics.md#kinematic-boundary-condition-for-a-free-surface-graph):

$$
\boxed{\eta_t+\phi_x\eta_x=\phi_z\quad\text{at }z=\eta(x,t)}.
$$

With upward vertical coordinate $z$, the gravitational potential per unit [mass](../classical-mechanics.md#mass) is $gz$. The [Unsteady Bernoulli equation](../euler-equations-for-an-inviscid-fluid.md#unsteady-bernoulli-equation), evaluated on the surface with the prescribed [pressure](../pressure.md) $p=B\eta_{xxxx}$, gives

$$
\boxed{\phi_t+\frac12(\phi_x^2+\phi_z^2)
+g\eta+\frac B\rho\eta_{xxxx}=0
\quad\text{at }z=\eta(x,t)}.
$$

Here the spatially uniform time-dependent Bernoulli term has been absorbed into the [velocity potential](../fluid-mechanics.md#velocity-potential). The [pressure](../pressure.md) is measured relative to the constant exterior pressure. This dynamic boundary condition uses the specified massless bending-cover model, with no ice inertia or tension.

<h4 id="3/b/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

<h5 id="3/b/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#3/b/iii)

Linearize about the resting state $\eta=0$, $\phi=0$. Introduce a dimensionless small parameter $\epsilon$ and write

$$
\eta=\epsilon\eta_1+O(\epsilon^2),\qquad
\phi=\epsilon\phi_1+O(\epsilon^2),
$$

holding the depth, [bending stiffness](../elasticity-physics.md#bending-stiffness) and variation scales fixed. Thus both boundary displacement and induced [velocity](../classical-mechanics.md#velocity) are small. For any first-order derivative $D\phi$, a [Taylor expansion](../calculus.md#taylor-expansion) at the moving surface gives

$$
(D\phi)(x,\eta,t)
=\epsilon(D\phi_1)(x,0,t)+O(\epsilon^2).
$$

The displacement correction to the leading field is $\epsilon^2\eta_1\partial_z(D\phi_1)(x,0,t)$, so evaluating it on $z=0$ changes it only at second order. Also $\phi_x\eta_x=O(\epsilon^2)$ and $|\nabla\phi|^2=O(\epsilon^2)$. The first-order [kinematic boundary condition](../fluid-mechanics.md#kinematic-boundary-condition) and dynamic boundary condition are therefore

$$
(\eta_1)_t=(\phi_1)_z(x,0,t),\qquad
(\phi_1)_t(x,0,t)+g\eta_1+\frac B\rho(\eta_1)_{xxxx}=0.
$$

Dropping the subscript, the [linearized boundary condition](../fluid-mechanics.md#linearized-boundary-condition) pair is

$$
\boxed{\eta_t=\phi_z,\qquad
\phi_t+g\eta+\frac B\rho\eta_{xxxx}=0\quad\text{at }z=0}.
$$

The fixed-bed condition remains $\phi_z(x,-h,t)=0$. The source's shorthand $|\eta|\ll1$ requires a length scale: for the subsequent [plane wave](../wave-function.md#plane-wave), small surface slope $k|\alpha|\ll1$ and displacement small compared with depth are sufficient conditions. Small height alone, with arbitrarily rapid spatial variation or a finite background disturbance, does not justify discarding the products.

<h4 id="3/b/iv">iv</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

<h5 id="3/b/iv/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#3/b/iv)

Let $\chi=kx-\omega t$, with $k>0$. The [surface elevation](../fluid-mechanics.md#surface-elevation) is $\eta=\alpha\cos\chi$, so $\eta_t=\alpha\omega\sin\chi$. Use [separation of variables](../partial-differential-equation.md#separation-of-variables) in the interior [Laplace equation](../partial-differential-equation.md#laplace-equation):

$$
\phi=A\cosh(k(z+h))\sin\chi.
$$

The $\sinh(k(z+h))$ term is excluded by the bed [no-penetration boundary condition](../viscous-fluid-flow.md#no-penetration-boundary-condition). The linear surface [kinematic boundary condition](../fluid-mechanics.md#kinematic-boundary-condition) determines

$$
Ak\sinh(kh)=\alpha\omega,
\qquad
\boxed{\phi(x,z,t)=\frac{\alpha\omega}{k\sinh(kh)}
\cosh(k(z+h))\sin(kx-\omega t)}.
$$

An irrelevant additive constant is omitted. At $z=0$,

$$
\phi_t=-\frac{\alpha\omega^2}{k}\coth(kh)\cos\chi,
\qquad \eta_{xxxx}=\alpha k^4\cos\chi.
$$

The linear dynamic boundary condition then reduces to

$$
-\frac{\omega^2}{k}\coth(kh)+g+\frac B\rho k^4=0.
$$

Consequently the [dispersion relation of a floating elastic plate](../elasticity-physics.md#dispersion-relation-of-a-floating-elastic-plate) with zero plate inertia is

$$
\boxed{\omega^2=\left(gk+\frac B\rho k^5\right)\tanh(kh),\qquad
\omega(k)=\sqrt{\left(gk+\frac B\rho k^5\right)\tanh(kh)}}.
$$

The negative-frequency branch gives propagation in the opposite direction. For signed [wavenumber](../wave-equation.md#wavenumber), replace $k$ by $|k|$ in the squared-frequency formula. Setting $B=0$ recovers the finite-depth [surface gravity wave](../fluid-mechanics.md#surface-gravity-wave) relation, a useful sign check.

<h3 id="3/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/c/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#3/c)

<h5 id="3/c/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#3/c/i)

In the deep-water limit $kh\gg1$, $\tanh(kh)\sim1$. Put $\beta=B/\rho>0$. The squared [phase speed](../dispersion-relation.md#phase-speed) of the [flexural-gravity wave](../elasticity-physics.md#flexural-gravity-wave) is then

$$
c_p^2\sim\frac gk+\beta k^3.
$$

It diverges as $k\to0^+$ and as $k\to\infty$ in this limiting model. Its unique stationary point satisfies

$$
-\frac g{k^2}+3\beta k^2=0,
\qquad \boxed{k_* =\left(\frac{\rho g}{3B}\right)^{1/4}}.
$$

The second derivative $2g/k^3+6\beta k$ is positive. Using $g/k_*=3\beta k_*^3$ gives the [minimum phase speed of a flexural-gravity wave](../elasticity-physics.md#minimum-phase-speed-of-a-flexural-gravity-wave):

$$
c_{\min}^2=4\beta k_*^3
=\frac4{3^{3/4}}\beta^{1/4}g^{3/4},
\qquad
\boxed{c_{\min}=\frac2{3^{3/8}}
\left(\frac{Bg^3}\rho\right)^{1/8}}.
$$

This is the deep-water minimum: applying it to a finite depth requires $k_*h\gg1$. Otherwise the $\tanh(kh)$ factor must be retained in the minimization. Within the deep-water approximation, [phase speed](../dispersion-relation.md#phase-speed) and [group velocity](../dispersion-relation.md#group-velocity) coincide at $k_*$, although the wave family remains dispersive.

<h4 id="3/c/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#3/c)

<h5 id="3/c/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#3/c/ii)

Define the dimensionless parameter

$$
\delta=\left(\frac{\rho gh^4}{B}\right)^{1/4}.
$$

The stipulated hierarchy $kh\ll\delta\ll1$ implies both $kh\ll1$ and

$$
\frac{Bk^4}{\rho g}=\left(\frac{kh}{\delta}\right)^4\ll1.
$$

Thus the finite-depth correction uses $\tanh(kh)\sim kh$, while the restoring effect of [bending stiffness](../elasticity-physics.md#bending-stiffness) is small compared with [gravity](../physics.md#gravity). The [dispersion relation](../dispersion-relation.md) becomes

$$
\omega^2
=ghk^2\left[1+\left(\frac{kh}{\delta}\right)^4\right]
\frac{\tanh(kh)}{kh}
\sim ghk^2.
$$

Hence, to leading order, these [surface gravity waves](../fluid-mechanics.md#surface-gravity-wave) are [nondispersive waves](../dispersion-relation.md#nondispersive-wave) with

$$
\boxed{\omega\sim k\sqrt{gh},\qquad c_p\sim c_g\sim c=\sqrt{gh}}.
$$

For clarity, the first corrections to the [phase speed](../dispersion-relation.md#phase-speed) are

$$
\frac{c_p}{\sqrt{gh}}
=1+\frac12\left(\frac{kh}{\delta}\right)^4
-\frac16(kh)^2
+O\!\left((kh/\delta)^8+(kh)^4+(kh/\delta)^4(kh)^2\right).
$$

They exhibit the weak residual [wave dispersion](../dispersion-relation.md#wave-dispersion) at finite [wavenumber](../wave-equation.md#wavenumber). The claimed nondispersive behavior is the asymptotic leading-order result, not exact equality for the complete relation.
