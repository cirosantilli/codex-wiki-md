## A2

↑ **Parent:** [2013](2013.md)

<h3 id="a2/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [A2](#a2)

A monic [quadratic polynomial](../polynomial.md#quadratic-polynomial) over a [field](../algebra.md#field) is irreducible exactly when it has no root in that [field](../algebra.md#field). Testing the nine choices of its two coefficients in $\mathbb F_3$ gives

$$
\boxed{x^2+1,\qquad x^2+x+2,\qquad x^2+2x+2.}
$$

For clarity, a zero constant term gives a root at zero. Among constant term one, only the zero linear coefficient avoids roots at $1,2$. Among constant term two, precisely the linear coefficients one and two avoid roots. This exhausts all choices.

Put $f=x^4+x+2$. Its values at $0,1,2$ are respectively $2,1,2$, so it has no linear factor. If this quartic were reducible, absence of a linear factor would force a factorization into two irreducible quadratics. Division by the three candidates above gives remainders respectively $x,x+1,x+1$, all nonzero. Thus **$f$ is an [irreducible polynomial](../polynomial.md#irreducible-polynomial)**.

The [quotient by an irreducible polynomial is a field](../polynomial.md#quotient-by-an-irreducible-polynomial-is-a-field) consequently gives a [field](../algebra.md#field) of $3^4=81$ elements. Its nonzero elements form a [multiplicative group of a finite field](../algebra.md#multiplicative-group-of-a-finite-field) of order $80$. In this [field](../algebra.md#field), $a^4=1+2a$. Successive squaring gives

$$
a^8=1+a+a^2,\qquad a^{16}=2+a+2a^3.
$$

The latter is not one: otherwise the nonzero [polynomial](../polynomial.md) $1+x+2x^3$ of degree below four would vanish at $a$, contradicting its [minimal polynomial](../linear-operator-theory.md#minimal-polynomial). By [Lagrange's theorem](../group-theory.md#lagrange-s-theorem), $(a^{16})^5=a^{80}=1$. Since five is prime, its order is exactly five. Hence one answer is

$$
\boxed{\zeta=2+a+2a^3.}
$$

Its other nonidentity powers are the remaining primitive fifth roots. It is unnecessary to prove that $a$ itself generates the entire multiplicative [group](../group.md).

## B2

↑ **Parent:** [2013](2013.md)

<h3 id="b2/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [B2](#b2)

<h4 id="b2/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#b2/a)

The [orbit](../dynamical-systems.md#orbit-dynamical-system) of $s$ is $O_s=\{gs:g\in G\}$, and its [stabilizer](../group-action.md#stabilizer-subgroup) is $G_s=\{g\in G:gs=s\}$. The identity fixes $s$; if $g,h$ fix it then $(gh)s=g(hs)=s$; and $gs=s$ implies $g^{-1}s=s$. Thus **$G_s$ is a [subgroup](../group.md#subgroup)**.

A [group action](../group-action.md) is [transitive](../group-action.md#transitive-group-action) when it has just one [orbit](../dynamical-systems.md#orbit-dynamical-system): for every $s,t\in S$ there is $g\in G$ with $gs=t$.

<h3 id="b2/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [B2](#b2)

<h4 id="b2/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b2/b)

On the set of left [cosets](../group-theory.md#coset) $G/H$, define $g\cdot(xH)=(gx)H$. Given $xH$ and $yH$, the element $yx^{-1}$ sends the first to the second. Thus **the [coset action](../group-action.md#coset-action) is [transitive](../group-action.md#transitive-group-action)**. In particular the [orbit](../dynamical-systems.md#orbit-dynamical-system) of $H$ is the entire [coset](../group-theory.md#coset) space, and its [stabilizer](../group-action.md#stabilizer-subgroup) is $H$.

<h3 id="b2/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [B2](#b2)

<h4 id="b2/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#b2/c)

Two [group actions](../group-action.md) on $S_1,S_2$ are equivalent if there is a [bijection](../function.md#bijection) $\phi:S_1\to S_2$ such that $\phi(gs)=g\phi(s)$ for all $g,s$. Such a bijection is [equivariant](../group-action.md#equivariant-map).

The [orbit-stabilizer theorem](../group-action.md#orbit-stabilizer-theorem) in this form says that the action on $O_s$ is equivalent to the left [coset](../group-theory.md#coset) action on $G/G_s$, by $gG_s\mapsto gs$. This map is well-defined and injective because $gs=hs$ exactly when $h^{-1}g\in G_s$, and it is onto by the definition of the [orbit](../dynamical-systems.md#orbit-dynamical-system). It is equivariant by multiplication.

Thus every transitive action is a [coset](../group-theory.md#coset) action. Two such actions $G/H$ and $G/K$ are equivalent exactly when $H,K$ are conjugate: [stabilizers](../group-action.md#stabilizer-subgroup) at different points of one [orbit](../dynamical-systems.md#orbit-dynamical-system) are conjugate, and a conjugacy explicitly supplies a coset-action equivalence. This last classification is what makes counting by [orbit](../dynamical-systems.md#orbit-dynamical-system) types possible.

<h3 id="b2/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [B2](#b2)

<h4 id="b2/d/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#b2/d)

<h5 id="b2/d/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#b2/d/i)

A [cyclic group](../group.md#cyclic-group) of order six has exactly one [subgroup](../group.md#subgroup) of each order $1,2,3,6$, and no others. Since it is abelian, these are also its four conjugacy classes of [subgroups](../group.md#subgroup). Thus there is one transitive [group action](../group-action.md) of each size $1,2,3,6$.

An action on six points is a disjoint union of its [orbits](../dynamical-systems.md#orbit-dynamical-system). Its equivalence type is therefore determined by nonnegative integers satisfying

$$
n_1+2n_2+3n_3+6n_6=6.
$$

If $n_6=1$ there is one possibility. If $n_6=0$, the possibilities $n_3=0,1,2$ allow respectively $4,2,1$ choices of $n_2$, with $n_1$ then fixed. Hence

$$
\boxed{1+4+2+1=8\text{ inequivalent actions}.}
$$

This is [counting finite group actions by transitive orbit types](../group-action.md#counting-finite-group-actions-by-transitive-orbit-types). Equivalently, a generator acts as a [permutation](../combinatorics.md#permutation) whose cycles have lengths dividing six, and these are its eight possible cycle types on six points.

<h4 id="b2/d/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#b2/d)

<h5 id="b2/d/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#b2/d/ii)

The [symmetric group](../finite-group-theory.md#symmetric-group) $S_3$ has [subgroups](../group.md#subgroup) of orders $1,2,3,6$. There is one trivial [subgroup](../group.md#subgroup), three conjugate [subgroups](../group.md#subgroup) generated by transpositions, one order-three [subgroup](../group.md#subgroup) $A_3$, and the whole [group](../group.md). These are all [subgroups](../group.md#subgroup) by [Lagrange's theorem](../group-theory.md#lagrange-s-theorem). Thus again there is one conjugacy class for each index $6,3,2,1$.

The [coset action](../group-action.md#coset-action) classification in part (c) supplies exactly one transitive [orbit](../dynamical-systems.md#orbit-dynamical-system) type of each of these sizes. The same orbit-multiplicity equation as in part (i) applies, giving

$$
\boxed{8\text{ inequivalent actions}.}
$$

The equality of the two counts comes from their identical lists of transitive [orbit](../dynamical-systems.md#orbit-dynamical-system) types, rather than from the [groups](../group.md) being isomorphic.

## C2

↑ **Parent:** [2013](2013.md)

<h3 id="c2/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [C2](#c2)

<h4 id="c2/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#c2/a)

<h5 id="c2/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#c2/a/i)

For an [odd prime](../prime-number.md#odd-prime) $p$, the [Legendre symbol](../quadratic-residue.md#legendre-symbol) is zero if $p\mid a$, one if $a$ is a nonzero [quadratic residue](../quadratic-residue.md) modulo $p$, and minus one if it is a [quadratic nonresidue](../quadratic-residue.md#quadratic-nonresidue). Thus

$$
\boxed{(a/p)\in\{0,1,-1\}.}
$$

It depends only on the congruence class of $a$ modulo $p$.

<h4 id="c2/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#c2/a)

<h5 id="c2/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#c2/a/ii)

The [first supplementary law for quadratic reciprocity](../quadratic-residue.md#first-supplementary-law-for-quadratic-reciprocity) is

$$
\boxed{(-1/p)=(-1)^{(p-1)/2}=\begin{cases}1,&p\equiv1\pmod4,\\-1,&p\equiv3\pmod4.\end{cases}}
$$

It also follows immediately from [Euler's criterion](../quadratic-residue.md#euler-s-criterion) applied to $-1$.

<h4 id="c2/a/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#c2/a)

<h5 id="c2/a/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#c2/a/iii)

The [second supplementary law for quadratic reciprocity](../quadratic-residue.md#second-supplementary-law-for-quadratic-reciprocity) is

$$
\boxed{(2/p)=(-1)^{(p^2-1)/8}=\begin{cases}1,&p\equiv1,7\pmod8,\\-1,&p\equiv3,5\pmod8.\end{cases}}
$$

The exponent is an integer because every odd square is one modulo eight.

<h4 id="c2/a/iv">iv</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#c2/a)

<h5 id="c2/a/iv/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#c2/a/iv)

[Multiplicativity of the Legendre symbol](../quadratic-residue.md#multiplicativity-of-the-legendre-symbol) gives

$$
\boxed{(ab/p)=(a/p)(b/p).}
$$

This includes the case where $p$ divides a factor, when both sides are zero.

<h4 id="c2/a/v">v</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#c2/a)

<h5 id="c2/a/v/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [V](#c2/a/v)

For distinct [odd primes](../prime-number.md#odd-prime) $p,q$, the [quadratic reciprocity](../number-theory.md#quadratic-reciprocity) law is

$$
\boxed{(p/q)(q/p)=(-1)^{\frac{p-1}{2}\frac{q-1}{2}}.}
$$

Thus the two symbols have opposite signs exactly when both primes are three modulo four; otherwise they agree. Distinctness excludes zero symbols.

<h3 id="c2/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [C2](#c2)

<h4 id="c2/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#c2/b)

Factor $a=\prod_i q_i$ and $b=\prod_j p_j$, listing prime factors with repetitions. Coprimality ensures $q_i\ne p_j$. By the definition of the [Jacobi symbol](../number-theory.md#jacobi-symbol) and [multiplicativity of the Legendre symbol](../quadratic-residue.md#multiplicativity-of-the-legendre-symbol),

$$
(a/b)(b/a)=\prod_{i,j}(q_i/p_j)(p_j/q_i)
=(-1)^{\left(\sum_i(q_i-1)/2\right)\left(\sum_j(p_j-1)/2\right)}.
$$

For odd integers $r,s$,

$$
\frac{rs-1}{2}\equiv\frac{r-1}{2}+\frac{s-1}{2}\pmod2,
$$

since the difference is $(r-1)(s-1)/2$, an even integer. Inductively the two sums in the exponent are congruent to $(a-1)/2$ and $(b-1)/2$ modulo two. Hence

$$
\boxed{(a/b)(b/a)=(-1)^{\frac{a-1}{2}\frac{b-1}{2}}.}
$$

If either number is one, the empty product convention proves the same identity. This extends the prime law to arbitrary coprime positive odd denominators.

<h3 id="c2/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [C2](#c2)

<h4 id="c2/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#c2/c)

Put $p=4272943$. Since $p\equiv7\pmod8$, the supplementary laws give $(-1/p)=-1$ and $(2/p)=1$. Therefore $(-286/p)=-(143/p)$.

Both $143$ and $p$ are three modulo four. Reciprocity for the [Jacobi symbol](../number-theory.md#jacobi-symbol), followed by the supplied remainder, gives

$$
(143/p)=-(p/143)=-(103/143).
$$

Again both denominators are three modulo four, so

$$
(103/143)=-(143/103)=-(40/103).
$$

Now $103\equiv7\pmod8$ and $40=8\cdot5$. Consequently

$$
(40/103)=(2/103)^3(5/103)=(103/5)=(3/5)=-1,
$$

because the nonzero squares modulo five are one and four. Thus $(103/143)=1$, $(143/p)=-1$, and finally

$$
\boxed{(-286/p)=1.}
$$

The congruence **is soluble**, with exactly two distinct solutions modulo this prime: a nonzero root and its negative. The computation uses a prime denominator in its final step; a positive Jacobi symbol with composite denominator would not by itself imply solubility.

## D3

↑ **Parent:** [2013](2013.md)

<h3 id="d3/solution">Solution</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [D3](#d3)

The [monotone convergence theorem](../measure-theory.md#monotone-convergence-theorem) states that if $0\leq h_n\uparrow h$ pointwise for measurable [functions](../function.md), then $h$ is measurable and $\int h=\lim_n\int h_n$, allowing infinity.

Apply it to the partial sums $S_N=\sum_{n=0}^Nf_n$. They are nonnegative, measurable, and increase pointwise to $f$. Finite additivity of the [integral](../integral.md) and monotone convergence yield

$$
\boxed{\int_{\mathbb R}f=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{n=0}^N\int_{\mathbb R}f_n
=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb R}f_n.}
$$

The assumed finite sum of [integrals](../integral.md) makes $f$ integrable. This proves the series-integration rule needed below directly from the stated theorem, without assuming an additional interchange theorem.

<h3 id="d3/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [D3](#d3)

<h4 id="d3/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#d3/a)

For $\alpha>0$, [integration by parts](../calculus.md#integration-by-parts) on $[0,R]$ gives

$$
\int_0^R xe^{-\alpha x}\,dx
=\frac1{\alpha^2}-e^{-\alpha R}\left(\frac R\alpha+\frac1{\alpha^2}\right).
$$

The exponential remainder tends to zero. Since the integrand is nonnegative, the [monotone convergence theorem](../measure-theory.md#monotone-convergence-theorem) applied to truncations identifies the improper [integral](../integral.md) with the Lebesgue [integral](../integral.md). Therefore

$$
\boxed{\int_0^\infty xe^{-\alpha x}\,dx=\alpha^{-2}.}
$$

Its finite value proves integrability as well as evaluating it.

<h3 id="d3/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [D3](#d3)

<h4 id="d3/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#d3/b)

For $x>0$, the [geometric series](../sequence-and-series.md#geometric-series) gives

$$
\frac{xe^{-\alpha x}}{1-e^{-x}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}xe^{-(n+\alpha)x}.
$$

Every summand is nonnegative and has [integral](../integral.md) $(n+\alpha)^{-2}$ by part (a). This numerical series converges by comparison with $\sum_{n\geq1}n^{-2}$. The series-integration rule proved above therefore yields

$$
\boxed{\int_0^\infty\frac{xe^{-\alpha x}}{1-e^{-x}}\,dx
=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac1{(n+\alpha)^2}<\infty.}
$$

The value at the single endpoint zero does not affect this Lebesgue [integral](../integral.md).

<h3 id="d3/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [D3](#d3)

<h4 id="d3/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#d3/c)

On $0<x<1$, the geometric expansion gives summands $h_n(x)=x^{n+\beta-1}-x^{n-\beta}$. Their [integrals](../integral.md) are

$$
\int_0^1h_n(x)\,dx
=\frac1{n+\beta}-\frac1{n+1-\beta}
=\frac{1-2\beta}{n^2+n+\beta-\beta^2}.
$$

Both powers are integrable because $0<\beta<1$. If $\beta<1/2$, each $h_n$ is nonnegative, since its first exponent is smaller. Their [integrals](../integral.md) form a convergent nonnegative series: the $n=0$ term is finite and subsequent terms are bounded by a constant times $n^{-2}$. The proved series-integration rule applies.

If $\beta>1/2$, apply precisely that rule to $-h_n$, which are nonnegative, and negate the result. At $\beta=1/2$ all summands and the integrand are zero. Thus in every case the [integral](../integral.md) is absolutely integrable and

$$
\boxed{\int_0^1\frac{x^{\beta-1}-x^{-\beta}}{1-x}\,dx
=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1-2\beta}{n^2+n+\beta-\beta^2}.}
$$

The common sign of the summands is the reason no unproved theorem for integrating arbitrary signed series is needed.

## D4

↑ **Parent:** [2013](2013.md)

<h3 id="d4/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [D4](#d4)

<h4 id="d4/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#d4/a)

<h5 id="d4/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#d4/a/i)

A [null set](../measure-theory.md#null-set) $E\subseteq\mathbb R$ has Lebesgue outer measure zero. Equivalently, for every $\epsilon>0$ it can be covered by countably many open intervals whose total length is less than $\epsilon$. This definition also applies before assuming $E$ measurable; every such set is Lebesgue measurable with measure zero.

<h4 id="d4/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#d4/a)

<h5 id="d4/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#d4/a/ii)

A property holds **[almost everywhere](../measure-theory.md#almost-everywhere)** when its exceptional set, where the property fails, is a [null set](../measure-theory.md#null-set). The exceptional set need not be empty or countable.

<h3 id="d4/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [D4](#d4)

<h4 id="d4/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#d4/b)

<h5 id="d4/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#d4/b/i)

Given $\epsilon>0$, cover $E_1$ by open intervals of total length less than $\epsilon/2$, and cover $E_2$ similarly. Combining the two countable covers gives a cover of their union of total length less than $\epsilon$. Thus **$E_1\cup E_2$ is a [null set](../measure-theory.md#null-set)**.

<h4 id="d4/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#d4/b)

<h5 id="d4/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#d4/b/ii)

For each $n\geq1$, choose a countable interval cover of $E_n$ of total length below $\epsilon/2^n$. The union of these covers is countable and covers $\bigcup_n E_n$, with total length less than $\sum_n\epsilon/2^n=\epsilon$. As $\epsilon$ is arbitrary, **a countable union of [null sets](../measure-theory.md#null-set) is null**.

<h3 id="d4/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [D4](#d4)

<h4 id="d4/c/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#d4/c)

<h5 id="d4/c/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#d4/c/i)

The relation is reflexive because the exceptional set for $f=f$ is empty, and symmetric because the exceptional sets for $f=g$ and $g=f$ agree. If $f=g$ off a [null set](../measure-theory.md#null-set) $N_1$ and $g=h$ off a [null set](../measure-theory.md#null-set) $N_2$, then $f=h$ off $N_1\cup N_2$, which is null by part (b). Hence it is transitive as well, so **equality [almost everywhere](../measure-theory.md#almost-everywhere) is an [equivalence relation](../set-theory.md#equivalence-relation)**.

<h4 id="d4/c/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#d4/c)

<h5 id="d4/c/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#d4/c/ii)

For each $n$, let $N_n$ be the [null set](../measure-theory.md#null-set) where $f_n\ne g_n$. Their union $N$ is null. At any $x\notin N$, the two [sequences](../sequence-and-series.md#sequence) agree term by term, so their [pointwise limits](../sequence-and-series.md#pointwise-limit) are equal: $f(x)=g(x)$. Therefore **$f\sim g$**. Taking the countable union first is essential: it produces one exceptional set outside which all equalities hold simultaneously.

<h3 id="d4/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [D4](#d4)

<h4 id="d4/d/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#d4/d)

<h5 id="d4/d/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#d4/d/i)

Exactly those integrable [functions](../function.md) that vanish [almost everywhere](../measure-theory.md#almost-everywhere):

$$
\boxed{\|f\|_1=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad f=0\text{ almost everywhere}.}
$$

Thus this expression is a seminorm on actual [functions](../function.md), becoming a norm after the quotient in part (ii).

<h4 id="d4/d/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#d4/d)

<h5 id="d4/d/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#d4/d/ii)

Let $N$ be the [vector](../vector-space.md#vector) subspace of integrable [functions](../function.md) that vanish [almost everywhere](../measure-theory.md#almost-everywhere). The [Lebesgue space](../measure-theory.md#lp-space) is the [quotient vector space](../vector-space.md#quotient-vector-space) $L^1(\mathbb R)=\mathcal L^1(\mathbb R)/N$: its elements are equivalence classes of [functions](../function.md) equal [almost everywhere](../measure-theory.md#almost-everywhere). Addition and scalar multiplication are $[f]+[g]=[f+g]$ and $c[f]=[cf]$.

Set $\|[f]\|_1=\int|f|$. This is well-defined because replacing $f$ by an equal-almost-everywhere representative does not change the [integral](../integral.md). It is nonnegative, homogeneous, and obeys the triangle inequality by $|f+g|\leq|f|+|g|$. Part (i) says its only zero element is the zero class. Hence **it is a [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm) on $L^1$**.

<h3 id="d4/e">e</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [D4](#d4)

<h4 id="d4/e/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [E](#d4/e)

<h5 id="d4/e/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#d4/e/i)

For each integer $m\geq1$, list the $m^2$ [functions](../function.md)

$$
f_{m,j}(x)=m\,\mathbf1_{[j/m^2,(j+1)/m^2)}(x),\qquad0\leq j<m^2,
$$

in increasing $j$, and concatenate the blocks in increasing $m$. Each [function](../function.md) has $L^1$ norm $1/m$, so the resulting [sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#sequence) has norm tending to zero. Every $x\in(0,1)$ lies in exactly one interval of each block, where the [function](../function.md) takes value $m$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\|f_n\|_1\to0,\qquad\limsup_n f_n(x)=\infty\quad(0<x<1).}
$$

This [unbounded typewriter sequence with vanishing L1 norm](../measure-theory.md#unbounded-typewriter-sequence-with-vanishing-l1-norm) is a moving-spike construction: the support shrinks fast enough to beat the increasing height.

<h4 id="d4/e/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [E](#d4/e)

<h5 id="d4/e/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#d4/e/ii)

**No such [sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#sequence) exists.** By [Fatou's lemma](../measure-theory.md#fatou-s-lemma) on $(0,1)$,

$$
\int_0^1\liminf_n|g_n(x)|\,dx
\leq\liminf_n\int_0^1|g_n(x)|\,dx
\leq\liminf_n\|g_n\|_1=0.
$$

But the proposed nonnegative measurable integrand is positive at every point of $(0,1)$, so its [integral](../integral.md) cannot be zero. Explicitly, the sets where it is at least $1/k$ cover $(0,1)$; at least one has positive measure, forcing a positive [integral](../integral.md). This contradicts the displayed bound.

## E3

↑ **Parent:** [2013](2013.md)

<h3 id="e3/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [E3](#e3)

<h4 id="e3/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#e3/a)

<h5 id="e3/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#e3/a/i)

If $A$ is closed and $a_n\in A$ converges to $x\in X$, then $x$ cannot lie in the open complement: an open ball about such an $x$ would eventually contain all $a_n$ while missing $A$. Thus $x\in A$.

Conversely, suppose $A$ contains every limit of a [sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#sequence) in $A$. If $x\in\overline A$, every ball $B(x,1/n)$ meets $A$; choose $a_n$ in that intersection. Then $a_n\to x$, so $x\in A$. Hence $\overline A=A$, proving that **a subset of a [metric space](../topological-analysis.md#metric-space) is a [closed set](../topology.md#closed-set) exactly when it is sequentially closed**.

<h4 id="e3/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#e3/a)

<h5 id="e3/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#e3/a/ii)

A [metric space](../topological-analysis.md#metric-space) is **[complete](../topological-analysis.md#completeness)** when every [Cauchy sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#cauchy-sequence) in it converges to a point of that space. A [sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#sequence) is Cauchy when, for each $\epsilon>0$, there is $N$ such that $d(x_m,x_n)<\epsilon$ whenever $m,n\geq N$. The requirement that the limit lie in the space distinguishes completeness from having limits only in a larger ambient space.

<h4 id="e3/a/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#e3/a)

<h5 id="e3/a/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#e3/a/iii)

If $A$ is closed in the complete ambient [metric space](../topological-analysis.md#metric-space), any [Cauchy sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#cauchy-sequence) in $A$ converges in $X$ by completeness, and its limit belongs to $A$ by part (i). Thus $A$ is complete with its restricted metric.

Conversely, suppose $A$ is complete and a [sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#sequence) $a_n\in A$ converges in $X$ to $x$. A convergent [sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#sequence) is Cauchy, also in the restricted metric, so completeness of $A$ gives $a_n\to a$ for some $a\in A$. Uniqueness of a metric limit gives $x=a\in A$. Part (i) now makes $A$ closed. Hence

$$
\boxed{A\text{ complete}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad A\text{ closed in }X.}
$$

<h3 id="e3/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [E3](#e3)

<h4 id="e3/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#e3/b)

<h5 id="e3/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#e3/b/i)

Let $a^{(m)}=(1,1/2,\ldots,1/m,0,0,\ldots)$. Each has finite support, and in the [supremum norm](../normed-vector-space.md#supremum-norm) metric it converges to $a=(1,1/2,1/3,\ldots)$, because $d(a^{(m)},a)=1/(m+1)$.

The limit is bounded but does not have finite support. Thus the finite-support subspace is not closed in $X$, and by part (a)(iii) **it is not complete**. Equivalently, the displayed [sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#sequence) is Cauchy in that subspace but has no limit there.

<h4 id="e3/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#e3/b)

<h5 id="e3/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#e3/b/ii)

Show that the subspace $C$ of convergent real [sequences](../sequence-and-series.md#sequence) is closed in $X$. Suppose $a^{(m)}\in C$ converges uniformly in the coordinate index to $a\in X$. Given $\epsilon>0$, choose $m$ so $\sup_j|a_j-a_j^{(m)}|<\epsilon/3$. Since the fixed [sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#sequence) $a^{(m)}$ converges, it is Cauchy, so for sufficiently large $j,k$ its coordinate difference is below $\epsilon/3$. Then

$$
|a_j-a_k|\leq|a_j-a_j^{(m)}|+|a_j^{(m)}-a_k^{(m)}|+|a_k^{(m)}-a_k|<\epsilon.
$$

Thus $a$ is a [Cauchy sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#cauchy-sequence) of real numbers and converges by completeness of $\mathbb R$. Hence $a\in C$, so $C$ is closed. Part (a)(iii) proves **the convergent-sequence subspace is complete**.

## E4

↑ **Parent:** [2013](2013.md)

<h3 id="e4/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [E4](#e4)

<h4 id="e4/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#e4/a)

<h5 id="e4/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#e4/a/i)

A [topological space](../topology.md#topological-space) is **[compact](../topology.md#compact-space)** if every [open cover](../topology.md#open-cover) of it has a finite subcover. That is, if $X=\bigcup_{i\in I}U_i$ with all $U_i$ open, finitely many of those same [open sets](../topology.md#open-set) already cover $X$.

<h4 id="e4/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#e4/a)

<h5 id="e4/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#e4/a/ii)

If the intersection were empty, the open complements $X\setminus F_i$ would cover the [compact](../topology.md#compact-space) space. A finite subcover would give an empty finite intersection of the corresponding $F_i$. But the sets are nested, so any finite intersection is the set with largest index, which is nonempty. This contradiction proves

$$
\boxed{\bigcap_iF_i\ne\varnothing.}
$$

This is the nested-closed-set form of the [finite intersection property](../topology.md#finite-intersection-property) for [compact](../topology.md#compact-space) spaces.

<h4 id="e4/a/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#e4/a)

<h5 id="e4/a/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#e4/a/iii)

Suppose no $F_N$ is contained in $U$. Then $K_i=F_i\cap(X\setminus U)$ are nested nonempty closed subsets of the [compact](../topology.md#compact-space) space. Part (ii) gives a point in their intersection. Such a point lies both in $\bigcap_iF_i$ and outside $U$, contradicting the hypothesis. Thus some $F_N\subseteq U$, and nesting gives

$$
\boxed{F_i\subseteq U\quad\text{for every }i\geq N.}
$$

[Compactness](../topology.md#compact-space) upgrades containment of the limiting intersection to eventual containment of the whole nested sets.

<h3 id="e4/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [E4](#e4)

<h4 id="e4/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#e4/b)

For each $b\in B$, use the supplied [Hausdorff condition](../topology.md#hausdorff-space) fact to choose disjoint [open sets](../topology.md#open-set) $U_b,V_b$ with $A\subseteq U_b$ and $b\in V_b$. [Compactness](../topology.md#compact-space) of $B$ gives finitely many $b_1,\ldots,b_r$ whose $V_{b_j}$ cover $B$. Set

$$
\boxed{U=\bigcap_{j=1}^rU_{b_j},\qquad V=\bigcup_{j=1}^rV_{b_j}.}
$$

Both are open; $U$ contains $A$ and $V$ contains $B$. They are disjoint because membership in $U\cap V$ would put a point in some $U_{b_j}\cap V_{b_j}$. If one [compact](../topology.md#compact-space) set is empty, use the empty set and $X$ directly. Thus **disjoint [compact](../topology.md#compact-space) subspaces of a [Hausdorff space](../topology.md#hausdorff-space) admit disjoint open neighborhoods**.

<h3 id="e4/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [E4](#e4)

<h4 id="e4/c/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#e4/c)

<h5 id="e4/c/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#e4/c/i)

A [topological space](../topology.md#topological-space) is **[connected](../geometry-and-topology.md#connected-space)** if it cannot be written as the union of two disjoint nonempty open subsets. Equivalently, its only subsets that are both open and closed are the empty set and the whole space. Under this definition the empty space is [connected](../geometry-and-topology.md#connected-space).

<h4 id="e4/c/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#e4/c)

<h5 id="e4/c/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#e4/c/ii)

If $A$ is disconnected, a separation writes it as $A=A_1\cup A_2$ with $A_1,A_2$ disjoint, nonempty and open in its [subspace topology](../topology.md#subspace-topology). By definition there are ambient [open sets](../topology.md#open-set) $U,V$ with $A_1=U\cap A$ and $A_2=V\cap A$. These give exactly the four conditions stated.

Conversely, those conditions make $U\cap A,V\cap A$ two nonempty disjoint relatively [open sets](../topology.md#open-set) whose union is $A$. They are therefore a separation. This proves the equivalence. **The ambient sets may overlap outside $A$**, which is why the condition here is $U\cap V\cap A=\varnothing$.

<h4 id="e4/c/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#e4/c)

<h5 id="e4/c/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#e4/c/iii)

If $A$ is disconnected, take a separation $A=A_1\cup A_2$. Each piece is also relatively closed, being the complement of the other. They are [compact](../topology.md#compact-space) as closed subsets of the [compact](../topology.md#compact-space) space $A$.

Part (b), using the ambient Hausdorff property, gives disjoint ambient open neighborhoods of $A_1$ and $A_2$. They meet $A$, cover it, and are disjoint everywhere, as required. Conversely, such ambient [open sets](../topology.md#open-set) give a separation on intersecting with $A$, by part (ii). Hence **[compactness](../topology.md#compact-space) and the Hausdorff property permit fully disjoint ambient neighborhoods**.

<h3 id="e4/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [E4](#e4)

<h4 id="e4/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#e4/d)

Put $K=\bigcap_iF_i$. If some $F_i$ is empty, so is $K$, which is [connected](../geometry-and-topology.md#connected-space). Otherwise the [compact](../topology.md#compact-space) sets are closed in the Hausdorff ambient space, and can all be considered as nested nonempty closed subsets of the [compact](../topology.md#compact-space) space $F_0$. Part (a)(ii) gives $K\ne\varnothing$, and $K$ is [compact](../topology.md#compact-space).

Suppose $K$ were disconnected. Part (c)(iii) gives disjoint ambient [open sets](../topology.md#open-set) $U,V$ covering $K$, both meeting it. Part (a)(iii), applied within $F_0$ to $U\cup V$, gives $F_N\subseteq U\cup V$ for some $N$. Both $U$ and $V$ meet $F_N$ because they meet $K\subseteq F_N$. Their intersections with $F_N$ would therefore disconnect $F_N$, a contradiction. Thus

$$
\boxed{K\text{ is connected}.}
$$

## F2

↑ **Parent:** [2013](2013.md)

<h3 id="f2/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [F2](#f2)

<h4 id="f2/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#f2/a)

<h5 id="f2/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#f2/a/i)

The [total derivative](../calculus.md#total-derivative) of $F$ at $x$ is the [linear map](../vector-space.md#linear-map) $DF_x:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m$ such that

$$
\boxed{F(x+h)=F(x)+DF_x(h)+o(\|h\|).}
$$

The [Jacobian matrix](../multivariable-calculus.md#jacobian-matrix) is the $m\times n$ [matrix](../vector-space.md#matrix) $J_F(x)=(\partial F_i/\partial x_j)(x)$. When the total [derivative](../calculus.md#derivative) exists, this is its [matrix](../vector-space.md#matrix) in standard bases: $DF_x(h)=J_F(x)h$. Existence of partial [derivatives](../calculus.md#derivative) alone need not imply existence of the total [derivative](../calculus.md#derivative).

<h4 id="f2/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#f2/a)

<h5 id="f2/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#f2/a/ii)

The map is **[continuously differentiable](../multivariable-calculus.md#continuously-differentiable-function)** if it is differentiable at every point of $U$ and the [derivative](../calculus.md#derivative) map $x\mapsto DF_x$ is continuous. In finite dimensions this is equivalent to all first partial [derivatives](../calculus.md#derivative) of all its components existing and being continuous on $U$. The class is denoted $C^1(U,\mathbb R^m)$.

<h4 id="f2/a/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#f2/a)

<h5 id="f2/a/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#f2/a/iii)

If $F:U\subseteq\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m$ is differentiable at $x$, and $G$ is defined on a neighborhood of $F(x)$ and differentiable there, the [chain rule](../calculus.md#chain-rule) states

$$
\boxed{D(G\circ F)_x=DG_{F(x)}\circ DF_x.}
$$

In standard coordinates the same statement is

$$
\boxed{J_{G\circ F}(x)=J_G(F(x))J_F(x).}
$$

The evaluation point in the first factor is $F(x)$, and the [matrix](../vector-space.md#matrix) order follows the order in which the [linear maps](../vector-space.md#linear-map) act on increments.

<h4 id="f2/a/iv">iv</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#f2/a)

<h5 id="f2/a/iv/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#f2/a/iv)

For an increment $h$, direct expansion and the [chain rule](../calculus.md#chain-rule) give

$$
\boxed{DF_x(h)=Ah,\qquad Dg_x(h)=2x^Th,\qquad Dk_x(h)=2(Ax)^TAh=2x^TA^TAh.}
$$

For example, $\|x+h\|^2-\|x\|^2=2x^Th+\|h\|^2$, so the quadratic remainder divided by $\|h\|$ tends to zero. The corresponding [gradients](../multivariable-calculus.md#gradient) are $\nabla g(x)=2x$ and $\nabla k(x)=2A^TAx$; the [derivative](../calculus.md#derivative) of $F$ is the constant [matrix](../vector-space.md#matrix) $A$.

<h3 id="f2/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [F2](#f2)

<h4 id="f2/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#f2/b)

<h5 id="f2/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#f2/b/i)

The [Lagrange multiplier theorem](../mathematical-optimization.md#lagrange-multiplier) for one regular constraint says: if $f,k$ are $C^1$ near $x$, $f(x)=0$, $\nabla f(x)\ne0$, and $x$ is a local extremum of $k$ restricted to $f^{-1}(0)$, then

$$
\boxed{\nabla k(x)=\lambda\nabla f(x)\quad\text{for some }\lambda\in\mathbb R.}
$$

For several constraints with linearly independent [gradients](../multivariable-calculus.md#gradient), the objective [gradient](../multivariable-calculus.md#gradient) lies in their linear span. The regularity assumption rules out singular points where the usual multiplier conclusion can fail.

<h4 id="f2/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#f2/b)

<h5 id="f2/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#f2/b/ii)

On the constraint set, $\|x\|=1$, so $\nabla f(x)=2x\ne0$. Part (a)(iv) and the [Lagrange multiplier theorem](../mathematical-optimization.md#lagrange-multiplier) give $2A^TAx=2\lambda x$, hence

$$
\boxed{A^TAx=\lambda x.}
$$

Since $x$ is nonzero, it is an [eigenvector](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvector). Moreover $\lambda=x^TA^TAx=\|Ax\|^2\geq0$, so the associated [eigenvalue](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) is real and nonnegative.

<h4 id="f2/b/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#f2/b)

<h5 id="f2/b/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#f2/b/iii)

For $n\geq1$, the unit sphere is nonempty and [compact](../topology.md#compact-space) by the [Heine-Borel theorem](../topology.md#heine-borel-theorem). The [function](../function.md) $k(x)=\|Ax\|^2$ is continuous and therefore attains a minimum and a maximum on it by the [extreme value theorem](../real-analysis.md#extreme-value-theorem). Either point supplies a nonzero [eigenvector](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvector) in part (ii), with a real multiplier. Thus **$A^TA$ always has a real [eigenvalue](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue)**, indeed a nonnegative one. This gives existence without assuming the spectral theorem first.

## J2

↑ **Parent:** [2013](2013.md)

<h3 id="j2/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [J2](#j2)

<h4 id="j2/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#j2/a)

Set $r_0=q\mathcal E/(m\omega^2)$ and $y=r-r_0$. Completing the square in the [potential energy](../energy.md#potential-energy) gives

$$
\frac12m\omega^2|r|^2-q\mathcal E\cdot r
=\frac12m\omega^2|y|^2-\frac{q^2|\mathcal E|^2}{2m\omega^2}.
$$

Translation leaves the [Laplacian](../multivariable-calculus.md#laplacian) unchanged, and the shifted three-dimensional [quantum harmonic oscillator](../quantum-harmonic-oscillator.md) separates into three one-dimensional oscillators. Their energies add, giving

$$
\boxed{E_N=(N+\tfrac32)\hbar\omega-\frac{q^2|\mathcal E|^2}{2m\omega^2},\qquad N=n_1+n_2+n_3.}
$$

The [energy-level degeneracy](../quantum-mechanics.md#energy-level-degeneracy) is the dimension of the energy eigenspace, or number of linearly independent states with that energy. Counting nonnegative integer triples of total $N$ by [stars and bars](../combinatorics.md#stars-and-bars-combinatorics) gives

$$
\boxed{d_N=\binom{N+2}{2}=\frac{(N+1)(N+2)}2.}
$$

The constant electric [field](../algebra.md#field) displaces the oscillator and shifts all levels by the same amount, preserving their degeneracies.

<h3 id="j2/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [J2](#j2)

<h4 id="j2/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#j2/b)

Put $\gamma=m\omega/\hbar$. Substitution of $\psi=F(y)e^{-\gamma y^2/2}$ in the one-dimensional [Schrödinger equation](../mathematical-physics.md#schrodinger-equation) with energy $(n+1/2)\hbar\omega$ gives

$$
F''-2\gamma yF'+2\gamma nF=0.
$$

For degrees zero, one and two, coefficient comparison yields, up to independent nonzero constants,

$$
\boxed{F_0=1,\qquad F_1=y,\qquad F_2=y^2-\frac1{2\gamma}.}
$$

For the shifted coordinates $y_i=x_i-(r_0)_i$, let $G(y)=e^{-\gamma|y|^2/2}$. A basis of [wave functions](../wave-function.md) for the first three energy levels is:

- $N=0$: $\boxed{G}$.
- $N=1$: $\boxed{y_1G,\ y_2G,\ y_3G}$.
- $N=2$: $\boxed{(y_i^2-1/(2\gamma))G\ (i=1,2,3),\quad y_1y_2G,\ y_1y_3G,\ y_2y_3G}$.

These are products of the one-dimensional [polynomial](../polynomial.md) solutions. Arbitrary linear combinations within each listed eigenspace are also stationary states. Their counts $1,3,6$ agree with part (a), so the degeneracies have all been included.

<h3 id="j2/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [J2](#j2)

<h4 id="j2/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#j2/c)

The [Gaussian integral](../integral.md#gaussian-integral) gives $\int_{\mathbb R^3}e^{-\gamma|y|^2}\,d^3y=(\pi/\gamma)^{3/2}$. Thus the normalized [ground state](../quantum-mechanics.md#ground-state) is, up to an overall phase,

$$
\boxed{\psi_0(r)=\left(\frac{m\omega}{\pi\hbar}\right)^{3/4}
\exp\left[-\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}\left|r-\frac{q\mathcal E}{m\omega^2}\right|^2\right].}
$$

Its [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability) density is even in each shifted coordinate, so all three expectations $\langle y_i\rangle$ vanish by oddness. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\langle r\rangle=\frac{q\mathcal E}{m\omega^2}.}
$$

The mean position is the minimum of the displaced potential, rather than the original coordinate origin.

## N2

↑ **Parent:** [2013](2013.md)

<h3 id="n2/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [N2](#n2)

<h4 id="n2/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#n2/a)

The [probability generating function](../probability-distribution.md#probability-generating-function) is

$$
\boxed{G_X(s)=\mathbb E[s^X]=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}p_ks^k,}
$$

where the series is defined, with the usual power-series convention $s^0=1$ even at $s=0$. In particular $G_X(0)=p_0$ and $G_X(1)=1$.

<h3 id="n2/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [N2](#n2)

<h4 id="n2/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#n2/b)

If $|s|\leq1$, then $\sum_k|p_ks^k|\leq\sum_kp_k=1$. Thus the power series converges absolutely at every such real $s$, including both endpoints. Therefore

$$
\boxed{[-1,1]\subseteq S.}
$$

The radius of convergence is at least one, though it can be larger for particular distributions.

<h3 id="n2/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [N2](#n2)

<h4 id="n2/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#n2/c)

Inside its radius of convergence a power series can be differentiated term by term. Hence

$$
\boxed{p_k=\frac{G_X^{(k)}(0)}{k!}\qquad(k\geq0).}
$$

Every mass is therefore recovered uniquely from the [function](../function.md), proving that the generating [function](../function.md) determines the entire distribution. No [derivatives](../calculus.md#derivative) at the potentially delicate endpoint one are needed.

<h3 id="n2/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [N2](#n2)

<h4 id="n2/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#n2/d)

For the [Poisson distribution](../discrete-probability-distribution.md#poisson-distribution), summing the exponential series gives

$$
\boxed{G_X(s)=e^{-\lambda}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\lambda s)^k}{k!}
=e^{\lambda(s-1)}.}
$$

The series is absolutely convergent for every real or complex $s$; its [derivative](../calculus.md#derivative) at one is $\lambda$, the expected offspring count.

<h3 id="n2/e">e</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [N2](#n2)

<h4 id="n2/e/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [E](#n2/e)

<h5 id="n2/e/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#n2/e/i)

Count the original infected person first. Conditional on their producing $X=k$ new infections, each of the $k$ new people initiates an independent descendant family with the same law as the original total. If $T_i$ denotes that family size, the branching property gives

$$
\boxed{T\overset d=1+\sum_{i=1}^{X}T_i.}
$$

The family variables are mutually independent and independent of $X$. At $X=0$ the sum is empty and equals zero, so the total is one. This is the total progeny decomposition of a [Galton-Watson process](../galton-watson-process.md).

<h4 id="n2/e/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [E](#n2/e)

<h5 id="n2/e/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#n2/e/ii)

For $|s|\leq1$, all the generating-function expectations are absolutely bounded. Conditional on $X=k$, independence gives

$$
\mathbb E[s^T\mid X=k]=s\prod_{i=1}^k\mathbb E[s^{T_i}]=sG_T(s)^k.
$$

At $k=0$ the product is one, consistent with the empty-family case. The [law of total expectation](../measure-theory.md#law-of-total-expectation) now yields

$$
\boxed{G_T(s)=s\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}p_kG_T(s)^k
=sG_X(G_T(s))=s\exp\{\lambda(G_T(s)-1)\}.}
$$

This is the [total progeny generating function of a branching process](../galton-watson-process.md#total-progeny-generating-function-of-a-branching-process). The given almost-sure finiteness of $T$ ensures $G_T(1)=1$. The conditional-expectation proof avoids assuming a finite expected total before it has been established.

<h4 id="n2/e/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [E](#n2/e)

<h5 id="n2/e/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#n2/e/iii)

Write $g(s)=G_T(s)$. For $0<s<1$, differentiate the power-series identity from part (ii):

$$
g'(s)=e^{\lambda(g(s)-1)}+s\lambda e^{\lambda(g(s)-1)}g'(s).
$$

Because $g(s)\leq1$ and $\lambda<1$, its denominator is positive. Using $g(s)=s e^{\lambda(g(s)-1)}$ gives

$$
g'(s)=\frac{g(s)/s}{1-\lambda g(s)}.
$$

As $s\uparrow1$, almost-sure finiteness and bounded convergence give $g(s)\to1$. On the other hand, monotone convergence in $g'(s)=\sum_{k\geq1}k\mathbb P(T=k)s^{k-1}$ gives $g'(s)\to\mathbb ET$, initially allowing infinity. The displayed formula makes this limit finite and proves

$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[T]=\frac1{1-\lambda}.}
$$

This establishes finiteness rather than assuming it when rearranging an expectation equation. It is the subcritical branching-process total-progeny mean.
