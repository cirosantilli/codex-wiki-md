<h4 id="1/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1/a)

<h5 id="1/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#1/a/i)

Writing $\mathcal L$ for the collection of [Lebesgue measurable sets](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-measurable-set), saying that $\mathcal L$ is a [sigma-algebra](../measure-theory.md#sigma-algebra) means that $\mathbb R\in\mathcal L$, that $\mathbb R\setminus A\in\mathcal L$ whenever $A\in\mathcal L$, and that $\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_n\in\mathcal L$ whenever every $A_n\in\mathcal L$. These properties also imply closure under countable intersections and differences.

A real-valued [function](../function.md) $f$ is a Lebesgue [measurable function](../measure-theory.md#measurable-function) precisely when the [inverse image](../set-theory.md#preimage) of every [Borel set](../measure-theory.md#borel-set) is in $\mathcal L$. Equivalently, it suffices to test the half-lines:

$$
\boxed{\{x:f(x)>a\}\in\mathcal L\quad\text{for every }a\in\mathbb R.}
$$

The equivalence follows because these half-lines generate the [Borel sigma-algebra](../measure-theory.md#borel-sigma-algebra), and [inverse images](../set-theory.md#preimage) preserve complements and countable unions.

<h4 id="1/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1/a)

<h5 id="1/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#1/a/ii)

The [density of the rational numbers](../number-theory.md#density-of-the-rational-numbers) gives a countable way to separate two unequal values:

$$
\boxed{\{f<g\}=\bigcup_{q\in\mathbb Q}\bigl(\{f<q\}\cap\{g>q\}\bigr).}
$$

Indeed, $f(x)<g(x)$ holds exactly when some rational number lies strictly between them. Every set inside the union is a [Lebesgue measurable set](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-measurable-set), by the definition of a [measurable function](../measure-theory.md#measurable-function) and the closure properties of a [sigma-algebra](../measure-theory.md#sigma-algebra). Since $\mathbb Q$ is countable, the union is measurable.

<h3 id="1/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

<h5 id="1/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#1/b/i)

First suppose $m(E)<\infty$. The interval-cover definition of [Lebesgue outer measure](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-outer-measure), together with $m^*(E)=m(E)$, provides bounded open intervals $I_j$ covering $E$ such that $\sum_j|I_j|<m(E)+\varepsilon$. If the definition uses intervals that are not open, enlarge each by an amount whose total is smaller than the available error. For the [open set](../topology.md#open-set) $U=\bigcup_j I_j$, [countable subadditivity](../measure.md#countable-subadditivity-of-a-measure) and additivity of [Lebesgue measure](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-measure) give

$$
m(U\setminus E)=m(U)-m(E)\leq\sum_j|I_j|-m(E)<\varepsilon.
$$

This subtraction is valid because both measures are finite.

For arbitrary $E$, including $m(E)=\infty$, set $E_n=E\cap[n,n+1)$ for $n\in\mathbb Z$, and choose positive weights $w_n=2^{-|n|}/3$, whose sum is one. Each $E_n$ has finite [Lebesgue measure](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-measure), so the preceding argument gives an [open set](../topology.md#open-set) $U_n\supseteq E_n$ with $m(U_n\setminus E_n)<\varepsilon w_n$. Then $U=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb Z}U_n$ is open, contains $E$, and

$$
\boxed{m(U\setminus E)\leq\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}m(U_n\setminus E_n)<\varepsilon.}
$$

Thus the required form of [regularity of Lebesgue measure](../measure-theory.md#regularity-of-lebesgue-measure) does not require $E$ itself to have finite measure.

<h4 id="1/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

<h5 id="1/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#1/b/ii)

The exact criterion is

$$
\boxed{m(E)<\infty.}
$$

Necessity follows already from one approximation with error less than one: a [compact set](../topology.md#compact-space) $K\subseteq\mathbb R$ is bounded, hence has finite [Lebesgue measure](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-measure), so $m(E)=m(K)+m(E\setminus K)<\infty$.

Conversely, suppose $m(E)<\infty$. By [continuity from above of a measure](../measure.md#continuity-from-above-of-a-measure), the decreasing sets $E\setminus[-N,N]$ have measures tending to zero. Choose $N\geq1$ so that $m(E\setminus[-N,N])<\varepsilon/2$. Put $B=[-N,N]\setminus E$. By part (b)(i), there is an [open set](../topology.md#open-set) $V\supseteq B$ with $m(V\setminus B)<\varepsilon/2$. Define

$$
K=[-N,N]\setminus V.
$$

It is closed and bounded, hence a [compact set](../topology.md#compact-space) by the [Heine-Borel theorem](../topology.md#heine-borel-theorem), and $K\subseteq E$. The discarded portion satisfies

$$
m(E\setminus K)\leq m(E\setminus[-N,N])+m(E\cap[-N,N]\cap V)<\varepsilon.
$$

The second term is at most $m(V\setminus B)$. This proves the claimed [inner regularity of Lebesgue measure](../measure-theory.md#inner-regularity-of-lebesgue-measure) with an arbitrarily small absolute error.

<h4 id="1/c/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#1/c)

<h5 id="1/c/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#1/c/i)

**False.** Take $E=\{0\}$ and any [non-measurable set](../measure-theory.md#non-measurable-set) $A\subseteq\mathbb R$. The vertical line $\{0\}\times\mathbb R$ is a planar [Lebesgue null set](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-null-set): each segment $\{0\}\times[-N,N]$ can be enclosed in rectangles of arbitrarily small area, and the line is a countable union of these segments. Consequently its [subset](../set.md#subset) $E\times A$ is a [Lebesgue measurable set](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-measurable-set) of planar measure zero, by [completeness of Lebesgue measure](../measure-theory.md#completeness-of-lebesgue-measure).

A nonempty factor can therefore have too little [Lebesgue measure](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-measure) to transmit the other factor's failure of measurability. Its exceptional vertical section at $x=0$ is consistent with [Lebesgue measurable sections](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-measurable-sections) being guaranteed only [almost everywhere](../measure-theory.md#almost-everywhere).

<h4 id="1/c/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#1/c)

<h5 id="1/c/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#1/c/ii)

**True.** Suppose instead that $B=A_1\times A_2$ were a [Lebesgue measurable set](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-measurable-set) in the plane. The section consequence of [Fubini's theorem](../measure-theory.md#fubini-s-theorem) says that there is a [Lebesgue null set](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-null-set) $N\subseteq\mathbb R$ such that

$$
B_x=\{y:(x,y)\in B\}
$$

is a [Lebesgue measurable set](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-measurable-set) for every $x\notin N$. This version applies to completed [Lebesgue measure](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-measure); it can be obtained on bounded rectangles and then extended by a countable exhaustion, so no finite-measure assumption on $B$ is needed.

For every $x\in A_1$ the section is exactly $A_2$, which is not measurable. Thus $A_1\subseteq N$. By [completeness of Lebesgue measure](../measure-theory.md#completeness-of-lebesgue-measure), every subset of $N$ is measurable, contradicting the hypothesis on $A_1$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{A_1\times A_2\text{ is not Lebesgue measurable}.}
$$

<h3 id="1/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#1/d)

This requires an argument using [Lebesgue outer measure](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-outer-measure), since $E$ need not be a [Lebesgue measurable set](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-measurable-set). Some bounded portion $F=E\cap[-N,N]$ has $a=m^*(F)>0$: otherwise [countable subadditivity](../measure.md#countable-subadditivity-of-a-measure) would give $m^*(E)=0$. Also $a\leq2N<\infty$.

Suppose, for a contradiction, that every nonempty bounded open [interval](../real-analysis.md#interval-mathematics) $I$ satisfies $m^*(E\cap I)\leq\lambda|I|$. Because $a/\lambda>a$, the definition of [Lebesgue outer measure](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-outer-measure) gives a countable cover $F\subseteq\bigcup_j I_j$ by nonempty bounded open intervals with

$$
\sum_j|I_j|<\frac a\lambda.
$$

Using [countable subadditivity](../measure.md#countable-subadditivity-of-a-measure) again,

$$
a=m^*(F)\leq\sum_jm^*(F\cap I_j)\leq\sum_jm^*(E\cap I_j)\leq\lambda\sum_j|I_j|<a,
$$

a contradiction. Hence at least one interval in such a cover satisfies

$$
\boxed{m^*(E\cap I)>\lambda|I|.}
$$

This [high-density interval for outer measure](../measure-theory.md#high-density-interval-for-outer-measure) applies even to nonmeasurable sets; the argument does not require the [Lebesgue density theorem](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-s-density-theorem).

<h5 id="2/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#2/a/i)

The [function](../function.md) is continuous on $(0,\infty)$, hence a [measurable function](../measure-theory.md#measurable-function). On $(0,1)$ its absolute value is at most one, while on $[1,\infty)$ the elementary estimate $|\sin u|\leq|u|$ gives $|\sin(1/x^3)|\leq x^{-3}$. By comparison for the [Lebesgue integral](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-integral),

$$
\boxed{\int_0^\infty|\sin(1/x^3)|\,dx\leq1+\int_1^\infty x^{-3}\,dx=\frac32<\infty.}
$$

It is therefore a [Lebesgue integrable function](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-integrable-function). The oscillations near zero do not obstruct absolute integrability because their amplitude stays bounded on an interval of finite measure.

<h4 id="2/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#2/a)

<h5 id="2/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#2/a/ii)

Again the [function](../function.md) is continuous on its domain and therefore a [measurable function](../measure-theory.md#measurable-function). For $0<x<1$, $|(\log x)e^{-x}|\leq-\log x$, and

$$
\int_0^1-\log x\,dx=1.
$$

This standard [improper integral](../real-analysis.md#improper-integral) follows from the [antiderivative](../integral.md#antiderivative) $x-x\log x$ and $x\log x\to0$ at zero. For $x\geq1$, $0\leq\log x\leq x$, and [integration by parts](../calculus.md#integration-by-parts) gives $\int_1^\infty xe^{-x}\,dx=2/e$. Thus

$$
\boxed{\int_0^\infty|(\log x)e^{-x}|\,dx\leq1+\frac2e<\infty.}
$$

The logarithmic singularity is integrable and the exponential factor controls the tail, so this too is a [Lebesgue integrable function](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-integrable-function).

<h3 id="2/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

<h5 id="2/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#2/b/i)

Let $J=\int_0^\infty e^{-x^2}\,dx$. It is positive and finite: on $(0,1)$ the integrand is at most one, and on $[1,\infty)$ it is at most $e^{-x}$. Since the planar integrand is nonnegative and measurable, [Tonelli's theorem](../measure-theory.md#tonelli-theorem) gives

$$
J^2=\int_{(0,\infty)^2}e^{-(x^2+y^2)}\,dx\,dy.
$$

The [change of variables formula](../multivariable-calculus.md#change-of-variables-formula) in [polar coordinates](../multivariable-calculus.md#polar-coordinates), with $x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$ and absolute [Jacobian determinant](../multivariable-calculus.md#jacobian-determinant) $r$, yields

$$
J^2=\int_0^{\pi/2}\int_0^\infty e^{-r^2}r\,dr\,d\theta
=\frac\pi2\cdot\frac12=\frac\pi4.
$$

One can apply the formula first on $\delta<r<R$, $0<\theta<\pi/2$, where the coordinate map is a [diffeomorphism](../geometry-and-topology.md#diffeomorphism), and then exhaust the quadrant using the [monotone convergence theorem](../measure-theory.md#monotone-convergence-theorem). Taking the positive square root proves the half-line [Gaussian integral](../integral.md#gaussian-integral):

$$
\boxed{J=\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2}.}
$$

Here $dx$ is understood in the one-dimensional integral; it is missing from the printed question.

<h4 id="2/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

<h5 id="2/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#2/b/ii)

Put $h(t,x)=e^{-x^2-t^2/x^2}$. For every $t\geq0$, $0<h(t,x)\leq e^{-x^2}$, and the upper bound is a [Lebesgue integrable function](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-integrable-function) by part (b)(i). Since $h(t,x)\to e^{-x^2}$ for each $x>0$ as $t\downarrow0$, the [dominated convergence theorem](../measure-theory.md#dominated-convergence-theorem) gives right [continuity](../calculus.md#continuous-function) at zero and

$$
F(0)=\lim_{t\downarrow0}F(t)=\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2}.
$$

To apply [dominated differentiation under the integral sign](../analysis.md#dominated-differentiation-under-the-integral-sign), fix $t_0>0$ and restrict $t$ to $[t_0/2,3t_0/2]$. Then

$$
|\partial_t h(t,x)|=\frac{2t}{x^2}e^{-x^2-t^2/x^2}
\leq D(x):=3t_0x^{-2}e^{-x^2-t_0^2/(4x^2)}.
$$

For $x\geq1$, $D(x)\leq3t_0e^{-x^2}$. For $0<x<1$, writing $a=t_0^2/4>0$, the [substitution rule](../integral.md#integration-by-substitution) $u=1/x$ gives

$$
\int_0^1x^{-2}e^{-a/x^2}\,dx=\int_1^\infty e^{-au^2}\,du<\infty.
$$

Thus $D$ is integrable. The [mean value theorem](../calculus.md#mean-value-theorem) bounds nearby difference quotients by $D$, so the [dominated convergence theorem](../measure-theory.md#dominated-convergence-theorem) justifies the derivative at $t_0$. As $t_0$ was arbitrary,

$$
F'(t)=-2t\int_0^\infty x^{-2}e^{-x^2-t^2/x^2}\,dx,\qquad t>0.
$$

Now make the [change of variables](../multivariable-calculus.md#change-of-variables-formula) $u=t/x$. The reversal of limits cancels the negative differential and gives

$$
\int_0^\infty x^{-2}e^{-x^2-t^2/x^2}\,dx
=\frac1t\int_0^\infty e^{-u^2-t^2/u^2}\,du=\frac{F(t)}t.
$$

Consequently $F'(t)=-2F(t)$. This also follows in sign from the fact that the positive integrand decreases strictly as $t$ increases: the printed equation with a positive sign cannot hold. Solving the [linear differential equation](../differential-equation.md#linear-differential-equation), or differentiating $e^{2t}F(t)$, and using right [continuity](../calculus.md#continuous-function) at zero gives

$$
\boxed{F'(t)=-2F(t)\ (t>0),\qquad F(t)=\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2}e^{-2t}\ (t\geq0).}
$$

This is a half-line instance of the [Gaussian integral with an inverse-square term](../integral.md#gaussian-integral-with-an-inverse-square-term).

↑ **Parent:** [2023](2023.md)

<h3 id="3/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

<h5 id="3/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#3/a/i)

[Minkowski inequality](../real-analysis.md#minkowski-inequality) states that on a [measure space](../measure.md#measure-space) $(X,\Sigma,\mu)$, for $1\leq p\leq\infty$ and measurable real or complex $f,g$ of finite [Lp norm](../real-analysis.md#lp-norm),

$$
\boxed{\|f+g\|_p\leq\|f\|_p+\|g\|_p.}
$$

For $p=1$, integrate the scalar [triangle inequality](../topological-analysis.md#triangle-inequality) $|f+g|\leq|f|+|g|$. For $p=\infty$, the [almost everywhere](../measure-theory.md#almost-everywhere) bounds $|f|\leq\|f\|_\infty$ and $|g|\leq\|g\|_\infty$ give the same result by taking the [essential supremum](../measure-theory.md#essential-supremum).

For $1<p<\infty$, first ensure that the left side is finite. The [convex function](../real-analysis.md#convex-function) $s\mapsto s^p$ on $[0,\infty)$ gives

$$
|f+g|^p\leq(|f|+|g|)^p\leq2^{p-1}(|f|^p+|g|^p),
$$

so $f+g$ has finite [Lp norm](../real-analysis.md#lp-norm). Set $q=p/(p-1)$ and $H=\|f+g\|_p$. Since $(p-1)q=p$, [Holder inequality](../functional-analysis.md#holder-inequality) gives

$$
H^p\leq\int_X(|f|+|g|)|f+g|^{p-1}\,d\mu
\leq(\|f\|_p+\|g\|_p)\left(\int_X|f+g|^p\,d\mu\right)^{1/q}
=(\|f\|_p+\|g\|_p)H^{p-1}.
$$

If $H=0$ the result is immediate; otherwise divide by $H^{p-1}$. This proves the [triangle inequality](../topological-analysis.md#triangle-inequality) for every claimed exponent.

<h4 id="3/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

<h5 id="3/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#3/a/ii)

Use [Lebesgue measure](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-measure) restricted to the [Lebesgue measurable set](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-measurable-set) $E$. For $1\leq p<\infty$, start with the measurable real-valued [functions](../function.md) $f$ on $E$ satisfying $\int_E|f|^p\,dm<\infty$. Define an [equivalence relation](../set-theory.md#equivalence-relation) by $f\sim g$ when $f=g$ [almost everywhere](../measure-theory.md#almost-everywhere), and let $L^p(E)$ consist of their [equivalence classes](../set-theory.md#equivalence-class). Addition and scalar multiplication are defined using representatives. The [Lp norm](../real-analysis.md#lp-norm) is

$$
\boxed{\|[f]\|_p=\left(\int_E|f|^p\,dm\right)^{1/p},\qquad1\leq p<\infty.}
$$

For $p=\infty$, use measurable [functions](../function.md) bounded [almost everywhere](../measure-theory.md#almost-everywhere), apply the same identification, and define the [L-infinity norm](../real-analysis.md#l-infinity-norm) by

$$
\boxed{\|[f]\|_\infty=\operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{x\in E}|f(x)|
=\inf\{C\geq0:m(\{x\in E:|f(x)|>C\})=0\}.}
$$

These expressions are independent of the representative. For finite $p$, zero [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm) is equivalent to $f=0$ [almost everywhere](../measure-theory.md#almost-everywhere): each set $\{|f|>1/n\}$ must have measure zero, and their union is $\{f\ne0\}$. The same implication follows from the [essential supremum](../measure-theory.md#essential-supremum) for $p=\infty$. Absolute homogeneity is immediate; [Minkowski inequality](../real-analysis.md#minkowski-inequality) proves the [triangle inequality](../topological-analysis.md#triangle-inequality) and closure under addition. Thus these are [normed vector spaces](../normed-vector-space.md). Using complex-valued representatives gives their complex versions by the same construction.

<h4 id="3/a/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

<h5 id="3/a/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#3/a/iii)

Let $(f_n)$ be a [Cauchy sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#cauchy-sequence) in the [L2 space](../measure-theory.md#l2-space-is-a-hilbert-space) $L^2(\mathbb R)$, choosing measurable representatives. There is a subsequence $(f_{n_k})$ with

$$
\|f_{n_{k+1}}-f_{n_k}\|_2\leq2^{-k}\qquad(k\geq1).
$$

Write $d_k=f_{n_{k+1}}-f_{n_k}$ and $S_N=\sum_{k=1}^N|d_k|$. Repeated [Minkowski inequality](../real-analysis.md#minkowski-inequality) gives $\|S_N\|_2\leq\sum_{k=1}^N2^{-k}\leq1$. As $S_N^2$ increases pointwise, the [monotone convergence theorem](../measure-theory.md#monotone-convergence-theorem) gives

$$
\int_{\mathbb R}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|d_k|\right)^2dm
=\lim_{N\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb R}S_N^2dm\leq1.
$$

Hence $S=\sum_k|d_k|$ is finite [almost everywhere](../measure-theory.md#almost-everywhere) and represents an [L2 function](../measure-theory.md#square-integrable-function). Define

$$
f=f_{n_1}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}d_k
$$

where the series converges, and set $f=0$ on the exceptional [Lebesgue null set](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-null-set). It is a [measurable function](../measure-theory.md#measurable-function), and $|f|\leq|f_{n_1}|+S$ [almost everywhere](../measure-theory.md#almost-everywhere), so [Minkowski inequality](../real-analysis.md#minkowski-inequality) gives $f\in L^2(\mathbb R)$.

For fixed $k$, [Fatou's lemma](../measure-theory.md#fatou-s-lemma) applied to squares of finite tails and then [Minkowski inequality](../real-analysis.md#minkowski-inequality) give

$$
\|f-f_{n_k}\|_2^2
\leq\liminf_{M\to\infty}\left\|\sum_{j=k}^{M}d_j\right\|_2^2
\leq\left(\sum_{j=k}^{\infty}2^{-j}\right)^2=2^{2-2k}.
$$

Thus the subsequence converges to $f$ in [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm). Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose a sufficiently late $n_k$ with $\|f-f_{n_k}\|_2<\varepsilon/2$ and with $\|f_n-f_{n_k}\|_2<\varepsilon/2$ for every sufficiently large $n$, using the original [Cauchy sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#cauchy-sequence) property. The [triangle inequality](../topological-analysis.md#triangle-inequality) then gives $\|f_n-f\|_2<\varepsilon$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{L^2(\mathbb R)\text{ is complete, hence a Banach space}.}
$$

This proves the needed instance of [completeness of Lp spaces](../measure-theory.md#completeness-of-lp-spaces) without assuming the result being proved.

<h3 id="3/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

<h5 id="3/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#3/b/i)

**False.** A [typewriter sequence](../measure-theory.md#typewriter-sequence) sweeps successively through finer dyadic partitions. For $k\geq0$ and $j=0,\ldots,2^k-1$, define the [indicator function](../measure-theory.md#indicator-function)

$$
f_{2^k+j}(x)=\mathbf1_{[j2^{-k},(j+1)2^{-k})}(x),\qquad x\in(0,1).
$$

Its [L2 norm](../real-analysis.md#l2-norm) satisfies

$$
\boxed{\|f_{2^k+j}\|_2^2=2^{-k}\longrightarrow0.}
$$

As $n$ tends to infinity so does its block index $k$, proving [norm convergence](../normed-vector-space.md#norm-convergence) to zero. However, every $x\in(0,1)$ belongs to exactly one of the half-open intervals in each block, so $f_n(x)=1$ infinitely often. For $k\geq1$ there is also an interval in each block that does not contain $x$, so $f_n(x)=0$ infinitely often. Thus there is no [pointwise convergence](../sequence-and-series.md#pointwise-convergence) at any $x\in(0,1)$, let alone convergence [almost everywhere](../measure-theory.md#almost-everywhere).

<h4 id="3/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

<h5 id="3/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#3/b/ii)

**True.** Replace the dominating representative by $|g|$ if necessary, and take the countable union of the exceptional [Lebesgue null sets](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-null-set) so that every bound holds off a single null set. Since the interval has [Lebesgue measure](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-measure) one, [Cauchy-Schwarz inequality](../probability-and-statistics.md#cauchy-schwarz-inequality) gives

$$
\int_0^1|g|^2\,dx\leq\left(\int_0^1|g|^4\,dx\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_0^1 1\,dx\right)^{1/2}<\infty.
$$

Thus $g^2$ is a [Lebesgue integrable function](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-integrable-function). The squared functions satisfy $|f_n|^2\to0$ pointwise and $|f_n|^2\leq|g|^2$ [almost everywhere](../measure-theory.md#almost-everywhere). The [dominated convergence theorem](../measure-theory.md#dominated-convergence-theorem) therefore yields

$$
\boxed{\|f_n\|_2^2=\int_0^1|f_n|^2\,dx\longrightarrow0.}
$$

This uses the [Lp inclusion on a finite measure space](../measure-theory.md#lp-inclusion-on-a-finite-measure-space) to turn the assumed fourth-power bound into the second-power domination actually needed.

<h3 id="3/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#3/c)

Let $M=\|f\|_\infty$, the [essential supremum](../measure-theory.md#essential-supremum) of $|f|$. If $M=0$, $f=0$ [almost everywhere](../measure-theory.md#almost-everywhere) and every [Lp norm](../real-analysis.md#lp-norm) is zero. Suppose $M>0$. Since $|f|\leq M$ [almost everywhere](../measure-theory.md#almost-everywhere) and $m((0,1))=1$,

$$
\|f\|_p\leq M\qquad(1\leq p<\infty).
$$

For any $a$ with $0<a<M$, the [measurable set](../measure-theory.md#measurable-set) $A_a=\{x\in(0,1):|f(x)|>a\}$ has positive [Lebesgue measure](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-measure); otherwise $a$ would be an essential upper bound. Hence

$$
\|f\|_p^p\geq\int_{A_a}|f|^p\,dx\geq a^pm(A_a),\qquad
\|f\|_p\geq a\,m(A_a)^{1/p}.
$$

For fixed $a$, $m(A_a)^{1/p}\to1$. Thus $\liminf_{p\to\infty}\|f\|_p\geq a$. Letting $a\uparrow M$ and combining with the upper bound proves

$$
\boxed{\lim_{p\to\infty}\|f\|_p=\|f\|_\infty.}
$$

The positive-measure superlevel sets explain why the [Lp norms converge to the supremum norm](../measure-theory.md#lp-norms-converge-to-the-supremum-norm) even when the essential maximum is never attained.
