## 1

↑ **Parent:** [2014](2014.md)

<h3 id="1/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1/a)

The [monotone convergence theorem](../measure-theory.md#monotone-convergence-theorem) states: on a [measure space](../measure.md#measure-space) $(X,\mathcal F,\mu)$, let $u_n:X\to[0,\infty]$ be [measurable functions](../measure-theory.md#measurable-function) with

$$
0\leq u_1(x)\leq u_2(x)\leq\cdots,\qquad u(x)=\lim_{n\to\infty}u_n(x).
$$

Then $u$ is measurable and

$$
\boxed{\int_Xu\,d\mu=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_Xu_n\,d\mu.}
$$

The [integrals](../integral.md) and their [limit](../calculus.md#limit-of-a-function) may be infinite. No integrable dominator, [finite measure](../measure.md#finite-measure) hypothesis, or [sigma-finiteness](../measure.md#sigma-finite-measure) is required. The nonnegativity and increasing order are the essential hypotheses in this form of the theorem.

<h3 id="1/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

Let $S_N(x)=\sum_{n=1}^N|f_n(x)|$. These are nonnegative [measurable functions](../measure-theory.md#measurable-function) increasing to the extended-valued [function](../function.md) $S(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|f_n(x)|$. The [monotone convergence theorem](../measure-theory.md#monotone-convergence-theorem) gives

$$
\int_ES(x)\,dx=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{n=1}^N\int_E|f_n(x)|\,dx
=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\|f_n\|_1<\infty.
$$

Therefore $S$ is finite [almost everywhere](../measure-theory.md#almost-everywhere): if $S=\infty$ on a [measurable set](../measure-theory.md#measurable-set) of positive [Lebesgue measure](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-measure), its [integral](../integral.md) would exceed every finite bound. Hence the [series](../sequence-and-series.md#series-mathematics) $\sum_nf_n(x)$ converges absolutely outside the [null set](../measure-theory.md#null-set) $N=\{S=\infty\}$. This is [summable L1 norms imply almost-everywhere absolute convergence](../measure-theory.md#summable-l1-norms-imply-almost-everywhere-absolute-convergence).

Define $s(x)=\sum_nf_n(x)$ off $N$ and set $s=0$ on $N$. The [function](../function.md) is [Lebesgue measurable](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-measurable-set), being the pointwise [limit](../calculus.md#limit-of-a-function) of measurable partial sums off a measurable [null set](../measure-theory.md#null-set). Since $|s|\leq S$ [almost everywhere](../measure-theory.md#almost-everywhere),

$$
\|s\|_1\leq\int_ES<\infty,
$$

so **the sum is [Lebesgue integrable](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-integrable-function)**.

For $s_N=\sum_{n=1}^Nf_n$, the absolute tail satisfies

$$
|s-s_N|\leq\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}|f_n|\qquad\text{almost everywhere}.
$$

Apply the [monotone convergence theorem](../measure-theory.md#monotone-convergence-theorem) to the nonnegative tail partial sums. We obtain

$$
\|s-s_N\|_1\leq\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}\|f_n\|_1\longrightarrow0.
$$

Thus the partial sums converge in [L1 norm](../normed-vector-space.md#l1-norm). The [triangle inequality for an integral](../topological-analysis.md#integral-triangle-inequality) yields

$$
\left|\int_Es-\sum_{n=1}^N\int_Ef_n\right|\leq\|s-s_N\|_1\longrightarrow0.
$$

Consequently

$$
\boxed{\int_E\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}f_n(x)\,dx=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_Ef_n(x)\,dx.}
$$

The scalar [series](../sequence-and-series.md#series-mathematics) on the right also converges absolutely, since $\sum_n|\int_Ef_n|\leq\sum_n\|f_n\|_1<\infty$. This proves [integration of a series with summable L1 norms](../measure-theory.md#integration-of-a-series-with-summable-l1-norms) without needing pointwise convergence on the exceptional [null set](../measure-theory.md#null-set).

<h3 id="1/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#1/c)

The [function](../function.md) is continuous for $x>0$, hence [Lebesgue measurable](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-measurable-set). On $(0,1)$, $|\sin x|\leq x$ and $|\cos x|\leq1$ give

$$
|f(x)|\leq\frac{x}{x\sqrt x}+\frac1{\sqrt x}=2x^{-1/2}.
$$

Since $\int_0^12x^{-1/2}\,dx=4$, **$f$ is [Lebesgue integrable](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-integrable-function) on $(0,1)$** by [power singularity integrability](../measure-theory.md#power-singularity-integrability).

On $(1,\infty)$, the first summand is [Lebesgue integrable](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-integrable-function):

$$
\int_1^\infty\frac{|\sin x|}{x^{3/2}}\,dx\leq\int_1^\infty x^{-3/2}\,dx=2.
$$

The second summand is not. To prove it, take the pairwise [disjoint](../set.md#disjoint-sets) [intervals](../real-analysis.md#interval-mathematics)

$$
I_k=\left[k\pi-\frac\pi3,\ k\pi+\frac\pi3\right],\qquad k\geq1.
$$

On $I_k$, $|\cos x|\geq1/2$, the length is $2\pi/3$, and $x\leq(k+1)\pi$. Hence

$$
\int_{I_k}\frac{|\cos x|}{\sqrt x}\,dx
\geq\frac{\pi}{3\sqrt{(k+1)\pi}}
=\frac{\sqrt\pi}{3\sqrt{k+1}}.
$$

The [p-series](../sequence-and-series.md#p-series) with exponent $1/2$ diverges, so $\int_1^\infty|\cos x|/\sqrt x\,dx=\infty$.

If $f$ were [Lebesgue integrable](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-integrable-function) on this tail, subtracting its integrable first summand would make $\cos x/\sqrt x$ integrable as well, a contradiction. Therefore

$$
\boxed{f\in L^1((0,1)),\qquad f\notin L^1((1,\infty)).}
$$

This uses the general fact that [an integrable perturbation cannot repair nonintegrability](../measure-theory.md#an-integrable-perturbation-cannot-repair-nonintegrability). The conclusion concerns [absolute integrability](../measure-theory.md#integrability); oscillatory cancellation in an [improper integral](../real-analysis.md#improper-integral) is a separate issue.

<h3 id="2/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#2/a)

The [dominated convergence theorem](../measure-theory.md#dominated-convergence-theorem) states: on a [measure space](../measure.md#measure-space) $(X,\mathcal F,\mu)$, let $u_n$ be [measurable functions](../measure-theory.md#measurable-function) converging [almost everywhere](../measure-theory.md#almost-everywhere) to a measurable $u$. Suppose there is a nonnegative [measurable function](../measure-theory.md#measurable-function) $G$ with $\int_XG\,d\mu<\infty$ and $|u_n|\leq G$ [almost everywhere](../measure-theory.md#almost-everywhere) for every $n$. Then $u$ is integrable and

$$
\boxed{\int_X|u_n-u|\,d\mu\longrightarrow0,\qquad
\int_Xu_n\,d\mu\longrightarrow\int_Xu\,d\mu.}
$$

Here integrability means integrability with respect to the given [measure](../measure.md); for [Lebesgue measure](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-measure) it is [Lebesgue integrability](../measure-theory.md#integrability). Neither a [finite measure](../measure.md#finite-measure) space nor monotonicity of the sequence is required. The same single integrable dominator must control the whole sequence.

<h3 id="2/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

<h5 id="2/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#2/b/i)

Fix $t_0\geq0$. On a compact parameter [interval](../real-analysis.md#interval-mathematics) containing all nonnegative $t$ sufficiently close to $t_0$, the corresponding rectangle with $x\in[0,1]$ is [compact](../topology.md#compact-space). The [continuous function](../calculus.md#continuous-function) $f$ is therefore bounded there by some $M<\infty$, by the [extreme value theorem](../real-analysis.md#extreme-value-theorem).

For any sequence $t_n\geq0$ tending to $t_0$, all sufficiently late $t_n$ lie in this rectangle's parameter range. For each $x\in[0,1]$, [continuity](../calculus.md#continuous-function) gives

$$
f(t_n,x)\longrightarrow f(t_0,x),\qquad |f(t_n,x)|\leq M.
$$

The constant [function](../function.md) $M$ is integrable on $[0,1]$. The [dominated convergence theorem](../measure-theory.md#dominated-convergence-theorem) therefore gives

$$
\boxed{F(t_n)=\int_0^1f(t_n,x)\,dx\longrightarrow\int_0^1f(t_0,x)\,dx=F(t_0).}
$$

The sequential criterion for [continuity](../calculus.md#continuous-function) on a real [interval](../real-analysis.md#interval-mathematics) proves **$F$ is continuous on $[0,\infty)$**. At zero this is right-continuity. This is [continuity of parameter integrals on compact rectangles](../measure-theory.md#continuity-of-parameter-integrals-on-compact-rectangles); the bound is local in the parameter, so $f$ need not be bounded on the entire unbounded strip.

<h4 id="2/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

<h5 id="2/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#2/b/ii)

We prove the required [differentiation under the integral sign](../analysis.md#differentiation-under-the-integral-sign) directly. Fix $t_0>0$, and take a compact parameter [interval](../real-analysis.md#interval-mathematics) $J\subseteq[0,\infty)$ containing $t_0$ in its interior. The jointly continuous [partial derivative](../multivariable-calculus.md#partial-derivative) $\partial_t f$ is bounded on the compact rectangle $J\times[0,1]$ by some $M_1$.

For nonzero $h$ sufficiently close to zero, define

$$
D_h(x)=\frac{f(t_0+h,x)-f(t_0,x)}h.
$$

For each $x$, the [mean value theorem](../calculus.md#mean-value-theorem) in the parameter gives $|D_h(x)|\leq M_1$. The definition of the [partial derivative](../multivariable-calculus.md#partial-derivative) also gives $D_h(x)\to\partial_t f(t_0,x)$. Since $M_1$ is integrable on $[0,1]$, [dominated convergence](../measure-theory.md#dominated-convergence-theorem) applied along any sequence $h_n\to0$ yields

$$
\lim_{h\to0}\frac{F(t_0+h)-F(t_0)}h
=\lim_{h\to0}\int_0^1D_h(x)\,dx
=\int_0^1\partial_t f(t_0,x)\,dx.
$$

Thus

$$
\boxed{F'(t)=\int_0^1\frac{\partial f(t,x)}{\partial t}\,dx\qquad(t>0).}
$$

At $t=0$, repeat the argument on $J=[0,\delta]$ with $h>0$. It proves that the [right derivative](../calculus.md#right-derivative) exists and satisfies the same formula at zero. This is the appropriate meaning of differentiability on the closed half-line. In fact $F'$ is continuous there: apply part (i) to the continuous [function](../function.md) $\partial_t f$.

The proof establishes [differentiation of parameter integrals on compact rectangles](../analysis.md#differentiation-of-parameter-integrals-on-compact-rectangles) under the exact regularity assumptions used here. In particular, it supplies the theorem's proof instead of assuming an interchange of a [limit](../calculus.md#limit-of-a-function) and an [integral](../integral.md).

<h3 id="2/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#2/c)

Remove the apparent singularity at $x=0$ by defining

$$
\phi(t,x)=\begin{cases}\sin(tx)/x,&x>0,\\t,&x=0.\end{cases}
$$

This is jointly continuous: write $\phi(t,x)=t\,\operatorname{sinc}(tx)$ using the [unnormalized sinc function](../fourier-transform.md#unnormalized-sinc-function), whose value at zero is one. Its [partial derivative](../multivariable-calculus.md#partial-derivative) in $t$ is

$$
\partial_t\phi(t,x)=\cos(tx)
$$

also at $x=0$, since there $\phi(t,0)=t$. This [partial derivative](../multivariable-calculus.md#partial-derivative) is jointly continuous on the whole strip.

The value at the single endpoint $x=0$ does not change a [Lebesgue integral](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-integral). Part (b), whose [dominated convergence](../measure-theory.md#dominated-convergence-theorem) proof applies to $\phi$, therefore makes $g$ continuously differentiable on $[0,\infty)$, with a [right derivative](../calculus.md#right-derivative) at zero, and

$$
g'(t)=\int_0^1\cos(tx)\,dx.
$$

For $t>0$, direct [integration](../integral.md) gives

$$
\boxed{g'(t)=\frac{\sin t}{t},\qquad tg'(t)=\sin t.}
$$

At zero the same integral gives $\boxed{g'_+(0)=1}$, so no division by zero is required. Also $g(0)=0$. The [substitution rule](../integral.md#integration-by-substitution) $u=tx$ identifies $g(t)=\int_0^t\sin u/u\,du$ for $t>0$, the [sine integral](../integral.md#sine-integral).

## 3

↑ **Parent:** [2014](2014.md)

<h3 id="3/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

Let $(X,\mathcal A,\mu)$ and $(Y,\mathcal B,\nu)$ be [measure spaces](../measure.md#measure-space) with [sigma-finite measures](../measure.md#sigma-finite-measure), and use their [product measure](../probability-theory.md#product-measure).

**[Fubini's theorem](../measure-theory.md#fubini-s-theorem):** if $u$ is a measurable absolutely integrable [function](../function.md) on $X\times Y$, then its sections are integrable for [almost every](../measure-theory.md#almost-everywhere) fixed value of the other coordinate, the resulting section-integral [functions](../function.md) are integrable, and

$$
\boxed{\int_{X\times Y}u\,d(\mu\times\nu)
=\int_X\left(\int_Yu(x,y)\,d\nu(y)\right)d\mu(x)
=\int_Y\left(\int_Xu(x,y)\,d\mu(x)\right)d\nu(y).}
$$

All three [integrals](../integral.md) are finite. Values of an inner [integral](../integral.md) on an exceptional [null set](../measure-theory.md#null-set) can be assigned arbitrarily.

**[Tonelli's theorem](../measure-theory.md#tonelli-theorem):** if $u:X\times Y\to[0,\infty]$ is measurable and nonnegative, the same equality holds for its nonnegative product and iterated [integrals](../integral.md), allowing the common value $+\infty$. No advance [absolute integrability](../measure-theory.md#integrability) assumption is needed. On a completed [Lebesgue measure](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-measure) product, the section and measurability assertions are interpreted [almost everywhere](../measure-theory.md#almost-everywhere).

Thus [Tonelli's theorem](../measure-theory.md#tonelli-theorem) can first establish that $\int|u|<\infty$; after this, [Fubini's theorem](../measure-theory.md#fubini-s-theorem) permits signed integration in either order.

<h3 id="3/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

Assign any value, for example zero, at $(0,0)$, where the displayed quotient is undefined. A single point is a [Lebesgue null set](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-null-set), so this choice does not affect [Lebesgue integrability](../measure-theory.md#integrability). Elsewhere the [function](../function.md) is continuous, hence it is [Lebesgue measurable](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-measurable-set) on the plane after this extension.

In [polar coordinates](../multivariable-calculus.md#polar-coordinates), $x^2+y^2=r^2$ and the area element is $r\,dr\,d\theta$. Applying the nonnegative [change of variables formula](../multivariable-calculus.md#change-of-variables-formula) to the [absolute value](../real-analysis.md#absolute-value) gives

$$
\int_{\mathbb R^2}|k(x,y)|\,dx\,dy
=2\pi\int_0^\infty|\sin(r^2)|r^{1-2\alpha}\,dr.
$$

For $0<r\leq1$, $|\sin(r^2)|\leq r^2$, so the radial [integrand](../integral.md#integrand) is bounded by $r^{3-2\alpha}$. This is integrable at zero when $\alpha<2$. For $r\geq1$, $|\sin(r^2)|\leq1$, giving the integrable tail bound $r^{1-2\alpha}$ when $\alpha>1$. In particular,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\|k\|_1
&\leq2\pi\left(\int_0^1r^{3-2\alpha}\,dr+\int_1^\infty r^{1-2\alpha}\,dr\right)\\
&=\pi\left(\frac1{2-\alpha}+\frac1{\alpha-1}\right)<\infty.
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{k\in L^1(\mathbb R^2)\qquad(1<\alpha<2).}
$$

Equivalently, the [substitution rule](../integral.md#integration-by-substitution) $u=r^2$ reduces its exact [L1 norm](../normed-vector-space.md#l1-norm) to

$$
\|k\|_1=\pi\int_0^\infty\frac{|\sin u|}{u^\alpha}\,du.
$$

This is [radial sine-power kernel integrability](../fourier-analysis.md#radial-sine-power-kernel-integrability), reducing a two-dimensional question to the [sine power kernel integrability](../real-analysis.md#sine-power-kernel-integrability) thresholds at zero and infinity.

<h3 id="3/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/c/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#3/c)

<h5 id="3/c/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#3/c/i)

Fix $0<x<1$. On the open [interval](../real-analysis.md#interval-mathematics) $(0,x)$, the [function](../function.md) $y\mapsto(x-y)^{\beta-1}$ is finite, positive and continuous, and $f$ is nonnegative and [Lebesgue measurable](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-measurable-set). Their product is therefore a nonnegative [measurable function](../measure-theory.md#measurable-function). Its [Lebesgue integral](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-integral) is always defined as an element of $[0,\infty]$, with no possibility of cancellation of two infinities. Thus **$g(x)$ is well defined as a nonnegative extended integral**.

The endpoint $y=x$ need not receive its formal power value: for $\beta<1$ that value would be singular. The integral is over $(0,x)$, and any chosen value at the endpoints has no effect because endpoints form a [null set](../measure-theory.md#null-set).

Finiteness is not implied by the present assumptions. For example, $f(y)=1/y$ is an admissible nonnegative [measurable function](../measure-theory.md#measurable-function). For any fixed $x$, the factor $(x-y)^{\beta-1}$ is bounded below by a positive constant on $0<y<x/2$, so $g(x)\geq C_x\int_0^{x/2}dy/y=\infty$. The later [L1 norm](../normed-vector-space.md#l1-norm) bound will establish finiteness [almost everywhere](../measure-theory.md#almost-everywhere) when $f$ is integrable. The construction is a [Volterra power-kernel integral](../functional-analysis.md#volterra-power-kernel-integral).

<h4 id="3/c/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#3/c)

<h5 id="3/c/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#3/c/ii)

Define the joint [function](../function.md) by an unambiguous piecewise prescription:

$$
H(x,y)=\begin{cases}(x-y)^{\beta-1}f(y),&0<y<x<1,\\0,&\text{otherwise in }(0,1)^2.\end{cases}
$$

It is nonnegative and [Lebesgue measurable](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-measurable-set): on the open triangle the power factor is continuous and $f(y)$ is measurable for the [product sigma-algebra](../probability-theory.md#product-sigma-algebra), and the zero extension is measurable. This also resolves the diagonal singularity and avoids evaluating a noninteger power of a negative number outside the triangle. The source's inclusion of $y=x$ in its indicator [set](../set.md) changes only a [null set](../measure-theory.md#null-set); set the value there to zero.

The [function](../function.md) $g(x)=\int_0^1H(x,y)\,dy$ is measurable by [Tonelli's theorem](../measure-theory.md#tonelli-theorem). Apply that theorem to exchange the nonnegative [integrals](../integral.md), with infinity allowed until the estimate proves otherwise:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^1g(x)\,dx
&=\int_0^1f(y)\left(\int_y^1(x-y)^{\beta-1}\,dx\right)dy\\
&=\frac1\beta\int_0^1(1-y)^\beta f(y)\,dy\\
&\leq\frac1\beta\int_0^1f(y)\,dy<\infty.
\end{aligned}
$$

The inner [integral](../integral.md) is $(1-y)^\beta/\beta$ because $\beta>0$. The weight is at most one because $0<y<1$. Since $g\geq0$, this proves

$$
\boxed{\|g\|_1=\frac1\beta\int_0^1(1-y)^\beta f(y)\,dy
\leq\frac1\beta\|f\|_1,\qquad g\in L^1((0,1)).}
$$

In particular $g$ is finite [almost everywhere](../measure-theory.md#almost-everywhere); any exceptional infinite values may be replaced by zero to obtain an everywhere finite representative. This is the [L1 bound for a Volterra power-kernel integral](../functional-analysis.md#l1-bound-for-a-volterra-power-kernel-integral).

An everywhere-finite conclusion would be stronger and can fail when $0<\beta<1$, even for integrable $f$. For example, put $x_0=1/2$ and $\gamma=(1+\beta)/2$, and take $f(y)=(x_0-y)^{-\gamma}$ for $0<y<x_0$ and zero otherwise. Since $\gamma<1$, $f$ is integrable. But

$$
g(x_0)=\int_0^{x_0}(x_0-y)^{\beta-1-\gamma}\,dy=\infty,
$$

because $\beta-1-\gamma=(\beta-3)/2<-1$. This does not contradict [Lebesgue integrability](../measure-theory.md#integrability), which requires finiteness only [almost everywhere](../measure-theory.md#almost-everywhere).
