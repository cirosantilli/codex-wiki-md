## 1

↑ **Parent:** [2021](2021.md)

<h3 id="1/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1/a)

Extend the integrand by zero to all of $(0,\infty)$:

$$
h_n(x)=\mathbf1_{(0,2n\pi)}(x)\frac{(n+x)\sin(x/n)}{x(1+x^2)}.
$$

For each fixed $x>0$, eventually the [indicator function](../measure-theory.md#indicator-function) is one and $\sin(x/n)/(x/n)\to1$. Hence $h_n(x)\to(1+x^2)^{-1}$ [pointwise](../sequence-and-series.md#pointwise-convergence). On its support, $x/n\leq2\pi$, so $|\sin t|\leq t$ for $t\geq0$ gives

$$
|h_n(x)|\leq\frac{1+x/n}{1+x^2}\leq\frac{1+2\pi}{1+x^2}.
$$

The final bound is [Lebesgue integrable](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-integrable-function) on $(0,\infty)$ and independent of $n$. The [dominated convergence theorem](../measure-theory.md#dominated-convergence-theorem) therefore gives

$$
\boxed{\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_0^{2n\pi}\frac{(n+x)\sin(x/n)}{x(1+x^2)}\,dx
=\int_0^\infty\frac{dx}{1+x^2}=\frac\pi2.}
$$

The last integral follows from the [antiderivative](../integral.md#antiderivative) $\arctan x$.

<h3 id="1/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

On $(0,1)$, the integrand is bounded by one. For $x\to\infty$, $\alpha x^\beta-2\log x\to\infty$, since every positive power grows faster than the [natural logarithm](../calculus.md#natural-logarithm). Thus $e^{-\alpha x^\beta}\leq x^{-2}$ eventually. The [comparison test for integrals](../integral.md#comparison-test-for-integrals) proves [Lebesgue integrability](../measure-theory.md#integrability).

To prove joint [continuity](../calculus.md#continuous-function), fix $(\alpha_0,\beta_0)$ with both coordinates positive. In a sufficiently small parameter rectangle, $\alpha\geq a:=\alpha_0/2>0$ and $\beta\geq b:=\beta_0/2>0$. For every parameter pair in this rectangle,

$$
0\leq e^{-\alpha x^\beta}\leq H(x):=\begin{cases}1,&0<x<1,\\e^{-a x^b},&x\geq1.\end{cases}
$$

The first argument shows that $H$ is [integrable](../measure-theory.md#integrability). Along any parameter [sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#sequence) tending to $(\alpha_0,\beta_0)$, the integrands converge [pointwise](../sequence-and-series.md#pointwise-convergence), and the same $H$ dominates their eventual tail. The [dominated convergence theorem](../measure-theory.md#dominated-convergence-theorem) gives $g(\alpha,\beta)\to g(\alpha_0,\beta_0)$. The [sequential criterion for continuity](../topological-analysis.md#sequential-characterization-of-continuity-in-metric-spaces) proves the required joint [continuity](../calculus.md#continuous-function) throughout the positive quadrant.

For comparison, the [change of variables](../multivariable-calculus.md#change-of-variables-formula) $u=\alpha x^\beta$ identifies the value with a [gamma function](../complex-analysis.md#gamma-function):

$$
\boxed{g(\alpha,\beta)=\frac{\Gamma(1/\beta)}{\beta\alpha^{1/\beta}}.}
$$

The domination argument establishes [continuity](../calculus.md#continuous-function) directly, without needing a separate theorem about parameter dependence of the [gamma function](../complex-analysis.md#gamma-function).

<h3 id="1/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/c/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#1/c)

<h5 id="1/c/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#1/c/i)

For each nonnegative [integer](../integer.md) $k$, the [change of variables](../multivariable-calculus.md#change-of-variables-formula) $x=e^{-t}$ gives

$$
\int_0^1|(x\log x)^k|\,dx=\int_0^\infty t^ke^{-(k+1)t}\,dt
=\frac{\Gamma(k+1)}{(k+1)^{k+1}}<\infty.
$$

The [gamma function](../complex-analysis.md#gamma-function) identity $\Gamma(k+1)=k!$ follows by repeated [integration by parts](../calculus.md#integration-by-parts). Since $\log x<0$ on $(0,1)$, the sign of the original integrand is $(-1)^k$, so

$$
\boxed{\int_0^1(x\log x)^k\,dx=\frac{(-1)^kk!}{(k+1)^{k+1}}.}
$$

For $k=0$, the integral is one, consistent with the formula.

<h4 id="1/c/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#1/c)

<h5 id="1/c/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#1/c/ii)

The [exponential series](../calculus.md#exponential-series) gives $x^x=e^{x\log x}=\sum_{k=0}^\infty(x\log x)^k/k!$. Since $|x\log x|\leq1/e$ on $(0,1)$, the [Weierstrass M-test](../probability-and-statistics.md#weierstrass-m-test) proves [uniform absolute convergence](../sequence-and-series.md#uniform-absolute-convergence), using the summable bounds $e^{-k}/k!$. Therefore one can integrate the [series](../sequence-and-series.md#series-mathematics) term by term over this finite interval. Using part [i](#1/c/i),

$$
\int_0^1x^x\,dx=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac1{k!}\int_0^1(x\log x)^k\,dx
=\boxed{\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^k}{(k+1)^{k+1}}.}
$$

This is the alternating identity in [sophomore's dream](../integral.md#sophomore-s-dream). The same estimate also proves [absolute convergence](../sequence-and-series.md#absolute-convergence) of the integrated [series](../sequence-and-series.md#series-mathematics); no cancellation is needed to justify the interchange.

## 2

↑ **Parent:** [2021](2021.md)

<h3 id="2/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#2/a)

Near zero, $\sin x/x\to1$, so $|\sin x|/x^\alpha$ is comparable to $x^{1-\alpha}$. The [power integral test](../real-analysis.md#improper-power-integral) shows that the integral near zero is finite exactly when $\alpha<2$.

For the tail, $|\sin x|\leq1$ proves integrability when $\alpha>1$. To prove necessity, take the disjoint [intervals](../real-analysis.md#interval-mathematics)

$$
I_k=[2k\pi+\pi/6,\ 2k\pi+5\pi/6],\qquad k\geq1.
$$

On each, $\sin x\geq1/2$, and $x\leq2\pi(k+1)$. Therefore $\int_{I_k}|\sin x|x^{-\alpha}\,dx\geq c_\alpha(k+1)^{-\alpha}$ with $c_\alpha>0$. The [p-series](../sequence-and-series.md#p-series) diverges when $\alpha\leq1$, proving that the absolute tail integral diverges in that range.

Combining the two conditions,

$$
\boxed{f_\alpha\text{ is Lebesgue integrable on }(0,\infty)\iff1<\alpha<2.}
$$

For $0<\alpha\leq1$, the tail [improper integral](../real-analysis.md#improper-integral) converges conditionally by the [Dirichlet test for improper integrals](../real-analysis.md#dirichlet-test-for-improper-integrals), but that does not give [Lebesgue integrability](../measure-theory.md#integrability).

<h3 id="2/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

<h5 id="2/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#2/b/i)

The absolute value is bounded by $e^{-ax}$, whose integral is $1/a$. Thus the [function](../function.md) is [Lebesgue integrable](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-integrable-function). The complex integral of an [exponential function](../calculus.md#exponential-function) is

$$
\int_0^\infty e^{(-a+ib)x}\,dx=\left[\frac{e^{(-a+ib)x}}{-a+ib}\right]_0^\infty=\frac1{a-ib}=\frac{a+ib}{a^2+b^2}.
$$

Taking the [real part](../complex-analysis.md#real-part), justified by [absolute integrability](../measure-theory.md#integrability), gives

$$
\boxed{\int_0^\infty e^{-ax}\cos(bx)\,dx=\frac a{a^2+b^2}.}
$$

<h4 id="2/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

<h5 id="2/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#2/b/ii)

Again $|e^{-ax}\sin(bx)|\leq e^{-ax}$ proves [Lebesgue integrability](../measure-theory.md#integrability). Taking the [imaginary part](../complex-analysis.md#imaginary-part) of the absolutely convergent complex integral in part [i](#2/b/i) gives

$$
\boxed{\int_0^\infty e^{-ax}\sin(bx)\,dx=\frac b{a^2+b^2}.}
$$

<h3 id="2/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/c/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#2/c)

<h5 id="2/c/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#2/c/i)

**The [function](../function.md) is not [Lebesgue integrable](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-integrable-function).** Fix $0<\eta<\pi/2$ and restrict to $x\geq1$, $0<y<\eta$. There $\cos y\geq\cos\eta>0$. [Tonelli's theorem](../measure-theory.md#tonelli-theorem) for the nonnegative absolute value gives the lower bound

$$
\begin{aligned}
\iint|g(x,y)|\,dx\,dy
&\geq\cos\eta\int_1^\infty|\cos2x|\int_0^\eta e^{-xy}\,dy\,dx\\
&=\cos\eta\int_1^\infty|\cos2x|\frac{1-e^{-\eta x}}x\,dx\\
&\geq\cos\eta(1-e^{-\eta})\int_1^\infty\frac{|\cos2x|}x\,dx.
\end{aligned}
$$

The last integral diverges. Indeed, on $[k\pi,k\pi+\pi/6]$, $|\cos2x|\geq1/2$; each such interval contributes at least a positive constant times $1/(k+1)$. The [harmonic series](../sequence-and-series.md#harmonic-series) diverges. Hence the integral of $|g|$ is infinite, regardless of possible signed cancellations. One cannot infer [Lebesgue integrability](../measure-theory.md#integrability) by formally applying [Fubini's theorem](../measure-theory.md#fubini-s-theorem) to the oscillatory integrand.

<h4 id="2/c/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#2/c)

<h5 id="2/c/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#2/c/ii)

**The [function](../function.md) is [Lebesgue integrable](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-integrable-function).** The inequalities $|\sin u|\leq\min\{u,1\}$ for $u>0$ give bounds that handle the two ends of the $x$-axis differently. For $0<x<1$,

$$
\int_0^\infty e^{-2xy}|\sin x\sin y|\,dy
\leq x\int_0^\infty e^{-2xy}\,dy=\frac12.
$$

For $x\geq1$,

$$
\int_0^\infty e^{-2xy}|\sin x\sin y|\,dy
\leq\int_0^\infty y e^{-2xy}\,dy=\frac1{4x^2}.
$$

[Tonelli's theorem](../measure-theory.md#tonelli-theorem) therefore proves

$$
\boxed{\iint_{(0,\infty)^2}e^{-2xy}|\sin x\sin y|\,dx\,dy
\leq\frac12+\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}{4x^2}=\frac34<\infty.}
$$

The factors of [sine](../geometry-and-topology.md#sine) suppress the near-axis contributions that caused divergence in part [i](#2/c/i).

## 3

↑ **Parent:** [2021](2021.md)

<h3 id="3/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

Set $C_0=[0,1]$. Inductively, replace each component interval of $C_n$ by its two closed end intervals, each of length $\ell_{n+1}:=q_{n+1}/2^{n+1}$. The current component length is $\ell_n=q_n/2^n$. Because $q_{n+1}<q_n$, the removed central gap has positive length

$$
\ell_n-2\ell_{n+1}=\frac{q_n-q_{n+1}}{2^n}.
$$

This is a [generalized Cantor set](../geometry-and-topology.md#generalized-cantor-set) construction. The three required properties are checked in parts [i](#3/a/i), [ii](#3/a/ii) and [iii](#3/a/iii).

The [sets](../set.md) are [closed](../topology.md#closed-set), [measurable](../measure-theory.md#measurability), decreasing, and contained in $[0,1]$, which has finite [Lebesgue measure](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-measure). By [continuity from above of a measure](../measure.md#continuity-from-above-of-a-measure),

$$
\boxed{m(C)=m\!\left(\bigcap_{n=0}^\infty C_n\right)=\lim_{n\to\infty}q_n.}
$$

This limit exists because $(q_n)$ is a bounded decreasing [monotone sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#monotone-sequence). To exclude a nondegenerate [interval](../real-analysis.md#interval-mathematics) $J\subseteq C$, note that its [connectedness](../geometry-and-topology.md#connected-space) forces it into a single component interval of every $C_n$: crossing two components would cross a removed gap. Its length would therefore be at most $2^{-n}$ for every $n$, which is impossible for a positive-length [interval](../real-analysis.md#interval-mathematics). Thus **$C$ contains no nondegenerate [interval](../real-analysis.md#interval-mathematics)**, even when its [Lebesgue measure](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-measure) is positive.

<h4 id="3/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

<h5 id="3/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#3/a/i)

At stage zero there is one [closed interval](../real-analysis.md#closed-real-interval). At every later stage each component is replaced by two closed end intervals separated by a positive open gap. Induction therefore gives exactly $2^n$ pairwise disjoint [closed intervals](../real-analysis.md#closed-real-interval) at stage $n$. Their common length is

$$
\boxed{\ell_n=\frac{q_n}{2^n}\leq2^{-n},}
$$

since the decreasing [sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#sequence) starts at $q_0=1$.

<h4 id="3/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

<h5 id="3/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#3/a/ii)

The $2^n$ component intervals are pairwise disjoint and each has length $q_n/2^n$. [Finite additivity](../function.md#finite-additivity-of-a-set-function) of [Lebesgue measure](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-measure) gives

$$
\boxed{m(C_n)=2^n\frac{q_n}{2^n}=q_n.}
$$

Their endpoints do not cause overlaps, because the central gaps have strictly positive length.

<h4 id="3/a/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

<h5 id="3/a/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#3/a/iii)

Each interval retained at stage $n+1$ lies inside the stage-$n$ interval from which it was cut. Taking the union over all parent components proves $\boxed{C_{n+1}\subseteq C_n}$. Consequently the intersection defining $C$ is a nested intersection of nonempty [compact sets](../topology.md#compact-space).

<h3 id="3/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

<h5 id="3/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#3/b/i)

The points belonging to infinitely many $E_n$ form their [limit superior of sets](../set-theory.md#limit-superior-of-sets):

$$
G=\bigcap_{N\geq1}\bigcup_{n\geq N}E_n.
$$

For every $N$, [countable subadditivity](../measure.md#countable-subadditivity-of-a-measure) gives

$$
0\leq m(G)\leq\sum_{n\geq N}m(E_n)\leq\sum_{n\geq N}2^{-n}=2^{1-N}\longrightarrow0.
$$

Thus $\boxed{m(G)=0}$. Outside this [null set](../measure-theory.md#null-set), each point belongs to only finitely many $E_n$, so its [indicator functions](../measure-theory.md#indicator-function) are eventually zero. Therefore $\boxed{\chi_{E_n}(x)\to0\text{ almost everywhere}}$. This is the summable-measure argument behind the first [Borel-Cantelli lemma](../probability-theory.md#borel-cantelli-lemmas); no [independence](../random-variable.md#independent-random-variables) assumption is needed.

<h4 id="3/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

<h5 id="3/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#3/b/ii)

By part [i](#3/b/i), $\chi_{E_n}|g|\to0$ [almost everywhere](../measure-theory.md#almost-everywhere), while $0\leq\chi_{E_n}|g|\leq|g|$ and $g$ is [Lebesgue integrable](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-integrable-function). The [dominated convergence theorem](../measure-theory.md#dominated-convergence-theorem) gives

$$
\boxed{\int_{E_n}|g(x)|\,dx\longrightarrow0.}
$$

To deduce [absolute continuity of the Lebesgue integral](../integral.md#absolute-continuity-of-the-lebesgue-integral), suppose the stated uniform conclusion failed for some $\varepsilon_0>0$. For each $n$ one could choose a [measurable set](../measure-theory.md#measurable-set) $A_n$ with $m(A_n)<2^{-n}$ but $\int_{A_n}|g|\geq\varepsilon_0$. Applying the result just proved to this [sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#sequence) contradicts the lower bound. Hence

$$
\boxed{\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists\delta>0:\quad m(A)<\delta\Longrightarrow\int_A|g|<\varepsilon}
$$

for every [measurable set](../measure-theory.md#measurable-set) $A$.

<h3 id="3/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#3/c)

The a.e. limit $f$ is nonnegative. For a fixed [measurable set](../measure-theory.md#measurable-set) $A$, [Fatou's lemma](../measure-theory.md#fatou-s-lemma) gives $\int_Af\leq\liminf_n\int_Af_n$. Apply the same lemma to $A^c$ and use convergence of the total integrals:

$$
\limsup_n\int_Af_n
=\int_{\mathbb R}f-\liminf_n\int_{A^c}f_n
\leq\int_{\mathbb R}f-\int_{A^c}f=\int_Af.
$$

The lower and upper bounds prove $\boxed{\int_Af_n\to\int_Af}$.

For the asserted [L1 convergence](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#convergence-in-l1), choose an [integer](../integer.md) $k$ so large that $\int_{|x|>k}f<\varepsilon$, possible by [Lebesgue integrability](../measure-theory.md#integrability). The first result applied to this fixed tail [set](../set.md) makes $\int_{|x|>k}f_n<2\varepsilon$ eventually. The additional local hypothesis makes $\int_{-k}^k|f_n-f|<\varepsilon$ eventually. Therefore

$$
\int_{\mathbb R}|f_n-f|
\leq\int_{-k}^k|f_n-f|+\int_{|x|>k}f_n+\int_{|x|>k}f<4\varepsilon.
$$

Thus $\boxed{\|f_n-f\|_{L^1(\mathbb R)}\to0}$.

In fact the extra local hypothesis is unnecessary here. [Scheffé's lemma](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#scheffe-lemma) follows directly from $\min\{f_n,f\}\to f$ [almost everywhere](../measure-theory.md#almost-everywhere) and domination by $f$:

$$
\|f_n-f\|_1=\int f_n+\int f-2\int\min\{f_n,f\}\longrightarrow0.
$$

This stronger argument uses precisely the already assumed nonnegativity, a.e. convergence, and convergence of total integrals.
