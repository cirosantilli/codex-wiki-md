## 1

↑ **Parent:** [2015](2015.md)

<h3 id="1/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1/a)

A [measurable space](../measure-theory.md#measurable-space) is a pair $(\Omega,\mathcal F)$ where $\mathcal F$ is a [sigma-algebra](../measure-theory.md#sigma-algebra) of [subsets](../set.md#subset) of $\Omega$: it contains $\Omega$, is closed under [complements](../set.md#complement-of-a-set) relative to $\Omega$, and is closed under [countable unions](../set.md#countable-union). A [measure](../measure.md) on it is a [function](../function.md) $\mu:\mathcal F\to[0,\infty]$ with $\mu(\varnothing)=0$ and [countable additivity](../measure.md#countable-additivity):

$$
\mu\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_n\right)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu(A_n)
$$

whenever the [measurable sets](../measure-theory.md#measurable-set) $A_n$ are pairwise [disjoint](../set.md#disjoint-sets). Infinite values are allowed. The triple $(\Omega,\mathcal F,\mu)$ is a [measure space](../measure.md#measure-space).

Because $E\in\mathcal F$, the family $\mathcal F_E$ contains $E$. If $A\in\mathcal F_E$, then $E\setminus A=E\cap(\Omega\setminus A)$ belongs to $\mathcal F$ and is a [subset](../set.md#subset) of $E$, so it belongs to $\mathcal F_E$. If each $A_n\in\mathcal F_E$, their [countable union](../set.md#countable-union) belongs to $\mathcal F$ and remains a [subset](../set.md#subset) of $E$. Thus **$\mathcal F_E$ is a [sigma-algebra](../measure-theory.md#sigma-algebra) on $E$**, with relative [complements](../set.md#complement-of-a-set) taken in $E$, not in $\Omega$.

Equivalently,

$$
\mathcal F_E=\{E\cap B:B\in\mathcal F\},
$$

the [trace sigma-algebra](../measure-theory.md#trace-sigma-algebra) on the [measurable set](../measure-theory.md#measurable-set) $E$. The measurability of $E$ is what makes this equal to the family in the question.

The [function](../function.md) $\nu$ takes values in $[0,\infty]$ and has $\nu(\varnothing)=0$. For any pairwise [disjoint](../set.md#disjoint-sets) $A_n\in\mathcal F_E$, [countable additivity](../measure.md#countable-additivity) of $\mu$ gives

$$
\nu\left(\bigcup_nA_n\right)=\mu\left(\bigcup_nA_n\right)
=\sum_n\mu(A_n)=\sum_n\nu(A_n).
$$

Therefore **$\nu$ is a [measure](../measure.md) on $(E,\mathcal F_E)$**: it is the [restriction of a measure to a measurable subset](../measure.md#restriction-of-a-measure-to-a-measurable-subset).

<h3 id="1/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

For the increasing family, define the [measurable sets](../measure-theory.md#measurable-set)

$$
D_1=A_1,\qquad D_n=A_n\setminus A_{n-1}\quad(n\geq2).
$$

They are pairwise [disjoint](../set.md#disjoint-sets), with $A_N=\bigcup_{n=1}^ND_n$ and $\bigcup_nA_n=\bigcup_nD_n$. By [countable additivity](../measure.md#countable-additivity),

$$
\mu\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_n\right)
=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu(D_n)
=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{n=1}^N\mu(D_n)
=\lim_{N\to\infty}\mu(A_N).
$$

The nonnegative [series](../sequence-and-series.md#series-mathematics) is defined by its increasing partial-sum [limit](../calculus.md#limit-of-a-function), so this proof also covers an infinite value. This is [continuity from below of a measure](../measure.md#continuity-from-below-of-a-measure).

For the decreasing family, put $C_n=B_1\setminus B_n$. These [measurable sets](../measure-theory.md#measurable-set) increase, and

$$
\bigcup_nC_n=B_1\setminus\bigcap_nB_n.
$$

Since $\mu(B_1)<\infty$, [finite additivity](../function.md#finite-additivity-of-a-set-function) gives legitimate finite subtractions:

$$
\mu(C_n)=\mu(B_1)-\mu(B_n),\qquad
\mu\left(B_1\setminus\bigcap_nB_n\right)=\mu(B_1)-\mu\left(\bigcap_nB_n\right).
$$

Apply the first result to $C_n$ and subtract from $\mu(B_1)$ to obtain

$$
\boxed{\mu\left(\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}B_n\right)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu(B_n).}
$$

This is [continuity from above of a measure](../measure.md#continuity-from-above-of-a-measure). Its finiteness hypothesis matters: for [Lebesgue measure](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-measure) and $B_n=[n,\infty)$, every $B_n$ has infinite [measure](../measure.md) but their [intersection](../set.md#set-intersection) is empty. The proof must not subtract $\infty-\infty$.

<h3 id="1/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#1/c)

For $x<y$, [finite additivity](../function.md#finite-additivity-of-a-set-function) on the [disjoint](../set.md#disjoint-sets) pieces of the half-line gives

$$
h(y)-h(x)=m\bigl(E\cap(x,y]\bigr).
$$

By [monotonicity of a measure](../measure.md#monotonicity-of-a-measure) and the [Lebesgue measure](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-measure) of an [interval](../real-analysis.md#interval-mathematics),

$$
0\leq h(y)-h(x)\leq m((x,y])=y-x.
$$

Exchanging the two points if necessary proves

$$
\boxed{|h(y)-h(x)|\leq|y-x|\qquad(x,y\in\mathbb R).}
$$

Thus the [cumulative measure of a finite-measure set](../measure-theory.md#cumulative-measure-of-a-finite-measure-set) is a [Lipschitz function](../real-analysis.md#lipschitz-continuity) with [Lipschitz constant](../real-analysis.md#lipschitz-constant) at most one, hence is **[uniformly continuous](../topological-analysis.md#uniform-continuity)**: given $\varepsilon>0$, the choice $\delta=\varepsilon$ works simultaneously at every point.

To locate its endpoint [limits](../calculus.md#limit-of-a-function), the [measurable sets](../measure-theory.md#measurable-set) $E\cap(-\infty,N]$ increase to $E$, so [continuity from below of a measure](../measure.md#continuity-from-below-of-a-measure) gives $h(N)\to m(E)$. The [measurable sets](../measure-theory.md#measurable-set) $E\cap(-\infty,-N]$ decrease to the [empty set](../set.md#empty-set) and all have finite [measure](../measure.md), so [continuity from above of a measure](../measure.md#continuity-from-above-of-a-measure) gives $h(-N)\to0$. Since $h$ is nondecreasing,

$$
\lim_{x\to-\infty}h(x)=0,\qquad
\lim_{x\to\infty}h(x)=m(E).
$$

For $0<c<m(E)$, choose $R>0$ with $h(-R)<c<h(R)$. The [intermediate value theorem](../calculus.md#intermediate-value-theorem) on $[-R,R]$ gives $t$ with $h(t)=c$. Then

$$
\boxed{A=E\cap(-\infty,t]\text{ is Lebesgue measurable and }m(A)=c.}
$$

The endpoint values $c=0,m(E)$ can also be achieved by $A=\varnothing,E$. This proves [divisibility of Lebesgue measure](../measure-theory.md#divisibility-of-lebesgue-measure) for arbitrary finite-measure [Lebesgue measurable sets](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-measurable-set) on the [real line](../real-analysis.md#real-line), including unbounded ones. The [non-atomic measure](../measure.md#non-atomic-measure) structure permits prescribed fractions of [measure](../measure.md); a [measure](../measure.md) concentrated at one point would not have this property.

<h3 id="2/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#2/a)

The [function](../function.md) is continuous for $x>0$, hence [Lebesgue measurable](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-measurable-set). Near zero, the bound $|\sin x|\leq x$ gives

$$
\int_0^1\left|\frac{\sin x}{x^q}\right|\,dx
\leq\int_0^1x^{1-q}\,dx=\frac{1}{2-q}<\infty\qquad(0<q<2).
$$

Thus **$f$ is [Lebesgue integrable](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-integrable-function) on $(0,1]$ for $q<2$**. The convergence is an instance of [power singularity integrability](../measure-theory.md#power-singularity-integrability).

For the tail, take the pairwise [disjoint](../set.md#disjoint-sets) [intervals](../real-analysis.md#interval-mathematics)

$$
I_k=\left[k\pi+\frac\pi6,\ k\pi+\frac{5\pi}6\right],\qquad k\geq1.
$$

On each $I_k$, $|\sin x|\geq1/2$, $x\leq(k+1)\pi$, and the interval length is $2\pi/3$. Therefore

$$
\int_{I_k}\frac{|\sin x|}{x^q}\,dx
\geq\frac{\pi}{3}\bigl((k+1)\pi\bigr)^{-q}
=\frac{\pi^{1-q}}{3(k+1)^q}.
$$

Summing the nonnegative [integrals](../integral.md) over these [disjoint](../set.md#disjoint-sets) [intervals](../real-analysis.md#interval-mathematics) yields

$$
\int_1^\infty\frac{|\sin x|}{x^q}\,dx
\geq\frac{\pi^{1-q}}3\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac1{(k+1)^q}=\infty
\qquad(0<q\leq1),
$$

by divergence of the [p-series](../sequence-and-series.md#p-series). Hence **$f$ is not [Lebesgue integrable](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-integrable-function) on $[1,\infty)$ for $0<q\leq1$**.

The test is [absolute integrability](../measure-theory.md#integrability): oscillatory sign cancellation does not make the absolute-value [integral](../integral.md) finite. A conditionally convergent [improper integral](../real-analysis.md#improper-integral) is a different notion. The [sine power kernel integrability](../real-analysis.md#sine-power-kernel-integrability) criterion separates these endpoint thresholds.

<h3 id="2/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

Define

$$
H(x)=\frac{e^{-x}}{\sqrt x}\qquad(x>0).
$$

This is a [Lebesgue integrable function](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-integrable-function): on $(0,1]$ it is bounded by $x^{-1/2}$, whose [integral](../integral.md) is two, while on $[1,\infty)$ it is bounded by $e^{-x}$, whose [integral](../integral.md) is $e^{-1}$. Since $|\sin(xy)|\leq x|y|$,

$$
|h_y(x)|\leq|y|H(x).
$$

Thus **$h_y$ is [Lebesgue integrable](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-integrable-function) for every $y\geq0$**, including $h_0=0$; the same argument actually works for all real $y$.

The [mean value theorem](../calculus.md#mean-value-theorem) for the [sine](../geometry-and-topology.md#sine) gives $|\sin u-\sin v|\leq|u-v|$. Hence

$$
|h_y(x)-h_z(x)|\leq|y-z|H(x)
$$

and the [triangle inequality for an integral](../topological-analysis.md#integral-triangle-inequality) gives

$$
\boxed{|F(y)-F(z)|\leq\left(\int_0^\infty H(x)\,dx\right)|y-z|.}
$$

Thus $F$ is a [globally Lipschitz function](../real-analysis.md#globally-lipschitz-function), in particular **continuous on $[0,\infty)$**, including the endpoint zero.

For the [derivative](../calculus.md#derivative), fix $y>0$ and take nonzero $t$ sufficiently small that $y+t>0$. The difference quotient has the [integrand](../integral.md#integrand)

$$
D_t(x)=\frac{e^{-x}}{x\sqrt x}\frac{\sin(x(y+t))-\sin(xy)}{t}.
$$

For every $x>0$, $D_t(x)\to H(x)\cos(xy)$ as $t\to0$. The same [mean value theorem](../calculus.md#mean-value-theorem) gives the uniform-in-$t$ bound $|D_t(x)|\leq H(x)$.

The [dominated convergence theorem](../measure-theory.md#dominated-convergence-theorem) states that if [measurable functions](../measure-theory.md#measurable-function) $u_n$ converge [almost everywhere](../measure-theory.md#almost-everywhere) to $u$ and $|u_n|\leq G$ for a single [Lebesgue integrable function](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-integrable-function) $G$, then $u$ is [Lebesgue integrable](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-integrable-function) and $\int|u_n-u|\to0$, hence $\int u_n\to\int u$. Apply it to the difference quotients along every sequence $t_n\to0$, using the common dominator $H$. The resulting [limit](../calculus.md#limit-of-a-function) is independent of the sequence, so

$$
\boxed{F'(y)=\int_0^\infty e^{-x}\frac{\cos(xy)}{\sqrt x}\,dx\qquad(y>0).}
$$

The right-hand [integral](../integral.md) is absolutely convergent because its [integrand](../integral.md#integrand) has absolute value at most $H$. This is [dominated differentiation under the integral sign](../analysis.md#dominated-differentiation-under-the-integral-sign), justified by an explicit integrable bound rather than a formal interchange.

<h3 id="2/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#2/c)

For every fixed $x>0$, $\log(x+n)/n\to0$, so $f_n(x)\to0$. To exchange the [limit](../calculus.md#limit-of-a-function) with the [Lebesgue integral](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-integral), we need a common integrable bound over the whole half-line.

For $n\geq1$, $x+n\leq n(1+x)$, so

$$
0\leq\frac{\log(x+n)}n
\leq\frac{\log n}{n}+\frac{\log(1+x)}n
\leq1+\log(1+x).
$$

Together with $|\sin x|\leq\min(x,1)$, this gives

$$
|f_n(x)|\leq G(x):=\bigl(1+\log(1+x)\bigr)\frac{\min(x,1)}{x^q}.
$$

For $0<x\leq1$, $\log(1+x)\leq x\leq1$, hence $G(x)\leq2x^{1-q}$, which is integrable because $q<2$. For $x\geq1$,

$$
G(x)\leq\frac{1+\log2+\log x}{x^q}.
$$

Both terms are integrable because $q>1$:

$$
\int_1^\infty x^{-q}\,dx=\frac1{q-1},\qquad
\int_1^\infty\frac{\log x}{x^q}\,dx=\frac1{(q-1)^2}.
$$

The second equality follows from [integration by parts](../calculus.md#integration-by-parts), or the [substitution](../algebra.md#substitution-algebra) $x=e^t$. Consequently $G$ is a [Lebesgue integrable function](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-integrable-function), and every $f_n$ is [Lebesgue integrable](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-integrable-function).

The [dominated convergence theorem](../measure-theory.md#dominated-convergence-theorem) states that [almost everywhere](../measure-theory.md#almost-everywhere) convergence of [measurable functions](../measure-theory.md#measurable-function) under one integrable absolute-value bound implies [L1 convergence](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#convergence-in-l1), and in particular convergence of their [integrals](../integral.md). Here the pointwise [limit](../calculus.md#limit-of-a-function) is zero and the dominator is $G$, so

$$
\boxed{\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_0^\infty f_n(x)\,dx=0.}
$$

In fact we obtain the stronger conclusion $\|f_n\|_1\to0$. A quantitative version follows by retaining the factors $1/n$ in the bound:

$$
\|f_n\|_1\leq\frac{A\log n+B}{n},
\quad
A=\int_0^\infty\frac{|\sin x|}{x^q}\,dx,
\quad
B=\int_0^\infty\frac{\log(1+x)|\sin x|}{x^q}\,dx.
$$

The preceding endpoint estimates show $A,B<\infty$, so the [L1 norm](../normed-vector-space.md#l1-norm) is $O(\log n/n)$ as $n\to\infty$, with $O$ denoting [Big O notation](../real-analysis.md#big-o-notation). This is [logarithmic reweighting under dominated convergence](../measure-theory.md#logarithmic-reweighting-under-dominated-convergence).

↑ **Parent:** [2015](2015.md)

<h3 id="3/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

Fix $y\ne0$. The [function](../function.md) $x\mapsto f(x,y)$ is continuous on $[-1,1]$, because its denominator is bounded away from zero, and it is an [odd function](../calculus.md#odd-function) of $x$. Its [integral](../integral.md) over this symmetric [interval](../real-analysis.md#interval-mathematics) is therefore zero. At $y=0$ the entire section is zero, including the specified value at the origin. Thus the inner $x$ [integral](../integral.md) exists for every $y$ and is identically zero. The same reasoning with $x$ and $y$ exchanged gives

$$
\boxed{\int_{-1}^1\left(\int_{-1}^1f(x,y)\,dx\right)dy
=\int_{-1}^1\left(\int_{-1}^1f(x,y)\,dy\right)dx=0.}
$$

To test [Lebesgue integrability](../measure-theory.md#integrability) in two dimensions, examine the [absolute value](../real-analysis.md#absolute-value) near the origin. For $0<\varepsilon<1$, the annulus $\varepsilon<\sqrt{x^2+y^2}<1$ lies inside the square. In [polar coordinates](../multivariable-calculus.md#polar-coordinates),

$$
|f(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)|=\frac{|\cos\theta\sin\theta|}{r^4},\qquad dx\,dy=r\,dr\,d\theta.
$$

The [change of variables formula](../multivariable-calculus.md#change-of-variables-formula) on this annulus, where the [integrand](../integral.md#integrand) has no singularity, gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_{[-1,1]^2}|f(x,y)|\,dx\,dy
&\geq\int_\varepsilon^1r^{-3}\,dr\int_0^{2\pi}|\cos\theta\sin\theta|\,d\theta\\
&=\frac{\varepsilon^{-2}-1}{2}\cdot2
=\varepsilon^{-2}-1.
\end{aligned}
$$

The lower bound tends to infinity as $\varepsilon\downarrow0$. Hence

$$
\boxed{\int_{[-1,1]^2}|f|=\infty,\qquad f\notin L^1([-1,1]^2).}
$$

Moreover reflection in either coordinate axis exchanges the [positive part](../function.md#positive-part-of-a-real-valued-function) and [negative part](../function.md#negative-part-of-a-real-valued-function) while preserving [Lebesgue measure](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-measure). Both parts therefore have infinite [integral](../integral.md), so a signed double [Lebesgue integral](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-integral) would involve the undefined expression $\infty-\infty$.

There is no contradiction with [Fubini's theorem](../measure-theory.md#fubini-s-theorem): its [absolute integrability](../measure-theory.md#integrability) hypothesis is missing. This is an example of [finite iterated integrals without joint integrability](../measure-theory.md#finite-iterated-integrals-without-joint-integrability), with every individual one-dimensional section integrable despite the two-dimensional singularity.

<h3 id="3/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

Define the one-dimensional [convolution kernel](../fourier-analysis.md#convolution-kernel)

$$
k(t)=\begin{cases}\sin t/|t|^{3/2},&t\ne0,\\0,&t=0.\end{cases}
$$

It is a [Borel measurable function](../measure-theory.md#borel-measurable-function), and $f(x,y)=k(x-y)g(y)$. The [function](../function.md) $k(x-y)$ is [Borel measurable](../measure-theory.md#borel-measurable-function) by composition with the continuous difference map. Lifting the [Lebesgue measurable function](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-measurable-function) $g$ to the second coordinate preserves measurability for the [product sigma-algebra](../probability-theory.md#product-sigma-algebra), hence also for [Lebesgue measure](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-measure) on $\mathbb R^2$. Products of [measurable functions](../measure-theory.md#measurable-function) are measurable, so $f$ is [Lebesgue measurable](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-measurable-set).

Near zero, $|\sin t|\leq|t|$; away from zero, $|\sin t|\leq1$. Consequently

$$
|k(t)|\leq\begin{cases}|t|^{-1/2},&0<|t|\leq1,\\|t|^{-3/2},&|t|>1.\end{cases}
$$

Both bounds are integrable on their respective regions by [power singularity integrability](../measure-theory.md#power-singularity-integrability). In particular,

$$
\boxed{\|k\|_1\leq2\int_0^1t^{-1/2}\,dt+2\int_1^\infty t^{-3/2}\,dt=4+4=8.}
$$

We use the following forms of the two product-integration theorems. On the product of [measure spaces](../measure.md#measure-space) equipped with [sigma-finite measures](../measure.md#sigma-finite-measure), [Tonelli's theorem](../measure-theory.md#tonelli-theorem) says that a nonnegative measurable [function](../function.md) has equal product and iterated [integrals](../integral.md), with infinity allowed. [Fubini's theorem](../measure-theory.md#fubini-s-theorem) says that for an absolutely integrable measurable [function](../function.md), its sections are integrable for [almost every](../measure-theory.md#almost-everywhere) fixed coordinate, and both orders of iterated [integration](../integral.md) equal the finite product [integral](../integral.md). The [Lebesgue measure](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-measure) on each factor is a [sigma-finite measure](../measure.md#sigma-finite-measure), because the [real line](../real-analysis.md#real-line) is a [countable union](../set.md#countable-union) of finite-length [intervals](../real-analysis.md#interval-mathematics).

Apply [Tonelli's theorem](../measure-theory.md#tonelli-theorem) first to $|f|$, before presuming finiteness. By [translation invariance of Lebesgue measure](../measure-theory.md#translation-invariance-of-lebesgue-measure),

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_{\mathbb R^2}|f(x,y)|\,dx\,dy
&=\int_{\mathbb R}|g(y)|\left(\int_{\mathbb R}|k(x-y)|\,dx\right)dy\\
&=\|k\|_1\int_{\mathbb R}|g(y)|\,dy
\leq8\int_{\mathbb R}|g(y)|\,dy<\infty.
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus **$f$ is [Lebesgue integrable](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-integrable-function) on $\mathbb R^2$**. The [triangle inequality for an integral](../topological-analysis.md#integral-triangle-inequality) now gives the requested estimate:

$$
\boxed{\left|\int_{\mathbb R^2}f(x,y)\,dx\,dy\right|
\leq\int_{\mathbb R^2}|f(x,y)|\,dx\,dy
\leq8\int_{\mathbb R}|g(y)|\,dy.}
$$

This is the [L1 norm identity for a translated kernel product](../fourier-analysis.md#l1-norm-identity-for-a-translated-kernel-product) followed by a simple bound on the [convolution kernel](../fourier-analysis.md#convolution-kernel).

There is also a stronger signed conclusion. The integrable [convolution kernel](../fourier-analysis.md#convolution-kernel) $k$ is an [odd function](../calculus.md#odd-function), so $\int_{\mathbb R}k(t)\,dt=0$. Having established [absolute integrability](../measure-theory.md#integrability), we may apply [Fubini's theorem](../measure-theory.md#fubini-s-theorem) to $f$ itself:

$$
\boxed{\int_{\mathbb R^2}f(x,y)\,dx\,dy
=\left(\int_{\mathbb R}k(t)\,dt\right)\left(\int_{\mathbb R}g(y)\,dy\right)=0.}
$$

Unlike part (a), the signed cancellation is justified by a finite [integral](../integral.md) of the [absolute value](../real-analysis.md#absolute-value). The constant eight is useful as an [L1 norm](../normed-vector-space.md#l1-norm) bound even though this particular signed total vanishes.
