# Logarithm

↑ **Parent:** [Exponential function](exponential-function.md)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logarithm)

For a positive real base $b\ne1$, the [logarithm](logarithm.md) is the [inverse function](inverse-function.md) of the [exponential function](exponential-function.md) $t\mapsto b^t$:

$$
\log_b x=t\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad b^t=x,\qquad x>0.
$$

It asks which exponent produces a given positive number. Thus $\log_2 8=3$ and $\log_2(1/8)=-3$. The base restrictions matter: $b=1$ would produce only one value, while a negative base does not define $b^t$ as a real-valued [function](function-split.md) for every real exponent.

The useful transformation is

$$
\log_b(xy)=\log_bx+\log_by,\qquad \log_b(x/y)=\log_bx-\log_by.
$$

For example, $8\cdot16=128$ corresponds to $3+4=7$ in base two. Products become sums and ratios become differences. The [logarithm](logarithm.md) does not generally convert a sum into a sum of logarithms. Base conversion uses the [natural logarithm](natural-logarithm.md): $\log_bx=\ln x/\ln b$. It also gives $d(\log_bx)/dx=1/(x\ln b)$, positive when $b>1$ and negative when $0<b<1$.

Historically, [John Napier](john-napier.md)'s 1614 Mirifici logarithmorum canonis descriptio presented logarithmic tables and their use, documented in [the Smithsonian Libraries' digitized original](https://library.si.edu/digital-library/book/mirificilogarit00napi). Tables made multiplication-heavy calculations more manageable by replacing products with addition and a lookup. The 1619 Constructio title page below belongs to a later publication from the same work on [logarithms](logarithm.md). These historical publications preceded the modern [inverse function](inverse-function.md) and [integral](integral-split.md) presentation used here.
