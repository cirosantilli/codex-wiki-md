## 1

↑ **Parent:** [2021](2021.md)

<h3 id="1/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1/a)

There are $n+2$ distinct [interpolation nodes](../interpolation.md#interpolation-node) but only $n+1$ free [coefficients](../vector-space.md#coefficient) in a [polynomial](../polynomial.md) of [polynomial degree](../polynomial.md#degree-of-a-polynomial) at most $n$. Thus [polynomial interpolation](../interpolation.md#polynomial-interpolation) is possible precisely when the data satisfy one compatibility equation. To identify it, form the [Lagrange interpolation polynomial](../interpolation.md#lagrange-polynomial) through all the data:

$$
q(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{n+1}f_i
\prod_{\substack{0\leq j\leq n+1\\j\ne i}}
\frac{x-x_j}{x_i-x_j}.
$$

This is the unique [interpolation polynomial](../interpolation.md#interpolation-polynomial) of [polynomial degree](../polynomial.md#degree-of-a-polynomial) at most $n+1$. Its [leading coefficient](../polynomial.md#leading-coefficient-of-a-polynomial), which is also the order-$(n+1)$ [divided difference](../interpolation.md#divided-difference), is

$$
D=\sum_{i=0}^{n+1}\frac{f_i}
{\displaystyle\prod_{\substack{0\leq j\leq n+1\\j\ne i}}(x_i-x_j)}.
$$

Consequently the required condition is

$$
\boxed{D=0.}
$$

Necessity follows because any qualifying $p_n$ must equal $q$ by uniqueness of [polynomial interpolation](../interpolation.md#polynomial-interpolation); sufficiency follows because $D=0$ removes the only possible term of [polynomial degree](../polynomial.md#degree-of-a-polynomial) $n+1$ from $q$.

When this condition holds, a slightly shorter expression uses only the first $n+1$ [interpolation nodes](../interpolation.md#interpolation-node):

$$
\boxed{p_n(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}f_i
\prod_{\substack{0\leq j\leq n\\j\ne i}}
\frac{x-x_j}{x_i-x_j}.}
$$

This [polynomial](../polynomial.md) agrees with $q$ at $n+1$ distinct points, so the [root bound for a polynomial](../polynomial.md#lagrange-root-bound-over-a-field) gives $p_n=q$ and hence $p_n(x_{n+1})=f_{n+1}$ as required. Equivalently, the compatibility condition says that this expression, evaluated at the last [interpolation node](../interpolation.md#interpolation-node), gives $f_{n+1}$.

<h3 id="1/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

<h5 id="1/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#1/b/i)

Write $\mathcal P_n$ for the [vector space](../vector-space.md) of [polynomials](../polynomial.md) of [polynomial degree](../polynomial.md#degree-of-a-polynomial) at most $n$, and put $p_n(x)=\sum_{j=0}^na_jx^j$. As the question calls the weighted integral an [inner product](../inner-product.md), its finiteness is implicit; for example, a positive integrable weight $w$ suffices for all the [continuous functions](../calculus.md#continuous-function) involved here.

For every $q\in\mathcal P_n$, differentiating the squared [weighted norm](../inner-product.md#weighted-norm) $\|f-p_n-tq\|_w^2$ at its minimum $t=0$ gives the [orthogonality](../inner-product.md#orthogonal-vectors) equations

$$
\langle f-p_n,q\rangle_w=0.
$$

In particular, the requested [linear system](../linear-algebra.md#system-of-linear-equations) is

$$
\boxed{\sum_{j=0}^n a_j\int_{-1}^1w(x)x^{j+k}\,dx
=\int_{-1}^1w(x)f(x)x^k\,dx,\qquad k=0,\ldots,n.}
$$

Its [Gram matrix](../inner-product.md#gram-matrix) $G_{kj}=\int_{-1}^1w(x)x^{k+j}\,dx$ is a [symmetric positive-definite matrix](../linear-algebra.md#symmetric-positive-definite-matrix): for any nonzero [vector](../vector-space.md#vector) $a$,

$$
a^TGa=\int_{-1}^1w(x)\left(\sum_{j=0}^na_jx^j\right)^2dx>0.
$$

Indeed a nonzero [polynomial](../polynomial.md) is nonzero on some open interval, and the weight is positive. Therefore $G$ is an [invertible matrix](../linear-algebra.md#invertible-matrix), proving uniqueness. Conversely, if these equations hold, the [Pythagorean theorem in an inner-product space](../inner-product.md#pythagorean-theorem-in-an-inner-product-space) gives, for every $q\in\mathcal P_n$,

$$
\|f-(p_n+q)\|_w^2=\|f-p_n\|_w^2+\|q\|_w^2,
$$

so the solution really is the unique [best approximation](../normed-vector-space.md#best-approximation-in-a-normed-space).

<h4 id="1/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

<h5 id="1/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#1/b/ii)

Let $r=f-p_n$ be the [continuous function](../calculus.md#continuous-function) describing the error. The preceding [orthogonality](../inner-product.md#orthogonal-vectors) equations say

$$
\int_{-1}^1w(x)r(x)q(x)\,dx=0\qquad(q\in\mathcal P_n).
$$

If $r$ vanishes identically, any $n+1$ distinct points work. If $r$ already has at least $n+1$ distinct zeros, the assertion also follows. Suppose instead that it has at most $n$ distinct zeros. Among its interior zeros, list those at which its sign changes as $t_1<\cdots<t_m$, where $m\leq n$, and define the [polynomial](../polynomial.md)

$$
q(x)=\prod_{j=1}^m(x-t_j),
$$

with $q=1$ if $m=0$. Between consecutive zeros of $r$, its sign is constant by the [intermediate value theorem](../calculus.md#intermediate-value-theorem). At each $t_j$, both $r$ and $q$ change sign; at every other zero of $r$, neither sign changes. Hence $rq$ has one fixed sign throughout $[-1,1]$ wherever it is nonzero. Replacing $q$ by $-q$ if necessary makes $rq\geq0$ everywhere.

Since $r$ is not identically zero and $q$ has only finitely many zeros, $rq$ is strictly positive on some open interval. Positivity of $w$ gives

$$
\int_{-1}^1w(x)r(x)q(x)\,dx>0,
$$

contradicting [orthogonality](../inner-product.md#orthogonal-vectors) because $q\in\mathcal P_n$. Thus

$$
\boxed{f(x_i)=p_n(x_i)\text{ at at least }n+1\text{ distinct points in }[-1,1].}
$$

This is the [interpolation property of weighted least-squares polynomial approximation](../interpolation.md#interpolation-property-of-weighted-least-squares-polynomial-approximation). It supplies [interpolation nodes](../interpolation.md#interpolation-node) depending on $f$ and $w$, rather than nodes prescribed in advance.

<h3 id="1/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/c/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#1/c)

<h5 id="1/c/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#1/c/i)

Let $L_{n+1}$ denote the conventional [Legendre polynomial](../linear-ordinary-differential-equation.md#legendre-polynomial), normalized by $L_{n+1}(1)=1$. The $n+1$ [quadrature nodes](../numerical-integration.md#quadrature-node) are its distinct [polynomial roots](../polynomial.md#root-of-a-polynomial) in $(-1,1)$. For these nodes form the [Lagrange cardinal polynomials](../interpolation.md#lagrange-cardinal-polynomial)

$$
\ell_i(x)=\prod_{\substack{0\leq j\leq n\\j\ne i}}
\frac{x-x_j}{x_i-x_j}
=\frac{L_{n+1}(x)}{(x-x_i)L_{n+1}'(x_i)}.
$$

Choose the [quadrature weights](../numerical-integration.md#quadrature-weight) by integrating these [polynomials](../polynomial.md):

$$
\boxed{w_i=\int_{-1}^1\ell_i(x)\,dx,\qquad
\widehat I_n(f)=\sum_{i=0}^nw_if(x_i).}
$$

An equivalent standard formula is

$$
\boxed{w_i=\frac{2}{(1-x_i^2)[L_{n+1}'(x_i)]^2}.}
$$

The reason for selecting these [quadrature nodes](../numerical-integration.md#quadrature-node) is the resulting [degree of precision of a quadrature rule](../numerical-integration.md#degree-of-precision-of-a-quadrature-rule). For any [polynomial](../polynomial.md) $p$ of [polynomial degree](../polynomial.md#degree-of-a-polynomial) at most $2n+1$, [polynomial division](../polynomial.md#polynomial-division) gives

$$
p=L_{n+1}s+r,\qquad \deg s\leq n,\quad\deg r\leq n.
$$

The [orthogonality](../inner-product.md#orthogonal-vectors) of the [Legendre polynomial](../linear-ordinary-differential-equation.md#legendre-polynomial) makes $\int_{-1}^1L_{n+1}s=0$, while $p(x_i)=r(x_i)$ at each [quadrature node](../numerical-integration.md#quadrature-node). The definition of the [quadrature weights](../numerical-integration.md#quadrature-weight) integrates $r$ exactly, so

$$
\boxed{\widehat I_n(p)=\int_{-1}^1p(x)\,dx\qquad(\deg p\leq2n+1).}
$$

In particular, $\sum_iw_i=2$. Applying this exactness to $\ell_i^2$ also proves $w_i=\int_{-1}^1\ell_i(x)^2dx>0$.

<h4 id="1/c/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#1/c)

<h5 id="1/c/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#1/c/ii)

Truncate the [Chebyshev series](../numerical-analysis.md#chebyshev-series) at the full [degree of precision of a quadrature rule](../numerical-integration.md#degree-of-precision-of-a-quadrature-rule):

$$
s_{2n+1}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{2n+1}c_jT_j(x),\qquad R_n=f-s_{2n+1}.
$$

Since $|T_j(x)|\leq1$ on $[-1,1]$, the [geometric series](../sequence-and-series.md#geometric-series) estimate gives

$$
\|R_n\|_\infty\leq\sum_{j=2n+2}^\infty e^{-Kj}
=\frac{e^{-K(2n+2)}}{1-e^{-K}}.
$$

The same bound proves [uniform convergence](../sequence-and-series.md#uniform-convergence) of the original [Chebyshev series](../numerical-analysis.md#chebyshev-series), so its sum and the tail estimate are justified. [Gauss-Legendre quadrature](../numerical-analysis.md#gauss-legendre-quadrature) is exact on $s_{2n+1}$. Its positive [quadrature weights](../numerical-integration.md#quadrature-weight) sum to $2$, hence

$$
\begin{aligned}
|I-\widehat I_n|
&=\left|\int_{-1}^1R_n(x)\,dx-\sum_{i=0}^nw_iR_n(x_i)\right|\\
&\leq2\|R_n\|_\infty+\left(\sum_{i=0}^nw_i\right)\|R_n\|_\infty\\
&\leq\frac{4e^{-2K}}{1-e^{-K}}e^{-2Kn}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore one explicit choice proving [exponential convergence of Gaussian quadrature](../numerical-analysis.md#exponential-convergence-of-gaussian-quadrature) is

$$
\boxed{C=\frac{4e^{-2K}}{1-e^{-K}},\qquad L=2K.}
$$

Using exactness through [polynomial degree](../polynomial.md#degree-of-a-polynomial) $2n+1$ doubles the exponent compared with truncating at [polynomial degree](../polynomial.md#degree-of-a-polynomial) $n$.

<h4 id="1/c/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#1/c)

<h5 id="1/c/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#1/c/iii)

Set $W=\operatorname{diag}(w_0,\ldots,w_n)$. For column indices $j,k\in\{1,\ldots,n+1\}$, the product $P_{j-1}P_{k-1}$ has [polynomial degree](../polynomial.md#degree-of-a-polynomial) at most $2n$, so exactness of [Gauss-Legendre quadrature](../numerical-analysis.md#gauss-legendre-quadrature) and [orthonormality](../inner-product.md#orthonormal-set) give

$$
(A^TWA)_{jk}=\sum_{i=0}^nw_iP_{j-1}(x_i)P_{k-1}(x_i)
=\int_{-1}^1P_{j-1}(x)P_{k-1}(x)\,dx=\delta_{jk}.
$$

Thus $Q=W^{1/2}A$ is a square [matrix](../vector-space.md#matrix) with $Q^TQ=I$, and therefore is an [orthogonal matrix](../linear-algebra.md#orthogonal-matrix): $QQ^T=I$ as well. It follows that

$$
\boxed{AA^T=W^{-1},\qquad
(AA^T)_{ij}=\begin{cases}
w_{i-1}^{-1},&i=j,\\
0,&i\ne j.
\end{cases}}
$$

Equivalently, the rows of the [generalized Vandermonde matrix](../galois-theory.md#generalized-vandermonde-matrix) are [orthogonal](../inner-product.md#orthogonal-vectors) and

$$
\sum_{k=0}^nP_k(x_i)^2=\frac1{w_i}.
$$

The normalization matters: here $P_{n+1}=\sqrt{(2n+3)/2}\,L_{n+1}$, where $L_{n+1}$ is the conventional [Legendre polynomial](../linear-ordinary-differential-equation.md#legendre-polynomial) used above. Thus a formula containing only the nodes and the question's [orthonormal](../inner-product.md#orthonormal-set) [polynomials](../polynomial.md) is

$$
\boxed{(AA^T)_{ij}=\delta_{ij}
\frac{(1-x_{i-1}^2)[P_{n+1}'(x_{i-1})]^2}{2n+3}.}
$$

This identity is the [discrete orthogonality of Gaussian quadrature](../numerical-analysis.md#discrete-orthogonality-of-gaussian-quadrature).

## 2

↑ **Parent:** [2021](2021.md)

Throughout Question 2(a) and 2(b), write $U=[u_1,\ldots,u_n]$, $V=[v_1,\ldots,v_n]$, and $\Sigma=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_n)$. Then the supplied full [singular value decomposition](../linear-algebra.md#singular-value-decomposition) can also be written $A=U\Sigma V^T$, with $U^TU=V^TV=I_n$ and $[U,U_\perp]$ an $m\times m$ [orthogonal matrix](../linear-algebra.md#orthogonal-matrix).

<h3 id="2/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#2/a)

<h5 id="2/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#2/a/i)

All $n$ [singular values](../linear-algebra.md#singular-value) are positive, so $\Sigma$ and $V$ are [invertible matrices](../linear-algebra.md#invertible-matrix) and $A$ has [matrix rank](../vector-space.md#matrix-rank) $n$. The [image of a linear map](../vector-space.md#image-of-a-linear-map) is therefore

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{range}(A)=\operatorname{span}\{u_1,\ldots,u_n\},}
$$

with those columns furnishing an [orthonormal basis](../inner-product.md#orthonormal-basis). Also $Ax=0$ implies $\Sigma V^Tx=0$ and hence $x=0$. Thus

$$
\boxed{\ker A=\{0\},\qquad\text{its basis is the empty set}.}
$$

Finally $A^Tz=V\Sigma U^Tz=0$ exactly when $U^Tz=0$. The remaining columns of the full [orthogonal matrix](../linear-algebra.md#orthogonal-matrix) span this [orthogonal complement](../hilbert-space.md#orthogonal-complement):

$$
\boxed{\ker A^T=\operatorname{range}(U_\perp),\qquad
\text{a basis consists of the }m-n\text{ columns of }U_\perp.}
$$

If $m=n$, this last [null space](../kernel-of-a-linear-map.md) also equals $\{0\}$ and has the empty basis.

<h4 id="2/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#2/a)

<h5 id="2/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#2/a/ii)

For $m=n$, $U$ and $V$ are square [orthogonal matrices](../linear-algebra.md#orthogonal-matrix). Multiply the [linear system](../linear-algebra.md#system-of-linear-equations) successively by $U^T$, $\Sigma^{-1}$ and $V$ to obtain

$$
\boxed{x=V\Sigma^{-1}U^Tb
=\sum_{j=1}^n\frac{u_j^Tb}{\sigma_j}v_j.}
$$

With the [singular value decomposition](../linear-algebra.md#singular-value-decomposition) already supplied, compute $c=U^Tb$, divide $c_j$ by $\sigma_j$, then form $x=Vc$. The two dense [matrix-vector products](../vector-space.md#matrix-vector-product) each cost $O(n^2)$ [arithmetic operations](../numerical-analysis.md#arithmetic-operation) in [Big O notation](../real-analysis.md#big-o-notation), and the diagonal scaling costs $O(n)$. Thus

$$
\boxed{\text{Cost per right-hand side with supplied factors: }O(n^2).}
$$

If the [singular value decomposition](../linear-algebra.md#singular-value-decomposition) first has to be computed, its usual dense cost is $O(n^3)$; that is a separate setup cost and can be reused for further right-hand sides.

<h4 id="2/a/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#2/a)

<h5 id="2/a/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#2/a/iii)

The [Euclidean norm](../normed-vector-space.md#euclidean-norm) is invariant under an [orthogonal matrix](../linear-algebra.md#orthogonal-matrix), so the full [singular value decomposition](../linear-algebra.md#singular-value-decomposition) gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\|Ax-b\|_2^2
&=\left\|\begin{pmatrix}\Sigma V^Tx-U^Tb\\-U_\perp^Tb\end{pmatrix}\right\|_2^2\\
&=\|\Sigma V^Tx-U^Tb\|_2^2+\|U_\perp^Tb\|_2^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

The second term is independent of $x$; the first attains its unique minimum zero because $\Sigma$ is an [invertible matrix](../linear-algebra.md#invertible-matrix). The [linear least squares](../probability-and-statistics.md#linear-least-squares) solution is therefore

$$
\boxed{x_*=V\Sigma^{-1}U^Tb=A^+b,\qquad
\min_x\|Ax-b\|_2=\|U_\perp^Tb\|_2.}
$$

Here $A^+=V\Sigma^{-1}U^T$ is the [Moore-Penrose inverse](../matrix-inverse.md#moore-penrose-inverse), and the fitted [vector](../vector-space.md#vector) is the [orthogonal projection](../hilbert-space.md#orthogonal-projection) $Ax_*=UU^Tb$ onto the [image of a linear map](../vector-space.md#image-of-a-linear-map) defined by $A$.

With the [singular value decomposition](../linear-algebra.md#singular-value-decomposition) supplied, forming $U^Tb$ costs $O(mn)$ [arithmetic operations](../numerical-analysis.md#arithmetic-operation), diagonal scaling costs $O(n)$, and multiplication by $V$ costs $O(n^2)$. Since $m\geq n$,

$$
\boxed{\text{Cost per right-hand side: }O(mn+n^2)=O(mn).}
$$

One need not form $U_\perp$, $A^+$ or $A^TA$ to calculate $x_*$. Computing the needed thin [singular value decomposition](../linear-algebra.md#singular-value-decomposition) first has standard dense cost $O(mn^2)$; explicitly forming an entire $m\times m$ orthogonal completion can require additional work when $m$ is much larger than $n$.

<h3 id="2/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

<h5 id="2/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#2/b/i)

Retain the first $r$ terms in the [singular value decomposition](../linear-algebra.md#singular-value-decomposition):

$$
\boxed{A_r=\sum_{j=1}^r\sigma_ju_jv_j^T
=U\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_r,0,\ldots,0)V^T.}
$$

The [Eckart-Young-Mirsky theorem](../linear-algebra.md#eckart-young-mirsky-theorem) says that this [truncated singular value decomposition](../inverse-problem.md#truncated-singular-value-decomposition) is a best [low-rank approximation](../linear-algebra.md#low-rank-approximation) in the [spectral norm](../continuous-dual-space.md#matrix-2-norm). The remaining [singular values](../linear-algebra.md#singular-value) of the difference are $\sigma_{r+1},\ldots,\sigma_n$, together with zeros, so

$$
\boxed{\|A-A_r\|_2=\sigma_{r+1}.}
$$

For $r\geq1$ the displayed [matrix](../vector-space.md#matrix) has [matrix rank](../vector-space.md#matrix-rank) exactly $r$, since every retained [singular value](../linear-algebra.md#singular-value) is positive. For $r=0$, the only [matrix](../vector-space.md#matrix) of [matrix rank](../vector-space.md#matrix-rank) zero is the zero [matrix](../vector-space.md#matrix), and the same formula gives error $\sigma_1$.

<h4 id="2/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

<h5 id="2/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#2/b/ii)

Take the [identity matrix](../vector-space.md#identity-matrix)

$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
B_1=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
B_2=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Both $B_1$ and $B_2$ have [matrix rank](../vector-space.md#matrix-rank) one, and

$$
\|A-B_1\|_2=\|A-B_2\|_2=1=\sigma_2(A).
$$

They are distinct and both attain the minimum [spectral norm](../continuous-dual-space.md#matrix-2-norm) error from the preceding part. Hence **a best [low-rank approximation](../linear-algebra.md#low-rank-approximation) of rank one need not be unique**. More generally, every [orthogonal projection](../hilbert-space.md#orthogonal-projection) $uu^T$ with $\|u\|_2=1$ gives another such minimizer.

<h4 id="2/b/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

<h5 id="2/b/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#2/b/iii)

**No. Distinct [singular values](../linear-algebra.md#singular-value) do not force uniqueness in the [spectral norm](../continuous-dual-space.md#matrix-2-norm).** For an explicit example take

$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
B_t=\begin{pmatrix}2+t&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad -1\leq t\leq1.
$$

The [singular values](../linear-algebra.md#singular-value) of $A$ are $2>1>0$. Every $B_t$ has [matrix rank](../vector-space.md#matrix-rank) one, and its error in the [spectral norm](../continuous-dual-space.md#matrix-2-norm) is

$$
\boxed{\|A-B_t\|_2=\max\{|t|,1\}=1=\sigma_2(A).}
$$

Thus there are infinitely many best rank-one approximations even with a strict gap between the [singular values](../linear-algebra.md#singular-value).

The same mechanism works for every $1\leq r<n$ under the question's positive [singular values](../linear-algebra.md#singular-value). Starting with $A_r$, define

$$
B_t=A_r+t u_1v_1^T,\qquad |t|\leq\sigma_{r+1}.
$$

When the [singular values](../linear-algebra.md#singular-value) are distinct, $\sigma_1+t\geq\sigma_1-\sigma_{r+1}>0$, so every $B_t$ has [matrix rank](../vector-space.md#matrix-rank) $r$, while

$$
\|A-B_t\|_2=\max\{|t|,\sigma_{r+1}\}=\sigma_{r+1}.
$$

The [spectral norm](../continuous-dual-space.md#matrix-2-norm) measures only the largest remaining [singular value](../linear-algebra.md#singular-value), leaving room to change retained components without increasing that maximum. This is the [nonuniqueness of spectral-norm low-rank approximation](../linear-algebra.md#nonuniqueness-of-spectral-norm-low-rank-approximation). The exceptional case $r=0$ is unique for the elementary reason given above.

<h3 id="2/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#2/c)

We use the [spectral theorem](../hilbert-space.md#spectral-theorem) to construct a sufficiently accurate approximate [eigenpair](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenpair). Complete $x$ to an [orthonormal eigenbasis](../linear-operator-theory.md#orthonormal-eigenbasis) $x,x_2,\ldots,x_n$ of the [symmetric matrix](../linear-algebra.md#symmetric-matrix) $B$, with corresponding [eigenvalues](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) $\lambda,\lambda_2,\ldots,\lambda_n$. Simplicity of $\lambda$ means that every $\lambda_j-\lambda$ below is nonzero. Put

$$
a=x^TEx,\qquad
v=-\sum_{j=2}^n\frac{x_j^TEx}{\lambda_j-\lambda}x_j.
$$

The [orthogonal decomposition](../hilbert-space.md#orthogonal-decomposition-by-a-closed-subspace) of $Ex$ shows that $x^Tv=0$ and

$$
(B-\lambda I)v=-(E-aI)x.
$$

With $u_\varepsilon=x+\varepsilon v$ and $\mu_\varepsilon=\lambda+\varepsilon a$, direct expansion cancels every first-order term:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(B+\varepsilon E)u_\varepsilon-\mu_\varepsilon u_\varepsilon
&=\varepsilon\big((B-\lambda I)v+(E-aI)x\big)
+\varepsilon^2(E-aI)v\\
&=\varepsilon^2(E-aI)v.
\end{aligned}
$$

Also $\|u_\varepsilon\|_2^2=1+\varepsilon^2\|v\|_2^2\geq1$. Thus its [eigenpair residual](../numerical-linear-algebra.md#eigenpair-residual) has [Euclidean norm](../normed-vector-space.md#euclidean-norm) $O(\varepsilon^2)$ in [Big O notation](../real-analysis.md#big-o-notation).

For completeness, a real [symmetric matrix](../linear-algebra.md#symmetric-matrix) $M$ always has an [eigenvalue](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) within $\|(M-\mu I)u\|_2/\|u\|_2$ of any real $\mu$ and nonzero $u$. Indeed, writing $u=\sum_jc_jq_j$ in an [orthonormal eigenbasis](../linear-operator-theory.md#orthonormal-eigenbasis) with [eigenvalues](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) $\theta_j$ gives

$$
\|(M-\mu I)u\|_2^2
=\sum_j(\theta_j-\mu)^2c_j^2
\geq\left(\min_j|\theta_j-\mu|\right)^2\|u\|_2^2.
$$

Apply this [symmetric eigenvalue residual bound](../numerical-linear-algebra.md#symmetric-eigenvalue-residual-bound) to $M=B+\varepsilon E$. There is an [eigenvalue](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) $\lambda_\varepsilon$ such that

$$
|\lambda_\varepsilon-(\lambda+\varepsilon a)|
\leq\varepsilon^2\|(E-aI)v\|_2,
$$

which proves

$$
\boxed{\lambda_\varepsilon=\lambda+\varepsilon(x^TEx)+O(\varepsilon^2).}
$$

This establishes the [first-order perturbation of a simple symmetric eigenvalue](../linear-algebra.md#first-order-perturbation-of-a-simple-symmetric-eigenvalue) without presupposing differentiability of the perturbed [eigenvector](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvector). If $n=1$, the sum defining $v$ is empty and the displayed [eigenvalue](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) formula is exact.

## 3

↑ **Parent:** [2021](2021.md)

Use $x_n=nh$, $h=1/N$, and $y_n$ for the numerical approximation in the scalar [initial value problem](../differential-equation.md#initial-value-problem) $y'=f(x,y)$, $y(0)=y_0$, on $[0,1]$.

<h3 id="3/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

<h5 id="3/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#3/a/i)

Here $y'=x/(1+y^2)$ and $y(0)=1$. Since $x\geq0$, the solution is nondecreasing, so $y(x)\geq1$. In fact $0\leq y'\leq1/2$ on $[0,1]$, which also bounds the solution and ensures it extends across this entire interval. The smooth right-hand side makes $y$ twice continuously differentiable, with [second derivative](../calculus.md#second-derivative)

$$
y''=\frac1{1+y^2}-\frac{2x^2y}{(1+y^2)^3}
=\frac{(1+y^2)^2-2x^2y}{(1+y^2)^3}.
$$

For $y\geq1$ and $0\leq x\leq1$,

$$
(1+y^2)^2\geq4y^2\geq2y\geq2x^2y,
$$

so $y''\geq0$. The subtracted term in the first expression is nonnegative, giving the useful bound

$$
\boxed{0\leq y''(x)\leq\frac1{1+y(x)^2}\leq\frac12\qquad(0\leq x\leq1).}
$$

The integral form of the [Taylor remainder](../calculus.md#taylor-remainder) now gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\tau_n
&=\frac{y(x_{n+1})-y(x_n)}h-f(x_n,y(x_n))\\
&=\frac1h\int_{x_n}^{x_{n+1}}(x_{n+1}-t)y''(t)\,dt.
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore the [normalized local truncation error](../numerical-analysis.md#normalized-local-truncation-error) satisfies the stronger estimate

$$
\boxed{0\leq\tau_n\leq\frac h4\leq\frac h2.}
$$

This proves the requested bound for $n=1,\ldots,N-1$, and also for $n=0$, which will be needed to start the [global error](../numerical-analysis.md#global-discretization-error) estimate. For $N=1$ the stated range is empty, but the estimate at $n=0$ still applies.

<h4 id="3/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

<h5 id="3/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#3/a/ii)

For $0\leq x\leq1$ and every real $z$,

$$
|\partial_yf(x,z)|=\frac{2x|z|}{(1+z^2)^2}
\leq\frac{x}{1+z^2}\leq1,
$$

where $2|z|\leq1+z^2$ was used. The [mean value theorem](../calculus.md#mean-value-theorem) therefore proves [Lipschitz continuity](../real-analysis.md#lipschitz-continuity) in $y$ with constant $1$.

Let $e_n=y(x_n)-y_n$. The [explicit Euler method](../numerical-analysis.md#euler-method) and the exact-solution identity from part (i) give

$$
e_{n+1}=e_n+h\big(f(x_n,y(x_n))-f(x_n,y_n)\big)+h\tau_n.
$$

Using the requested [local truncation error](../numerical-analysis.md#local-truncation-error) bound $|\tau_n|\leq h/2$, including at $n=0$, yields

$$
|e_{n+1}|\leq(1+h)|e_n|+\frac{h^2}{2},\qquad e_0=0.
$$

Iteration and the finite [geometric series](../sequence-and-series.md#geometric-series) give

$$
|e_N|\leq\frac{h^2}{2}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}(1+h)^j
=\frac h2\big((1+h)^N-1\big).
$$

Since $1+h\leq e^h$ and $Nh=1$,

$$
\boxed{|y(1)-y_N|\leq\frac{e-1}{2}h.}
$$

Keeping the stronger bound $|\tau_n|\leq h/4$ from part (i) improves the same proof to $(e-1)h/4$. The requested inequality follows already from the weaker bound.

<h3 id="3/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

<h5 id="3/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#3/b/i)

For $f(x,y)=-xy^2$, the [explicit Euler method](../numerical-analysis.md#euler-method) evaluates the right-hand side at the known point $(x_n,y_n)$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{y_{n+1}=y_n-hx_ny_n^2=y_n(1-hx_ny_n),\qquad y_0=1.}
$$

This update requires no [root finding](../numerical-analysis.md#root-finding).

<h4 id="3/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

<h5 id="3/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#3/b/ii)

The [Backward Euler method](../numerical-analysis.md#backward-euler-method) uses the new point:

$$
y_{n+1}=y_n-hx_{n+1}y_{n+1}^2,
\qquad x_{n+1}=x_n+h.
$$

Writing $a=h(x_n+h)>0$ gives the [quadratic equation](../polynomial.md#quadratic-equation) $ay_{n+1}^2+y_{n+1}-y_n=0$. Starting from $y_n>0$, its two [polynomial roots](../polynomial.md#root-of-a-polynomial) have opposite signs. The positive root, which continues the solution as $h\to0$, is

$$
\boxed{y_{n+1}=\frac{-1+\sqrt{1+4h(x_n+h)y_n}}{2h(x_n+h)}.}
$$

The other root is negative and does not approach $y_n$ as $h\to0$. Rationalizing the numerator gives an equivalent form that avoids [cancellation error](../numerical-analysis.md#cancellation-error) when the [step size](../convex-optimization.md#step-size) is small:

$$
\boxed{y_{n+1}=\frac{2y_n}{1+\sqrt{1+4h(x_n+h)y_n}}.}
$$

The defining [quadratic equation](../polynomial.md#quadratic-equation) gives $0<y_{n+1}<y_n$ for this root, so positivity is maintained inductively from $y_0=1$. The branch choice is part of specifying the [Backward Euler method](../numerical-analysis.md#backward-euler-method) here: the algebraic update alone has two solutions.

<h4 id="3/b/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

<h5 id="3/b/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#3/b/iii)

With $f(x,y)=-xy^2-y^5$, write $t=x_{n+1}=x_n+h$ and $z=y_{n+1}$. The [Backward Euler method](../numerical-analysis.md#backward-euler-method) requires

$$
z=y_n-htz^2-hz^5,
$$

or the monic [quintic polynomial](../polynomial.md#quintic-polynomial) equation

$$
p(z)=z^5+tz^2+h^{-1}z-h^{-1}y_n=0.
$$

Construct its $5\times5$ [companion matrix](../linear-operator-theory.md#companion-matrix)

$$
\boxed{C_n=\begin{pmatrix}
0&0&0&0&y_n/h\\
1&0&0&0&-1/h\\
0&1&0&0&-t\\
0&0&1&0&0\\
0&0&0&1&0
\end{pmatrix}.}
$$

Its [characteristic polynomial](../linear-operator-theory.md#characteristic-polynomial) is $\det(zI-C_n)=p(z)$. Thus the [polynomial roots](../polynomial.md#root-of-a-polynomial) needed for this update are exactly the [eigenvalues](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) of $C_n$.

At each step, assemble $C_n$, compute its [eigenvalues](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue), for example with the [QR algorithm](../numerical-analysis.md#qr-algorithm), and choose its unique positive real [eigenvalue](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) as $y_{n+1}$. To justify this selection, let

$$
F(z)=hz^5+htz^2+z-y_n.
$$

If $y_n>0$, then $F(0)=-y_n<0$, $F(y_n)=hy_n^5+hty_n^2>0$, and

$$
F'(z)=5hz^4+2htz+1>0\qquad(z\geq0).
$$

The [intermediate value theorem](../calculus.md#intermediate-value-theorem) and strict [monotonicity](../calculus.md#monotonic-function) prove there is exactly one positive [polynomial root](../polynomial.md#root-of-a-polynomial), located in $(0,y_n)$. Starting at $y_0=1$ therefore makes every update well defined, positive and decreasing. In numerical arithmetic, select the nearly real [eigenvalue](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) in this interval and check the [polynomial residual](../polynomial.md#polynomial-residual) $|F(z)|$; a final [Newton root-finding iteration](../numerical-analysis.md#newton-root-finding-iteration) can refine it. This reduces each update to the requested [eigenvalue problem](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue-problem).

<h3 id="3/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/c/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#3/c)

<h5 id="3/c/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#3/c/i)

**No.** A necessary condition for [consistency of a numerical method](../numerical-analysis.md#consistency-of-a-numerical-method) is reproduction of the constant solution $y(x)=1$ of the [ordinary differential equation](../differential-equation.md#ordinary-differential-equation) $y'=0$. In a [linear multistep method](../linear-multistep-method.md) this gives

$$
\rho(1)=\sum_{j=0}^k\alpha_j=0.
$$

But the reverse [triangle inequality](../topological-analysis.md#triangle-inequality) gives, under the strict coefficient inequality,

$$
\boxed{|\rho(1)|\geq|\alpha_k|-\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}|\alpha_j|>0.}
$$

Thus the necessary [order conditions for a linear multistep method](../linear-multistep-method.md#order-conditions-for-a-linear-multistep-method) already fail at order zero, regardless of the values of the $\beta_j$.

<h4 id="3/c/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#3/c)

<h5 id="3/c/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#3/c/ii)

As appropriate for a genuine $k$-step [linear multistep method](../linear-multistep-method.md), take $\alpha_k\ne0$. Suppose the [characteristic polynomial](../linear-operator-theory.md#characteristic-polynomial) has a [polynomial root](../polynomial.md#root-of-a-polynomial) $\xi$ with [modulus](../complex-analysis.md#modulus) $|\xi|>1$. From $\rho(\xi)=0$ and the [triangle inequality](../topological-analysis.md#triangle-inequality),

$$
\begin{aligned}
|\alpha_k||\xi|^k
&=\left|\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\alpha_j\xi^j\right|\\
&\leq\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}|\alpha_j||\xi|^j\\
&\leq|\xi|^{k-1}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}|\alpha_j|\\
&\leq|\alpha_k||\xi|^{k-1}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Dividing by the positive number $|\alpha_k||\xi|^{k-1}$ gives $|\xi|\leq1$, a contradiction. Hence

$$
\boxed{|\xi_i|\leq1\qquad(i=1,\ldots,k).}
$$

The nonzero leading [coefficient](../vector-space.md#coefficient) matters: if $\alpha_k=0$, the assumed inequality forces all the $\alpha_j$ to vanish, so $\rho$ is the zero [polynomial](../polynomial.md) rather than a [characteristic polynomial](../linear-operator-theory.md#characteristic-polynomial) with $k$ roots. This degenerate case does not define the method in the question.

<h4 id="3/c/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#3/c)

<h5 id="3/c/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#3/c/iii)

**Yes: for $k=2$ the stated inequality guarantees [zero-stability](../linear-multistep-method.md#zero-stability).** Part (ii) puts every [polynomial root](../polynomial.md#root-of-a-polynomial) in the closed [unit disc](../topology.md#unit-disc). The remaining [root condition for a multistep method](../linear-multistep-method.md#root-condition-for-a-multistep-method) is that every root on the [unit circle](../complex-analysis.md#complex-unit-circle) has [multiplicity of a root](../polynomial.md#multiplicity-of-a-root) one.

If this failed for a [quadratic polynomial](../polynomial.md#quadratic-polynomial), it would have a repeated root $\xi$ with $|\xi|=1$, and

$$
\rho(z)=\alpha_2(z-\xi)^2
=\alpha_2z^2-2\alpha_2\xi z+\alpha_2\xi^2.
$$

Consequently $|\alpha_1|=2|\alpha_2|$ and $|\alpha_0|=|\alpha_2|$, contradicting

$$
|\alpha_2|\geq|\alpha_0|+|\alpha_1|=3|\alpha_2|,
$$

because $\alpha_2\ne0$. Thus all roots on the [unit circle](../complex-analysis.md#complex-unit-circle) are simple and

$$
\boxed{\text{The method is zero-stable; no counterexample satisfies the assumption.}}
$$

This conclusion does not assert [consistency of a numerical method](../numerical-analysis.md#consistency-of-a-numerical-method): part (i) rules out [consistency of a numerical method](../numerical-analysis.md#consistency-of-a-numerical-method) whenever the coefficient inequality is strict. [Zero-stability](../linear-multistep-method.md#zero-stability) and [consistency of a numerical method](../numerical-analysis.md#consistency-of-a-numerical-method) are separate requirements.
