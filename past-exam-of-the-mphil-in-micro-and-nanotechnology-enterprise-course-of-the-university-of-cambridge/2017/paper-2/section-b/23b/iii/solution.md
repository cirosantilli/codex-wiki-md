<h1 id="section-b/23b/iii/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](../iii.md)

For fluid [density](../../../../../../density.md) $\rho_f$, speed $U$ and characteristic length $d$, inertial kinetic [energy](../../../../../../energy-split.md) scales as $\rho_fd^3U^2$ while viscous work over distance $d$ scales as $\eta Ud^2$. Their ratio is the **[Reynolds number](../../../../../../reynolds-number.md)**,

$$
\boxed{\mathrm{Re}=\frac{\rho_fUd}{\eta}.}
$$

Smaller particles at a fixed speed have smaller Re, so viscous stresses dominate fluid inertia when $\mathrm{Re}\ll1$. Increasing speed increases Re and eventually makes inertia more important, rather than making viscous dominance stronger.

Particle inertia is a separate issue. Its [particle velocity-relaxation time](../../../../../../particle-velocity-relaxation-time.md) is $\tau_p=\rho_pd^2/(18\eta)$; comparing it with a travel time $d/U$ gives a [Stokes number](../../../../../../stokes-number.md) $\tau_pU/d$. This uses particle [density](../../../../../../density.md) $\rho_p$, whereas the fluid [Reynolds number](../../../../../../reynolds-number.md) uses fluid [density](../../../../../../density.md) $\rho_f$.
