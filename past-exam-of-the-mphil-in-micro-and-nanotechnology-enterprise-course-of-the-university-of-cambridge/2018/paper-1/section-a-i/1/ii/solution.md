<h1 id="section-a-i/1/ii/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](../ii.md)

For a displayed width $D$ representing a scanned specimen width $d$, **the linear image [magnification](../../../../../../magnification.md) is**

$$
\boxed{M=\frac{D}{d}.}
$$

The user changes the scan-coil excursion, hence $d$. A smaller raster displayed at the same width gives larger [magnification](../../../../../../magnification.md). Changing the display or print size also changes displayed [magnification](../../../../../../magnification.md), so the calibrated scale bar is more portable than a [magnification](../../../../../../magnification.md) label. This is the scan/display principle described in the manufacturer's microscope guide.[Magnification of SEM, printed p.7: scan width and display width; the same raster-display geometry applies in STEM](../../../../../../scanning-electron-microscope-a-to-z-by-jeol.md)

At the low-magnification extreme, the probe scans the widest accessible field. Coil range, apertures, scan distortion and maintaining focus constrain the actual usable width. Formally increasing $d$ without bound would make $M$ arbitrarily small, but a real column has a finite accessible field.

At the high-magnification extreme, the scan covers a very small region. Formally $d\to0$ makes $D/d$ diverge, but exactly zero scan width samples one location and supplies no spatial image. [Electron microscope resolution](../../../../../../electron-microscope-resolution.md), finite probe size, pixel spacing, drift, noise and specimen damage set the useful limit. Enlarging a field already sampled more finely than its resolvable information produces **[empty magnification](../../../../../../empty-magnification.md)**, not new structural detail. For $N$ pixels across a field, the specimen sampling interval is $d/N$; this is distinct from the probe's [spatial resolution](../../../../../../spatial-resolution.md).
