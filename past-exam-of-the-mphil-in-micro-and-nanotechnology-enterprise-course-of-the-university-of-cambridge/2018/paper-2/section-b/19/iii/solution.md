<h1 id="section-b/19/iii/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](../iii.md)

A larger source–drain bias opens an [energy](../../../../../../energy-split.md) window between reservoir [electrochemical potentials](../../../../../../electrochemical-potential.md). When a charge-addition/removal transition enters that window, sequential tunnelling becomes allowed even away from a zero-bias degeneracy. The low-conductance regions in a gate/bias map are **[Coulomb diamonds](../../../../../../coulomb-diamond.md)**.

For an illustrative symmetric device with reservoir potentials $\pm eV_{\rm SD}/2$, let $q=Q_{\rm ext}/e$ and $E_{\rm ch}=e^2/C$. In the charge-$N$ interval, the classical blockade domain is

$$
|eV_{\rm SD}|<E_{\rm ch}[1-2|q-N|].
$$

It shrinks to zero at $q=N\pm1/2$. Unequal [capacitances](../../../../../../capacitance.md) change edge slopes and voltage division; the figure's symmetry is a chosen model, not a universal device property.

Quantum confinement adds discrete single-particle levels and spin structure. In a constant-interaction approximation, successive addition energies contain $e^2/C$ plus the relevant orbital spacing, so diamond sizes need not all match. Excited-state tunnelling can produce extra conductance lines. At stronger coupling, [cotunnelling](../../../../../../cotunnelling.md) and charge fluctuations can give leakage beyond a simple sequential classical model. Thus [temperature](../../../../../../temperature-split.md) and coupling must be small enough to resolve the charging and level structure separately.
