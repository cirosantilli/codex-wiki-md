<h1 id="8/i/c/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [C](../c.md)

Atomic-number contrast can explain why the gold-rich regions are bright and why the phosphorus-rich NiP$_y$ layer should be somewhat darker than the other [nickel](../../../../../../nickel.md)–[phosphorus](../../../../../../phosphorus.md) layers. However, the calculated alloy yields differ only modestly: the NiP$_y$ and NiP$_z$ yields differ by about $5.5\%$ relative to NiP$_z$. **Composition-only backscattering does not explain all of the strong local edge and defect contrast.**

An [Everhart–Thornley detector](../../../../../../everhart-thornley-detector.md) normally collects [secondary electrons](../../../../../../secondary-electron.md), not a pure backscattered-electron signal. Secondary-electron escape and collection depend strongly on local slope and edge geometry. Differential polishing, relief at interfaces, a crack or cavity, surface [oxidation](../../../../../../oxidation.md) and local charging can therefore alter brightness without the corresponding change in bulk [atomic number](../../../../../../atomic-number.md). [backscattered electrons](../../../../../../backscattered-electron.md) can also generate [secondary electrons](../../../../../../secondary-electron.md) elsewhere. Crystallographic channeling and orientation can contribute additional contrast. The observed dark and bright areas should therefore be correlated with topography and composition rather than assigned a phase from intensity alone.
