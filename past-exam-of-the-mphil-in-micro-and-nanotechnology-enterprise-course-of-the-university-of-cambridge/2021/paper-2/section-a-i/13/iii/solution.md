<h1 id="section-a-i/13/iii/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](../iii.md)

One application is a **semiconducting nanotube transistor array**. Removing metallic tubes prevents conducting bridges that would bypass gate control; a narrow chirality distribution also narrows the spread of [band gaps](../../../../../../band-gap.md) and switching behaviour. High [charge carrier mobility](../../../../../../charge-carrier-mobility.md) and thin flexible channels are attractive, but [contact resistance](../../../../../../contact-resistance.md), tube alignment, residual metallic contamination, [dielectric](../../../../../../dielectric.md) traps and the cost/yield of sorting still limit uniform large-area circuits.

A second is a **wavelength-selective near-infrared [photodetector](../../../../../../photodetector.md) or emitter**. A selected chirality concentrates absorption and emission around its characteristic [nanotube optical transitions](../../../../../../nanotube-optical-transition.md), avoiding the broad mixed-tube response. Optical selectivity can be tailored by choosing the species. Limitations include low absolute emission yield, dark [exciton](../../../../../../exciton.md) states, contact-related recombination, environmental spectral shifts and quenching by bundles or metallic contaminants. Optical and electrical characterization after device processing is needed; the sorting label alone does not guarantee device performance.
