<h1 id="section-b/19/iii/b/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [B](../b.md)

I would choose **[liquid-phase exfoliation](../../../../../../../liquid-phase-exfoliation.md) of [graphite](../../../../../../../graphite.md) into few-layer [graphene](../../../../../../../graphene.md)**, followed by controlled electrode formation. It uses bulk feedstock and preserves much of the $sp^2$ framework; a battery electrode does not require a wafer-sized perfect monolayer.

Disperse [graphite](../../../../../../../graphite.md) in a compatible liquid, apply shear or sonication, and classify the dispersion to separate thin flakes from unexfoliated material. Recover and wash the selected fraction, remove contaminants and dispersants, then coat a Cu current collector with a suitably bound, porous and electrically connected film. Drying and consolidation should establish contacts without collapsing every transport pore. Characterize thickness and size distributions, residual impurities and conductivity after processing.

The advantages are solution compatibility, accessible feedstock and less basal-plane chemical disruption than oxidation–reduction routes. Limitations include a distribution of layer numbers, limited single-pass yield, flake fragmentation, solvent recovery and drying-induced restacking. Solvent toxicity and retained surfactants matter for manufacturing. In the original exfoliation study, microscopy and spectroscopy distinguished thin flakes from [graphite](../../../../../../../graphite.md) and checked structural preservation; those controls precede any performance claim.[2008, pp. 563–568, preparation and microscopy/spectroscopy characterization](../../../../../../../high-yield-production-of-graphene-by-liquid-phase-exfoliation-of-graphite-by-yenny-hernandez-and-others.md)

Oxidation–exfoliation–reduction is another scalable route, but irreversible lattice damage and residual oxygen make conductivity and first-cycle losses separate concerns.
