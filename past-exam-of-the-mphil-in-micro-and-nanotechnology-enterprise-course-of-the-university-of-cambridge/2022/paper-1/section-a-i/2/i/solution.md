In [piezoresponse force microscopy](../../../../../../piezoresponse-force-microscopy.md), a conducting [atomic force microscopy](../../../../../../atomic-force-microscopy.md) tip contacts a sample with a counter-electrode. Apply $V(t)=V_{\rm dc}+V_{\rm ac}\cos\omega t$. The [converse piezoelectric effect](../../../../../../converse-piezoelectric-effect.md) produces an oscillatory displacement, approximately $u_\omega=d_{\rm eff}V_{\rm ac}$. Contact with the moving surface bends the cantilever, and a [lock-in amplifier](../../../../../../lock-in-amplifier.md) records the displacement amplitude and phase relative to the drive.

For opposite out-of-plane [ferroelectric domains](../../../../../../ferroelectric-domain.md), the effective piezoelectric coefficient changes sign. Their vertical response therefore differs by approximately **$180^\circ$ in phase**. Equivalent antiparallel domains can have similar amplitudes, while cancellation or reduced response near a wall can produce an amplitude minimum. Phase identifies the relative response sign; an uncalibrated detector phase is not an absolute polarization direction.

Use a small drive below the switching threshold while imaging, a stable contact force and a well-characterized contact resonance. Electrostatic forces, contact stiffness and surface charging can also create signals at the drive frequency; observing contrast alone is insufficient evidence for polarization switching.[2002, electromechanical and electrostatic contrast regimes](../../../../../../imaging-mechanism-of-piezoresponse-force-microscopy-of-ferroelectric-surfaces-by-sergei-v-kalinin-and-dawn-a-bonnell.md)
