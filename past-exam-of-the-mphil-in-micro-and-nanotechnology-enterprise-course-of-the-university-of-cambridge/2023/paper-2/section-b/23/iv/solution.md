<h1 id="section-b/23/iv/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](../iv.md)

Let $A$ be projected area. Close-packed circular footprints cover $\phi A$ and the exposed flat substrate covers $(1-\phi)A$. A hemisphere's curved area is twice its circular footprint, so the actual wetted solid area is

$$
A_{\mathrm{wet}}=2\phi A+(1-\phi)A=(1+\phi)A.
$$

The [Wenzel roughness factor](../../../../../../wenzel-roughness-factor.md) is therefore $r=A_{\mathrm{wet}}/A=1+\phi=1.9069$, not $\phi$ or $2$.

Using the [Wenzel wetting](../../../../../../wenzel-wetting.md) relation,

$$
\cos\theta^*=1.9069\cos68^\circ\simeq0.7143,
\qquad \theta^*\simeq44.4^\circ.
$$

The nanostructure makes this surface **more wettable**, because planar PMMA already has $\theta<90^\circ$. Under complete Wenzel wetting, roughening amplifies the underlying hydrophilic character; it does not turn this material hydrophobic.

The calculation assumes identical chemistry on spheres and substrate, complete liquid penetration, ideal hemispheres, a macroscopic contact angle and negligible pinning/line-tension effects. Actual dried particles may have different contact geometry, deformation, contamination or trapped air. A hydrophobic coating with planar angle above $90^\circ$ would make roughness increase the angle instead. Designing a texture that retains air in a [Cassie–Baxter state](../../../../../../cassie-baxter-state.md) is another route, but its stability against liquid penetration must be checked.
