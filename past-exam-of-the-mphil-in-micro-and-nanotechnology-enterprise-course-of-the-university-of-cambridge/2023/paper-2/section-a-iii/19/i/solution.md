<h1 id="section-a-iii/19/i/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [I](../i.md)

Refraction changes the propagation direction, and hence the [momentum](../../../../../../momentum-split.md), of transmitted [light](../../../../../../light.md). The particle receives the opposite momentum change by [conservation of momentum](../../../../../../momentum-conservation.md). For a ray carrying power $P_r$, a useful force scale is

$$
\mathbf F_r\sim\frac{n_mP_r}{c}
(\hat{\mathbf s}_{\mathrm{in}}-\hat{\mathbf s}_{\mathrm{out}}),
$$

with corrections for reflected rays and their momentum fluxes. Here $n_m$ is the surrounding refractive index; this is a ray-force scaling rather than a complete electromagnetic stress calculation.

For fixed geometry and refractive indices, $P_r=I\,\delta A$, so the force contribution is proportional to the local intensity $I$. Refraction toward the normal on entry and away from the normal on exit must both be included: a force inferred from only the first interface can have the wrong direction.

The specified size is larger than the wavelength, so a [geometrical optics](../../../../../../geometrical-optics-split.md) treatment is appropriate despite the generic “nanoparticle” label. A dipole/Rayleigh-particle formula would use the opposite size limit.
