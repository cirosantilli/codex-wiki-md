↑ **Parent:** [Iv](../iv.md)

Roughness can produce a distribution of local switching fields and nonuniform reversal. The abrupt edges in the ideal [pseudo spin valve](../../../../../../pseudo-spin-valve.md) loops then become rounded or sloping, the antiparallel plateau becomes less flat, and overlapping layer-reversal distributions can narrow or partly eliminate the clear high-resistance window. The middle panel of the original diagram illustrates only this broadening mechanism.

Correlated roughness on the two interfaces can also introduce [orange-peel coupling](../../../../../../orange-peel-coupling.md), a magnetostatic coupling that favors parallel alignment. Such coupling shifts switching fields and can suppress independent reversal. Sufficiently severe roughness or pinholes can have a similar qualitative effect by directly coupling the ferromagnets.

Changes in amplitude require care. Some spin-dependent interface scattering contributes to [giant magnetoresistance](../../../../../../giant-magnetoresistance.md), so a small increase in roughness is not guaranteed to lower the signal monotonically. Intermixing, spin-flip scattering and an incompletely antiparallel state generally reduce useful contrast. The detailed width, displacement and height of $R(H)$ therefore depend on how the interfaces become rough, rather than on a single roughness number.
