<h1 id="section-b/4/b/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [B](../b.md)

Substitute harmonic amplitudes with the specified $e^{-i\omega t}$ convention:

$$
m(\omega_0^2-\omega^2-i\gamma\omega)\mathbf u_0=q\mathbf E_0.
$$

One electron contributes a dipole moment $\mathbf p=q\mathbf u_0$, so the [electric polarization](../../../../../polarization-density.md) of a density $n$ is

$$
\mathbf P_0=nq\mathbf u_0
=\frac{nq^2}{m(\omega_0^2-\omega^2-i\gamma\omega)}\mathbf E_0.
$$

Using $\mathbf D=\epsilon_0\mathbf E+\mathbf P=\epsilon_0\epsilon_r\mathbf E$ gives

$$
\boxed{\epsilon_r(\omega)=1+\frac{\omega_p^2}{\omega_0^2-\omega^2-i\gamma\omega},\qquad
\omega_p^2=\frac{nq^2}{m\epsilon_0}.}
$$

Thus the paper's dimensionless $\epsilon_\omega$ is the [relative permittivity](../../../../../relative-permittivity.md), rather than the absolute [permittivity](../../../../../permittivity-split.md) $\epsilon_0\epsilon_r$. The electron's negative charge disappears from the [electric susceptibility](../../../../../electric-susceptibility.md) because both the force and dipole moment contain $q$.
