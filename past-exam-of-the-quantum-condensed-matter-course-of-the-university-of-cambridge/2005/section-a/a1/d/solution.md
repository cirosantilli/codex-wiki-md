<h1 id="section-a/a1/d/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [D](../d.md)

[Wetting](../../../../../wetting.md) concerns how a liquid covers a solid in contact with another phase, such as its vapor. For an ideal flat homogeneous surface, balance of the three interfacial tensions gives [Young's equation](../../../../../young-s-equation.md)

$$
\boxed{\gamma_{SV}-\gamma_{SL}=\gamma_{LV}\cos\theta,}
$$

where the [contact angle](../../../../../contact-angle.md) $\theta$ is measured through the liquid. Small angle means favorable contact; partial wetting gives a finite angle. The spreading coefficient $S=\gamma_{SV}-\gamma_{SL}-\gamma_{LV}$ compares a covered surface with a dry one. Positive $S$ favors [complete wetting](../../../../../complete-wetting.md), while negative $S$ permits a finite-angle droplet in the partial-wetting regime.

A sessile-drop experiment images the tangent at the contact line on a clean level substrate. Varying drop volume slowly distinguishes advancing and receding angles; their difference is [contact-angle hysteresis](../../../../../contact-angle-hysteresis.md) from pinning and heterogeneity, so a single observed angle need not equal the ideal equilibrium value. A capillary-rise experiment gives a second consequence: the [Young–Laplace equation](../../../../../young-laplace-equation.md) and hydrostatic balance predict $h=2\gamma_{LV}\cos\theta/(\rho g r)$ in a narrow circular tube when meniscus-volume corrections are negligible. A wetting liquid rises, whereas a sufficiently nonwetting one is depressed.
