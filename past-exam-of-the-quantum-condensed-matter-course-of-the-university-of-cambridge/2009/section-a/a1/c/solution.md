<h1 id="section-a/a1/c/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [C](../c.md)

In the planar [Debye–Hückel approximation](../../../../../debye-huckel-approximation.md), a surface potential decays as $\psi(x)=\psi_s e^{-\kappa x}$, with $\kappa\propto\sqrt I$ at fixed [temperature](../../../../../temperature-split.md) and solvent [permittivity](../../../../../permittivity-split.md); $I$ is the [ionic strength](../../../../../ionic-strength.md). For a monovalent salt, reducing its [concentration](../../../../../concentration.md) by ten reduces $\kappa$ by $\sqrt{10}$. Calibrating the first decay from the supplied potential ratio, and assuming unchanged $\psi_s$, gives

$$
\boxed{\psi_{\rm new}(5\ \mathrm{nm})=35\left(\frac{6.6}{35}\right)^{1/\sqrt{10}}\ \mathrm{meV}=20.7\ \mathrm{meV}.}
$$

Here the printed meV unit is interpreted as the electrostatic potential energy of a singly charged probe; the same ratio calculation applies to potentials in mV.

This numerical answer uses an effective exponential decay calibrated from the data. The inferred initial [Debye length](../../../../../debye-length.md) is $5/\log(35/6.6)=3.00\ \mathrm{nm}$, whereas the standard dilute aqueous-electrolyte expression at room temperature gives about $0.96\ \mathrm{nm}$ for $0.1\ \mathrm M$ monovalent salt. The numbers therefore are not consistent with that absolute ideal-electrolyte model. Moreover a spherical colloid has an additional radius-dependent geometric factor, and its surface potential need not remain constant when salt is changed. Without a surface boundary condition and geometry, the new potential is not uniquely specified beyond the stated planar, fixed-potential approximation.
