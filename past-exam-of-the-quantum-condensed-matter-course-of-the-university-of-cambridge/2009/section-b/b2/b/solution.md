<h1 id="section-b/b2/b/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [B](../b.md)

[Electrons](../../../../../electron.md) contribute magnetic response through both [spin](../../../../../spin.md) and orbital motion. In an ordinary weakly interacting metal, a field splits the two spin energies. Only states near the [Fermi energy](../../../../../fermi-energy.md) can change occupancy because of the [Pauli exclusion principle](../../../../../pauli-exclusion-principle.md), producing [Pauli paramagnetism](../../../../../pauli-paramagnetism.md). For a total two-spin density of states $g(E_F)$, the simple result is $\chi_P=\mu_0\mu_B^2g(E_F)$. Orbital cyclotron motion gives [Landau diamagnetism](../../../../../landau-diamagnetism.md); for an isotropic parabolic free-electron band it contributes $-\chi_P/3$. Filled shells can also supply a diamagnetic background.

Interactions can favor parallel spin occupancy. In an itinerant description, the exchange-energy gain competes with the kinetic-energy cost of redistributing electrons between spin bands. The [Stoner criterion](../../../../../stoner-criterion.md) describes when an unpolarized state becomes unstable, with the precise factor in $Ig(E_F)$ depending on whether the density of states counts one spin or both. This mechanism can yield spontaneous [ferromagnetism](../../../../../ferromagnetism.md) and magnetic domains. Alternatively, sufficiently localized moments in a metal can interact through conduction electrons. A high density of states alone is therefore not a complete microscopic explanation of a material's magnetism; band structure, electron interactions and temperature matter.
