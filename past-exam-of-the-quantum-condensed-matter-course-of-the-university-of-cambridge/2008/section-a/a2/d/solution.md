<h1 id="section-a/a2/d/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [D](../d.md)

For a microscopic swimmer, the [Reynolds number](../../../../../reynolds-number.md) $\mathrm{Re}=\rho UL/\eta$ is usually small. Taking $L=2\ \mu\mathrm m$, $U=20\ \mu\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ and water-like properties gives $\mathrm{Re}\simeq4\times10^{-5}$. The [Stokes equations](../../../../../stokes-equation.md) then balance viscous [stress](../../../../../stress.md) and [pressure](../../../../../pressure-split.md) almost instantaneously, so a swimmer cannot coast appreciably when its propulsive stroke stops.

The [scallop theorem](../../../../../scallop-theorem.md) rules out a reciprocal opening-and-closing stroke in an otherwise unchanged Newtonian environment: reversing the sequence of shapes reverses the motion, and changing the stroke speed does not prevent cancellation. Organisms therefore use nonreciprocal motion. A sperm-like bending wave travels along a flagellum; a bacterium can rotate a helical flagellar bundle. The unequal parallel and perpendicular drag of a slender filament converts those motions into a net thrust. A freely suspended swimmer is [force-free](../../../../../force-free.md) and [torque-free](../../../../../torque-free.md) overall, so body drag balances thrust and the body counter-rotates against the rotating bundle. Tracking a body and its flagellum together can reveal these opposite rotations. The [helical microswimmer with a spherical head](../../../../../helical-microswimmer-with-a-spherical-head.md) makes that force and torque balance quantitative.
