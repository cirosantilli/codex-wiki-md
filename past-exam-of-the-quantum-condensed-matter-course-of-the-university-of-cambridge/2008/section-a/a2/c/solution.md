<h1 id="section-a/a2/c/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [C](../c.md)

[DLVO theory](../../../../../dlvo-theory.md) combines the attractive [Van der Waals force](../../../../../van-der-waals-force.md) with repulsion between overlapping electrical double layers. A charged colloid attracts counterions; their electrostatic and osmotic response screens its field over the [Debye length](../../../../../debye-length.md). Adding salt generally shortens this range and can lower the repulsive aggregation barrier, allowing particles to reach the strongly bound primary minimum. A shallow secondary minimum can instead give weak reversible association. Whether Brownian encounters cause rapid aggregation depends on barrier heights relative to $k_BT$, not just on the sign of the force at one separation.

Adsorbed or grafted polymer layers can provide [steric stabilization](../../../../../steric-stabilization.md): overlap costs chain [entropy](../../../../../entropy-split.md) and increases the local [osmotic pressure](../../../../../osmotic-pressure.md). Nonadsorbing smaller particles or polymers can create a [depletion force](../../../../../depletion-force.md), because bringing large surfaces together increases the volume accessible to the smaller species. Polymer bridging can be attractive when one chain binds more than one particle. A useful comparison experiment varies salt concentration while monitoring scattered light or aggregation, then repeats with a sterically protected suspension. Charge, solvent quality, polymer coverage and particle size determine which interaction dominates; none is a universal constant of all colloids.
