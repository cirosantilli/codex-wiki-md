# Electrolytic cell

↑ **Parent:** [Electrolysis](electrolysis.md)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrolytic_cell)

An [electrolytic cell](electrolytic-cell.md) uses an external source of electrical [work](work.md) to drive [electrode](electrode.md) [chemical reactions](chemical-reaction-split.md). In the ordinary two-terminal driven mode, its positive [anode](anode.md) supports [oxidation](oxidation.md) and its negative [cathode](cathode.md) supports [reduction](reduction-chemistry.md). The source removes [electrons](electron.md) from the [anode](anode.md) and supplies [electrons](electron.md) to the [cathode](cathode.md); through the [electrolyte](electrolyte.md), net [conventional current](conventional-current.md) goes from [anode](anode.md) toward [cathode](cathode.md). Terminal signs refer to the driven cell's terminals relative to each other. The source supplies electrical [power](power.md) to the cell. See [17.7 Electrolysis](chemistry-2e-by-paul-flowers-and-others/17-7-electrolysis.md) (§17.7: electrolysis and electroplating).

For example, transferring [copper](copper.md) from a soluble [copper](copper.md) [anode](anode.md) onto a conducting object at the [cathode](cathode.md) has the ideal [half-reactions](half-reaction.md)

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{positive anode:}\quad&\mathrm{Cu}(s)\longrightarrow\mathrm{Cu}^{2+}(aq)+2e^-,\\
\text{negative cathode:}\quad&\mathrm{Cu}^{2+}(aq)+2e^-\longrightarrow\mathrm{Cu}(s).
\end{aligned}
$$

Their sum transfers [copper](copper.md) between surfaces. For identical bulk states, this transfer need not have a positive bulk [Gibbs free energy](gibbs-free-energy.md) change; finite [electric current](electric-current.md) still needs applied drive to overcome losses. Thus "consumes electrical [work](work.md)" does not mean every [electrolytic cell](electrolytic-cell.md) stores all that [work](work.md) as chemical [Gibbs free energy](gibbs-free-energy.md). [Electrolysis](electrolysis.md) can instead redistribute material. The charge-to-amount relation remains $\xi=Q/(2F)$ when only these [half-reactions](half-reaction.md) carry the [electric current](electric-current.md); competing [water](water.md) reactions would change the deposited amount. The electroplating principle and charge accounting are described in [17.7 Electrolysis](chemistry-2e-by-paul-flowers-and-others/17-7-electrolysis.md) (§17.7: electroplating and quantitative aspects).

The University of Washington's teaching demonstration makes this material transfer visible with a [copper](copper.md) [electrode](electrode.md) and a stainless-steel [electrode](electrode.md) in a glass cell containing $0.5\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ $\mathrm{CuSO_4}$. The external source connects its positive lead to [copper](copper.md) and negative lead to steel; the reported observation is a [copper](copper.md) coating on the steel. Reversing the source polarity removes the deposited [copper](copper.md) again: the coated surface now supports [oxidation](oxidation.md) and acts as the [anode](anode.md). This polarity reversal changes the drive, unlike swapping the measurement leads in the [Daniell cell voltage and load experiment](daniell-cell-voltage-and-load-experiment.md). Coating appearance alone does not measure charge efficiency or exclude competing [chemical reactions](chemical-reaction-split.md). See [University of Washington, Electroplating Copper on Stainless Steel: apparatus and reversible coating demonstration](https://chem.washington.edu/lecture-demos/electroplating-copper-stainless-steel).

The [copper](copper.md) [cathode](cathode.md) in this driven example is negative, while the [copper](copper.md) [cathode](cathode.md) of a discharging [Daniell cell](daniell-cell.md) is positive: both support exactly the same [reduction](reduction-chemistry.md) [half-reaction](half-reaction.md). Likewise, an [anode](anode.md) need not lose material if its conducting surface is absent from its net [half-reaction](half-reaction.md). Neither terminal sign nor a universal mass-change rule defines an [anode](anode.md) or [cathode](cathode.md).
