**Prevention can reduce the probability of later [mental disorders](../../../mental-disorder.md) by changing modifiable risk and protective processes before disorder develops; it cannot guarantee that every case will be avoided.** A convincing claim needs an appropriate comparison group, assessment of later disorder or relevant functioning, and evidence that the intervention preceded the outcome. Improving knowledge immediately after a lesson is not equivalent to preventing a first episode years later.

The [universal prevention](../../../universal-prevention.md), [selective prevention](../../../selective-prevention.md) and [indicated prevention](../../../indicated-prevention.md) distinction concerns _who is offered the intervention_. Universal programs address a whole population regardless of individual risk; selective programs target groups with elevated risk; indicated programs target individuals already showing early signs who do not currently meet the relevant diagnostic threshold. This classification differs from the treatment of an established disorder and from [relapse prevention](../../../relapse-prevention.md). It also differs from whether delivery occurs in a school, family or clinic. The 2009 National Academies framework explicitly distinguishes these complementary approaches.[Chapter 3, Defining the Scope of Prevention; the 1994 IOM framework and Recommendation 3-1](../../../preventing-mental-emotional-and-behavioral-disorders-among-young-people-2009.md)

A universal example is the [Good Behavior Game](../../../good-behavior-game.md), tested in early primary-school classrooms by [Sheppard G. Kellam](../../../sheppard-g-kellam.md) and colleagues. Teachers use explicit classroom rules, mixed teams and contingent group rewards to support cooperative behavior and reduce disruption. The proposed mechanism is a change in daily peer and classroom contingencies, improving behavioral regulation at an early developmental stage rather than waiting for a later diagnostic crisis. Because the whole class participates, children are not selected solely by a clinical risk score.

The Baltimore [cluster-randomized trials](../../../cluster-randomized-trial.md) followed participants into young adulthood. Benefits included reductions in several substance-use and antisocial outcomes among males, especially those initially more aggressive and disruptive. A second cohort with less teacher mentoring showed smaller or less consistent effects for several outcomes. Thus a universal offer can have heterogeneous benefits, and implementation support matters. The findings do not show prevention of every disorder or establish equal effects in all populations. Classroom allocation and school context also mean that statistical analysis must respect clustering rather than treating every child as an independently randomized unit.[2008, Baltimore design, young-adult outcomes and replication cohort](../../../effects-of-a-universal-classroom-behavior-management-program-in-first-and-second-grades-on-young-adult-behavioral-psychiatric-and-social-outcomes-by-sheppard-g-kellam-and-others.md)

A selective example is the [Family Bereavement Program](../../../family-bereavement-program.md), evaluated by [Irwin Sandler](../../../irwin-sandler.md) and colleagues. Eligibility follows parental bereavement, an elevated-risk circumstance, rather than requiring every child already to have a disorder. Parallel caregiver and youth groups develop positive parenting, [coping](../../../coping.md) and communication skills; the program seeks to change the family processes through which a difficult event may affect later adjustment. Bereavement is not itself evidence that a child is psychiatrically ill, so a preventive offer should avoid pathologizing ordinary grief.

The [randomized controlled trial](../../../randomized-controlled-trial.md) used a literature-control condition providing bereavement books. At six years, intervention participants showed benefits on several measures, including externalizing problems, teacher-rated internalizing problems and self-esteem; parents also had lower [depression](../../../depression.md) scores. Diagnostic effects were moderated by baseline problems rather than being a uniform reduction in all diagnoses. This is evidence for a durable change in several outcomes, with the advantage of multiple informants, but the control did not match group contact or [attention](../../../attention.md). Attrition and informant disagreement must be examined before interpreting the program as a universally effective causal package.[2010, 12-session intervention, literature control, six-year outcomes and moderation](../../../six-year-follow-up-of-a-preventive-intervention-for-parentally-bereaved-youths-by-irwin-sandler-and-others.md)

[Indicated prevention](../../../indicated-prevention.md) is illustrated by cognitive-behavioral skills programs for young people with elevated but subdiagnostic symptoms. These teach ways to evaluate negative predictions, solve problems and manage stress before a current depressive disorder is established. [Judy Garber](../../../judy-garber.md) and colleagues randomized 316 adolescents with parental [depression](../../../depression.md) histories and either elevated symptoms, previous [depression](../../../depression.md) or both to a group program or usual care. The program included eight weekly sessions and six monthly continuation sessions. It combines selective familial-risk targeting with indicated or recurrence-prevention elements; it should not be relabeled as a pure first-onset trial.

By postcontinuation follow-up, depressive episodes occurred in approximately $21.4\%$ of the intervention group versus $32.7\%$ with usual care. The absolute [risk difference](../../../risk-difference.md) is $11.3$ percentage points, corresponding to roughly nine participants treated per additional episode prevented over this follow-up, using the reported rounded rates; this is an approximate [number needed to treat](../../../number-needed-to-treat.md), not an individual guarantee. Benefit was not demonstrated when the parent was currently depressed at baseline. These results support a preventive contribution while identifying family context as a potential limit; because some adolescents had previous episodes, they do not establish an identical effect on first-ever disorder. A subgroup result also needs [replication](../../../replication-in-experimental-design.md) rather than being turned into an absolute eligibility rule.[2009, inclusion criteria, program, incident-depression results and baseline parental-depression interaction](../../../prevention-of-depression-in-at-risk-adolescents-by-judy-garber-and-others.md)

These programs suggest several routes to prevention: alter a high-frequency social environment, strengthen family support after an identifiable risk event, and teach [coping](../../../coping.md) before symptoms become a current disorder. They also show why a [risk factor](../../../risk-factor.md) is not automatically a useful intervention target. It may be a marker rather than a cause, or may be impractical to change. Randomizing a program tests its overall effect; identifying its active mechanism additionally requires measurement of the proposed mediator before the outcome and care about alternative pathways.

Evaluation should distinguish symptom reduction, new diagnoses, recurrence and functioning, with sufficiently long follow-up and assessors unaware of allocation where feasible. Universal programs can reach many future cases and reduce selection barriers, but dilute average effects and require substantial resources. Targeted approaches concentrate effort, but can miss people whose risk is not recognized and can introduce labeling or access inequalities. Adverse effects, participation, fidelity, costs and transportability therefore belong alongside [statistical significance](../../../statistical-significance.md). **The strongest strategy combines developmentally appropriate universal support with proportionate targeted intervention, while making only the preventive claims that the design and outcomes actually establish.**
