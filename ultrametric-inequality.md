# Ultrametric inequality

↑ **Parent:** [Non-Archimedean absolute value](non-archimedean-absolute-value.md)

The ultrametric inequality, also called the strong [triangle inequality](triangle-inequality.md), bounds a direct distance by the larger of the two distances through an intermediate point:

$$
d(x,z)\leq\max\{d(x,y),d(y,z)\}.
$$

A [metric space](metric-space.md) satisfying this rule is an [ultrametric space](ultrametric-space.md). For a [Non-Archimedean absolute value](non-archimedean-absolute-value.md), the corresponding [inequality](inequality-mathematics.md) is $|a+b|\leq\max\{|a|,|b|\}$, and the associated [metric](metric.md) is $d(x,y)=|x-y|$. The ordinary [triangle inequality](triangle-inequality.md) follows because the maximum of two nonnegative [real numbers](real-number.md) is at most their [sum](sum.md).

For example, the [2-adic absolute value](2-adic-absolute-value.md) gives the three points $0,1,8$ distances

$$
d_2(0,1)=1,\qquad d_2(1,8)=|-7|_2=1,\qquad d_2(0,8)=\frac18.
$$

Each distance is bounded by the maximum of the other two. The two largest distances are equal: every triangle in an [ultrametric space](ultrametric-space.md) has this property. Otherwise its unique longest side would exceed the maximum of the other two, contradicting the [ultrametric inequality](ultrametric-inequality.md). The drawn lengths of a triangle on paper need not represent these distances.

The [ultrametric inequality](ultrametric-inequality.md) gives a useful exact rule when $|a|\ne|b|$:

$$
|a+b|=\max\{|a|,|b|\}.
$$

To see why, suppose $|a|>|b|$. The upper bound is $|a+b|\leq|a|$; applying the [ultrametric inequality](ultrametric-inequality.md) to $a=(a+b)-b$ forces the reverse bound. Equal sizes can instead cancel: $|1|_2=|1|_2=1$, but $|1+1|_2=1/2$. This makes a uniquely largest term decisive in a [sum](sum.md), a principle used in the [Newton polygon](newton-polygon.md) and in arguments about [roots of a polynomial](root-of-a-polynomial.md).
