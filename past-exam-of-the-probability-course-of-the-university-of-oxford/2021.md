↑ **Parent:** [2021](2021.md)

<h3 id="1/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1/a)

The [cumulative distribution function](../probability-distribution.md#cumulative-distribution-function) of the [uniform distribution](../continuous-probability-distribution.md#continuous-uniform-distribution) on $[0,1/n]$ is

$$
F_n(t)=\begin{cases}0,&t<0,\\nt,&0\leq t\leq1/n,\\1,&t>1/n.\end{cases}
$$

The constant zero has [cumulative distribution function](../probability-distribution.md#cumulative-distribution-function) $F(t)=\mathbf1_{[0,\infty)}(t)$. Its [continuity points](../calculus.md#continuity-point) are precisely $t\ne0$. For fixed $t<0$, $F_n(t)=0=F(t)$; for fixed $t>0$, $F_n(t)=1=F(t)$ eventually. This proves [convergence in distribution](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#convergence-in-distribution) to zero directly from the definition.

Also, $\mathbb P(0\leq U_n\leq1/n)=1$. For every $\varepsilon>0$ and $n>1/\varepsilon$, $\mathbb P(|U_n|>\varepsilon)=0$. Hence

$$
\boxed{U_n\longrightarrow0\text{ both in distribution and in probability}.}
$$

The [convergence in probability](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#convergence-in-probability) conclusion holds for any realization of these [random variables](../random-variable.md) on a common [probability space](../probability-space.md); no [independence of random variables](../random-variable.md#independent-random-variables) is needed.

<h3 id="1/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

<h5 id="1/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#1/b/i)

Fix $\delta>0$. Once $|c_n-c|<\delta$, the following event inclusions hold outside $\{|X_n-X|>\delta\}$:

$$
\{X\leq c-2\delta\}\subseteq\{X_n\leq c_n\}\subseteq\{X\leq c+2\delta\}.
$$

Taking [probabilities](../probability-theory.md#probability) gives the explicit bounds

$$
F(c-2\delta)-\mathbb P(|X_n-X|>\delta)\leq F_n(c_n)\leq F(c+2\delta)+\mathbb P(|X_n-X|>\delta).
$$

By the assumed [convergence in probability](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#convergence-in-probability), the error tends to zero. Therefore

$$
F(c-2\delta)\leq\liminf_nF_n(c_n)\leq\limsup_nF_n(c_n)\leq F(c+2\delta).
$$

The [continuity](../calculus.md#continuous-function) of the [cumulative distribution function](../probability-distribution.md#cumulative-distribution-function) $F$ at $c$ lets $\delta$ decrease to zero, proving $\boxed{F_n(c_n)\to F(c).}$ This proves the needed implication directly, without invoking any theorem comparing [convergence of random variables](../convergence-of-random-variables.md).

<h4 id="1/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

<h5 id="1/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#1/b/ii)

The definition of [convergence in distribution](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#convergence-in-distribution) gives $F_n(t)\to F(t)$ at each [continuity point](../calculus.md#continuity-point) $t$ of $F$. We must handle a moving argument rather than assume this convergence is uniform.

A [cumulative distribution function](../probability-distribution.md#cumulative-distribution-function) has at most countably many [discontinuities](../calculus.md#discontinuity): each is a jump of positive size, and there are at most $r$ jumps of size at least $1/r$, since their sizes sum to at most one. Thus we can choose [continuity points](../calculus.md#continuity-point) $u_j<c<v_j$ with $u_j\uparrow c$ and $v_j\downarrow c$. For fixed $j$, eventually $u_j<c_n<v_j$. The [monotonicity](../calculus.md#monotonic-function) of each [cumulative distribution function](../probability-distribution.md#cumulative-distribution-function) implies

$$
F_n(u_j)\leq F_n(c_n)\leq F_n(v_j).
$$

First let $n\to\infty$, using the definition of [convergence in distribution](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#convergence-in-distribution) at $u_j,v_j$. Then let $j\to\infty$, using [continuity](../calculus.md#continuous-function) at $c$:

$$
F(u_j)\leq\liminf_nF_n(c_n)\leq\limsup_nF_n(c_n)\leq F(v_j),\qquad
\boxed{F_n(c_n)\longrightarrow F(c).}
$$

No result relating different [convergence of random variables](../convergence-of-random-variables.md) has been assumed.

<h4 id="1/b/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

<h5 id="1/b/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#1/b/iii)

Use $X_n\sim\operatorname{Unif}[0,1/n]$ from part (a). We already proved [convergence in distribution](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#convergence-in-distribution) to zero. For any prescribed $p\in[0,1]$, put $c_n=p/n$. Then $c_n\to0$, and the [cumulative distribution function](../probability-distribution.md#cumulative-distribution-function) satisfies

$$
\boxed{F_n(c_n)=F_n(p/n)=p\quad\text{for every }n.}
$$

This includes $p=0$ and $p=1$, since the [uniform distribution](../continuous-probability-distribution.md#continuous-uniform-distribution) assigns zero [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability) to each endpoint. The limit [cumulative distribution function](../probability-distribution.md#cumulative-distribution-function) is discontinuous at zero, explaining why the conclusion of (ii) does not apply there.

<h3 id="1/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

Write $\Phi$ for the [standard normal distribution function](../probability-distribution.md#standard-normal-distribution-function). The [Poisson process](../poisson-process.md) in this part has rate one and starts at $N_0=0$.

<h4 id="1/c/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#1/c)

<h5 id="1/c/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#1/c/i)

Set $T_0=0$ and $E_j=T_j-T_{j-1}$. The [Poisson interarrival times](../poisson-process.md#poisson-interarrival-times) $E_j$ are [independent and identically distributed random variables](../random-variable.md#independent-and-identically-distributed-random-variables) with the rate-one [exponential distribution](../continuous-probability-distribution.md#exponential-distribution). In particular, $\mathbb EE_j=1$ and $\operatorname{Var}(E_j)=1$, so the finite, positive [variance](../variance.md) hypothesis of the [central limit theorem](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#central-limit-theorem) holds. Since $T_n=\sum_{j=1}^nE_j$,

$$
\boxed{\frac{T_n-n}{\sqrt n}=\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_{j=1}^n(E_j-1)\ \xrightarrow{\mathrm d}\ N(0,1).}
$$

<h4 id="1/c/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#1/c)

<h5 id="1/c/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#1/c/ii)

The unit-time increments $D_j=N_j-N_{j-1}$ are [independent and identically distributed random variables](../random-variable.md#independent-and-identically-distributed-random-variables), by [independent increments](../stochastic-process.md#independent-increments) and [stationary increments](../stochastic-process.md#stationary-increments) of the [Poisson process](../poisson-process.md). Each has the [Poisson distribution](../discrete-probability-distribution.md#poisson-distribution) of parameter one, hence [expected value](../expected-value.md) one and [variance](../variance.md) one. The [central limit theorem](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#central-limit-theorem) therefore gives

$$
\boxed{\frac{N_n-n}{\sqrt n}=\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_{j=1}^n(D_j-1)\ \xrightarrow{\mathrm d}\ N(0,1).}
$$

<h4 id="1/c/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#1/c)

<h5 id="1/c/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#1/c/iii)

There is a stronger conclusion than the requested limit. For $x>0$, the event $N_n-n\geq x\sqrt n$ forces $N_n\geq n+1$, because $N_n$ is an [integer](../integer.md). In particular, at least $n$ arrivals have occurred by time $n$, so the $n$-th [Poisson arrival time](../poisson-process.md#poisson-arrival-time) satisfies $T_n\leq n$. This contradicts $T_n-n\geq y\sqrt n$ for $y>0$. Thus the two events are disjoint:

$$
\boxed{\mathbb P\!\left(\frac{N_n-n}{\sqrt n}\geq x,\frac{T_n-n}{\sqrt n}\geq y\right)=0\quad\text{for every }n.}
$$

<h4 id="1/c/iv">iv</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#1/c)

<h5 id="1/c/iv/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#1/c/iv)

Separate a long future count from the early [Poisson arrival time](../poisson-process.md#poisson-arrival-time). Let $\mathcal F_t=\sigma(N_s:0\leq s\leq t)$ be the [natural filtration](../stochastic-process.md#natural-filtration), and define

$$
A_n=\frac{N_{n^4}-N_{n^2}-(n^4-n^2)}{n^2},\qquad
B_n=\frac{\min(T_n,n^2)-n}{\sqrt n},\qquad
C_n=\frac{N_{n^2}-n^2}{n^2}.
$$

The truncated [Poisson arrival time](../poisson-process.md#poisson-arrival-time) in $B_n$ is $\mathcal F_{n^2}$-measurable. The [independent increments](../stochastic-process.md#independent-increments) property makes $A_n$ independent of $\mathcal F_{n^2}$, and hence of $B_n$.

The numerator of $A_n$ is a centered [Poisson random variable](../discrete-probability-distribution.md#poisson-distribution) of parameter $n^4-n^2$. Apply the [central limit theorem](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#central-limit-theorem) to the $n^4-n^2$ unit-time increments, then use $\sqrt{n^4-n^2}/n^2\to1$ and [Slutsky's theorem](../statistical-inference.md#slutsky-theorem). This proves $A_n\xrightarrow{\mathrm d}N(0,1)$. Also, [Markov's inequality](../probability-inequality.md#markov-s-inequality) and $\mathbb ET_n=n$ give

$$
\mathbb P(T_n>n^2)\leq\frac1n\longrightarrow0.
$$

Thus $B_n$ equals $(T_n-n)/\sqrt n$ outside an event of vanishing [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability), so part (i) and [Slutsky's theorem](../statistical-inference.md#slutsky-theorem) give $B_n\xrightarrow{\mathrm d}N(0,1)$. Exact [independence of random variables](../random-variable.md#independent-random-variables) for each $n$ now gives the joint limit $(A_n,B_n)\xrightarrow{\mathrm d}(Z_1,Z_2)$ with independent [standard normal random variables](../probability-distribution.md#standard-normal-random-variable).

Finally, $\mathbb EC_n=0$ and $\operatorname{Var}(C_n)=n^{-2}$, so [Chebyshev's inequality](../probability-inequality.md#chebyshev-s-inequality) gives $C_n\to0$ in [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability). The first coordinate in the question is $A_n+C_n$. The second differs from $B_n$ only on $\{T_n>n^2\}$, and so its difference tends to zero in [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability). The vector version of [Slutsky's theorem](../statistical-inference.md#slutsky-theorem) proves the required joint [convergence in distribution](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#convergence-in-distribution). The boundary of $(-\infty,x]\times[y,\infty)$ has zero limiting [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability), since both [marginal distributions](../probability-distribution.md#marginal-distribution) have [continuous](../calculus.md#continuous-function) [cumulative distribution functions](../probability-distribution.md#cumulative-distribution-function). The [Portmanteau theorem](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#portmanteau-theorem) therefore yields

$$
\boxed{\mathbb P\!\left(\frac{N_{n^4}-n^4}{n^2}\leq x,\frac{T_n-n}{\sqrt n}\geq y\right)\longrightarrow\Phi(x)\bigl(1-\Phi(y)\bigr).}
$$

This is $\mathbb P(Z_1\leq x,Z_2\geq y)$ by [independence of random variables](../random-variable.md#independent-random-variables).

<h4 id="1/c/v">v</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#1/c)

<h5 id="1/c/v/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [V](#1/c/v)

The possible limiting [correlation coefficients](../variance.md#pearson-correlation-coefficient) are

$$
\boxed{-1\leq\rho\leq0.}
$$

The direction of the dependence comes from inversion of the same [counting process](../stochastic-process.md#counting-process): extra arrivals produce a larger count but an earlier $n$-th [Poisson arrival time](../poisson-process.md#poisson-arrival-time). Shared fluctuations therefore enter the two coordinates with opposite signs. Positive limiting [correlation](../variance.md#pearson-correlation-coefficient) cannot arise; this can be formalized using [negative quadrant dependence of a Poisson count and an arrival time](../poisson-process.md#negative-quadrant-dependence-of-a-poisson-count-and-an-arrival-time).

At the same time scale, $f(n)=n$, part (iii) rules out every positive-positive quadrant. A [bivariate normal distribution](../probability-and-statistics.md#bivariate-normal-distribution) with standard [normal](../probability-distribution.md#normal-distribution) [marginal distributions](../probability-distribution.md#marginal-distribution) and $\rho>-1$ assigns positive [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability) to such a quadrant. Thus, under the assumed joint limit, this choice forces $\rho=-1$. At very different time scales, $f(n)=n^4$, part (iv) gives $\rho=0$.

Intermediate time scales retain an intermediate fraction of the same arrival fluctuations. In particular, the [Poisson count-arrival joint central limit theorem](../poisson-process.md#poisson-count-arrival-joint-central-limit-theorem) gives $\rho=-1/\sqrt\lambda$ for $f(n)=\lambda n$ with $1\leq\lambda<\infty$. These choices realize every $\rho\in[-1,0)$, and $f(n)=n^4$ supplies zero. This identifies the whole interval without requiring an additional limit calculation in this part.

The premise about a [bivariate normal distribution](../probability-and-statistics.md#bivariate-normal-distribution) is understood to apply when a joint limit exists. Merely requiring $f(n)\to\infty$ cannot guarantee one: taking $f(n)=n$ for even $n$ and $f(n)=n^4$ for odd $n$ gives different limits along the two [subsequences](../sequence-and-series.md#subsequence) just identified.

↑ **Parent:** [2021](2021.md)

<h3 id="2/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#2/a)

The [uniqueness theorem for moment-generating functions](../probability-distribution.md#uniqueness-theorem-for-moment-generating-functions) states: if two real [random variables](../random-variable.md) $V,W$ have finite [moment-generating functions](../probability-distribution.md#moment-generating-function) on a common open [interval](../real-analysis.md#interval-mathematics) $(-\delta,\delta)$ with $\delta>0$, and

$$
M_V(t)=\mathbb E e^{tV}=\mathbb E e^{tW}=M_W(t)\qquad(|t|<\delta),
$$

then $\boxed{V\text{ and }W\text{ have the same probability distribution}.}$ Finiteness throughout a neighborhood of zero is a hypothesis; the identity $M_V(0)=M_W(0)=1$ alone carries no information.

<h3 id="2/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

Let $X_f$ have the specified [probability density function](../continuous-probability-distribution.md#probability-density-function) $f_m$. Splitting the [integral](../integral.md) into the positive and negative half-lines, and using the [Gamma integral](../complex-analysis.md#gamma-integral) $\int_0^\infty x^re^{-sx}\,dx=r!/s^{r+1}$ for a nonnegative [integer](../integer.md) $r$ and $s>0$, gives

$$
M_f(t)=\frac{2^m}{m!}\int_0^\infty x^m\bigl(e^{-(2-t)x}+e^{-(2+t)x}\bigr)\,dx
=2^m\left(\frac1{(2-t)^{m+1}}+\frac1{(2+t)^{m+1}}\right),\quad |t|<2.
$$

At $t=2$ or $t=-2$, one term becomes the divergent [integral](../integral.md) of $x^m$; beyond these endpoints it grows exponentially. Consequently, the full extended-valued [moment-generating function](../probability-distribution.md#moment-generating-function) is

$$
\boxed{M_f(t)=\begin{cases}2^m\bigl((2-t)^{-m-1}+(2+t)^{-m-1}\bigr),&|t|<2,\\+\infty,&|t|\geq2.\end{cases}}
$$

In particular $M_f(0)=1$, checking [normalization of a probability distribution](../probability-distribution.md#normalization-of-a-probability-distribution). All [absolute moments](../expected-value.md#absolute-moment) exist. Direct integration yields

$$
\mathbb E|X_f|^k=\frac{2^{m+1}}{m!}\frac{(m+k)!}{2^{m+k+1}}=\frac{(m+k)!}{2^k m!}.
$$

The [symmetric distribution](../probability-distribution.md#symmetric-distribution) makes every odd [moment](../expected-value.md#moment) zero, so

$$
\boxed{\mathbb EX_f^k=\begin{cases}0,&k\text{ odd},\\(m+k)!/(2^k m!),&k\text{ even},\end{cases}\qquad k\geq1.}
$$

Now let $X_g$ have [probability density function](../continuous-probability-distribution.md#probability-density-function) $g_m$. Its [moment-generating function](../probability-distribution.md#moment-generating-function) would be

$$
M_g(t)=\frac{m+1}{2}\int_1^\infty x^{-m-2}\bigl(e^{tx}+e^{-tx}\bigr)\,dx.
$$

For every $t\ne0$, one summand has exponential growth, which dominates the polynomial decay. Hence

$$
\boxed{M_g(0)=1,\qquad M_g(t)=+\infty\quad(t\ne0).}
$$

There is no finite [moment-generating function](../probability-distribution.md#moment-generating-function) on any neighborhood of zero, but some [moments](../expected-value.md#moment) still exist. Specifically,

$$
\mathbb E|X_g|^k=(m+1)\int_1^\infty x^{k-m-2}\,dx
=\frac{m+1}{m+1-k}\quad\text{if }k<m+1.
$$

Thus

$$
\boxed{\mathbb EX_g^k=\begin{cases}0,&k\text{ odd and }k<m+1,\\(m+1)/(m+1-k),&k\text{ even and }k<m+1.\end{cases}}
$$

For even $k\geq m+1$, the [moment](../expected-value.md#moment) is $+\infty$, so it is not a finite [moment](../expected-value.md#moment). For odd $k\geq m+1$, both the positive and negative contributions have infinite [integral](../integral.md), so the [expected value](../expected-value.md) is undefined. Cancellation in a [Cauchy principal value](../complex-analysis.md#cauchy-principal-value) does not define an [expected value](../expected-value.md). In particular, when $m=0$ there are no finite positive-order [moments](../expected-value.md#moment).

<h3 id="2/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/c/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#2/c)

<h5 id="2/c/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#2/c/i)

First justify that the [moments](../expected-value.md#moment) being used exist. For every $r>0$,

$$
\mathbb E e^{r|Y|}\leq\mathbb E e^{rY}+\mathbb E e^{-rY}=M_Y(r)+M_Y(-r)<\infty.
$$

The bound $|y|^k\leq k!r^{-k}e^{r|y|}$ gives $\mathbb E|Y|^k<\infty$ for all $k$.

If $Y$ has a [symmetric distribution](../probability-distribution.md#symmetric-distribution), then $Y$ and $-Y$ have the same [probability distribution](../probability-distribution.md), so for odd $k$,

$$
\mathbb EY^k=\mathbb E(-Y)^k=-\mathbb EY^k,
$$

which forces $\mathbb EY^k=0$.

Conversely, suppose every odd [moment](../expected-value.md#moment) vanishes. For fixed $t\in\mathbb R$, the absolute values of the partial [Taylor series](../calculus.md#taylor-series) for $e^{tY}$ are bounded by $e^{|t||Y|}$, whose [expected value](../expected-value.md) is finite by the preceding inequality. The [dominated convergence theorem](../measure-theory.md#dominated-convergence-theorem) therefore permits termwise [expectation](../expected-value.md):

$$
M_Y(t)=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{t^k\mathbb EY^k}{k!}
=\sum_{j=0}^\infty\frac{t^{2j}\mathbb EY^{2j}}{(2j)!}=M_Y(-t)=M_{-Y}(t).
$$

The [uniqueness theorem for moment-generating functions](../probability-distribution.md#uniqueness-theorem-for-moment-generating-functions) implies equality of the [probability distributions](../probability-distribution.md) of $Y$ and $-Y$. Hence

$$
\boxed{Y\text{ has a symmetric distribution}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \mathbb EY^k=0\text{ for every odd }k.}
$$

<h4 id="2/c/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#2/c)

<h5 id="2/c/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#2/c/ii)

Let $X$ have the [Pareto distribution](../continuous-probability-distribution.md#pareto-distribution) with [probability density function](../continuous-probability-distribution.md#probability-density-function) $f_X(x)=3x^{-4}$ for $x\geq1$, and zero otherwise. Its [normalization of a probability distribution](../probability-distribution.md#normalization-of-a-probability-distribution) and first two [moments](../expected-value.md#moment) follow from

$$
\int_1^\infty3x^{-4}\,dx=1,\qquad
\mathbb EX=3\int_1^\infty x^{-3}\,dx=\frac32,\qquad
\mathbb EX^2=3\int_1^\infty x^{-2}\,dx=3.
$$

Set $Y=X-3/2$. Then

$$
\boxed{\mathbb EY=0,\qquad\operatorname{Var}(Y)=3-\frac94=\frac34.}
$$

The [probability distribution](../probability-distribution.md) of $Y$ is not a [symmetric distribution](../probability-distribution.md#symmetric-distribution): $Y\geq-1/2$ [almost surely](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#almost-sure-convergence), whereas $\mathbb P(Y>1/2)=\mathbb P(X>2)=1/8>0$ and $\mathbb P(Y<-1/2)=0$. For $X\geq3$ we have $|Y|\geq X/2$, so

$$
\mathbb E|Y|^3\geq\frac18\mathbb E\bigl[X^3\mathbf1_{\{X\geq3\}}\bigr]
=\frac38\int_3^\infty\frac{dx}{x}=\infty.
$$

Thus $\boxed{Y=X-3/2}$ has every property required.

<h4 id="2/c/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#2/c)

<h5 id="2/c/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#2/c/iii)

Take a [discrete probability distribution](../discrete-probability-distribution.md) on three points:

$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(Y=-2)=\frac25,\qquad\mathbb P(Y=1)=\frac12,\qquad\mathbb P(Y=3)=\frac1{10}.}
$$

The [probabilities](../probability-theory.md#probability) are positive and sum to one. Direct calculation gives

$$
\mathbb EY=-\frac45+\frac12+\frac3{10}=0,\qquad
\mathbb EY^3=-\frac{16}5+\frac12+\frac{27}{10}=0,
$$

and

$$
\boxed{\mathbb EY^4=\frac{32}5+\frac12+\frac{81}{10}=15<\infty.}
$$

Nevertheless it is not a [symmetric distribution](../probability-distribution.md#symmetric-distribution), since $\mathbb P(Y=3)=1/10$ while $\mathbb P(Y=-3)=0$. Vanishing of just the first and third [moments](../expected-value.md#moment) does not force all odd [moments](../expected-value.md#moment) to vanish.

<h4 id="2/c/iv">iv</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#2/c)

<h5 id="2/c/iv/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#2/c/iv)

It suffices that the [moment-generating function](../probability-distribution.md#moment-generating-function) be finite on some open [interval](../real-analysis.md#interval-mathematics) containing zero:

$$
\boxed{M_Y(t)<\infty\text{ for every }|t|<\delta\text{, for some }\delta>0.}
$$

Equivalently, some two-sided [exponential moment](../probability-distribution.md#exponential-moment) exists: $\mathbb E e^{r|Y|}<\infty$ for some $r>0$. For the forward implication, choose $0<r<\delta$ and use $e^{r|Y|}\leq e^{rY}+e^{-rY}$; the converse follows from $e^{tY}\leq e^{r|Y|}$ for $|t|\leq r$.

Under this weaker hypothesis, all [moments](../expected-value.md#moment) exist. For $|t|<r$, [dominated convergence](../measure-theory.md#dominated-convergence-theorem) justifies the [Taylor series](../calculus.md#taylor-series) argument in (i). Vanishing odd [moments](../expected-value.md#moment) gives $M_Y(t)=M_{-Y}(t)$ near zero, and the local [uniqueness theorem for moment-generating functions](../probability-distribution.md#uniqueness-theorem-for-moment-generating-functions) gives the [symmetric distribution](../probability-distribution.md#symmetric-distribution). The reverse implication follows directly from equality of the two [probability distributions](../probability-distribution.md).

More generally, it suffices that all [moments](../expected-value.md#moment) exist and the [probability distribution](../probability-distribution.md) have [moment determinacy](../expected-value.md#moment-determinacy): $Y$ and $-Y$ then have identical [moments](../expected-value.md#moment) and hence identical [probability distributions](../probability-distribution.md). Existence of all [moments](../expected-value.md#moment) by itself does not provide that uniqueness.

<h3 id="2/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#2/d)

The support condition $-y<x<y$ forces $y>0$. As a check, the [joint probability density](../continuous-probability-distribution.md#joint-probability-density) integrates to

$$
\int_0^\infty\int_{-y}^{y}2e^{-2y}\,dx\,dy=\int_0^\infty4ye^{-2y}\,dy=1.
$$

For any real $x$, the [marginal density](../probability-distribution.md#marginal-density) of $X$ is

$$
f_X(x)=\int_{|x|}^\infty2e^{-2y}\,dy=e^{-2|x|}>0.
$$

Dividing the [joint probability density](../continuous-probability-distribution.md#joint-probability-density) by this [marginal density](../probability-distribution.md#marginal-density) gives the [conditional density](../probability-theory.md#conditional-density):

$$
\boxed{f_{Y\mid X=x}(y)=2e^{-2(y-|x|)}\mathbf1_{\{y>|x|\}}.}
$$

Thus, conditionally on $X=x$, $Y-|x|$ has the rate-two [exponential distribution](../continuous-probability-distribution.md#exponential-distribution).

For $U=X^2$, use the two inverse branches $x=\sqrt u$ and $x=-\sqrt u$. Each branch has absolute [Jacobian determinant](../multivariable-calculus.md#jacobian-determinant) $1/(2\sqrt u)$. Both lie in the original support exactly when $u>0$ and $y>\sqrt u$. The [change of variables for probability densities](../continuous-probability-distribution.md#change-of-variables-for-probability-densities) therefore yields

$$
\boxed{f_{U,Y}(u,y)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{2e^{-2y}}{\sqrt u},&u>0,\ y>\sqrt u,\\0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}}
$$

No [atom of a measure](../measure.md#atom-measure-theory) is created at $u=0$, since $\mathbb P(X=0)=0$. The value of the [probability density function](../continuous-probability-distribution.md#probability-density-function) at this boundary may be assigned arbitrarily. Checking the [integral](../integral.md) over $0<u<y^2$ gives $4ye^{-2y}$, which integrates to one.

## 3

↑ **Parent:** [2021](2021.md)

Put $q=1-p=a/(a+b)$. The [random walk](../random-walk.md) has [independent and identically distributed](../random-variable.md#independent-and-identically-distributed-random-variables) increments $Y_n$ equal to $a$ with [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability) $p=b/(a+b)$ and $-b$ with [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability) $q$. Thus $S_0=0$, $S_n=\sum_{j=1}^nY_j$, and $\mathbb EY_n=0$.

<h3 id="3/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

The history $\sigma(S_0,\ldots,S_n)$ is contained in $\sigma(Y_1,\ldots,Y_n)$. The increment $Y_{n+1}$ is independent of this history by the given [independence of random variables](../random-variable.md#independent-random-variables). Therefore

$$
\mathbb P(S_{n+1}=j\mid S_0,\ldots,S_n)
=\mathbb P(Y_{n+1}=j-S_n\mid S_0,\ldots,S_n)
=p\mathbf1_{\{j=S_n+a\}}+q\mathbf1_{\{j=S_n-b\}}.
$$

This depends only on the current state and not on time. It is precisely the [time-homogeneous Markov property](../markov-chain.md#time-homogeneous-markov-property), so $S_n$ is a [Markov chain](../markov-chain.md) with [transition probabilities](../markov-chain.md#transition-probability)

$$
\boxed{p_{i,j}=\begin{cases}b/(a+b),&j=i+a,\\a/(a+b),&j=i-b,\\0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}\qquad i,j\in\mathbb Z.}
$$

The conditional identity is an identity that holds [almost surely](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#almost-sure-convergence); histories of zero [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability) require no division by their [probabilities](../probability-theory.md#probability).

<h3 id="3/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

Here $a,b$ are [coprime integers](../divisibility.md#coprime-integers).

<h4 id="3/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

<h5 id="3/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#3/b/i)

Fix any two [integers](../integer.md) $i,j$. By [Bézout's identity](../algebra.md#bezout-s-identity), there are [integers](../integer.md) $u,v$ with $au-bv=j-i$. Replacing $(u,v)$ by $(u+kb,v+ka)$ leaves this identity unchanged. Choose $k$ large enough that both entries are nonnegative. A path with $u+kb$ upward steps and $v+ka$ downward steps then takes $i$ to $j$, with strictly positive [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability) $p^{u+kb}q^{v+ka}$. There are no boundary restrictions on this [state space](../dynamical-systems.md#state-space).

This applies in both directions, so every pair of states communicates. Hence

$$
\boxed{\text{The Markov chain is irreducible, with sole communicating class }\mathbb Z.}
$$

<h4 id="3/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

<h5 id="3/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#3/b/ii)

A return path with $u$ upward steps and $v$ downward steps satisfies $au=bv$. Since $a,b$ are [coprime](../divisibility.md#coprime-integers), this is equivalent to $u=bk$ and $v=ak$ for an [integer](../integer.md) $k\geq1$. Its length is therefore $k(a+b)$.

Conversely, for each such $k$, any ordering of $bk$ upward and $ak$ downward steps is an allowed positive-[probability](../probability-theory.md#probability) return path. The possible strictly positive return times are exactly the positive multiples of $a+b$. By the definition of the [period of a state in a Markov chain](../markov-chain.md#period-of-a-state-in-a-markov-chain) as their [greatest common divisor](../divisibility.md#greatest-common-divisor),

$$
\boxed{\text{Every state has period }a+b\geq2.}
$$

In particular, none of the states is [aperiodic](../markov-chain.md#aperiodic-markov-chain).

<h4 id="3/b/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

<h5 id="3/b/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#3/b/iii)

Suppose a [stationary distribution](../markov-chain.md#stationary-distribution) $\pi$ exists. Its [stationarity equations](../markov-chain.md#stationarity-equations) are

$$
\pi_j=p\pi_{j-a}+q\pi_{j+b}\qquad(j\in\mathbb Z).
$$

Since $\sum_j\pi_j=1$, its masses tend to zero as $|j|\to\infty$, and a positive maximum $M=\pi_{j_0}$ is attained. Both terms on the right are at most $M$. Since $p,q>0$ and $p+q=1$, equality forces $\pi_{j_0-a}=\pi_{j_0+b}=M$. Applying the same argument repeatedly at $j_0-a,j_0-2a,\ldots$ gives

$$
\pi_{j_0-ka}=M>0\qquad(k\geq0).
$$

These are infinitely many distinct states, contradicting $\sum_j\pi_j=1$. Thus

$$
\boxed{\text{There is no stationary distribution.}}
$$

This [maximum principle](../partial-differential-equation.md#maximum-principle) proof uses neither a finite [expected value](../expected-value.md) of the state nor the [coprimality](../divisibility.md#coprime-integers) assumption.

<h4 id="3/b/iv">iv</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

<h5 id="3/b/iv/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#3/b/iv)

First identify the recurrence type, rather than infer an occupation limit from a one-time [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability). At time $k(a+b)$, return to zero requires exactly $kb$ upward steps. The [binomial distribution](../discrete-probability-distribution.md#binomial-distribution) gives

$$
\mathbb P_0(S_{k(a+b)}=0)=\binom{k(a+b)}{kb}\left(\frac b{a+b}\right)^{kb}\left(\frac a{a+b}\right)^{ka}
\sim\sqrt{\frac{a+b}{2\pi abk}},
$$

where the last step is [Stirling's approximation](../real-analysis.md#stirling-formula). At all other positive times the return [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability) is zero. Hence $\sum_{n\geq0}\mathbb P_0(S_n=0)=\infty$. The [recurrence criterion by return probabilities](../markov-chain.md#recurrence-criterion-by-return-probabilities) proves the [recurrent](../markov-chain.md#recurrent-markov-chain) property; by the [irreducible Markov chain](../markov-chain.md#irreducible-markov-chain) property established in (i), every state is [recurrent](../markov-chain.md#recurrent-markov-chain). The equivalence of [positive recurrence](../markov-chain.md#positive-recurrent-markov-chain) and existence of a [stationary distribution](../markov-chain.md#stationary-distribution) for an [irreducible Markov chain](../markov-chain.md#irreducible-markov-chain) on a [countable](../cardinal-number.md#countable-set) [state space](../dynamical-systems.md#state-space), together with (iii), makes this chain [null recurrent](../markov-chain.md#null-recurrent-state).

For completeness, the zero occupation proportion follows directly by treating returns as a [renewal process](../probability-theory.md#renewal-process). Let $L_1,L_2,\ldots$ be successive strictly positive return-cycle lengths to zero. The [Strong Markov property](../markov-chain.md#strong-markov-property) makes them [independent and identically distributed](../random-variable.md#independent-and-identically-distributed-random-variables), the [recurrent](../markov-chain.md#recurrent-markov-chain) property makes them finite [almost surely](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#almost-sure-convergence), and the [null recurrent](../markov-chain.md#null-recurrent-state) property gives $\mathbb EL_1=\infty$. For each positive [integer](../integer.md) $K$, the [strong law of large numbers](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#strong-law-of-large-numbers) applied to $\min(L_j,K)$ gives

$$
\liminf_{r\to\infty}\frac{L_1+\cdots+L_r}{r}\geq\mathbb E\min(L_1,K)\quad\text{almost surely}.
$$

Take a common event of [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability) one for these countably many $K$. By the [monotone convergence theorem](../measure-theory.md#monotone-convergence-theorem), the right side increases to infinity. Thus the number of completed return cycles by time $n$, divided by $n$, tends to zero. Counting the initial visit changes this ratio by at most $1/n$. Consequently,

$$
\boxed{\frac1n\sum_{r=0}^{n-1}\mathbf1_{\{S_r=0\}}\longrightarrow0\quad\text{almost surely}.}
$$

The walk visits zero infinitely often, but the asymptotic fraction of time spent there is zero.

<h3 id="3/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

Take an [integer](../integer.md) $c\geq\max\{a,b\}$. On $I=\{-c,\ldots,c\}$, the modified [transition matrix](../markov-chain.md#stochastic-matrix) $Q=(q_{i,j})$ agrees with $P$. Above $c$, it stays put with [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability) $a/(a+b)$ and jumps to each of $c-b+1,\ldots,c$ with [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability) $1/(a+b)$. Below $-c$, it stays put with [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability) $b/(a+b)$ and jumps to each of $-c,\ldots,-c+a-1$ with [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability) $1/(a+b)$. All unspecified entries are zero. These rows sum to one. The [integer](../integer.md) qualification is necessary: if $c$ were nonintegral, $i=\lfloor c\rfloor+1$ would fit none of the printed row conditions.

<h4 id="3/c/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#3/c)

<h5 id="3/c/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#3/c/i)

Define

$$
L=\{-c-b,\ldots,-c-1\},\quad U=\{c+1,\ldots,c+a\},\quad
C=L\cup I\cup U=\{-c-b,\ldots,c+a\}.
$$

An interior jump cannot go below $-c-b$ or above $c+a$, and every exterior reset lands in $I$. Thus $C$ is closed.

To prove that it is one [communicating class](../markov-chain.md#communicating-class), start anywhere in $C$. From $L$ or $U$, a reset can reach $I$ with positive [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability). From any state of $I$, repeated $+a$ steps reach some state of $U$, after staying in $I$ up to the last step. A reset from this state can reach every point of the consecutive block

$$
B=\{c-b+1,\ldots,c\}.
$$

Every [integer](../integer.md) $j\in\{-c-b,\ldots,c\}$ has the form $t-kb$ for some $t\in B$ and an [integer](../integer.md) $k\geq0$. Repeated $-b$ steps from $t$ reach $j$, with all departure states in $I$ until a possible last step into $L$. This gives access to all of $I\cup L$. Finally, each $j\in U$ can be reached from $j-a\in I$. Every path described has strictly positive [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability). Therefore every state in $C$ reaches every other state in $C$.

Each state outside $C$ can only stay put or reset into $I$, and no transition from $C$ returns to it. Different states outside $C$ cannot reach one another. Hence the complete list of [communicating classes](../markov-chain.md#communicating-class) is

$$
\boxed{C=\{-c-b,\ldots,c+a\}\text{, and }\{i\}\text{ for each }i\notin C.}
$$

The finite class $C$ is the unique [closed communicating class](../markov-chain.md#closed-communicating-class). The exterior singleton classes are [transient communicating classes](../markov-chain.md#transient-communicating-class), since their self-transition [probabilities](../probability-theory.md#probability) are strictly less than one. Notice that this reachability proof does not require $a,b$ to be [coprime](../divisibility.md#coprime-integers).

<h4 id="3/c/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#3/c)

<h5 id="3/c/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#3/c/ii)

The restriction $Q_C$ is a [finite Markov chain](../markov-chain.md#finite-markov-chain) that is an [irreducible Markov chain](../markov-chain.md#irreducible-markov-chain). It is [aperiodic](../markov-chain.md#aperiodic-markov-chain), because its upper states have self-transition [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability) $a/(a+b)>0$, and the [period is constant on a communicating class](../markov-chain.md#period-is-constant-on-a-communicating-class). We now find its [stationary distribution](../markov-chain.md#stationary-distribution).

Every column of $Q_C$ sums to one. Here is the full incoming-mass check, with each total measured in units of $1/(a+b)$. For $j\in L$, the incoming contributions are $a$ from $j+b\in I$ and $b$ from the self-transition. For $j\in U$, they are $b$ from $j-a\in I$ and $a$ from the self-transition.

For $j\in I$, the upward predecessor $j-a$ lies in $I$ exactly when $j\geq-c+a$, contributing $b$ units. When $j\leq-c+a-1$, that predecessor is replaced by resets from all $b$ states of $L$, again contributing $b$ units. Independently, the downward predecessor $j+b$ contributes $a$ units when $j\leq c-b$; when $j\geq c-b+1$, resets from the $a$ states of $U$ contribute those $a$ units instead. Thus every column total is $a+b$ units.

Therefore $Q_C$ is a [doubly stochastic matrix](../vector-space.md#doubly-stochastic-matrix), and the [uniform distribution](../continuous-probability-distribution.md#continuous-uniform-distribution) on $C$ is stationary:

$$
\boxed{\pi_j=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac1{2c+a+b+1},&-c-b\leq j\leq c+a,\\0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}}
$$

The [countable-state Markov chain convergence theorem](../markov-chain.md#countable-state-markov-chain-convergence-theorem), applied to the finite closed class $C$, gives $Q_C^n(i,j)\to\pi_j$ for every $i\in C$. Its hypotheses hold: $C$ is finite and irreducible, hence [positive recurrent](../markov-chain.md#positive-recurrent-markov-chain), and it is [aperiodic](../markov-chain.md#aperiodic-markov-chain).

If $i_0>c+a$, the [hitting time](../markov-chain.md#first-passage-time) $H$ of $C$ has $\mathbb P(H>n)=(a/(a+b))^n$. If $i_0<-c-b$, the corresponding tail is $(b/(a+b))^n$. Thus $H<\infty$ [almost surely](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#almost-sure-convergence) from every exterior initial state. Conditioning on $H$ and $R_H$, use convergence within $C$ after entrance and the [bounded convergence theorem](../measure-theory.md#bounded-convergence-theorem); equivalently, first restrict to $H\leq K$ and then let $K\to\infty$. This proves, for every $i_0\in\mathbb Z$,

$$
\boxed{R_n\xrightarrow{\mathrm d}\operatorname{Unif}\{-c-b,\ldots,c+a\}.}
$$

The [limiting distribution](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#limiting-distribution) is independent of $i_0$.

<h3 id="3/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#3/d)

Let $d=\gcd(a,b)>1$ and put $a'=a/d$, $b'=b/d$. Then $a',b'$ are [coprime integers](../divisibility.md#coprime-integers), and $p=b'/(a'+b')$. The six requested revisions are as follows.

**(b)(i): [communicating classes](../markov-chain.md#communicating-class).** Every increment is divisible by $d$, so the [residue class](../modular-arithmetic.md#residue-class) modulo $d$ is preserved. Conversely, if $j-i$ is divisible by $d$, [Bézout's identity](../algebra.md#bezout-s-identity) applied to $a',b'$ gives a path just as in (b)(i). Thus the original [Markov chain](../markov-chain.md) is reducible on $\mathbb Z$, with exactly the $d$ closed [communicating classes](../markov-chain.md#communicating-class)

$$
\boxed{r+d\mathbb Z,\qquad r=0,1,\ldots,d-1.}
$$

The specified walk starting at zero stays in $d\mathbb Z$.

**(b)(ii): [period of a state in a Markov chain](../markov-chain.md#period-of-a-state-in-a-markov-chain).** The return equation $au=bv$ reduces to $a'u=b'v$. Hence $u=b'k$, $v=a'k$. For every state, the [period of a state in a Markov chain](../markov-chain.md#period-of-a-state-in-a-markov-chain) is therefore

$$
\boxed{a'+b'=\frac{a+b}{d}.}
$$

This is still at least two, so no state is [aperiodic](../markov-chain.md#aperiodic-markov-chain).

**(b)(iii): [stationary distribution](../markov-chain.md#stationary-distribution).** The conclusion is unchanged: there is no [stationary distribution](../markov-chain.md#stationary-distribution) on $\mathbb Z$. The maximum-mass proof in (b)(iii) applies verbatim, without using [coprimality](../divisibility.md#coprime-integers). Equivalently, each closed [communicating class](../markov-chain.md#communicating-class), after translating and dividing its state labels by $d$, is the same centered [random walk](../random-walk.md) with step sizes $a',-b'$ and has no [stationary distribution](../markov-chain.md#stationary-distribution).

**(b)(iv): occupation of zero.** On $d\mathbb Z$, the rescaled walk $S_n/d$ has exactly the [transition probabilities](../markov-chain.md#transition-probability) of the coprime walk with parameters $a',b'$. The return-probability argument in (b)(iv) therefore makes this class [null recurrent](../markov-chain.md#null-recurrent-state). The [renewal process](../probability-theory.md#renewal-process) proof of the occupation limit applies unchanged:

$$
\boxed{\frac1n\sum_{r=0}^{n-1}\mathbf1_{\{S_r=0\}}\longrightarrow0\quad\text{almost surely}.}
$$

Starting in another [residue class](../modular-arithmetic.md#residue-class) would make every visit to zero impossible, also giving occupation proportion zero.

**(c)(i): modified [communicating classes](../markov-chain.md#communicating-class).** These are unchanged: $C=\{-c-b,\ldots,c+a\}$ is one finite [closed communicating class](../markov-chain.md#closed-communicating-class), and all other states are transient singleton classes. The original proof used consecutive reset destinations and never used [coprimality](../divisibility.md#coprime-integers). Resetting to the $b$ consecutive integers $c-b+1,\ldots,c$ connects different [residue classes](../modular-arithmetic.md#residue-class), so it would be incorrect to split $C$ according to [residue classes](../modular-arithmetic.md#residue-class) modulo $d$.

**(c)(ii): [limiting distribution](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#limiting-distribution).** The column-sum calculation, the [irreducible Markov chain](../markov-chain.md#irreducible-markov-chain) property on a finite class, positive self-transition [probabilities](../probability-theory.md#probability), and geometric entrance argument also required no [coprimality](../divisibility.md#coprime-integers). Thus for every initial state the same [convergence in distribution](../convergence-of-random-variables.md#convergence-in-distribution) holds:

$$
\boxed{R_n\xrightarrow{\mathrm d}\operatorname{Unif}\{-c-b,\ldots,c+a\}.}
$$

In particular, the denominator $2c+a+b+1$ is unchanged; it is not divided by $d$.
