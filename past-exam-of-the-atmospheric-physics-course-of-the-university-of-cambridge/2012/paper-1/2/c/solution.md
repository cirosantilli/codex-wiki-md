<h1 id="2/c/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [C](../c.md)

A droplet can remain liquid below its equilibrium melting temperature through [supercooling](../../../../../supercooling.md): a microscopic ice embryo must first pay an interfacial free-energy cost. The [nucleation barrier](../../../../../nucleation-barrier.md) can therefore delay freezing even when bulk ice is thermodynamically favoured. Solutes, available surfaces, temperature, droplet volume and exposure time affect the probability; zero degrees Celsius is not an automatic freezing threshold for every atmospheric droplet.

At least four physical routes are relevant:

- [Homogeneous ice nucleation](../../../../../homogeneous-ice-nucleation.md): an ice embryo forms within deeply supercooled liquid without a foreign surface.
- [Immersion freezing](../../../../../immersion-freezing.md): a suitable particle already inside a liquid droplet catalyses an ice embryo.
- [Contact freezing](../../../../../contact-freezing.md): a suitable particle contacting the droplet surface triggers heterogeneous freezing.
- [Deposition nucleation](../../../../../deposition-nucleation.md): ice appears from vapour on a suitable solid surface under ice-supersaturated conditions; pore condensation or a thin liquid film can participate, so the observed mode need not imply a single direct molecular pathway.

Mineral particles provide nonbiological examples of heterogeneous nuclei. A particle's presence alone does not guarantee activity at a given temperature. The [cloud ice nucleation versus ice growth](../../../../../cloud-ice-nucleation-versus-ice-growth.md) distinction matters: the processes listed concern the formation of new ice; subsequent depositional growth, riming and aggregation are separate from initiating that first crystal.
