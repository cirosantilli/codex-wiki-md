↑ **Parent:** [Ii](../ii.md)

The [Petersen graph](../../../../../petersen-graph.md) has ten [vertices](../../../../../vertex-graph-theory.md), fifteen [edges](../../../../../edge-of-a-graph.md), and girth five. One way to verify the girth is its description by the two-element subsets of a five-element set, with adjacency meaning disjointness. A triangle would require three pairwise disjoint two-element subsets, which is impossible. Two distinct [vertices](../../../../../vertex-graph-theory.md) have at most one common neighbor: the only possible common neighbor is a two-element subset of the complement of their union. Therefore a four-cycle is impossible. A five-cycle exists in its standard pentagon drawing.

If it were a [planar graph](../../../../../planar-graph-split.md), part (i) would give $15\le(5/3)(10-2)=40/3$, a contradiction. Thus **the Petersen [graph](../../../../../graph-split.md) is nonplanar**. The held source has blank space where its drawing is mentioned; the named [graph](../../../../../graph-split.md) determines this argument without that missing illustration.
