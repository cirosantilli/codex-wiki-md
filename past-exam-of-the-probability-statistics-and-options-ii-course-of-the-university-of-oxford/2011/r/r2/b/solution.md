<h1 id="r/r2/b/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [B](../b.md)

Represent the two sides of the marriage problem as the parts $L,R$ of a [bipartite graph](../../../../../bipartite-graph-split.md), with an [edge](../../../../../edge-of-a-graph.md) for each allowed pairing. A [matching](../../../../../matching-graph-theory-split.md) is a set of edges with no common endpoint; a complete [matching](../../../../../matching-graph-theory-split.md) of $L$ covers every [vertex](../../../../../vertex-graph-theory.md) in $L$. When the two parts have equal size it also covers $R$, giving a [perfect matching](../../../../../perfect-matching.md).

[Hall's marriage theorem](../../../../../hall-s-marriage-theorem.md) gives the exact finite-[graph](../../../../../graph-split.md) criterion:

$$
\boxed{|N(S)|\geq|S|\quad\text{for every }S\subseteq L.}
$$

Here $N(S)$ is the set of right-side neighbours of $S$. Necessity follows because distinct matched vertices of $S$ need distinct neighbours; Hall's theorem supplies sufficiency.
