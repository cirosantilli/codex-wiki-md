<h3 id="1/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1/a)

<h5 id="1/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#1/a/i)

In a real [Hilbert space](../hilbert-space.md), [weak convergence in a Hilbert space](../hilbert-space.md#weak-convergence-in-a-hilbert-space) means

$$
\boxed{x_n\rightharpoonup x\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\langle x_n,z\rangle\longrightarrow\langle x,z\rangle\ \text{for every }z\in X.}
$$

By the [Riesz representation theorem](../hilbert-space.md#riesz-representation-theorem), every [continuous linear functional](../topological-vector-space.md#continuous-linear-functional) on $X$ has the form $u\mapsto\langle u,z\rangle$. Thus this is exactly [weak convergence](../weak-topology.md#weak-convergence), defined by convergence under every [continuous linear functional](../topological-vector-space.md#continuous-linear-functional).

<h4 id="1/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1/a)

<h5 id="1/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#1/a/ii)

Define the [continuous linear functionals](../topological-vector-space.md#continuous-linear-functional) $f_n(z)=\langle x_n,z\rangle$. For each fixed $z\in X$, [weak convergence](../weak-topology.md#weak-convergence) makes $f_n(z)$ a convergent, hence bounded, scalar sequence. The [Uniform boundedness principle](../banach-space.md#uniform-boundedness-principle) states that a pointwise bounded family of [bounded linear operators](../topological-vector-space.md#continuous-linear-operator) from a [Banach space](../banach-space.md) to a [normed space](../normed-vector-space.md) has uniformly bounded [operator norms](../continuous-dual-space.md#operator-norm). Since a [Hilbert space](../hilbert-space.md) is a [Banach space](../banach-space.md), it applies to $(f_n)$.

The [Cauchy-Schwarz inequality](../probability-and-statistics.md#cauchy-schwarz-inequality) gives $\|f_n\|\leq\|x_n\|$, and evaluation at $z=x_n/\|x_n\|$ gives equality when $x_n\ne0$; equality also holds at zero. Consequently

$$
\boxed{\sup_n\|x_n\|=\sup_n\|f_n\|<\infty.}
$$

<h4 id="1/a/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1/a)

<h5 id="1/a/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#1/a/iii)

By [weakly convergent sequence is bounded](../weak-topology.md#weakly-convergent-sequence-is-bounded), $M=\sup_n\|x_n\|^2$ is finite. Choose any $n_1$. Once $n_1<\cdots<n_k$ have been chosen, put $s_k=\sum_{j=1}^kx_{n_j}$. Since $s_k$ is fixed, [weak convergence](../weak-topology.md#weak-convergence) to zero gives $\langle s_k,x_n\rangle\to0$. Choose $n_{k+1}>n_k$ with $|\langle s_k,x_{n_{k+1}}\rangle|\leq1$. This gives the required one-sided inequality as well.

The useful estimate is for the **squared** [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm). Indeed, $\|s_1\|^2\leq M$, and the [inner product](../inner-product.md) expansion gives

$$
\|s_{k+1}\|^2
=\|s_k\|^2+2\langle s_k,x_{n_{k+1}}\rangle+\|x_{n_{k+1}}\|^2
\leq\|s_k\|^2+2+M.
$$

By [mathematical induction](../foundations-of-mathematics.md#mathematical-induction), $\|s_k\|^2\leq Mk+2(k-1)\leq(M+2)k$. Hence

$$
\boxed{\left\|\frac1m\sum_{k=1}^m x_{n_k}\right\|^2\leq\frac{M+2}{m}\longrightarrow0.}
$$

This proves [norm convergence](../normed-vector-space.md#norm-convergence) of the averages, the [weak Banach–Saks theorem in a Hilbert space](../hilbert-space.md#weak-banach-saks-theorem-in-a-hilbert-space). The printed inequality omits the square on its left side. Our stronger estimate also proves that printed bound, since $\sqrt{(M+2)k}\leq(M+2)k$ for $k\geq1$, but the printed bound alone only bounds the averages and cannot imply their [norm convergence](../normed-vector-space.md#norm-convergence) to zero.

<h3 id="1/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

<h5 id="1/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#1/b/i)

First suppose the [convex set](../convex-set.md) $K$ is closed in the [norm topology](../normed-vector-space.md#norm-topology), and $x_n\in K$ satisfies $x_n\rightharpoonup x$. Apply the [weak Banach–Saks theorem in a Hilbert space](../hilbert-space.md#weak-banach-saks-theorem-in-a-hilbert-space) to the [weakly null sequence](../weak-topology.md#weakly-null-sequence) $x_n-x$. Along the resulting subsequence,

$$
z_m=\frac1m\sum_{k=1}^m x_{n_k}\longrightarrow x
$$

in [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm). Each $z_m$ is a [convex combination](../convex-set.md#convex-combination) of points in $K$, so $z_m\in K$. Since $K$ is a [closed set](../topology.md#closed-set) in the [norm topology](../normed-vector-space.md#norm-topology), $x\in K$.

Conversely, suppose $K$ is a [sequentially weakly closed set](../weak-topology.md#sequentially-weakly-closed-set). If $x\in\overline K$ in the [norm topology](../normed-vector-space.md#norm-topology), choose $x_n\in K$ with $\|x_n-x\|<1/n$. The [Cauchy-Schwarz inequality](../probability-and-statistics.md#cauchy-schwarz-inequality) gives $|\langle x_n-x,z\rangle|\leq\|x_n-x\|\|z\|\to0$ for every $z$, hence [weak convergence](../weak-topology.md#weak-convergence) to $x$. The assumed closure property gives $x\in K$. The empty [set](../set.md) satisfies both properties too. Thus

$$
\boxed{K\text{ convex}:\quad K\text{ norm-closed}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ K\text{ sequentially weakly closed}.}
$$

<h4 id="1/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

<h5 id="1/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#1/b/ii)

The [orthogonal complement](../hilbert-space.md#orthogonal-complement) $x^\perp$ is infinite-dimensional: otherwise $X=\operatorname{span}\{x\}\oplus x^\perp$ would be finite-dimensional. Inductively choose an [orthonormal sequence](../hilbert-space.md#orthonormal-sequence) $(e_n)$ in $x^\perp$, each new vector being orthogonal to the preceding finite [linear span](../vector-space.md#linear-span). The [Bessel inequality](../hilbert-space.md#bessel-s-inequality) gives $\sum_n|\langle e_n,z\rangle|^2\leq\|z\|^2$ for every $z\in X$, so $\langle e_n,z\rangle\to0$. Thus $(e_n)$ is a [weakly null orthonormal sequence](../hilbert-space.md#weakly-null-orthonormal-sequence).

Set

$$
\boxed{x_n=x+\sqrt{1-\|x\|^2}\,e_n.}
$$

By [orthogonality](../inner-product.md#orthogonal-vectors), $\|x_n\|^2=\|x\|^2+(1-\|x\|^2)=1$, while $\langle x_n-x,z\rangle\to0$ for every $z$. Hence $x_n\rightharpoonup x$. When $\|x\|=1$, this formula simply gives the constant sequence $x_n=x$. In particular, the [weak closure of the unit sphere](../weak-topology.md#weak-closure-of-the-unit-sphere) contains the whole [closed unit ball](../normed-vector-space.md#closed-unit-ball).

<h3 id="1/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#1/c)

For each real $a$, define the [sublevel set](../calculus.md#sublevel-set) $K_a=\{u\in K:F(u)\leq a\}$. Continuity of $F$ and closure of $K$ make $K_a$ a [closed set](../topology.md#closed-set) in $X$; the [convex function](../real-analysis.md#convex-function) inequality makes it a [convex set](../convex-set.md). The preceding result therefore makes every $K_a$ a [sequentially weakly closed set](../weak-topology.md#sequentially-weakly-closed-set). Equivalently, $F$ has [weak lower semicontinuity](../functional-analysis.md#weak-lower-semicontinuity) along sequences in $K$.

Every $K_a$ is bounded. This is immediate if $K$ is bounded. Otherwise, an unbounded $K_a$ would contain $u_n$ with $\|u_n\|\geq n$, contradicting the assumed [coercive function](../real-analysis.md#coercive-function) condition $F(u_n)\to\infty$.

Choose $z\in K$ and put $\ell=\inf_KF\in[-\infty,F(z)]$. Choose a [minimizing sequence](../calculus-of-variations.md#minimizing-sequence) $(u_n)$ entirely in $K_{F(z)}$, with $F(u_n)\to\ell$; when $\ell=-\infty$, require $F(u_n)\leq-n$ for sufficiently large $n$. It is bounded by the previous paragraph.

The [weak subsequence of a bounded Hilbert-space sequence](../hilbert-space.md#weak-subsequence-of-a-bounded-hilbert-space-sequence) theorem gives $u_{n_j}\rightharpoonup u_0$. This applies even if $X$ is not separable: the closed [linear span](../vector-space.md#linear-span) of the sequence is a [separable Hilbert space](../hilbert-space.md#separable-hilbert-space); successive extraction makes every coefficient in a countable [Hilbertian basis](../hilbert-space.md#hilbertian-basis) converge, the common [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm) bound gives square-summable limiting coefficients by the [Bessel inequality](../hilbert-space.md#bessel-s-inequality), and approximation by finite [linear combinations](../vector-space.md#linear-combination) proves [weak convergence](../weak-topology.md#weak-convergence). [Orthogonal decomposition](../hilbert-space.md#orthogonal-decomposition-by-a-closed-subspace) extends that conclusion to all test vectors in $X$. Since $K$ is a [sequentially weakly closed set](../weak-topology.md#sequentially-weakly-closed-set), $u_0\in K$.

For any real $a>\ell$, a tail of $(u_{n_j})$ lies in $K_a$, so $u_0\in K_a$. If $\ell=-\infty$, this would give $F(u_0)\leq a$ for every real $a$, impossible because $F$ is real-valued. Thus $\ell$ is finite; letting $a\downarrow\ell$ gives $F(u_0)\leq\ell$, while the definition of [infimum](../real-analysis.md#infimum) gives the reverse inequality. Therefore

$$
\boxed{u_0\in K,\qquad F(u_0)=\min_{u\in K}F(u).}
$$

This proves [minimum of a coercive convex functional on a Hilbert space](../functional-analysis.md#minimum-of-a-coercive-convex-functional-on-a-hilbert-space) by the [direct method in the calculus of variations](../calculus-of-variations.md#direct-method-in-the-calculus-of-variations), with bounded [sublevel sets](../calculus.md#sublevel-set) supplying the required [weak sequential compactness in a Hilbert space](../functional-analysis.md#weak-sequential-compactness-in-a-hilbert-space).

<h4 id="2/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#2/a)

<h5 id="2/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#2/a/i)

The [Banach-Steinhaus convergence theorem](../banach-space.md#banach-steinhaus-convergence-theorem) can be stated as follows. Let $X$ be a [normed space](../normed-vector-space.md), $Y$ a [Banach space](../banach-space.md), and $A_n\in\mathcal B(X,Y)$. If

$$
\sup_n\|A_n\|<\infty
$$

and $A_nd$ converges in $Y$ for every $d$ in a [dense subset](../topology.md#dense-set) $D\subseteq X$, then $A_nx$ converges for every $x\in X$. The pointwise limit $A$ is a [bounded linear operator](../topological-vector-space.md#continuous-linear-operator) and

$$
\boxed{A_nx\longrightarrow Ax\quad(x\in X),\qquad\|A\|\leq\liminf_n\|A_n\|.}
$$

If $X$ is also a [Banach space](../banach-space.md), the uniform [operator norm](../continuous-dual-space.md#operator-norm) bound is necessary: pointwise convergence implies pointwise boundedness, so the [Banach-Steinhaus theorem](../banach-space.md#uniform-boundedness-principle), namely the [Uniform boundedness principle](../banach-space.md#uniform-boundedness-principle), supplies it. The limit is convergence in the [strong operator topology](../functional-analysis.md#strong-operator-topology); it need not be convergence in [operator norm](../continuous-dual-space.md#operator-norm).

To see the sufficient direction, write $C=\sup_n\|A_n\|$. For $d\in D$ the [triangle inequality](../topological-analysis.md#triangle-inequality) gives

$$
\|A_nx-A_mx\|\leq2C\|x-d\|+\|A_nd-A_md\|.
$$

First approximate $x$ by $d$, then let $m,n\to\infty$. Completeness of $Y$ gives the limit. Linearity passes to limits, and $\|Ax\|=\lim_n\|A_nx\|\leq(\liminf_n\|A_n\|)\|x\|$ gives the displayed bound. If a candidate [bounded linear operator](../topological-vector-space.md#continuous-linear-operator) $A:X\to Y$ is already given and $A_nd\to Ad$ on $D$, the same approximation proves convergence to $Ax$ even when $Y$ is merely a [normed space](../normed-vector-space.md).

<h4 id="2/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#2/a)

<h5 id="2/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#2/a/ii)

Composition with $t\mapsto t^{1+\alpha_n}$ preserves continuity, so $A_n$ maps the [space of continuous functions on a compact space](../functional-analysis.md#space-of-continuous-functions-on-a-compact-space) $C([0,1])$ into itself and is linear. Since $t\mapsto t^{1+\alpha_n}$ maps $[0,1]$ onto itself,

$$
\|A_nx\|_\infty=\|x\|_\infty,\qquad\|A_n\|=1.
$$

For $0<t\leq1$, the [mean value theorem](../calculus.md#mean-value-theorem) applied to $r\mapsto t^{1+r}$ gives

$$
0\leq t-t^{1+\alpha_n}\leq\alpha_n\,t|\log t|\leq\frac{\alpha_n}{e}.
$$

The same bound holds at $t=0$. Each $x\in C([0,1])$ has [uniform continuity](../topological-analysis.md#uniform-continuity) by the [Heine-Cantor theorem](../topological-analysis.md#heine-cantor-theorem). With its [modulus of continuity](../topological-analysis.md#modulus-of-continuity) $\omega_x(\delta)=\sup_{|s-t|\leq\delta}|x(s)-x(t)|$, we obtain

$$
\boxed{\|A_nx-x\|_\infty\leq\omega_x(\alpha_n/e)\longrightarrow0.}
$$

Alternatively, the bound proves convergence on every [polynomial](../polynomial.md); the [Weierstrass approximation theorem](../functional-analysis.md#weierstrass-approximation-theorem) makes these a [dense subset](../topology.md#dense-set) of $C([0,1])$, and the [Banach-Steinhaus convergence theorem](../banach-space.md#banach-steinhaus-convergence-theorem) with $\|A_n\|=1$ gives the same conclusion.

<h3 id="2/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

<h5 id="2/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#2/b/i)

The [open mapping theorem](../functional-analysis.md#open-mapping-theorem-functional-analysis) states that a surjective [bounded linear operator](../topological-vector-space.md#continuous-linear-operator) $T:X\to Y$ between [Banach spaces](../banach-space.md) is an [open map](../multivariable-calculus.md#open-map): $T(U)$ is an [open set](../topology.md#open-set) in $Y$ whenever $U$ is an [open set](../topology.md#open-set) in $X$. Equivalently, there is $\delta>0$ such that

$$
\boxed{B_Y(0,\delta)\subseteq T(B_X(0,1)),}
$$

where both displayed [balls](../topological-analysis.md#ball-mathematics) are open. In particular, a bijective [bounded linear operator](../topological-vector-space.md#continuous-linear-operator) between [Banach spaces](../banach-space.md) has a bounded inverse, the [bounded inverse theorem](../functional-analysis.md#bounded-inverse-theorem).

<h4 id="2/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

<h5 id="2/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#2/b/ii)

Use the [closed unit ball](../normed-vector-space.md#closed-unit-ball) $\overline B_X$ first, and put $C=\overline{T(\overline B_X)}$. This is a [convex set](../convex-set.md) symmetric about zero. If $B_Y(y_0,r)\subseteq C$, then $-y_0\in C$, so taking midpoints gives

$$
B_Y(0,r/2)\subseteq C.
$$

Write $\delta=r/2$. By scaling,

$$
B_Y(0,2^{-j}\delta)\subseteq\overline{T(2^{-j}\overline B_X)}\qquad(j\geq1).
$$

Fix $y$ with $\|y\|<\delta/2$. Starting with $r_0=y$, recursively choose $x_j\in X$ such that

$$
\|x_j\|\leq2^{-j},\qquad
r_j=r_{j-1}-Tx_j,\qquad
\|r_j\|<2^{-j-1}\delta.
$$

At each step the previous residual lies in the open [ball](../topological-analysis.md#ball-mathematics) on the left of the scaled inclusion, so the definition of [closure](../topology.md#closure-topology) permits this approximation. The [series](../sequence-and-series.md#series-mathematics) $\sum_jx_j$ converges to some $x$ because $X$ is a [Banach space](../banach-space.md), and the [triangle inequality](../topological-analysis.md#triangle-inequality) gives $\|x\|\leq\sum_j2^{-j}=1$. Continuity of $T$ gives $Tx=\lim_N\sum_{j=1}^NTx_j=y$, since $r_N\to0$. Thus

$$
B_Y(0,\delta/2)\subseteq T(\overline B_X).
$$

Scaling by $1/2$ now gives the slightly smaller open [ball](../topological-analysis.md#ball-mathematics)

$$
\boxed{B_Y(0,\delta/4)\subseteq T(B_X(0,1)).}
$$

This proves the assertion whether the original [unit ball](../normed-vector-space.md#unit-ball) is taken open or closed. Only completeness of $X$ was used; the [normed space](../normed-vector-space.md) $Y$ need not be complete. The argument is [geometric correction for approximate surjectivity](../functional-analysis.md#geometric-correction-for-approximate-surjectivity).

<h4 id="2/b/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

<h5 id="2/b/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#2/b/iii)

For $n\geq1$ put $C_n=\overline{T(n\overline B_X)}$, with [closure](../topology.md#closure-topology) taken in $Y$. Then

$$
T(X)=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}T(n\overline B_X)\subseteq\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}C_n.
$$

If every $C_n$ has empty [interior](../topology.md#interior-topology), then each $C_n$ is a [nowhere dense set](../topological-analysis.md#nowhere-dense-set), because it is closed. Each $T(n\overline B_X)$ is then a [nowhere dense set](../topological-analysis.md#nowhere-dense-set) too. Consequently $T(X)$ is a [meagre set](../topological-analysis.md#meagre-set), that is, a set of the first category in $Y$.

Otherwise some $C_n$ has nonempty [interior](../topology.md#interior-topology). Scaling gives nonempty [interior](../topology.md#interior-topology) of $\overline{T(\overline B_X)}$. The [geometric correction for approximate surjectivity](../functional-analysis.md#geometric-correction-for-approximate-surjectivity) proved in the preceding part gives an open [ball](../topological-analysis.md#ball-mathematics) about zero inside $T(\overline B_X)$. Since $T(X)$ is a [vector subspace](../vector-space.md#vector-subspace), it then equals $Y$: scale any $y\in Y$ into this [ball](../topological-analysis.md#ball-mathematics) and scale its preimage back. Hence $T(X)$ is a [closed set](../topology.md#closed-set).

Thus, in fact, the stronger [operator range category dichotomy](../functional-analysis.md#operator-range-category-dichotomy) holds:

$$
\boxed{T(X)=Y\quad\text{or}\quad T(X)\text{ is meagre in }Y.}
$$

In particular $T(X)$ is closed or of the first category, as required. The only substantive theorem used here is the preceding [geometric correction for approximate surjectivity](../functional-analysis.md#geometric-correction-for-approximate-surjectivity): for a [bounded linear operator](../topological-vector-space.md#continuous-linear-operator) from a [Banach space](../banach-space.md) to a [normed space](../normed-vector-space.md), nonempty [interior](../topology.md#interior-topology) of the closure of the image of the [unit ball](../normed-vector-space.md#unit-ball) implies nonempty [interior](../topology.md#interior-topology) of the image itself. Its series proof was given there, so this proof does not require the [Baire category theorem](../topological-analysis.md#baire-category-theorem) or completeness of $Y$.

<h4 id="2/b/iv">iv</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

<h5 id="2/b/iv/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#2/b/iv)

Let $J:C([0,1])\to L^1(0,1)$ send a [continuous function](../calculus.md#continuous-function) to its [almost everywhere](../measure-theory.md#almost-everywhere) equivalence class. The domain with the [supremum norm](../normed-vector-space.md#supremum-norm) and the target with the [L1 norm](../normed-vector-space.md#l1-norm) are [Banach spaces](../banach-space.md), and

$$
\|Jf\|_{L^1}=\int_0^1|f(t)|\,dt\leq\|f\|_\infty.
$$

Thus $J$ is a [bounded linear operator](../topological-vector-space.md#continuous-linear-operator), and the requested [vector subspace](../vector-space.md#vector-subspace) is $J(C([0,1]))$.

To show this [image of a linear map](../vector-space.md#image-of-a-linear-map) is not closed, consider the [continuous functions](../calculus.md#continuous-function)

$$
f_n(t)=\min\{1,\max\{0,n(t-1/2)\}\}\qquad(n\geq2)
$$

and $g=\mathbf1_{(1/2,1)}$. Their [L1 norm](../normed-vector-space.md#l1-norm) difference is the area of one triangle:

$$
\|Jf_n-g\|_{L^1}=\int_{1/2}^{1/2+1/n}\bigl(1-n(t-1/2)\bigr)\,dt=\frac1{2n}\longrightarrow0.
$$

But $g$ has no [continuous function](../calculus.md#continuous-function) representative. Indeed, a [continuous function](../calculus.md#continuous-function) equal to zero [almost everywhere](../measure-theory.md#almost-everywhere) on $(0,1/2)$ must be identically zero there: any nonzero value would persist on an open interval of positive [Lebesgue measure](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-measure). Likewise any continuous representative would be identically one on $(1/2,1)$. Continuity at $1/2$ makes these requirements incompatible. Therefore $g\notin J(C([0,1]))$, although it lies in its [closure](../topology.md#closure-topology).

The preceding [operator range category dichotomy](../functional-analysis.md#operator-range-category-dichotomy) proves [continuous functions are meagre in L1](../functional-analysis.md#continuous-functions-are-meagre-in-l1):

$$
\boxed{J(C([0,1]))\text{ is of the first category in }L^1(0,1).}
$$

## 3

↑ **Parent:** [2023](2023.md)

<h3 id="3/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

Use the convention that the [inner product](../inner-product.md) is linear in its first argument. By the defining identity for the [adjoint operator](../hilbert-space.md#adjoint-operator),

$$
\begin{aligned}
x\in(\operatorname{Im}T^*)^\perp
&\Longleftrightarrow\langle x,T^*y\rangle=0\quad\text{for every }y\in X\\
&\Longleftrightarrow\langle Tx,y\rangle=0\quad\text{for every }y\in X\\
&\Longleftrightarrow Tx=0.
\end{aligned}
$$

For the last implication choose $y=Tx$. This proves the first [image-kernel orthogonality for an adjoint](../hilbert-space.md#image-kernel-orthogonality-for-an-adjoint) identity. Taking [orthogonal complements](../hilbert-space.md#orthogonal-complement) and using the [double orthogonal complement](../hilbert-space.md#double-orthogonal-complement) theorem for the [vector subspace](../vector-space.md#vector-subspace) $\operatorname{Im}T^*$ gives

$$
\boxed{\ker T=(\operatorname{Im}T^*)^\perp,\qquad(\ker T)^\perp=\overline{\operatorname{Im}T^*}.}
$$

<h4 id="3/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

<h5 id="3/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#3/a/i)

Put $A=\lambda I-T$. Since $T$ is a [self-adjoint operator](../linear-operator-theory.md#self-adjoint-operator), $A^*=\overline\lambda I-T$. Applying the preceding [image-kernel orthogonality for an adjoint](../hilbert-space.md#image-kernel-orthogonality-for-an-adjoint) identities to $A^*$ gives

$$
\overline{\operatorname{Im}A}=(\ker A^*)^\perp.
$$

If $\lambda$ is real, $A^*=A$ and $\ker A=\{0\}$ because $\lambda$ is not an [eigenvalue](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue). If $\lambda$ is nonreal and $A^*x=0$, then $Tx=\overline\lambda x$. The scalar $\langle Tx,x\rangle$ is real by [self-adjointness](../linear-operator-theory.md#self-adjoint-operator), while it equals $\overline\lambda\|x\|^2$. Its imaginary part forces $x=0$. Thus $\ker A^*=\{0\}$ in both cases, and

$$
\boxed{\overline{\operatorname{Im}(\lambda I-T)}=X.}
$$

The conjugation on $\lambda$ in the [adjoint operator](../hilbert-space.md#adjoint-operator) is essential when $\lambda$ is nonreal.

<h4 id="3/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

<h5 id="3/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#3/a/ii)

Again put $A=\lambda I-T$. Surjectivity gives, by [image-kernel orthogonality for an adjoint](../hilbert-space.md#image-kernel-orthogonality-for-an-adjoint),

$$
\ker A^*=(\operatorname{Im}A)^\perp=X^\perp=\{0\}.
$$

If $\lambda$ is real, $A=A^*$, so $\ker A=\{0\}$. If $\lambda$ is nonreal and $Ax=0$, then $Tx=\lambda x$; reality of $\langle Tx,x\rangle=\lambda\|x\|^2$ again implies $x=0$. Thus $A$ is injective as well as surjective, without needing to assume separately that $\lambda$ is not an [eigenvalue](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue).

Since $A$ is a [bounded linear operator](../topological-vector-space.md#continuous-linear-operator) on the [Banach space](../banach-space.md) $X$, the [bounded inverse theorem](../functional-analysis.md#bounded-inverse-theorem) makes $A^{-1}$ bounded. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\lambda\in\rho(T),\qquad(\lambda I-T)^{-1}\in\mathcal B(X).}
$$

This is exactly membership in the [resolvent set of an operator](../functional-analysis.md#resolvent-set-of-an-operator).

<h3 id="3/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

<h5 id="3/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#3/b/i)

Here the intended space must consist of **bounded** [linear operators](../vector-space.md#linear-operator), namely [Hilbert-Schmidt operators](../compact-operator.md#hilbert-schmidt-operator). The printed restriction to “all linear mappings” is insufficient. To see this, choose $v\in X$ outside the algebraic [linear span](../vector-space.md#linear-span) of $(e_k)$, for example $v=\sum_{k\geq1}2^{-k}e_k$. Extend the linearly independent family $\{e_k:k\geq1\}\cup\{v\}$ to a [Hamel basis](../vector-space.md#basis). Define a [linear functional](../dual-space.md#linear-functional) $\phi$ on this [Hamel basis](../vector-space.md#basis) by $\phi(v)=1$ and zero on every other basis vector, and extend it linearly. Then the everywhere-defined [linear operator](../vector-space.md#linear-operator) $Rx=\phi(x)e_1$ satisfies

$$
Re_k=0\quad(k\geq1),\qquad Rv=e_1\ne0.
$$

Its printed column sum is zero although $R\ne0$. More explicitly, $v_N=\sum_{k=1}^N2^{-k}e_k\to v$ but $R(v-v_N)=e_1$ for every $N$. Thus $R$ is discontinuous; it has no everywhere-defined [adjoint operator](../hilbert-space.md#adjoint-operator) of the bounded kind used in the question. This proves [finite column sum does not imply boundedness](../compact-operator.md#finite-column-sum-does-not-imply-boundedness). We use $T\in\mathcal B(X)$ in the intended argument below.

By the [Parseval identity for a Hilbertian basis](../hilbert-space.md#parseval-identity-for-a-hilbertian-basis) for $(e_j)$, followed by the [adjoint operator](../hilbert-space.md#adjoint-operator) identity and then the [Parseval identity for a Hilbertian basis](../hilbert-space.md#parseval-identity-for-a-hilbertian-basis) for $(h_k)$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_k\|Th_k\|^2
&=\sum_k\sum_j|\langle Th_k,e_j\rangle|^2\\
&=\sum_j\sum_k|\langle h_k,T^*e_j\rangle|^2\\
&=\sum_j\|T^*e_j\|^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

The interchange is valid by [Tonelli's theorem](../measure-theory.md#tonelli-theorem) for nonnegative double [series](../sequence-and-series.md#series-mathematics), even before finiteness is known. Repeating with $h_k=e_k$ shows that the right side also equals $\sum_k\|Te_k\|^2$. Hence

$$
\boxed{\sum_k\|Th_k\|^2=\sum_j\|T^*e_j\|^2=\sum_k\|Te_k\|^2.}
$$

In particular the [Hilbert-Schmidt norm](../compact-operator.md#hilbert-schmidt-norm) is independent of the [Hilbertian basis](../hilbert-space.md#hilbertian-basis), and $\|T^*\|_2=\|T\|_2$.

<h4 id="3/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

<h5 id="3/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#3/b/ii)

As in the preceding part, take $T$ to be a [bounded linear operator](../topological-vector-space.md#continuous-linear-operator). For $x\in X$, the [Hilbertian basis](../hilbert-space.md#hilbertian-basis) expansion and continuity of $T$ give

$$
Tx=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{k=1}^N\langle x,e_k\rangle Te_k.
$$

The [triangle inequality](../topological-analysis.md#triangle-inequality), the [Cauchy-Schwarz inequality](../probability-and-statistics.md#cauchy-schwarz-inequality) for finite sums, and the [Parseval identity for a Hilbertian basis](../hilbert-space.md#parseval-identity-for-a-hilbertian-basis) give

$$
\begin{aligned}
\left\|\sum_{k=1}^N\langle x,e_k\rangle Te_k\right\|
&\leq\sum_{k=1}^N|\langle x,e_k\rangle|\,\|Te_k\|\\
&\leq\left(\sum_{k=1}^N|\langle x,e_k\rangle|^2\right)^{1/2}
\left(\sum_{k=1}^N\|Te_k\|^2\right)^{1/2}
\leq\|x\|\|T\|_2.
\end{aligned}
$$

Passing to the limit and taking the supremum over the [unit ball](../normed-vector-space.md#unit-ball) proves

$$
\boxed{\|T\|\leq\|T\|_2.}
$$

Thus the [Hilbert-Schmidt norm](../compact-operator.md#hilbert-schmidt-norm) controls the [operator norm](../continuous-dual-space.md#operator-norm). Without the boundedness interpretation, the nonzero $R$ constructed in the preceding part has zero printed column sum and makes the requested inequality false.

<h4 id="3/b/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

<h5 id="3/b/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#3/b/iii)

Use the intended space of [Hilbert-Schmidt operators](../compact-operator.md#hilbert-schmidt-operator), with the given [Hilbert-Schmidt inner product](../compact-operator.md#hilbert-schmidt-inner-product) linear in its first argument. It remains to prove completeness in its [Hilbert-Schmidt norm](../compact-operator.md#hilbert-schmidt-norm). Let $(T_n)$ be a [Cauchy sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#cauchy-sequence) in that [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm). The bound $\|T_n-T_m\|\leq\|T_n-T_m\|_2$ makes it a [Cauchy sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#cauchy-sequence) in [operator norm](../continuous-dual-space.md#operator-norm). The space $\mathcal B(X)$ is a [Banach space](../banach-space.md), so $T_n\to T$ in [operator norm](../continuous-dual-space.md#operator-norm) for some [bounded linear operator](../topological-vector-space.md#continuous-linear-operator) $T$.

For completeness, that last standard fact follows by taking $Tx=\lim_nT_nx$ for each $x$: the [Cauchy sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#cauchy-sequence) $(T_n)$ is uniformly bounded in [operator norm](../continuous-dual-space.md#operator-norm), so its limit is linear and bounded, and the uniform Cauchy estimate on the [unit ball](../normed-vector-space.md#unit-ball) gives [operator norm](../continuous-dual-space.md#operator-norm) convergence.

Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose $N$ so that $\|T_n-T_m\|_2<\varepsilon$ for $n,m\geq N$. Fix $n\geq N$ and any positive integer $K$. Since $T_me_k\to Te_k$ for each $k$, passage to the limit in a finite sum gives

$$
\sum_{k=1}^K\|(T_n-T)e_k\|^2
=\lim_{m\to\infty}\sum_{k=1}^K\|(T_n-T_m)e_k\|^2
\leq\varepsilon^2.
$$

Taking the supremum over $K$ gives $\sum_k\|(T_n-T)e_k\|^2\leq\varepsilon^2$. Also,

$$
\sum_k\|Te_k\|^2
\leq2\sum_k\|T_Ne_k\|^2+2\sum_k\|(T-T_N)e_k\|^2<\infty.
$$

Thus $T\in S_2(X)$, and $\|T_n-T\|_2\leq\varepsilon$ for $n\geq N$. This proves [Hilbert-Schmidt operators form a Hilbert space](../compact-operator.md#hilbert-schmidt-operators-form-a-hilbert-space):

$$
\boxed{S_2(X)\text{ is complete in }\|\cdot\|_2,\text{ hence is a Hilbert space}.}
$$

Under the literal unrestricted “linear mappings” definition, the nonzero $R$ from part (i) satisfies $\langle R,R\rangle_{S_2(X)}=0$. The form would fail positive definiteness and would not be an [inner product](../inner-product.md); the boundedness correction is needed here too.

<h3 id="3/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#3/c)

Write $u(t)=e^t$ and $v(t)=e^{-t}$. Both lie in the [Hilbert space](../hilbert-space.md) $L^2(-\pi,\pi)$, and the [integral operator](../functional-analysis.md#integral-operator) factors as the [rank-one operator](../compact-operator.md#rank-one-operator)

$$
Tf=\langle f,v\rangle u.
$$

The [Cauchy-Schwarz inequality](../probability-and-statistics.md#cauchy-schwarz-inequality) gives $\|Tf\|\leq\|u\|\|v\|\|f\|$, and equality is attained at $f=v/\|v\|$. Hence the [operator norm](../continuous-dual-space.md#operator-norm) is $\|T\|=\|u\|\|v\|$. Direct integration gives

$$
\|u\|^2=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{2t}\,dt
=\frac{e^{2\pi}-e^{-2\pi}}2=\sinh(2\pi),\qquad
\|v\|^2=\sinh(2\pi).
$$

For any [Hilbertian basis](../hilbert-space.md#hilbertian-basis) $(h_k)$, the [Parseval identity for a Hilbertian basis](../hilbert-space.md#parseval-identity-for-a-hilbertian-basis) gives

$$
\|T\|_2^2=\sum_k\|\langle h_k,v\rangle u\|^2
=\|u\|^2\sum_k|\langle h_k,v\rangle|^2
=\|u\|^2\|v\|^2.
$$

This is the general formula for the [norms of a rank-one operator](../compact-operator.md#norms-of-a-rank-one-operator). In particular $T$ is a [Hilbert-Schmidt operator](../compact-operator.md#hilbert-schmidt-operator), and

$$
\boxed{\|T\|=\|T\|_2=\sinh(2\pi)=\frac{e^{2\pi}-e^{-2\pi}}2.}
$$

This computation needs no explicit [Fourier basis](../fourier-series.md#fourier-basis). The printed optional basis formula omits the variable $t$: the intended [Hilbertian basis](../hilbert-space.md#hilbertian-basis) is $e_n(t)=(2\pi)^{-1/2}e^{int}$, $n\in\mathbb Z$. Taken literally, the printed expressions are constant [functions](../function.md) and cannot form an [orthonormal basis](../inner-product.md#orthonormal-basis) of $L^2(-\pi,\pi)$.
