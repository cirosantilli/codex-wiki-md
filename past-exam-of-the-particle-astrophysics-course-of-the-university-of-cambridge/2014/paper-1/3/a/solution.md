[Supersymmetry](../../../../../supersymmetry-split.md) relates bosons and fermions whose spins differ by one-half. Broken supersymmetry can soften Higgs-mass sensitivity and improve gauge-coupling unification in suitable spectra; neither feature establishes that nature realizes it. Collider searches look for squarks/gluinos or electroweak superpartners through jets/leptons, missing transverse momentum and appropriate cascade-decay kinematics. Null searches constrain specified masses and branching fractions, with weaker reach for compressed or nonstandard spectra.

With conserved [R-parity](../../../../../r-parity.md), superpartners are pair-produced and the lightest supersymmetric particle is stable. A neutral weakly coupled lightest state, such as a [neutralino](../../../../../neutralino.md), can be a dark-matter candidate. Its thermal abundance follows annihilation freeze-out when $n_\chi\langle\sigma v\rangle\sim H$; later expansion dilutes number density while leaving the comoving relic abundance approximately fixed. Coannihilation and resonances can change the result. Recoil searches and cosmic-ray/photon searches test complementary couplings.

Cosmology also constrains unstable long-lived relics through nucleosynthesis and reheating; not every supersymmetric spectrum gives the observed dark matter. R-parity is an additional assumption: supersymmetry by itself does not guarantee a stable neutral relic. Laboratory discovery and a matching cosmological abundance would both be needed.
