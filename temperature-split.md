# Temperature

↑ **Parent:** [Thermodynamics](thermodynamics-split.md)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperature)

Temperature labels [thermal equilibrium](thermal-equilibrium-split.md): two internally equilibrated bodies have the same [temperature](temperature-split.md) when contact permitting only [heat](heat-split.md) exchange produces no net [heat transfer](heat-transfer.md). The [zeroth law of thermodynamics](zeroth-law-of-thermodynamics.md) makes the comparison transitive. If bodies A and B separately reach [thermal equilibrium](thermal-equilibrium-split.md) with the same [thermometer](thermometer.md) without appreciably disturbing either body, they agree with each other. This is what lets a [gas thermometer](gas-thermometer.md) compare a metal block with [water](water.md), despite their different [internal energies](internal-energy-split.md) and [heat capacities](heat-capacity.md). Insulation alone makes no such comparison: it can prevent [heat transfer](heat-transfer.md) between bodies at different [temperatures](temperature-split.md). [Ling, Moebs and Sanny, University Physics Volume 2, section 1.1: temperature and the zeroth law](university-physics-volume-2-by-samuel-j-ling-and-others/1-1-temperature-and-thermal-equilibrium.md)

The [zeroth law of thermodynamics](zeroth-law-of-thermodynamics.md) establishes equality comparisons, but does not choose a numerical scale. [Thermodynamic temperature](thermodynamic-temperature.md) supplies an absolute scale consistent with [Carnot's theorem](carnot-s-theorem.md) and [entropy](entropy-split.md). For a [simple compressible system](simple-compressible-system.md) at [thermodynamic equilibrium](thermodynamic-equilibrium.md) with fixed particle number $N$ and composition,

$$
T=\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial S}\right)_{V,N},\qquad
\frac1T=\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial U}\right)_{V,N}.
$$

Here $U$ is [internal energy](internal-energy-split.md), $S$ is [entropy](entropy-split.md), and $V$ is [volume](volume-thermodynamics.md); the second equality assumes the derivative can be inverted. Temperature is a [thermodynamic conjugate variable](conjugate-variables-thermodynamics.md) to [entropy](entropy-split.md) and belongs to the [intensive and extensive thermodynamic quantities](intensive-and-extensive-thermodynamic-quantities.md) as an intensive quantity. Its [partial derivative](partial-derivative.md) tells us how much [internal energy](internal-energy-split.md) must change per unit change of [entropy](entropy-split.md) under the stated constraints. More general systems require their other mechanical and material variables to be held fixed too. [David Tong, Statistical Physics, section 1.2.2: temperature from the entropy derivative](https://davidtong.org/teaching/statistical-physics/statmechhtml/S1#S1.SS2.SSS2)

For an infinitesimal quantity $\delta Q>0$ of [heat](heat-split.md) transferred from A to B, with no [work](work.md) or matter exchange, the equilibrium [entropy](entropy-split.md) changes give

$$
dS_{\rm total}=\delta Q\left(\frac1{T_B}-\frac1{T_A}\right).
$$

For ordinary positive [temperatures](temperature-split.md), spontaneous [heat transfer](heat-transfer.md) from $T_A>T_B$ increases total [entropy](entropy-split.md); [thermal equilibrium](thermal-equilibrium-split.md) has $T_A=T_B$. This comparison uses changes in [entropy](entropy-split.md), rather than the total [internal energy](internal-energy-split.md) contained in either body. A small hot object can have less [internal energy](internal-energy-split.md) than a large cool one. See [entropy maximization under thermal contact](entropy-maximization-under-thermal-contact.md) for the equilibrium criterion and [negative temperature](negative-temperature.md) for the special case of bounded [energy](energy-split.md) spectra.

Express absolute [thermodynamic temperature](thermodynamic-temperature.md) $T$ in [kelvin](kelvin.md), and a Celsius reading $t_C$ in [degree Celsius](degree-celsius.md):

$$
\frac{t_C}{{}^\circ\mathrm C}=\frac{T}{\mathrm K}-273.15,\qquad
\frac{\Delta t_C}{{}^\circ\mathrm C}=\frac{\Delta T}{\mathrm K}.
$$

The offset is exact: $300\,\mathrm K$ corresponds to $26.85\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$, and $400\,\mathrm K$ to $126.85\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$. Their absolute [temperature](temperature-split.md) ratio is $4/3$; dividing their Celsius readings gives a different number. Thus [gas thermometer](gas-thermometer.md) pressure ratios and [Carnot's theorem](carnot-s-theorem.md) require [thermodynamic temperature](thermodynamic-temperature.md). The modern [SI](international-system-of-units.md) definition of the [kelvin](kelvin.md) fixes the [Boltzmann constant](boltzmann-constant.md) $k_B=1.380649\times10^{-23}\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$; a reference bath realizes or transfers the scale rather than defining that constant.[section 2.3.1: kelvin and Celsius temperature](the-international-system-of-units-si-ninth-edition-si-brochure.md)

Temperature is not universally the [mean](expected-value-split.md) [kinetic energy](kinetic-energy.md) of a particle. In a classical, nonrelativistic [ideal gas](ideal-gas-split.md) at [thermal equilibrium](thermal-equilibrium-split.md), the [equipartition theorem](equipartition-theorem.md) gives the mean translational [kinetic energy](kinetic-energy.md) relative to the gas's bulk motion:

$$
\left\langle\frac12m|\mathbf v-\mathbf u|^2\right\rangle=\frac32k_BT.
$$

Here $m$ is particle [mass](mass.md), $\mathbf v$ its [velocity](velocity.md), and $\mathbf u$ the mean flow [velocity](velocity.md). In this regime, different particle [masses](mass.md) at the same [temperature](temperature-split.md) have equal mean translational [kinetic energies](kinetic-energy.md) but different characteristic speeds. A moving gas can also carry bulk [kinetic energy](kinetic-energy.md) without being hotter in its rest frame. Internal molecular modes and [quantum statistics](quantum-ideal-gas-statistics-split.md) require further care; the [equipartition theorem](equipartition-theorem.md) has classical assumptions, whereas the equilibrium [entropy](entropy-split.md) definition applies more widely. [Tong, Statistical Physics, section 2.2.1: classical equipartition and its limits](https://davidtong.org/teaching/statistical-physics/statmechhtml/S2#S2.SS2.SSS1)
