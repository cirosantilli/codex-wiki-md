# Ecology

↑ **Parent:** [Biology](biology-split.md)

The goal of [ecology](ecology-split.md) is to explain where [organisms](organism-split.md) live, how many there are, and how their interactions with one another and their surroundings change those patterns. Living partners include [prey](prey.md), [predators](predator.md) and [parasites](parasite.md); nonliving conditions include [light](light.md), [temperature](temperature-split.md) and [water](water.md). An [ecosystem](ecosystem.md) brings these interacting parts into one study system. See [Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e, 44.1: the scope of ecology](https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/44-1-the-scope-of-ecology).

Start with an invented aquatic [food web](food-web.md): photosynthetic [algae](algae.md) feed [zooplankton](zooplankton.md), which feed small [fish](fish.md), which feed large [fish](fish.md). That [food chain](food-chain.md) has four feeding positions and three feeding arrows. Dead material also reaches [decomposers](decomposer.md) through [detritus](detritus.md), so the [food web](food-web.md) branches rather than forming a single ladder.

**[Figure 1](#image-an-illustrative-aquatic-food-web-feeding-arrows-point-from-resource-to-consumer). An illustrative aquatic food web: feeding arrows point from resource to consumer**. Original schematic, not a measured [ecosystem](ecosystem.md). Solid arrows mean RESOURCE → CONSUMER; dashed arrows mean transfer of dead material to [detritus](detritus.md). Box sizes and arrow widths do not represent abundance or transfer rates. [Algae](algae.md), [zooplankton](zooplankton.md), small [fish](fish.md) and large [fish](fish.md) are generic groups, not identified [species](species.md). Selected dead-material transfers are shown; further feeding and recycling routes are omitted.

The appeal of [ecology](ecology-split.md) is that an [organism](organism-split.md)'s surroundings include other [organisms](organism-split.md) that change those surroundings. [Photosynthesis](photosynthesis.md) links [light](light.md) to living material; [predation](predation.md) transfers that material between [organisms](organism-split.md); [decomposers](decomposer.md) return its constituent [chemical elements](chemical-element.md) to forms that can be used again. These connections help explain why changing one part of an [ecosystem](ecosystem.md) can affect distant parts of its [food web](food-web.md), although an arrow alone does not predict the size or even the direction of an indirect change. [Population dynamics](population-dynamics.md) supplies quantitative tools when the question concerns changing counts.

In 1866, Ernst Haeckel's _Generelle Morphologie der Organismen_ included a section titled _Oecologie und Chorologie_ in volume 2, beginning on page 286. This places the terminology of [ecology](ecology-split.md) in an early systematic treatment of [organisms](organism-split.md) and their relationships. See [Haeckel, Generelle Morphologie der Organismen, volume 2 (1866), contents and p. 286](https://books.google.com/books?id=tKFbIVgJS8wC). A later quantitative landmark is Raymond L. Lindeman's 1942 treatment of [ecosystems](ecosystem.md) through feeding relationships and [energy flow](energy-flow-ecology.md). See [Lindeman, The Trophic-Dynamic Aspect of Ecology, Ecology 23 (1942), 399–417](https://doi.org/10.2307/1930126).
