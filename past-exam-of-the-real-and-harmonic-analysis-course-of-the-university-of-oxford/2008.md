<h3 id="1/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1/a)

<h5 id="1/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#1/a/i)

The [restriction of a measure to a measurable subset](../measure.md#restriction-of-a-measure-to-a-measurable-subset) is $(\mu|_M)(A)=\mu(A\cap M)$ for every measurable $A$. [Absolute continuity of measures](../measure-theory.md#absolute-continuity-of-measures), written $\mu\ll\kappa$, means $\kappa(A)=0\implies\mu(A)=0$. The [measures](../measure.md) are [mutually singular](../measure-theory.md#mutually-singular-measures) when a measurable $S$ satisfies $\kappa(S)=0$ and $\mu(X\setminus S)=0$. Thus singularity means concentration on a [null set](../measure-theory.md#null-set) of the other [measure](../measure.md); it does not assert that every [measurable set](../measure-theory.md#measurable-set) is null for one of them.

<h4 id="1/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1/a)

<h5 id="1/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#1/a/ii)

Suppose $\mu=\alpha+\sigma=\beta+\tau$ are two such decompositions. Choose measurable supports $S,T$ of the singular parts with $\kappa(S)=\kappa(T)=0$, and put $N=S\cup T$. [Absolute continuity](../sobolev-space.md#absolutely-continuous-function) makes both $\alpha$ and $\beta$ vanish on $N$. On $X\setminus N$ the two singular parts vanish, so for every measurable $A$,

$$
\alpha(A)=\alpha(A\setminus N)=\mu(A\setminus N)=\beta(A\setminus N)=\beta(A).
$$

Subtracting from the [finite measure](../measure.md#finite-measure) $\mu$ gives $\sigma=\tau$. This proves **uniqueness of the [Lebesgue decomposition](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-decomposition-theorem)** without subtracting infinite masses.

For the specified $M$, the restriction to $M$ is singular because $\kappa(M)=0$, and the complementary restriction is absolutely continuous by assumption. Their sum is $\mu$. Uniqueness therefore gives

$$
\boxed{\mu^s=\mu|_M,\qquad\mu^a=\mu|_{X\setminus M}.}
$$

<h4 id="1/a/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1/a)

<h5 id="1/a/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#1/a/iii)

For the finite nonnegative [measures](../measure.md) there exist a unique nonnegative integrable density $h\in L^1(\kappa)$, up to $\kappa$-almost-everywhere equality, and a unique nonnegative [measure](../measure.md) $\mu^s\perp\kappa$, such that

$$
\boxed{\mu(A)=\int_Ah\,d\kappa+\mu^s(A)\quad(A\in\mathfrak M).}
$$

This combines the [Lebesgue decomposition theorem](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-decomposition-theorem) with the [Radon-Nikodym theorem](../measure-theory.md#radon-nikodym-theorem). In particular $\mu^a=h\kappa$ and $h=d\mu^a/d\kappa$; if $\mu\ll\kappa$ the singular term is zero.

<h3 id="1/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

Write $J(A)$ for the partition supremum. Empty cells are permitted, so $J\geq\mu_i$ for each $i$, and $J(A)\leq\sum_i\mu_i(A)$ is finite. Also $J(\varnothing)=0$.

Let $A=\bigsqcup_{j\geq1}B_j$. Any measurable partition $A=\bigsqcup_iA_i$ yields

$$
\sum_i\mu_i(A_i)=\sum_j\sum_i\mu_i(A_i\cap B_j)\leq\sum_jJ(B_j).
$$

Taking its supremum gives one inequality. Conversely, for any $\varepsilon>0$ choose for each $j$ a partition $B_j=\bigsqcup_iB_{ji}$ whose sum exceeds $J(B_j)-\varepsilon2^{-j}$. The sets $A_i=\bigsqcup_jB_{ji}$ partition $A$, and [countable additivity](../measure.md#countable-additivity) of each $\mu_i$ gives $J(A)\geq\sum_jJ(B_j)-\varepsilon$. Let $\varepsilon\downarrow0$. Thus **$J$ is a [finite measure](../measure.md#finite-measure)**.

If another [measure](../measure.md) $\nu$ dominates every $\mu_i$, then for every such partition $\sum_i\mu_i(A_i)\leq\sum_i\nu(A_i)=\nu(A)$. Consequently $J\leq\nu$. Hence **$J=\bigvee_i\mu_i$ is the least dominating [measure](../measure.md)**, the [finite supremum of positive measures](../measure.md#finite-supremum-of-positive-measures). It generally differs from the pointwise maximum of the numbers $\mu_i(A)$: the maximizing index may differ between pieces of $A$.

<h3 id="1/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#1/c)

Choose [measurable sets](../measure-theory.md#measurable-set) $S_i$ carrying $\mu_i^s$ with $\kappa(S_i)=0$, and put $S=\bigcup_iS_i$. Every absolutely continuous component vanishes on $S$, and every singular component vanishes on its complement. The given restriction identity therefore gives

$$
\mu|_S=\bigvee_i\mu_i^s,\qquad
\mu|_{S^c}=\bigvee_i\mu_i^a.
$$

The first is singular with respect to $\kappa$. The second is absolutely continuous because it is bounded above by $\sum_i\mu_i^a$. They sum to $\mu$, so uniqueness identifies them as its Lebesgue components.

Let $h_i=d\mu_i^a/d\kappa$ and $h=\max_i h_i$, using measurable representatives. Each partition sum is at most $\int_Ah\,d\kappa$. For the reverse inequality, partition $A$ measurably by assigning a point to the smallest index attaining this finite maximum. On its assigned cell $h_i=h$, and the partition sum is exactly $\int_Ah\,d\kappa$. Thus

$$
\boxed{\frac{d\mu^a}{d\kappa}=\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\frac{d\mu_i^a}{d\kappa}\quad\kappa\text{-almost everywhere}.}
$$

The maximum of finitely many integrable nonnegative densities is integrable, since it is at most their sum.

<h3 id="2/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#2/a)

The [Lebesgue outer measure](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-outer-measure) is

$$
\mathcal L^*(E)=\inf\left\{\sum_j|Q_j|:E\subseteq\bigcup_jQ_j,\ Q_j\text{ bounded open axis-parallel boxes}\right\},
$$

where $|Q|$ is the product of its side lengths. The infimum ranges over countable covers; allowing other standard endpoint conventions gives the same [outer measure](../measure-theory.md#outer-measure) by arbitrarily small enlargement. [Lebesgue measure](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-measure) is its restriction to [Lebesgue measurable sets](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-measurable-set).

Enlarged unit cubes give $\mathcal L^*([0,1]^n)\leq1$. For any countable open-box cover of this compact cube choose a finite subcover. A finite grid formed from its box endpoints and the cube endpoints partitions the cube into rectangular cells. The sum of the volumes of covering boxes is at least the sum of these cell volumes, which is one. Boundary faces have elementary zero volume and do not affect the finite volume comparison. Thus every covering sum is at least one, proving

$$
\boxed{\mathcal L^*([0,1]^n)=1.}
$$

<h3 id="2/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

A set $H$ is Lebesgue measurable when the [Caratheodory measurability criterion](../measure-theory.md#caratheodory-s-criterion) holds:

$$
\mathcal L^*(E)=\mathcal L^*(E\cap H)+\mathcal L^*(E\setminus H)\quad\text{for every }E\subseteq\mathbb R^n.
$$

Subadditivity always gives the inequality with left side at most right side. For $H=\{x_i>t\}$, split each covering box $Q_j$ across the plane $x_i=t$. Its two pieces have total volume $|Q_j|$. The piece with a closed cut face can be enlarged by arbitrarily little into an open box; hence its [outer measure](../measure-theory.md#outer-measure) is at most its corresponding volume, just as for the other piece. Subadditivity applied separately on the two sides consequently gives

$$
\mathcal L^*(E\cap H)+\mathcal L^*(E\setminus H)\leq\sum_j|Q_j|.
$$

For finite [outer measure](../measure-theory.md#outer-measure) take a covering sum arbitrarily close to $\mathcal L^*(E)$; infinite [outer measure](../measure-theory.md#outer-measure) follows already from subadditivity. Thus **every coordinate half space is measurable**.

Carathéodory's theorem says that the [measurable sets](../measure-theory.md#measurable-set) form a [sigma-algebra](../measure-theory.md#sigma-algebra) and the [outer measure](../measure-theory.md#outer-measure) restricted to it is a [complete measure](../measure.md#complete-measure). Coordinate half spaces generate all open boxes and then all open sets, using countably many rational-endpoint boxes. They therefore generate the [Borel sigma-algebra](../measure-theory.md#borel-sigma-algebra). Hence **every [Borel set](../measure-theory.md#borel-set) is Lebesgue measurable**.

<h3 id="2/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#2/c)

A [regular outer measure](../measure-theory.md#regular-outer-measure) has a measurable hull of equal [outer measure](../measure-theory.md#outer-measure) for every set: for each $A$ there is measurable $G\supseteq A$ with $\mathcal L^*(G)=\mathcal L^*(A)$. [Lebesgue outer measure](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-outer-measure) has even a Borel hull. If its value on $A$ is finite, choose open-box covers of total cost below $\mathcal L^*(A)+1/j$, and let $U_j$ be their open unions. Then $G=\bigcap_jU_j$ is Borel and

$$
\mathcal L^*(A)\leq\mathcal L^*(G)\leq\mathcal L^*(U_j)\leq\mathcal L^*(A)+1/j.
$$

For infinite [outer measure](../measure-theory.md#outer-measure) the whole space is a suitable hull. Thus **[Lebesgue outer measure](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-outer-measure) is regular**.

If $A$ is measurable, additivity immediately gives the requested equality. Conversely suppose the equality holds. Choose a measurable hull $G$ of $A$ and put $B=G\cap M$. Then $A\subseteq B\subseteq M$ and $\mathcal L(B)=\mathcal L^*(A)$. Since $B$ is measurable, its splitting of $M\setminus A$ gives

$$
\mathcal L^*(M\setminus A)=\mathcal L(M\setminus B)+\mathcal L^*(B\setminus A).
$$

The assumed equality and $\mathcal L(M)=\mathcal L(B)+\mathcal L(M\setminus B)$ imply $\mathcal L^*(B\setminus A)=0$. These subtractions are legitimate because $\mathcal L(M)<\infty$. Outer-[measure](../measure.md)-[null sets](../measure-theory.md#null-set) are measurable by the [Caratheodory criterion](../measure-theory.md#caratheodory-s-criterion). Hence $A=B\setminus(B\setminus A)$ is measurable, proving **the equivalence**.

<h3 id="3/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

For a [complex measure](../measure.md#complex-measure), its [total variation measure](../measure.md#variation-measure) is

$$
|\mu|(E)=\sup\left\{\sum_j|\mu(E_j)|:E=\bigsqcup_{j=1}^mE_j,\ E_j\text{ Borel}\right\}.
$$

Its norm is **$\|\mu\|=|\mu|(\mathbb T)$**. For [Radon measures](../measure.md#radon-measure) on the compact circle this is also $\sup_{\|f\|_\infty\leq1,f\in C(\mathbb T)}|\int f\,d\mu|$. The stated convergence is weak-star convergence against continuous tests:

$$
\boxed{\mu_k\overset{*}{\rightharpoonup}\mu\iff\int f\,d\mu_k\to\int f\,d\mu\quad\text{for every }f\in C(\mathbb T).}
$$

It is the [weak-star topology](../weak-topology.md#weak-star-topology) from the identification of these [measures](../measure.md) with the dual of $C(\mathbb T)$, rather than convergence of their values on every [Borel set](../measure-theory.md#borel-set).

<h3 id="3/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

Set $C=\sup_k\|\mu_k\|$. Given $f\in C(\mathbb T)$, choose $g\in X$ with $\|f-g\|_\infty<\varepsilon$. Then

$$
\left|\int f\,d\mu_k-\int f\,d\mu\right|\leq(C+\|\mu\|)\varepsilon+\left|\int g\,d\mu_k-\int g\,d\mu\right|.
$$

Take the upper limit in $k$ and then $\varepsilon\downarrow0$. Thus **convergence on the dense test family suffices**; no assumption that $X$ is a vector space is needed.

For nonempty compact $K$, put $f_j(x)=\max\{0,1-j\operatorname{dist}(x,K)\}$. These [continuous functions](../calculus.md#continuous-function) decrease to $\mathbf1_K$ and satisfy $\mu_k(K)\leq\int f_j\,d\mu_k$. Consequently $\limsup_k\mu_k(K)\leq\int f_j\,d\mu$ for every $j$. [Dominated convergence](../measure-theory.md#dominated-convergence-theorem) for the finite [positive measure](../measure.md#positive-measure) $\mu$ yields

$$
\boxed{\limsup_k\mu_k(K)\leq\mu(K).}
$$

The empty-set case is immediate. Positivity is essential for the comparison with these cutoff functions.

<h3 id="3/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#3/c)

Write $m(dx)=dx/(2\pi)$ for the normalized [Haar measure](../measure.md#haar-measure). The identity $\sin^2(kx)=\tfrac12-\tfrac12\cos(2kx)$ gives $\mu_k=\tfrac12m-\tfrac12\cos(2kx)m$. For any fixed [trigonometric polynomial](../fourier-series.md#trigonometric-polynomial), [orthogonality of complex exponentials](../fourier-analysis.md#orthogonality-of-complex-exponentials) makes the oscillatory integral zero once $2k$ exceeds its largest frequency. The norms satisfy $\|\mu_k\|=\mu_k(\mathbb T)=1/2$, so the dense-test criterion applies. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\mu_k\overset{*}{\rightharpoonup}\tfrac12m,\qquad d\mu=\frac{dx}{4\pi}.}
$$

In particular the limit has mass one half; treating these [measures](../measure.md) as probabilities would introduce a factor-of-two error.

<h3 id="3/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#3/d)

The [Fourier coefficient of a measure on the circle](../fourier-transform.md#fourier-coefficient-of-a-measure-on-the-circle) and [convolution of measures on the circle](../fourier-analysis.md#convolution-of-measures-on-the-circle) use the angular group law:

$$
\widehat\mu(n)=\int_{\mathbb T}e^{-inx}\,d\mu(x),\qquad
(\mu*\nu)(E)=\int_{\mathbb T}\int_{\mathbb T}\mathbf1_E(x+y)\,d\mu(x)d\nu(y).
$$

Addition is modulo $2\pi$. This is the pushforward of the product [complex measure](../measure.md#complex-measure) under addition; equivalently its integral against a continuous $f$ is $\iint f(x+y)d\mu(x)d\nu(y)$. The integrands are bounded and the variation [measures](../measure.md) finite, so Fubini applies. In particular,

$$
\widehat{\mu*\nu}(n)=\iint e^{-in(x+y)}d\mu(x)d\nu(y)=\left(\int e^{-inx}d\mu(x)\right)\left(\int e^{-iny}d\nu(y)\right).
$$

Thus **$\widehat{\mu*\nu}(n)=\widehat\mu(n)\widehat\nu(n)$**. These [measure](../measure.md) coefficients use no additional Haar normalization factor: a [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability) has zeroth coefficient one.

<h3 id="3/e">e</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/e/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [E](#3/e)

Read the coefficient inside the limit as $\widehat{\mu_k}(n)$; the printed omission of its index would otherwise make that condition independent of the sequence. Weak-star convergence immediately implies convergence of every coefficient by testing $e^{-inx}$. Conversely coefficient convergence gives convergence against every [trigonometric polynomial](../fourier-series.md#trigonometric-polynomial). Since $\|\mu_k\|=1$, the dense-test argument yields **weak-star convergence to $\mu$**.

The limit is necessarily a [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability). Testing the constant function gives $\mu(\mathbb T)=1$. For every nonnegative continuous $f$, $\int f\,d\mu=\lim_k\int f\,d\mu_k\geq0$. A [Radon measure](../measure.md#radon-measure) whose integrals against all such functions are nonnegative is positive, by the positive case of the [Riesz representation theorem](../hilbert-space.md#riesz-representation-theorem) (or continuous approximation of Borel indicators). Thus **$\mu$ is nonnegative and has total mass one**.

<h3 id="3/f">f</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/f/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [F](#3/f)

[Convolution](../fourier-analysis.md#convolution) preserves [probability measures](../probability-space.md#probability-measure): its defining pushforward is nonnegative and its total mass is the product of the two unit masses. For every fixed integer $n$ the previous coefficient identity gives

$$
\widehat{\mu_k*\nu_k}(n)=\widehat\mu_k(n)\widehat\nu_k(n)\longrightarrow\widehat\mu(n)\widehat\nu(n)=\widehat{\mu*\nu}(n).
$$

The [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability) version of the Fourier coefficient criterion therefore proves

$$
\boxed{\mu_k*\nu_k\overset{*}{\rightharpoonup}\mu*\nu.}
$$

The fixed compact underlying group and the uniform [probability](../probability-theory.md#probability) masses supply precisely the bounds needed in the dense-test argument.

## 4

↑ **Parent:** [2008](2008.md)

<h3 id="4/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#4)

<h4 id="4/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#4/a)

<h5 id="4/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#4/a/i)

The centered [Hardy-Littlewood maximal function](../analysis.md#hardy-littlewood-maximal-function) is

$$
Mf(x)=\sup_{r>0}\frac1{2r}\int_{x-r}^{x+r}|f(y)|dy.
$$

The [Hardy-Littlewood maximal inequality](../analysis.md#hardy-littlewood-maximal-inequality) gives, for example,

$$
\boxed{\lambda(\{Mf>t\})\leq\frac3t\|f\|_1\quad(t>0).}
$$

This valid constant follows from the disjoint-interval covering argument: a compact subset of the superlevel set has a finite cover by intervals of average above $t$; select disjoint intervals whose triples cover it, bound their total length by $\|f\|_1/t$, and use [inner regularity](../measure.md#inner-regular-measure). An optimal constant is not required.

<h4 id="4/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#4/a)

<h5 id="4/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#4/a/ii)

Let $Df(x)$ denote the upper limit of the displayed mean oscillation as $r\downarrow0$. For a continuous integrable approximation $g$, continuity gives $Dg(x)=0$. With $h=f-g$, the [triangle inequality](../topological-analysis.md#triangle-inequality) yields

$$
Df(x)\leq Mh(x)+|h(x)|.
$$

Therefore the maximal inequality and [Chebyshev's inequality](../probability-inequality.md#chebyshev-s-inequality) give

$$
\lambda(\{Df>2\varepsilon\})\leq\lambda(\{Mh>\varepsilon\})+\lambda(\{|h|>\varepsilon\})\leq\frac4\varepsilon\|f-g\|_1.
$$

Density makes the right side arbitrarily small. For each positive rational $\varepsilon$ the exceptional set is null, proving **$Df=0$ [almost everywhere](../measure-theory.md#almost-everywhere)**, the [Lebesgue differentiation theorem](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-differentiation-theorem). Values where the chosen representative is not finite already form a [null set](../measure-theory.md#null-set).

For the fundamental theorem define $F(x)=\int_a^xf(t)dt$, with oriented integration for $x<a$. This function is absolutely continuous: for disjoint intervals the sum of $|F(b_j)-F(a_j)|$ is at most the integral of $|f|$ over their union, which is small when its [measure](../measure.md) is small by [absolute continuity](../sobolev-space.md#absolutely-continuous-function) of the integral. At a [Lebesgue point](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-point) of $f$, for $h\ne0$,

$$
\left|\frac{F(x+h)-F(x)}h-f(x)\right|\leq\frac1{|h|}\int_{x-|h|}^{x+|h|}|f(t)-f(x)|dt\to0.
$$

Thus **$F'=f$ [almost everywhere](../measure-theory.md#almost-everywhere)** and $F(b)-F(a)=\int_a^bf$. Differentiability at every point is not claimed for an arbitrary integrable function.

<h4 id="4/a/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#4/a)

<h5 id="4/a/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#4/a/iii)

For a set $M$ of [finite measure](../measure.md#finite-measure) apply the preceding result to $f=\mathbf1_M$. Since its local average minus its point value is bounded in absolute value by its mean absolute oscillation, it gives

$$
\boxed{\frac{\lambda(M\cap(x-r,x+r))}{2r}\longrightarrow\mathbf1_M(x)\quad\text{almost everywhere}.}
$$

For a general measurable $M$, apply the same argument to $\mathbf1_{M\cap[-j,j]}\in L^1(\mathbb R)$. At every interior point of $(-j,j)$, sufficiently short averages agree with those of $\mathbf1_M$. The [countable union](../set.md#countable-union) of these interiors covers the line, and a [countable union](../set.md#countable-union) of null exceptional sets remains null. This proves **the [Lebesgue density theorem](../measure-theory.md#lebesgue-s-density-theorem) without a finite-[measure](../measure.md) assumption on $M$**. The source's $\chi$ is the indicator of $M$.

<h3 id="4/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#4)

<h4 id="4/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#4/b)

<h5 id="4/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#4/b/i)

Every $x$ in the maximal superlevel set has a first index $k(x)$ at which the average on its unique cell $M_k(x)\in\mathcal P_k$ exceeds $t$. Refinement implies that all earlier cells containing $x$ contain this cell, and their averages are at most $t$. Thus $M_{k(x)}(x)\in\mathcal S_t$ and the selected cells cover the superlevel set.

Two cells at different levels are either disjoint or nested, because of refinement and the partition property. If selected cells of levels $k<l$ intersect, the level-$l$ one lies in the earlier cell whose average is above $t$, contradicting its first-crossing property. Cells at the same level are disjoint unless identical. Hence **the selected cells form a countable disjoint partition of the superlevel set**. A cell repeated unchanged at successive levels is counted once, at its first crossing; equal earlier ancestors are included in the comparison condition.

<h4 id="4/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#4/b)

<h5 id="4/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#4/b/ii)

The [first-crossing maximal inequality for refining partitions](../measure-theory.md#first-crossing-maximal-inequality-for-refining-partitions) follows from countable disjointness and the strict average bound:

$$
\mu(\{\widetilde Mf>t\})=\sum_{M\in\mathcal S_t}\mu(M)\leq\frac1t\sum_{M\in\mathcal S_t}\int_M|f|\,d\mu\leq\frac{\|f\|_1}t.
$$

Thus **this partition maximal operator has weak-type constant one**.

The conclusion of [differentiation along refining metric partitions](../measure-theory.md#differentiation-along-refining-metric-partitions) is

$$
\boxed{\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac1{\mu(M_k(x))}\int_{M_k(x)}|f(y)-f(x)|d\mu(y)=0\quad\text{for }\mu\text{-almost every }x.}
$$

The crucial geometric property is **shrinking [diameter](../topological-analysis.md#diameter)**, not merely refinement: since $x\in M_k(x)$ and $\operatorname{diam}M_k(x)<1/k$, continuity makes the corresponding oscillation tend to zero for every continuous test function. Continuous $L^1$ approximation and the maximal inequality then transfer this to $f$, exactly as before: the exceptional-set bound is $2\|f-g\|_1/\varepsilon$ for an oscillation threshold $2\varepsilon$. Countability makes the maximal function measurable, and positive cell [measure](../measure.md) makes every average defined. Refinement supplied the disjoint first-crossing argument; shrinking [diameter](../topological-analysis.md#diameter) supplies differentiation.
