# Limit superior

↑ **Parent:** [Sequence](sequence.md)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limit_superior)

The limit superior of a real [sequence](sequence.md) is

$$
\limsup_{n\to\infty}a_n=\inf_N\sup_{n\geq N}a_n,
$$

allowing a value in the [extended real number line](extended-real-number-line.md). For a finite value $U$, every $\varepsilon>0$ gives an eventual upper bound $a_n<U+\varepsilon$, while arbitrarily late terms still satisfy $a_n>U-\varepsilon$. The second condition concerns recurrence at arbitrarily large indices, not the fraction of indices where a large value occurs.

In [limit inferior and limit superior](limit-inferior-and-limit-superior.md), $a_n=(-1)^n(1+1/n)$ has [limit superior](limit-superior.md) one because its positive branch approaches one from above and all later values eventually stay below $1+\varepsilon$. Changing any finite number of terms leaves the [limit superior](limit-superior.md) unchanged: once the [tail of a sequence](tail-of-a-sequence.md) begins beyond those positions, all its [suprema](supremum.md) are the same as before. The [sparse peaks can determine a limit superior](sparse-peaks-can-determine-a-limit-superior.md) example shows that even a [set](set-split.md) of peak indices with zero [natural density](natural-density.md) can decide the upper long-run edge.
