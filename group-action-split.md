# Group action

↑ **Parent:** [Group theory](group-theory-split.md)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action)

A left [group action](group-action-split.md) of a [group](group-split.md) $G$ on a [set](set-split.md) $X$ is a [function](function-split.md) $(g,x)\mapsto g\cdot x$ satisfying

$$
e\cdot x=x,\qquad (gh)\cdot x=g\cdot(h\cdot x)\qquad(g,h\in G,\ x\in X),
$$

where $e$ is the [identity element](identity-element.md) of $G$. The second rule means that $h$ moves the point first and $g$ moves it next. These rules let [group](group-split.md) multiplication describe consistent rearrangements of a [set](set-split.md); see [Ackerman's algebra notes, definition 0.4.1.1](https://people.math.harvard.edu/~nate/teaching/UPenn/2007/fall/math_371/lectures/week_1/lecture_1/lecture_1.pdf).

For each $g$, the [function](function-split.md) $x\mapsto g\cdot x$ is a [bijection](bijection.md): its inverse is $x\mapsto g^{-1}\cdot x$, using the [inverse element](inverse-element.md) of $g$. Equivalently, a [group action](group-action-split.md) is a [group homomorphism](group-homomorphism.md) $\rho:G\to\operatorname{Sym}(X)$ into the [symmetric group](symmetric-group.md) of all [permutations](permutation.md) of $X$, with $\rho(g)(x)=g\cdot x$ and $\rho(gh)=\rho(g)\circ\rho(h)$. See [MIT's group-action notes, definition 23.1](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-703-modern-algebra-spring-2013/96221ce2d5b1077b3e5d3c5e08c88afe_MIT18_703S13_pra_l_23.pdf).

For a concrete [group action](group-action-split.md), put the [vertices of a polygon](vertex-of-a-polygon.md) of a [square](square.md) at $A=(1,1)$, $B=(-1,1)$, $C=(-1,-1)$ and $D=(1,-1)$. Let $r(x,y)=(-y,x)$ be a counterclockwise quarter-turn [rotation](rotation-mathematics.md) and let $s(x,y)=(y,x)$ be a [reflection](reflection-mathematics.md). Their eight [permutations](permutation.md) of the [vertices of a polygon](vertex-of-a-polygon.md) form the [dihedral group](dihedral-group.md)

$$
D_4=\{e,r,r^2,r^3,s,rs,r^2s,r^3s\},\qquad r^4=s^2=e,\quad srs=r^{-1}.
$$

Here $D_4$ means the [symmetry group](symmetry-group.md) of a four-sided [polygon](polygon.md), with [group order](order-of-a-finite-group.md) eight; the alternative convention calls this same [group](group-split.md) $D_8$. Products apply the rightmost operation first. For example, $s\cdot B=D$ and $(rs)\cdot B=r\cdot D=A$. The same [group](group-split.md) also has separate [group actions](group-action-split.md) on the [sides of a polygon](side-of-a-polygon.md), the [diagonals of a polygon](diagonal-of-a-polygon.md), and the [singleton set](singleton-mathematics.md) containing the [centroid](centroid.md) of the [square](square.md). Specifying $X$ matters: changing what is moved changes the [orbits of a group action](orbit-group-theory.md) and the [stabilizer subgroups](stabilizer-subgroup.md).

![](square-symmetry-orbits.png)

**[Figure 1](#image-eight-square-symmetry-group-elements-grouped-by-the-image-of-one-vertex-of-a-polygon). Eight square symmetry group elements grouped by the image of one vertex of a polygon**. The [group action](group-action-split.md) sends the marked [vertex of a polygon](vertex-of-a-polygon.md) $A$ to four possible [vertices of a polygon](vertex-of-a-polygon.md). Each column contains two [group](group-split.md) elements with the same image, giving a [group orbit](orbit-group-theory.md) of size four and a [stabilizer subgroup](stabilizer-subgroup.md) of [group order](order-of-a-finite-group.md) two. The labels show where every original [vertex of a polygon](vertex-of-a-polygon.md) goes.

A [group action](group-action-split.md) need not be a [faithful group action](faithful-group-action.md). For example, the [cyclic group](cyclic-group.md) $C_4=\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z$ acts on $X=\{0,1\}$ by $k\cdot x=x+k\pmod2$, using [modular arithmetic](modular-arithmetic-split.md). Only the [parity](parity.md) of $k$ matters: $0,2$ fix both points and $1,3$ exchange them. The associated [group homomorphism](group-homomorphism.md) $C_4\to\operatorname{Sym}(X)$ has [group kernel](kernel-of-a-group-homomorphism.md) $\{0,2\}$, of [group order](order-of-a-finite-group.md) two. Either point has a [group orbit](orbit-group-theory.md) of size two and a [stabilizer subgroup](stabilizer-subgroup.md) of [group order](order-of-a-finite-group.md) two, so the [orbit-stabilizer theorem](orbit-stabilizer-theorem.md) gives $4=2\cdot2$.

The useful questions are which positions can be exchanged, and which transformations survive after imposing a constraint such as keeping one marked point fixed. An [orbit of a group action](orbit-group-theory.md) answers the first question; a [stabilizer subgroup](stabilizer-subgroup.md) answers the second. This turns a list of transformations into a count of distinct configurations, and explains why several transformations can produce the same configuration.
