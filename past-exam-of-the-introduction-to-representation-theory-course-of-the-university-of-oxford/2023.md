<h3 id="1/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1/a)

<h5 id="1/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#1/a/i)

An $A$-[module](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics) is **completely reducible** if it is a [direct sum of modules](../module-theory.md#direct-sum-of-modules) each of which is a [simple module](../module-theory.md#irreducible-module):

$$
V=\bigoplus_{j\in J}S_j.
$$

Here a [simple module](../module-theory.md#irreducible-module) is nonzero and has no [submodules](../module-theory.md#submodule) other than zero and itself. This is precisely the definition of a [semisimple module](../module-theory.md#semisimple-module). The indexing set need not be finite unless $V$ has finite [dimension](../vector-space.md#dimension-vector-space); the zero [module](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics) is included as the empty [direct sum of modules](../module-theory.md#direct-sum-of-modules).

<h4 id="1/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1/a)

<h5 id="1/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#1/a/ii)

Construct the sum successively, retaining a [simple module](../module-theory.md#irreducible-module) only when it adds a new [direct summand](../vector-space.md#direct-summand). Suppose $S=V_1+\cdots+V_{j-1}$ is already expressed as a [direct sum of modules](../module-theory.md#direct-sum-of-modules) selected from these [simple modules](../module-theory.md#irreducible-module). The intersection $S\cap V_j$ is a [submodule](../module-theory.md#submodule) of the [simple module](../module-theory.md#irreducible-module) $V_j$, so it is either zero or all of $V_j$. In the latter case $S+V_j=S$. In the former case

$$
S+V_j=S\oplus V_j.
$$

Starting with $S=0$ and proceeding by [mathematical induction](../foundations-of-mathematics.md#mathematical-induction) gives a [direct sum of modules](../module-theory.md#direct-sum-of-modules) equal to $V_1+\cdots+V_n$. Thus the sum is a [semisimple module](../module-theory.md#semisimple-module). The argument proves the useful [finite sum of simple submodules](../module-theory.md#finite-sum-of-simple-submodules) lemma without claiming that the originally listed [submodules](../module-theory.md#submodule) are themselves independent.

<h3 id="1/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

Since $A$ is a nonzero [associative algebra](../associative-algebra.md) of finite [dimension](../vector-space.md#dimension-vector-space), it has a nonzero [left ideal](../associative-algebra.md#left-ideal) $I$ of least positive [dimension](../vector-space.md#dimension-vector-space). Such a [minimal left ideal](../associative-algebra.md#minimal-left-ideal) is a [simple module](../module-theory.md#irreducible-module) under left multiplication.

For $a\in A$, right multiplication defines the [module homomorphism](../module-theory.md#module-homomorphism)

$$
R_a:I\longrightarrow A,\qquad x\longmapsto xa.
$$

Its [kernel](../kernel-of-a-linear-map.md) is a [submodule](../module-theory.md#submodule) of $I$, so its [image](../set-theory.md#image-of-a-function) $Ia$ is either zero or a [simple module](../module-theory.md#irreducible-module) [isomorphic](../algebra.md#isomorphism) to $I$. Each $Ia$ is a [left ideal](../associative-algebra.md#left-ideal), since $c(xa)=(cx)a$.

Consider

$$
J=\sum_{a\in A}Ia.
$$

It is a [left ideal](../associative-algebra.md#left-ideal). It is also stable under right multiplication, because $(Ia)b=I(ab)$, so it is a [two-sided ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#two-sided-ideal). Moreover $I=I1\subseteq J$, giving $J\ne0$. The assumption that $A$ is a [simple ring](../commutative-algebra.md#simple-ring) therefore forces $J=A$.

Only finitely many summands are needed: express each member of a finite [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) of $A$ as a finite sum of elements of the $Ia$, and collect the finitely many indices used. After discarding zero summands, $A$ is a finite sum of [simple modules](../module-theory.md#irreducible-module). Part (a)(ii) makes its [left regular module](../module-theory.md#left-regular-module) a [semisimple module](../module-theory.md#semisimple-module). This is exactly the definition of a [semisimple algebra](../associative-algebra.md#semisimple-algebra), proving that a [finite-dimensional simple algebra is semisimple](../associative-algebra.md#finite-dimensional-simple-algebra-is-semisimple). The finite [dimension](../vector-space.md#dimension-vector-space) assumption is essential to this assertion about a [simple ring](../commutative-algebra.md#simple-ring).

<h3 id="1/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/c/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#1/c)

<h5 id="1/c/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#1/c/i)

Use the left $A$-[module](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics) structure $c\cdot(a\otimes b)=ca\otimes b$ on the [tensor product of associative algebras](../multilinear-algebra.md#tensor-product-of-associative-algebras). Expanding each $b\in B$ in the [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) $b_1,\ldots,b_m$ shows that every [tensor](../multilinear-algebra.md#tensor) can be written

$$
x=\sum_{j=1}^m a_j\otimes b_j
=\sum_{j=1}^m a_j\cdot(1\otimes b_j).
$$

Thus the displayed family generates the [module](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics).

For uniqueness, let $\beta_j:B\to k$ be the corresponding [dual basis](../dual-space.md#dual-basis), with $\beta_j(b_i)=\delta_{ij}$. Applying the [linear map](../vector-space.md#linear-map) $\operatorname{id}_A\otimes\beta_j$ to a [linear relation](../vector-space.md#linear-relation) $\sum_i a_i\otimes b_i=0$ gives $a_j=0$. Hence the family is [linearly independent in a module](../module-theory.md#linear-independence-in-a-module), and

$$
\boxed{A\otimes_kB\cong A^{\oplus m}\quad\text{as left }A\text{-modules}.}
$$

In particular $1\otimes b_1,\ldots,1\otimes b_m$ is a [basis of a module](../module-theory.md#basis-of-a-module), with coefficients uniquely in $A$.

<h4 id="1/c/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#1/c)

<h5 id="1/c/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#1/c/ii)

Write $x\in Z(A\otimes_kB)$ uniquely as $x=\sum_j a_j\otimes b_j$ using part (i). For each $a\in A$, the equality $(a\otimes1)x=x(a\otimes1)$ becomes

$$
\sum_j(aa_j-a_ja)\otimes b_j=0.
$$

The coefficient uniqueness from part (i) gives $aa_j=a_ja$ for every $a$, so each $a_j$ belongs to the [center of an associative algebra](../associative-algebra.md#center-of-an-associative-algebra) $Z(A)$. Consequently $x\in Z(A)\otimes_kB$.

Choose a [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) $z_1,\ldots,z_t$ of the [vector space](../vector-space.md) $Z(A)$ and rewrite $x=\sum_{i=1}^t z_i\otimes d_i$. For $b\in B$, commuting with $1\otimes b$ gives

$$
\sum_i z_i\otimes(bd_i-d_ib)=0.
$$

Extend the [linearly independent](../vector-space.md#linear-independence) family $z_i$ to a [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) of $A$ and apply its [dual basis](../dual-space.md#dual-basis) to the first factor. It follows that $bd_i=d_ib$ for every $b$, hence $d_i\in Z(B)$. This proves $Z(A\otimes_kB)\subseteq Z(A)\otimes_k Z(B)$.

Conversely, if $z\in Z(A)$ and $w\in Z(B)$, then

$$
(z\otimes w)(a\otimes b)=za\otimes wb=az\otimes bw=(a\otimes b)(z\otimes w).
$$

The [pure tensors](../module-theory.md#pure-tensor) $a\otimes b$ span the [tensor product](../multilinear-algebra.md#tensor-product), so $z\otimes w$ is central, as is every sum of such [pure tensors](../module-theory.md#pure-tensor). Therefore the [center of a tensor product of associative algebras](../multilinear-algebra.md#center-of-a-tensor-product-of-associative-algebras) is

$$
\boxed{Z(A\otimes_kB)=Z(A)\otimes_k Z(B).}
$$

These [tensor products](../multilinear-algebra.md#tensor-product) are identified with their natural [vector subspaces](../vector-space.md#vector-subspace) of $A\otimes_kB$; extending [bases](../vector-space.md#basis) justifies these embeddings over the [field](../algebra.md#field) $k$.

<h3 id="1/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/d/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#1/d)

<h5 id="1/d/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#1/d/i)

Let $J\ne0$ be a [two-sided ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#two-sided-ideal) of $A\otimes_kB$. Choose a nonzero $x\in J$ of least possible [two-factor tensor rank](../multilinear-algebra.md#two-factor-tensor-rank) $n$, and take a shortest expression

$$
x=\sum_{i=1}^n a_i\otimes b_i.
$$

Both families $(a_i)$ and $(b_i)$ are [linearly independent](../vector-space.md#linear-independence): a [linear dependence](../vector-space.md#linear-dependence) in either family would combine terms and shorten the expression. In particular $a_1,b_1\ne0$.

Because $A$ is a [simple ring](../commutative-algebra.md#simple-ring), the [two-sided ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#two-sided-ideal) generated by $a_1$ is $A$. Thus there are finitely many $u_j,v_j\in A$ with $\sum_j u_ja_1v_j=1$. Form

$$
y=\sum_j(u_j\otimes1)x(v_j\otimes1)
=\sum_{i=1}^n c_i\otimes b_i\in J,\qquad
c_i=\sum_j u_ja_iv_j,\qquad c_1=1.
$$

Since the $b_i$ are [linearly independent](../vector-space.md#linear-independence), applying a [linear functional](../dual-space.md#linear-functional) selecting $b_1$ shows $y\ne0$. Its [two-factor tensor rank](../multilinear-algebra.md#two-factor-tensor-rank) is at most $n$ and hence, by the choice of $x$, exactly $n$. Consequently $c_1=1,c_2,\ldots,c_n$ are [linearly independent](../vector-space.md#linear-independence).

Similarly, simplicity of $B$ gives finitely many $p_j,q_j\in B$ with $\sum_jp_jb_1q_j=1$. Now

$$
z=\sum_j(1\otimes p_j)y(1\otimes q_j)
=\sum_{i=1}^n c_i\otimes d_i\in J,\qquad
d_i=\sum_jp_jb_iq_j,\qquad d_1=1.
$$

The [linear independence](../vector-space.md#linear-independence) of the $c_i$ shows $z\ne0$, and minimality again makes its [two-factor tensor rank](../multilinear-algebra.md#two-factor-tensor-rank) exactly $n$. We have obtained

$$
\boxed{z=1\otimes1+\sum_{i=2}^n c_i\otimes d_i\in J\setminus\{0\}.}
$$

Both coefficient families in this expression are [linearly independent](../vector-space.md#linear-independence). Retaining this additional property is the key to the next part. This is [coefficient normalization in an ideal of a tensor product](../associative-algebra.md#coefficient-normalization-in-an-ideal-of-a-tensor-product).

<h4 id="1/d/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#1/d)

<h5 id="1/d/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#1/d/ii)

Keep the nonzero $z$ of minimal [two-factor tensor rank](../multilinear-algebra.md#two-factor-tensor-rank) constructed in part (i). For every $a\in A$, its [commutator](../lie-algebra.md#commutator) with $a\otimes1$ lies in $J$ and equals

$$
(a\otimes1)z-z(a\otimes1)
=\sum_{i=2}^n(ac_i-c_ia)\otimes d_i.
$$

If this [tensor](../multilinear-algebra.md#tensor) were nonzero, its [two-factor tensor rank](../multilinear-algebra.md#two-factor-tensor-rank) would be at most $n-1$, contradicting minimality. Thus it is zero. The [linear independence](../vector-space.md#linear-independence) of $d_2,\ldots,d_n$ forces $ac_i=c_ia$ for every $a$ and $i\geq2$. Since $Z(A)=k$, each $c_i$ is a [scalar multiple](../vector-space.md#scalar-multiple) of $1$.

But $1,c_2,\ldots,c_n$ are [linearly independent](../vector-space.md#linear-independence). Hence $n=1$, so $z=1\otimes1\in J$. A [two-sided ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#two-sided-ideal) containing the multiplicative identity is the entire [ring](../commutative-algebra.md#ring); therefore $J=A\otimes_kB$.

We have proved the stronger result that $A\otimes_kB$ is a [simple ring](../commutative-algebra.md#simple-ring). It has finite [dimension](../vector-space.md#dimension-vector-space) $(\dim_kA)(\dim_kB)$, so part (b) proves

$$
\boxed{A\otimes_kB\text{ is a semisimple ring}.}
$$

Part (c)(ii) also gives $Z(A\otimes_kB)=k$, so this proves that a [tensor product of central simple algebras](../associative-algebra.md#tensor-product-of-central-simple-algebras) is again a [central simple algebra](../associative-algebra.md#central-simple-algebra).

<h3 id="2/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#2/a)

The [character formula for an induced representation](../representation-theory.md#character-formula-for-an-induced-representation) is

$$
\boxed{\chi_{\operatorname{Ind}_H^GW}(g)
=\frac1{|H|}\sum_{\substack{x\in G\\x^{-1}gx\in H}}\chi_W(x^{-1}gx).}
$$

Equivalently, extend the [character](../representation-theory.md#character-of-a-representation) $\chi_W$ by zero outside the [subgroup](../group.md#subgroup) $H$ and sum over all $x\in G$. The factor $1/|H|$ accounts for passing from elements of $G$ to representatives of left [cosets](../group-theory.md#coset) of $H$.

<h3 id="2/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

For each $h\in g^G\cap H$, count the solutions of $x^{-1}gx=h$. Once a solution $x_0$ is fixed, every solution is uniquely $cx_0$ with $c\in C_G(g)$, the [centralizer](../group-action.md#centralizer) of $g$. Thus each $h$ contributes $|C_G(g)|\chi_W(h)$ to the sum in part (a).

The [orbit-stabilizer theorem](../group-action.md#orbit-stabilizer-theorem) for the [conjugation action](../group-action.md#conjugation-action) gives $|C_G(g)|=|G|/|g^G|$. The intersection $g^G\cap H$ is the disjoint union of the [conjugacy classes](../group-action.md#conjugacy-class) $h_1^H,\ldots,h_\ell^H$, and the [character](../representation-theory.md#character-of-a-representation) $\chi_W$ is constant on each such [conjugacy class](../group-action.md#conjugacy-class). Hence

$$
\chi_V(g)=\frac{|C_G(g)|}{|H|}\sum_{h\in g^G\cap H}\chi_W(h)
=\boxed{\frac{|G|}{|H|}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\frac{|h_i^H|}{|g^G|}\chi_W(h_i)}.
$$

If the intersection is empty, the empty sum gives zero. This is the [conjugacy-class formula for an induced character](../representation-theory.md#conjugacy-class-formula-for-an-induced-character).

<h3 id="2/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#2/c)

Order the [conjugacy classes](../group-action.md#conjugacy-class) of the [symmetric group](../finite-group-theory.md#symmetric-group) $S_4$ by representatives $1,(12),(12)(34),(123),(1234)$; their sizes are $1,6,3,8,6$. For a three-dimensional [rotation](../riemannian-geometry.md#rotation-mathematics) through an [angle](../geometry-and-topology.md#angle) $\theta$, the [eigenvalues](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) are $1,e^{i\theta},e^{-i\theta}$, so the [trace of a three-dimensional rotation](../linear-algebra.md#trace-of-a-three-dimensional-rotation) is $1+2\cos\theta$.

Under the identification with the [rotational symmetry group of a cube](../group-action.md#rotational-symmetry-group-of-a-cube), elements of order two give half-turns, elements of order three give turns through $\pm2\pi/3$, and elements of order four give turns through $\pm\pi/2$. A half-turn can have an axis through opposite edges or opposite faces, but both kinds have [trace](../linear-algebra.md#matrix-trace) $-1$. Thus

$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|rrrrr}
\text{representative}&1&(12)&(12)(34)&(123)&(1234)\\\hline
\text{class size}&1&6&3&8&6\\
\chi_C&3&-1&-1&0&1
\end{array}.}
$$

Its [character inner product](../representation-theory.md#character-inner-product) with itself is

$$
\langle\chi_C,\chi_C\rangle_{S_4}
=\frac{9+6+3+0+6}{24}=1.
$$

By [character orthogonality](../representation-theory.md#character-orthogonality), a nonzero [complex representation](../representation-theory.md#complex-representation) has [character inner product](../representation-theory.md#character-inner-product) one with itself exactly when it is an [irreducible representation](../representation-theory.md#irreducible-representation). Therefore $C$ is an [irreducible representation](../representation-theory.md#irreducible-representation) of [dimension](../vector-space.md#dimension-vector-space) three.

For comparison useful in part (e), the [standard representation of the symmetric group](../representation-theory.md#standard-representation-of-the-symmetric-group) has [character](../representation-theory.md#character-of-a-representation) equal to the number of fixed letters minus one, namely $(3,1,-1,0,-1)$ in this order. Multiplication by the [sign representation](../representation-theory-of-the-symmetric-group.md#sign-representation) changes this [character](../representation-theory.md#character-of-a-representation) to $(3,-1,-1,0,1)$. Thus $C$ is the [tensor product](../multilinear-algebra.md#tensor-product) of the [standard representation of the symmetric group](../representation-theory.md#standard-representation-of-the-symmetric-group) with the [sign representation](../representation-theory-of-the-symmetric-group.md#sign-representation).

<h3 id="2/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#2/d)

The given [permutations](../combinatorics.md#permutation) satisfy $r^4=s^2=1$ and $srs=r^{-1}$. The eight elements $r^j,r^js$ for $0\leq j<4$ are distinct: $s\notin\langle r\rangle$, whose only element of order two is $r^2=(13)(24)$. Thus $H$ is the order-eight [dihedral group](../finite-group-theory.md#dihedral-group), and we may use the [matrices](../vector-space.md#matrix)

$$
R=\sigma(r)=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
S=\sigma(s)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

They obey the same relations and describe a quarter-turn and a [reflection](../linear-algebra.md#reflection-mathematics) of a [square](../euclidean-geometry.md#square).

The [eigenvalues](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) of $R$ over the [complex numbers](../complex-analysis.md#complex-number) are $i$ and $-i$, with respective [eigenlines](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenline) spanned by $(1,-i)$ and $(1,i)$. Since $S$ interchanges these [eigenlines](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenline), neither is an [invariant subspace](../representation-theory.md#invariant-subspace) for $H$. Any proper nonzero [subrepresentation](../representation-theory.md#subrepresentation) of this two-dimensional [complex representation](../representation-theory.md#complex-representation) would be a line invariant under both $R$ and $S$, and an $R$-invariant line must be one of those [eigenlines](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenline). None qualifies, so **$W$ is irreducible**.

For the calculation in part (e), its [character](../representation-theory.md#character-of-a-representation) is

$$
\chi_W(1)=2,\qquad \chi_W(r^2)=-2,\qquad
\chi_W(r)=\chi_W(r^3)=\chi_W(r^js)=0\quad(0\leq j<4).
$$

Indeed $R^2=-I$, while a quarter-turn or a [reflection](../linear-algebra.md#reflection-mathematics) has [trace](../linear-algebra.md#matrix-trace) zero. The [character inner product](../representation-theory.md#character-inner-product) $(2^2+(-2)^2)/8=1$ provides a second irreducibility check.

<h3 id="2/e">e</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/e/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [E](#2/e)

In the order of [conjugacy classes](../group-action.md#conjugacy-class) used in part (c), the elements of $H$ have the following types:

$$
\begin{array}{c|c}
\text{type in }S_4&\text{elements in }H\\\hline
1&1\\
(12)&rs=(13),\ r^3s=(24)\\
(12)(34)&r^2=(13)(24),\ s=(12)(34),\ r^2s=(14)(23)\\
(123)&\text{none}\\
(1234)&r,\ r^3
\end{array}.
$$

By part (d), only $1$ and $r^2$ have nonzero [character](../representation-theory.md#character-of-a-representation) values. Formula (b), or equivalently the element-sum version proved there, therefore gives

$$
\chi_V(1)=[G:H]\dim W=3\cdot2=6,
\qquad
\chi_V((12)(34))=\frac{|C_G((12)(34))|}{|H|}(-2)=\frac88(-2)=-2,
$$

with zero on the other three [conjugacy classes](../group-action.md#conjugacy-class). Here the [centralizer](../group-action.md#centralizer) order is $24/3=8$. Thus $\chi_V=(6,0,-2,0,0)$.

Let $\varepsilon$ denote the [sign representation](../representation-theory-of-the-symmetric-group.md#sign-representation) and take the explicit two-dimensional $G$-[module](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics)

$$
U=\mathbf1\oplus\varepsilon.
$$

Its [character](../representation-theory.md#character-of-a-representation) is $\chi_U=(2,0,2,2,0)$. The [character](../representation-theory.md#character-of-a-representation) of a [tensor product of group representations](../representation-theory.md#tensor-product-of-group-representations) is the pointwise product of their [characters](../representation-theory.md#character-of-a-representation), because $\operatorname{tr}(M\otimes N)=\operatorname{tr}(M)\operatorname{tr}(N)$. Consequently

$$
\chi_{C\otimes U}
=(3,-1,-1,0,1)(2,0,2,2,0)
=(6,0,-2,0,0)=\chi_V.
$$

By [Maschke's theorem](../representation-theory.md#maschke-s-theorem) both [complex representations](../representation-theory.md#complex-representation) are [semisimple representations](../representation-theory.md#semisimple-representation), and [characters distinguish finite-dimensional complex semisimple representations](../representation-theory.md#characters-distinguish-finite-dimensional-complex-semisimple-representations). Therefore

$$
\boxed{V\cong C\otimes(\mathbf1\oplus\varepsilon)
\cong C\oplus(C\otimes\varepsilon).}
$$

This is the [induction of the planar square representation to the symmetric group on four letters](../representation-theory.md#induction-of-the-planar-square-representation-to-the-symmetric-group-on-four-letters).

The [dimension](../vector-space.md#dimension-vector-space) of the [fixed-point subspace of a group action](../representation-theory.md#fixed-point-subspace-of-a-group-action) is the multiplicity of the [trivial representation](../representation-theory.md#trivial-representation). Its [character inner product](../representation-theory.md#character-inner-product) here is

$$
\dim V^G=\langle\chi_V,1\rangle_G
=\frac{6+3(-2)}{24}=0.
$$

Hence **$V^G=0$**. Equivalently, [Frobenius reciprocity](../representation-theory.md#frobenius-reciprocity) identifies this multiplicity with $\langle\chi_W,1_H\rangle_H=(2-2)/8=0$.

## 3

↑ **Parent:** [2023](2023.md)

<h3 id="3/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

Let $\rho$ afford the [character](../representation-theory.md#character-of-a-representation) $\chi$, and let $m$ be the [order of a group element](../group-theory.md#order-of-a-group-element) $g$. Then $\rho(g)^m=I$, so its [minimal polynomial](../linear-operator-theory.md#minimal-polynomial) divides $T^m-1$. Over the [complex numbers](../complex-analysis.md#complex-number) this [polynomial](../polynomial.md) has distinct [polynomial roots](../polynomial.md#root-of-a-polynomial), since its [formal derivative](../galois-theory.md#formal-derivative) $mT^{m-1}$ does not vanish at any of its [polynomial roots](../polynomial.md#root-of-a-polynomial). Thus $\rho(g)$ is [diagonalizable](../linear-operator-theory.md#diagonalizable-matrix), with [eigenvalues](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_d$ that are [roots of unity](../algebra.md#root-of-unity).

Each $\lambda_j$ has [absolute value](../real-analysis.md#absolute-value) one, so $\lambda_j^{-1}=\overline{\lambda_j}$. The [eigenvalues](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) of $\rho(g^{-1})=\rho(g)^{-1}$ are $\lambda_j^{-1}$, giving

$$
\boxed{\chi(g^{-1})=\sum_j\lambda_j^{-1}
=\sum_j\overline{\lambda_j}
=\overline{\chi(g)}.}
$$

This is the [character inversion identity](../representation-theory.md#character-inversion-identity).

<h3 id="3/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

On the [complex vector space](../vector-space.md#complex-vector-space) $\operatorname{CF}(G)$ of [class functions](../representation-theory.md#class-function), define the [linear operator](../vector-space.md#linear-operator)

$$
Tf(g)=f(g^{-1}).
$$

This is a complex-[linear map](../vector-space.md#linear-map) and satisfies $T^2=I$. It is well-defined on [class functions](../representation-theory.md#class-function), because [inversion of conjugacy classes](../group-action.md#inversion-of-conjugacy-classes) takes an entire [conjugacy class](../group-action.md#conjugacy-class) $K$ to another [conjugacy class](../group-action.md#conjugacy-class) $K^{-1}$.

Use first the [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) consisting of the [indicator functions](../measure-theory.md#indicator-function) $1_K$ of the [conjugacy classes](../group-action.md#conjugacy-class). The [linear operator](../vector-space.md#linear-operator) $T$ permutes this [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) by $1_K\mapsto1_{K^{-1}}$. A [permutation matrix](../vector-space.md#permutation-matrix) has [trace](../linear-algebra.md#matrix-trace) equal to the number of fixed [basis vectors](../vector-space.md#basis-vector), so

$$
\operatorname{tr}T=\#\{K:K=K^{-1}\}.
$$

The right-hand side counts the [real conjugacy classes](../group-action.md#real-conjugacy-class).

By [character orthogonality](../representation-theory.md#character-orthogonality) and completeness, the [irreducible characters](../representation-theory.md#irreducible-character) are another [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) of $\operatorname{CF}(G)$. Part (a) gives $T\chi=\overline\chi$. The pointwise [complex conjugate](../complex-analysis.md#complex-conjugate) of an [irreducible character](../representation-theory.md#irreducible-character) is again an [irreducible character](../representation-theory.md#irreducible-character): conjugate the representing [matrices](../vector-space.md#matrix), and conjugate any hypothetical proper [invariant subspace](../representation-theory.md#invariant-subspace) back to contradict irreducibility. Thus $T$ also permutes this [basis](../vector-space.md#basis), and

$$
\operatorname{tr}T=\#\{\chi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G):\chi=\overline\chi\}.
$$

Here $\chi=\overline\chi$ means exactly that $\chi$ is a [real-valued character](../representation-theory.md#real-valued-character). Equality of the [trace](../linear-algebra.md#matrix-trace) in these two [bases](../vector-space.md#basis) proves

$$
\boxed{\#\{\text{real-valued irreducible characters}\}
=\#\{\text{real conjugacy classes}\}.}
$$

This is the [real character and real conjugacy class counting theorem](../representation-theory.md#real-character-and-real-conjugacy-class-counting-theorem). The [linear operator](../vector-space.md#linear-operator) used in the proof is $f\mapsto f\circ\operatorname{inv}$; on the [irreducible character](../representation-theory.md#irreducible-character) [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) it agrees with pointwise [complex conjugation](../complex-analysis.md#complex-conjugation), although pointwise [complex conjugation](../complex-analysis.md#complex-conjugation) itself is not a complex-[linear map](../vector-space.md#linear-map) on all [class functions](../representation-theory.md#class-function).

<h3 id="3/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#3/c)

Write $N=\langle a\rangle$. The relation $bab^{-1}=a^2$ lets every element be expressed as $a^ib^j$ with $0\leq i<7$ and $0\leq j<3$. There are 21 such expressions, so the assumption $|G|=21$ makes them distinct. In particular $a$ has order seven, $b$ has order three, and $N$ is a [normal subgroup](../group-theory.md#normal-subgroup). This is the [nonabelian group of order 21](../finite-group-theory.md#nonabelian-group-of-order-21), the [semidirect product](../group-theory.md#semidirect-product) $C_7\rtimes C_3$ with the action given by multiplication by two modulo seven.

Conjugation by $a$ fixes $N$, while conjugation by $b$ sends $a^i$ to $a^{2i}$. The nonidentity [conjugacy classes](../group-action.md#conjugacy-class) inside $N$ are therefore

$$
K_1=\{a,a^2,a^4\},\qquad
K_2=\{a^3,a^5,a^6\}.
$$

For the other elements, direct calculation gives

$$
a^t(a^ib^j)a^{-t}=a^{i+(1-2^j)t}b^j.
$$

When $j=1$ or $2$, the coefficient $1-2^j$ is nonzero modulo seven, so letting $t$ vary gives every member of the [coset](../group-theory.md#coset) $Nb^j$. These [cosets](../group-theory.md#coset) cannot merge under [conjugation](../group-action.md#conjugation), since $G/N$ is an [abelian group](../group.md#abelian-group) and conjugation acts trivially on that [quotient group](../group-theory.md#quotient-group). Thus the five [conjugacy classes](../group-action.md#conjugacy-class) have representatives and sizes

$$
\begin{array}{c|ccccc}
\text{representative}&1&a&a^3&b&b^2\\\hline
\text{class size}&1&3&3&7&7
\end{array}.
$$

To find the [linear characters](../representation-theory.md#linear-character), note that the [commutator subgroup](../group-theory.md#commutator-subgroup) $G'$ contains $bab^{-1}a^{-1}=a$. The [quotient group](../group-theory.md#quotient-group) $G/N\cong C_3$ is abelian, so $G'\subseteq N$; hence $G'=N$. The [abelianization](../group-theory.md#abelianization) is $C_3$. With $\omega=e^{2\pi i/3}$, its three [linear characters](../representation-theory.md#linear-character) are

$$
\lambda_j(a)=1,\qquad\lambda_j(b)=\omega^j,\qquad j=0,1,2.
$$

This exhausts the [linear characters](../representation-theory.md#linear-character) because [one-dimensional characters factor through the abelianization](../representation-theory.md#one-dimensional-characters-factor-through-the-abelianization).

For the remaining [irreducible characters](../representation-theory.md#irreducible-character), put $\zeta=e^{2\pi i/7}$ and take the [linear characters](../representation-theory.md#linear-character) $\theta_t$ of $N$ defined by $\theta_t(a)=\zeta^t$. The six nontrivial [linear characters](../representation-theory.md#linear-character) form two orbits under conjugation, with exponent sets $\{1,2,4\}$ and $\{3,5,6\}$. Define the [induced characters](../representation-theory.md#induced-character)

$$
\psi=\operatorname{Ind}_N^G\theta_1,\qquad
\overline\psi=\operatorname{Ind}_N^G\theta_3.
$$

For $a^i\in N$, the [character formula for an induced representation](../representation-theory.md#character-formula-for-an-induced-representation) gives

$$
\psi(a^i)=\zeta^i+\zeta^{2i}+\zeta^{4i},\qquad
\overline\psi(a^i)=\zeta^{3i}+\zeta^{5i}+\zeta^{6i}.
$$

Both vanish outside $N$, since no element outside a [normal subgroup](../group-theory.md#normal-subgroup) can be conjugate into it. This is [induction from an abelian normal subgroup with a free character orbit](../representation-theory.md#induction-from-an-abelian-normal-subgroup-with-a-free-character-orbit). For completeness, its irreducibility here follows directly from [Frobenius reciprocity](../representation-theory.md#frobenius-reciprocity) and [character orthogonality](../representation-theory.md#character-orthogonality) on the [cyclic group](../group.md#cyclic-group) $N$:

$$
\langle\psi,\psi\rangle_G
=\langle\theta_1,\operatorname{Res}_N^G\psi\rangle_N
=\langle\theta_1,\theta_1+\theta_2+\theta_4\rangle_N=1.
$$

The same calculation with $\theta_3,\theta_5,\theta_6$ proves that $\overline\psi$ is an [irreducible character](../representation-theory.md#irreducible-character).

Let

$$
\alpha=\zeta+\zeta^2+\zeta^4,\qquad
\beta=\zeta^3+\zeta^5+\zeta^6=\overline\alpha.
$$

The sum of the nontrivial seventh [roots of unity](../algebra.md#root-of-unity) gives $\alpha+\beta=-1$. Squaring and reducing exponents modulo seven gives

$$
\alpha^2=\alpha+2\beta=-\alpha-2.
$$

Thus $\alpha,\beta$ are the two [polynomial roots](../polynomial.md#root-of-a-polynomial) of $T^2+T+2$, and

$$
\alpha=\frac{-1+i\sqrt7}{2},\qquad
\beta=\frac{-1-i\sqrt7}{2}.
$$

To fix the signs with our specified $\zeta$, observe that $\operatorname{Im}\alpha=\sin(2\pi/7)+\sin(3\pi/7)-\sin(\pi/7)>0$, using the increase of sine on $(0,\pi/2)$. These [Gaussian periods for seventh roots of unity](../galois-theory.md#gaussian-periods-for-seventh-roots-of-unity) make the answer explicit.

The resulting [character table](../representation-theory.md#character-table) is

$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|ccccc}
&1&a&a^3&b&b^2\\\hline
\text{class size}&1&3&3&7&7\\\hline
\lambda_0&1&1&1&1&1\\
\lambda_1&1&1&1&\omega&\omega^2\\
\lambda_2&1&1&1&\omega^2&\omega\\
\psi&3&\alpha&\beta&0&0\\
\overline\psi&3&\beta&\alpha&0&0
\end{array}.}
$$

The two degree-three [irreducible characters](../representation-theory.md#irreducible-character) are distinct because $\alpha\ne\beta$. Together with the three [linear characters](../representation-theory.md#linear-character), they exhaust the [irreducible characters](../representation-theory.md#irreducible-character): the [sum of squares of irreducible degrees](../representation-theory.md#sum-of-squares-of-irreducible-degrees) is $3\cdot1^2+2\cdot3^2=21=|G|$. Equivalently, we have five distinct [irreducible characters](../representation-theory.md#irreducible-character) for the five [conjugacy classes](../group-action.md#conjugacy-class).

As a check on part (b), inversion interchanges $K_1$ and $K_2$, and also interchanges the two [conjugacy classes](../group-action.md#conjugacy-class) outside $N$. Only the identity [conjugacy class](../group-action.md#conjugacy-class) is real. Correspondingly $\lambda_0$ is the only [real-valued character](../representation-theory.md#real-valued-character) among the [irreducible characters](../representation-theory.md#irreducible-character); $\lambda_1,\lambda_2$ and $\psi,\overline\psi$ are two conjugate pairs.
