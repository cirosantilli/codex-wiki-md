<h4 id="a/1/1">1</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#a/1)

<h5 id="a/1/1/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#a/1/1)

A left [module](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics) over the unital $K$-[algebra](../algebra.md) $A$ is a $K$-[vector space](../vector-space.md) $V$ with a $K$-bilinear action $A\times V\to V$ satisfying $(ab)v=a(bv)$ and $1v=v$. For a vector $v$, the map $A\to V$, $a\mapsto av$, is an $A$-[module homomorphism](../module-theory.md#module-homomorphism). Its [kernel](../kernel-of-a-linear-map.md) is $\operatorname{Ann}(v)$; explicitly this is a [linear subspace](../vector-space.md#vector-subspace) and $b(av)=(ba)v=0$ for $a\in\operatorname{Ann}(v)$, so it is a [left ideal](../associative-algebra.md#left-ideal).

If $V=Av$, this homomorphism is [surjective](../algebra.md#surjective-function), and the [module isomorphism](../module-theory.md#module-isomorphism) theorem gives $V\cong A/\operatorname{Ann}(v)$. Conversely the quotient by a [left ideal](../associative-algebra.md#left-ideal) is generated by $1+I$. Thus **[cyclic modules](../module-theory.md#cyclic-module) are exactly the quotients $A/I$ by [left ideals](../associative-algebra.md#left-ideal)**.

For the first example set $e=\operatorname{diag}(1,1,0)$. Right multiplication by $e$ keeps the first two columns and zeroes the third. Every [matrix](../vector-space.md#matrix) with third column zero equals its product with $e$. Hence **$V=Ae$ is cyclic**, generated by $e$.

<h5 id="a/1/2/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#a/1/2)

For a [matrix](../vector-space.md#matrix) $v$ supported in its first row and any [upper triangular matrix](../linear-algebra.md#upper-triangular-matrix) $a$, multiplication gives $av=a_{11}v$. Thus $Av=Kv$ has [dimension](../vector-space.md#dimension-vector-space) at most one for every $v$, whereas the entire [module](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics) has [dimension](../vector-space.md#dimension-vector-space) three. Hence **this [module](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics) is not cyclic**, over every [field](../algebra.md#field) $K$.

↑ **Parent:** [1](#a/1)

<h5 id="a/1/3/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#a/1/3)

Coordinatewise multiplication gives $V=A(1,1,0)$, so **the third [module](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics) is cyclic**.

A [submodule](../module-theory.md#submodule) of a [cyclic module](../module-theory.md#cyclic-module) need not be cyclic. In the second example the first-row space is a [left ideal](../associative-algebra.md#left-ideal) and hence a [submodule](../module-theory.md#submodule) of the regular [module](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics) $A$. The regular [module](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics) is cyclic, generated by $1_A$, whereas its first-row [submodule](../module-theory.md#submodule) has just been shown not to be cyclic. Thus **the proposed inheritance property fails even for a finite-dimensional [algebra](../algebra.md)**.

<h3 id="a/2">2</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a)

<h4 id="a/2/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#a/2)

A [simple module](../module-theory.md#irreducible-module) is nonzero and has no [submodules](../module-theory.md#submodule) other than zero and itself. A [semisimple module](../module-theory.md#semisimple-module) is a [direct sum](../vector-space.md#direct-sum) of simple [submodules](../module-theory.md#submodule); the zero [module](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics) is allowed as the empty sum.

For (1)$\Rightarrow$(2), argue by [dimension](../vector-space.md#dimension-vector-space). Choose a minimal nonzero [submodule](../module-theory.md#submodule) $S$, which is simple, and split $V=S\oplus C$ using (1). The property passes to $C$: for $U\subseteq C$, write $V=U\oplus D$; then $C=U\oplus(C\cap D)$. Apply induction to $C$ and append $S$. The zero-dimensional case starts the induction. The implication (2)$\Rightarrow$(3) is immediate. For (3)$\Rightarrow$(2), repeatedly append a simple [submodule](../module-theory.md#submodule) not contained in the sum already chosen. Its intersection with that sum is zero by simplicity. [Dimension](../vector-space.md#dimension-vector-space) strictly increases, so after finitely many steps their [direct sum](../vector-space.md#direct-sum) is $V$.

For (2)$\Rightarrow$(1), write $V=\bigoplus_iS_i$ and fix $U$. Choose a sum $C$ of a maximal subset of these $S_i$ whose sum is disjoint from $U$. If $U+C\ne V$, some $S_j$ is not contained in $U+C$, so simplicity gives $S_j\cap(U+C)=0$. Then $C+S_j$ is a larger [direct sum](../vector-space.md#direct-sum) still disjoint from $U$, a contradiction. Hence $V=U\oplus C$.

Condition (1) implies (1\*). Conversely suppose (1\*) and let $W$ be the sum of all simple [submodules](../module-theory.md#submodule). If $W\ne V$, finite [dimension](../vector-space.md#dimension-vector-space) supplies a maximal proper [submodule](../module-theory.md#submodule) $M$ containing $W$. By (1\*), $V=M\oplus C$. Projection to the simple quotient $V/M$ identifies the nonzero complement $C$ with a [simple module](../module-theory.md#irreducible-module). Thus $C\subseteq W\subseteq M$, contradicting the [direct sum](../vector-space.md#direct-sum). Consequently $W=V$, which is (3). This proves **all four conditions are equivalent**.

For the concrete complement, $x^2=x$. [Orthogonal idempotents](../commutative-algebra.md#orthogonal-idempotent) $x$ and $1-x$ give $A=Ax\oplus A(1-x)$: the sum follows from $1=x+(1-x)$, and right multiplication by $x$ shows their intersection is zero. In explicit [matrix](../vector-space.md#matrix) form,

$$
\boxed{U=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&\lambda a\\b&\lambda b\end{pmatrix}:a,b\in K\right\},\qquad C=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}0&c\\0&d\end{pmatrix}:c,d\in K\right\}=A(1-x).}
$$

The decomposition of $\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}$ uses second-column remainders $b-\lambda a,d-\lambda c$ in $C$.

<h3 id="a/3">3</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a)

<h4 id="a/3/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#a/3)

Each $M_i$ is an [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) because the [algebra](../algebra.md) is commutative. For $m\in M_i$, the product $me_j$ belongs both to $M_i$ and $M_j$, so is zero when $j\ne i$. Multiplying $1=\sum_je_j$ by $m$ therefore gives $m=me_i$. In particular $e_i^2=e_i$, different $e_i$ have zero product, and

$$
\boxed{M_i=Ae_i,\qquad e_i^2=e_i,\qquad e_ie_j=0\ (i\ne j).}
$$

For any [idempotent](../commutative-algebra.md#idempotent) $e$, the set $Ae$ is closed under multiplication and $e$ acts as its identity. With $f=1-e$ the map $a\mapsto(ae,af)$ is an [algebra](../algebra.md) isomorphism $A\to Ae\times Af$: multiplication is componentwise because $ef=0$, and its inverse sends $(u,v)$ to $u+v$.

The $A$-[submodules](../module-theory.md#submodule) of $Ae$ are exactly its [ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) as a unital [algebra](../algebra.md), since acting by $a$ on $Ae$ is the same as acting by $ae$. A nonzero commutative unital [algebra](../algebra.md) is a [simple module](../module-theory.md#irreducible-module) over itself exactly when every nonzero principal [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) is the whole [algebra](../algebra.md), which is equivalent to every nonzero element having an inverse. Thus **a nonzero component $Ae$ is simple exactly when it is a [field](../algebra.md#field)**. If $e=0$, both properties fail under the nonzero-[field](../algebra.md#field) convention.

A semisimple regular [module](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics) is a finite [direct sum](../vector-space.md#direct-sum) of simple [ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal). Applying the preceding decomposition gives a finite direct product of [fields](../algebra.md#field). Conversely the coordinate [ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) of a finite product of [fields](../algebra.md#field) are [simple modules](../module-theory.md#irreducible-module), so its regular [module](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics) is semisimple. The [fields](../algebra.md#field) are finite extensions of $K$, because $A$ has finite $K$-[dimension](../vector-space.md#dimension-vector-space).

For a generator $g$ of the [cyclic group](../group.md#cyclic-group) of order three, evaluation $X\mapsto g$ gives a surjection $\mathbb Q[X]\to\mathbb QG$ with [kernel](../kernel-of-a-linear-map.md) $(X^3-1)$, by division and independence of $1,g,g^2$. The factors $X-1$ and $X^2+X+1$ are coprime, and the quadratic is irreducible over $\mathbb Q$. The [Chinese remainder theorem](../mathematics.md#chinese-remainder-theorem) yields

$$
\boxed{\mathbb QG\cong\frac{\mathbb Q[X]}{(X^3-1)}\cong\mathbb Q\times\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-3}).}
$$

The explicit map sends $g$ to $(1,\zeta_3)$, where $\zeta_3=(-1+\sqrt{-3})/2$.

<h4 id="a/4/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#a/4)

<h5 id="a/4/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#a/4/i)

An $R$-[module isomorphism](../module-theory.md#module-isomorphism) $T:V_\alpha\to V_\beta$ is a $K$-linear isomorphism satisfying $T\alpha=\beta T$, by applying it to multiplication by $X$. Conversely that identity implies $Tf(\alpha)=f(\beta)T$ for every [polynomial](../polynomial.md), so a similarity is an $R$-[module isomorphism](../module-theory.md#module-isomorphism).

The cyclic decomposition theorem over the [principal ideal domain](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal-domain) $R=K[X]$ states that any [finitely generated module](../module-theory.md#finitely-generated-module) is

$$
R^r\oplus R/(d_1)\oplus\cdots\oplus R/(d_s),\qquad d_1\mid d_2\mid\cdots\mid d_s,
$$

with nonunit monic invariant factors, uniquely determined, and free rank $r$ uniquely determined. A finite-dimensional $K$-space has $r=0$. In each cyclic summand the basis $1,X,\ldots,X^{\deg d_i-1}$ makes multiplication by $X$ the [companion matrix](../linear-operator-theory.md#companion-matrix) of $d_i$. Their block diagonal sum is the [rational canonical form](../linear-operator-theory.md#rational-canonical-form).

Here the two [polynomials](../polynomial.md) are already in divisibility order. With the companion convention having ones below the diagonal,

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{RCF}(\alpha)=\operatorname{diag}\left(\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&2\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\1&0&-3\\0&1&3\end{pmatrix}\right).}
$$

Integer coefficients are interpreted in $K$, so this remains valid in characteristics two and three.

In the cyclic case there is one companion block $C(d)$. Its defining [polynomial](../polynomial.md) is both characteristic and minimal: the vector represented by $1$ is cyclic and a [polynomial](../polynomial.md) annihilates it exactly when divisible by $d$. Thus **the [minimal polynomial](../linear-operator-theory.md#minimal-polynomial) is read directly from that block**.

<h4 id="a/4/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#a/4)

<h5 id="a/4/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#a/4/ii)

The [eigenvalues](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) in $K$ are precisely the roots in $K$ of the companion [polynomial](../polynomial.md) $d$; over an [algebraic closure](../algebra.md#algebraic-closure) take all its roots. Thus the form determines their root set, but **an explicit list generally requires factoring or solving $d$ and cannot be read off by inspection**. This is the relevant distinction in the qualification “with no further calculations”. Over a general [field](../algebra.md#field) the [polynomial](../polynomial.md) may have no roots in $K$ at all, and its diagonal entries are not its [eigenvalues](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue). If “determinable” means only uniquely specified rather than immediately readable, all the information is of course fixed by the [matrix](../vector-space.md#matrix), including this root set.

<h4 id="a/4/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#a/4)

<h5 id="a/4/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#a/4/iii)

For $d(X)=X^n+a_{n-1}X^{n-1}+\cdots+a_0$, the [determinant](../multilinear-algebra.md#determinant) of its [companion matrix](../linear-operator-theory.md#companion-matrix) is $(-1)^na_0$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\alpha\text{ invertible}\iff a_0\ne0.}
$$

Thus **invertibility is readable from the constant coefficient**, with no factorization or elimination.

<h4 id="a/4/iv">iv</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#a/4)

<h5 id="a/4/iv/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#a/4/iv)

In the cyclic quotient $R/(d)$, the [kernel](../kernel-of-a-linear-map.md) is formed by residue classes of [polynomials](../polynomial.md) $h$ such that $d\mid Xh$. If $X\nmid d$, coprimality forces $d\mid h$, so the [kernel](../kernel-of-a-linear-map.md) is zero. If $d=Xe$, divisibility is equivalent to $e\mid h$, and the residue classes of multiples of $e$ modulo $Xe$ form a one-dimensional $K$-space. Hence

$$
\boxed{\dim\ker\alpha=\begin{cases}0,&d(0)\ne0,\\1,&d(0)=0.\end{cases}}
$$

This too is immediately readable in the cyclic case, even if zero is a repeated root. The zero-dimensional [module](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics) has [kernel](../kernel-of-a-linear-map.md) [dimension](../vector-space.md#dimension-vector-space) zero separately.

<h3 id="b/5">5</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b)

<h4 id="b/5/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#b/5)

For $|G|=p^rm$ with $p\nmid m$, Sylow's theorem gives subgroups of order $p^r$, says that every $p$-subgroup lies in a conjugate of one of them, and that all such [Sylow subgroups](../finite-group-theory.md#sylow-subgroup) are conjugate. Their number $n_p$ divides $m$ and satisfies $n_p\equiv1\pmod p$. A [group](../group.md) is soluble when its [derived series](../group-theory.md#derived-series), obtained by repeatedly taking the [commutator subgroup](../group-theory.md#commutator-subgroup), reaches the identity. Equivalently it has a subnormal series with abelian factors.

For a [group](../group.md) of order $p^2$, the [class equation](../group-action.md#class-equation) shows its center is nontrivial: noncentral conjugacy classes have sizes divisible by $p$. If the center had order $p$, the quotient by the center would be cyclic; a [group](../group.md) with cyclic central quotient is abelian, since representatives are powers of one element times central elements. This would make the center the whole [group](../group.md). Thus **every [group](../group.md) of order $p^2$ is abelian**.

Now consider $|G|=pq^a$ with $0\leq a\leq2$. Cases with a normal [Sylow subgroup](../finite-group-theory.md#sylow-subgroup) are soluble: that subgroup has prime or prime-square order and is abelian, and its quotient also has such order and is abelian. This gives a subnormal series with abelian factors.

If $p<q$, the Sylow number $n_q$ divides $p$ and is $1$ modulo $q$, so is one. If $p>q$ and $a\leq1$, $n_p$ divides $q^a<p$, so again is one. For $p>q$ and $a=2$, a nontrivial $n_p$ can only be $q^2$, since $q<p$. The congruence would force $p\mid(q-1)(q+1)$, hence $p\mid q+1$. Since $p>q$, this means $p=q+1$, and primality forces $(p,q)=(3,2)$.

In the remaining order-twelve case, either a [Sylow subgroup](../finite-group-theory.md#sylow-subgroup) is already normal, or there are four [Sylow subgroups](../finite-group-theory.md#sylow-subgroup) of order three. Those have pairwise trivial intersection and contribute eight distinct elements of order three. Only four elements remain, including the identity. Every [Sylow subgroup](../finite-group-theory.md#sylow-subgroup) of order four lies entirely among those four elements, so it is unique and normal. It and its quotient are abelian. Therefore **all [groups](../group.md) of the required orders are soluble**, by elementary Sylow arguments alone.

<h3 id="b/6">6</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b)

<h4 id="b/6/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#b/6)

A [composition series](../finite-group-theory.md#composition-series) is a chain $1=G_0\triangleleft G_1\triangleleft\cdots\triangleleft G_r=G$ with simple nontrivial factors. Every [finite group](../group.md#finite-group) has one, by recursively choosing a maximal proper [normal subgroup](../group-theory.md#normal-subgroup). The [Jordan–Hölder theorem](../finite-group-theory.md#jordan-holder-theorem) states that any two such series have the same length and the same multiset of simple factors up to isomorphism.

For a proof induct on $|G|$. Let $M,N$ be the penultimate terms in two series. If $M=N$, apply induction there. Otherwise their maximal normality gives $MN=G$. Set $D=M\cap N$. The second isomorphism theorem gives

$$
M/D\cong G/N,\qquad N/D\cong G/M,
$$

so these quotients are simple. Choose a [composition series](../finite-group-theory.md#composition-series) of $D$ and extend it once to $M$ and once to $N$. Induction in the smaller [groups](../group.md) $M,N$ compares these with the respective original prefixes. Appending $G/M$ or $G/N$ gives in both cases the factors of $D$ together with $G/M,G/N$. This completes the proof, including the case $D=1$.

**Exactly one:** take $S_5$. Its only nontrivial proper [normal subgroup](../group-theory.md#normal-subgroup) is $A_5$. Indeed a normal $N$ has $N\cap A_5$ either $1$ or $A_5$ by the supplied simplicity. In the first case $N$ injects into $S_5/A_5\cong C_2$, so a nontrivial $N$ would be a [normal subgroup](../group-theory.md#normal-subgroup) of order two and hence central. But $S_5$ has trivial center: conjugating every transposition trivially forces preservation of each unordered pair, and therefore of every point. Thus the unique series is $1\triangleleft A_5\triangleleft S_5$.

**Exactly two:** take $A_5\times C_2$. A [normal subgroup](../group-theory.md#normal-subgroup) containing $A_5\times1$ is that subgroup or the whole product. For a [normal subgroup](../group-theory.md#normal-subgroup) $N$ meeting $A_5\times1$ trivially, commutators of its elements with $A_5\times1$ vanish; its first coordinates therefore lie in $Z(A_5)=1$. Hence $N\subseteq1\times C_2$. The two nontrivial proper [normal subgroups](../group-theory.md#normal-subgroup) are exactly the two factors, each simple, so the two series pass respectively through $A_5\times1$ and $1\times C_2$.

**More than two:** take $C_{120}$. A [cyclic group](../group.md#cyclic-group) has exactly one subgroup of each divisor order, and composition steps have prime indices. The orders of those indices are the distinct permutations of $2,2,2,3,5$, giving

$$
\boxed{5!/3!=20\text{ composition series}.}
$$

All three example [groups](../group.md) have order $120$.

<h3 id="b/7">7</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b)

<h4 id="b/7/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [7](#b/7)

An extension of $A$ by $B$ is a [short exact sequence](../module-theory.md#short-exact-sequence) $1\to A\to G\to B\to1$. It splits if the quotient map has a homomorphic section. Equivalently the embedded $A$ has a subgroup complement isomorphic to $B$, and $G$ is the corresponding [semidirect product](../group-theory.md#semidirect-product).

For $t\notin K$ and $a\in K$, both $t$ and $ta$ lie outside $K$, so $t^2=(ta)^2=1$. It follows that $tat=a^{-1}$, and $t=t^{-1}$ gives the claimed [conjugation](../group-action.md#conjugation) identity. [Conjugation](../group-action.md#conjugation) is an [automorphism](../algebra.md#automorphism) of $K$, so inversion is an [automorphism](../algebra.md#automorphism) there. Comparing the image of $ab$ with the product of the images gives $b^{-1}a^{-1}=a^{-1}b^{-1}$. Therefore **$K$ is abelian**.

Conversely inversion is an [automorphism](../algebra.md#automorphism) of any [abelian group](../group.md#abelian-group) $A$, of order dividing two. Form the [semidirect product](../group-theory.md#semidirect-product) $G=A\rtimes C_2$ with multiplication

$$
(a,\varepsilon)(b,\delta)=(ab^{(-1)^\varepsilon},\varepsilon+\delta),\qquad\varepsilon,\delta\in\mathbb Z/2.
$$

For completeness associativity follows because $b\mapsto b^{(-1)^\varepsilon}$ is an [automorphism](../algebra.md#automorphism) and these automorphisms compose according to addition modulo two; $(1,0)$ is the identity and inverses follow from this formula. The subgroup $H=A\times\{0\}$ is normal with quotient $C_2$, and every outside element satisfies $(a,1)^2=(aa^{-1},0)=1$. Thus **the [generalized dihedral group](../group-theory.md#generalized-dihedral-group) supplies the required extension**, including when $A$ is infinite or has elements of order two.

<h3 id="b/8">8</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b)

<h4 id="b/8/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [8](#b/8)

The degree-sum formula for [irreducible characters](../representation-theory.md#irreducible-character) gives

$$
\boxed{|G|=1^2+1^2+1^2+3^2+3^2=21.}
$$

The number of [linear characters](../representation-theory.md#linear-character) is the order of the [abelianization](../group-theory.md#abelianization), so **$|G/G'|=3$** and $|G'|=7$. The intersection of the kernels of all [linear characters](../representation-theory.md#linear-character) is $G'$. Consequently $g_4,g_5$ are nonidentity elements of this order-seven subgroup and have order seven. The images of $g_2,g_3$ under a displayed [linear character](../representation-theory.md#linear-character) have order three, so their orders are divisible by three. [Lagrange's theorem](../group-theory.md#lagrange-s-theorem) leaves three or twenty-one; an element of order twenty-one would make $G$ cyclic, contradicting its degree-three [irreducible characters](../representation-theory.md#irreducible-character). Therefore

$$
\boxed{(|g_2|,|g_3|,|g_4|,|g_5|)=(3,3,7,7).}
$$

The center is trivial. A nontrivial proper center would have order three or seven, giving a cyclic quotient and hence an [abelian group](../group.md#abelian-group), again a contradiction. Thus a [centralizer](../group-action.md#centralizer) of a nonidentity element is proper. It has order three for an order-three element, and seven for an order-seven element. The class sizes are therefore $1,7,7,3,3$.

Column orthogonality says $\sum_i|\chi_i(g)|^2=|C_G(g)|$. For $g_2,g_3$, the three [linear characters](../representation-theory.md#linear-character) already contribute three, so the remaining two entries must both vanish. Orthogonality with the identity column says $\sum_i\chi_i(1)\overline{\chi_i(g)}=0$ for $g\ne1$. At $g_4$ this gives $1+\alpha+\chi_5(g_4)=0$, so $\chi_5(g_4)=\beta$, using $\alpha+\beta=-1$. Similarly $\chi_5(g_5)=\alpha$. Thus the completed table is

$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|ccccc}
&e&g_2&g_3&g_4&g_5\\\hline
\text{class size}&1&7&7&3&3\\
\chi_1&1&1&1&1&1\\
\chi_2&1&\omega&\omega^2&1&1\\
\chi_3&1&\omega^2&\omega&1&1\\
\chi_4&3&0&0&\alpha&\beta\\
\chi_5&3&0&0&\beta&\alpha
\end{array}}
$$

Here $\beta=\overline\alpha$, $|\alpha|^2=|\beta|^2=2$ and $\alpha\beta=2$. These relations, together with $1+\omega+\omega^2=0$, verify all weighted row orthogonality relations and the [centralizer](../group-action.md#centralizer) column norms.
