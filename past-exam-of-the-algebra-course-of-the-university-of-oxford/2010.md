<h6 id="a/1/a/i/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#a/1/a/i)

An $R$-[module](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics) $M$ is a [finitely generated module](../module-theory.md#finitely-generated-module) if there are $m_1,\ldots,m_s\in M$ such that every element of $M$ has the form $r_1m_1+\cdots+r_sm_s$ with $r_i\in R$. Equivalently, **$M=Rm_1+\cdots+Rm_s$**, or $M$ is a [quotient module](../module-theory.md#quotient-module) of $R^s$.

<h5 id="a/1/a/ii">ii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a/1/a)

<h6 id="a/1/a/ii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#a/1/a/ii)

The [structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain](../module-theory.md#structure-theorem-for-finitely-generated-modules-over-a-principal-ideal-domain) applies because every [Euclidean domain](../commutative-algebra.md#euclidean-domain) is a [principal ideal domain](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal-domain). In its cyclic decomposition form, every [finitely generated module](../module-theory.md#finitely-generated-module) has a decomposition

$$
\boxed{M\cong R^r\oplus R/(d_1)\oplus\cdots\oplus R/(d_t),\qquad d_1\mid d_2\mid\cdots\mid d_t,}
$$

where the $d_i$ are nonzero nonunits. The nonnegative integer $r$ and the [invariant factors of a finitely generated module](../module-theory.md#invariant-factor-of-a-finitely-generated-module) $d_i$ are unique, with each $d_i$ determined up to multiplication by a unit. The zero module corresponds to no summands. Equivalently, factor the $d_i$ into powers of prime elements and use the [Chinese remainder theorem](../mathematics.md#chinese-remainder-theorem) to obtain a direct sum of cyclic prime-power [torsion modules](../module-theory.md#torsion-module) and a free summand.

<h5 id="a/1/a/iii">iii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a/1/a)

<h6 id="a/1/a/iii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#a/1/a/iii)

Give $V$ a $k[t]$-[module](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics) structure by $t\cdot v=\alpha v$. A finite [vector space basis](../vector-space.md#basis) generates it as a [module](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics). It is a [torsion module](../module-theory.md#torsion-module): the [Cayley-Hamilton theorem](../cayley-hamilton-theorem.md) gives a nonzero polynomial annihilating all of $V$. Since $k[t]$ is a [Euclidean domain](../commutative-algebra.md#euclidean-domain), cyclic decomposition gives

$$
V\cong\bigoplus_{j=1}^s k[t]/(f_j),\qquad f_1\mid\cdots\mid f_s,
$$

with monic nonconstant [invariant factors of a linear operator](../linear-operator-theory.md#invariant-factors-of-a-linear-operator) $f_j$ and no free summand. On $k[t]/(f_j)$, use the [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) $1,t,\ldots,t^{\deg f_j-1}$. Multiplication by $t$ then has the [companion matrix](../linear-operator-theory.md#companion-matrix) $C(f_j)$, because the relation $f_j(t)=0$ expresses the last power in terms of earlier ones. Concatenating these [bases](../vector-space.md#basis) yields

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{diag}(C(f_1),\ldots,C(f_s)),}
$$

the [rational canonical form](../linear-operator-theory.md#rational-canonical-form) of $\alpha$. In particular, its [minimal polynomial](../linear-operator-theory.md#minimal-polynomial) is $f_s$ and its [characteristic polynomial](../linear-operator-theory.md#characteristic-polynomial) is $\prod_jf_j$.

The first block is the [companion matrix](../linear-operator-theory.md#companion-matrix) of $t-3$. The last block is the [companion matrix](../linear-operator-theory.md#companion-matrix) of $f(t)=t^3-3t^2+9t-27=(t-3)(t^2+9)$. Since $t-3$ divides $f$, these are the ordered [invariant factors of a linear operator](../linear-operator-theory.md#invariant-factors-of-a-linear-operator). Thus

$$
\boxed{m_\alpha(t)=(t-3)(t^2+9),\qquad\chi_\alpha(t)=(t-3)^2(t^2+9).}
$$

The [minimal polynomial](../linear-operator-theory.md#minimal-polynomial) is the largest invariant factor, and the [characteristic polynomial](../linear-operator-theory.md#characteristic-polynomial) is their product.

<h5 id="a/1/b/ii">ii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#a/1/b/ii)

The [minimal polynomial](../linear-operator-theory.md#minimal-polynomial) factors over $\mathbb C$ as $(t-3)(t-3i)(t+3i)$ with distinct roots. The [diagonalizability criterion using the minimal polynomial](../linear-operator-theory.md#diagonalizability-criterion-using-the-minimal-polynomial) therefore makes $\alpha$ diagonalizable. Its [characteristic polynomial](../linear-operator-theory.md#characteristic-polynomial) gives multiplicities two, one and one, so its [Jordan normal form](../linear-operator-theory.md#jordan-normal-form) is

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{diag}(3,3,3i,-3i),}
$$

up to reordering the one-dimensional [Jordan blocks](../linear-operator-theory.md#jordan-block).

<h4 id="a/1/c">c</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#a/1)

<h5 id="a/1/c/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#a/1/c)

For a two-by-two matrix over a [principal ideal domain](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal-domain), its first [Smith normal form](../algebra.md#smith-normal-form) invariant is the [greatest common divisor](../divisibility.md#greatest-common-divisor) of its entries, while the product of its two invariants is its [determinant](../multilinear-algebra.md#determinant), up to a unit. Here

$$
1=3-(1-i)(1+i),
$$

so the entries generate the unit ideal in the [Gaussian integers](../commutative-algebra.md#gaussian-integer). The first invariant is therefore $1$. Also

$$
\det B=3(1+i)-(2+2i)(5+2i)=-3-11i.
$$

Hence a valid [Smith normal form](../algebra.md#smith-normal-form) is

$$
\boxed{\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-3-11i\end{pmatrix}.}
$$

Multiplying the second diagonal entry by a unit $\pm1,\pm i$ gives an equivalent normal form. The divisibility condition holds because $1$ divides every [Gaussian integer](../commutative-algebra.md#gaussian-integer).

A [simple module](../module-theory.md#irreducible-module) is a nonzero [module](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics) with no [submodules](../module-theory.md#submodule) other than zero and itself. Equivalently, **every nonzero element generates the entire [module](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics)**: $Am=M$ whenever $m\ne0$.

<h5 id="a/2/a/ii">ii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a/2/a)

<h6 id="a/2/a/ii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#a/2/a/ii)

A [composition series of a module](../module-theory.md#composition-series-of-a-module) is a finite chain

$$
0=M_0\subsetneq M_1\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq M_r=M
$$

of [submodules](../module-theory.md#submodule) whose factors $M_j/M_{j-1}$ are [simple modules](../module-theory.md#irreducible-module). A finite-dimensional [module](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics) has such a series by induction on its [dimension](../vector-space.md#dimension-vector-space), choosing a maximal proper [submodule](../module-theory.md#submodule) at each step.

The [Jordan–Hölder theorem for modules](../module-theory.md#jordan-holder-theorem-for-modules) states that **any two [composition series of a module](../module-theory.md#composition-series-of-a-module) have the same length and the same simple factors up to [isomorphism](../algebra.md#isomorphism) and reordering**. Thus the multiset of [composition factors](../module-theory.md#composition-factor) is an invariant of the [module](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics), even when the series itself is not unique.

<h5 id="a/2/a/iii">iii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a/2/a)

<h6 id="a/2/a/iii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#a/2/a/iii)

The general [Schur's lemma](../representation-theory.md#schur-s-lemma) says that a homomorphism $f:M\to N$ between [simple modules](../module-theory.md#irreducible-module) over any unital [ring](../commutative-algebra.md#ring) is either zero or an [isomorphism](../algebra.md#isomorphism). Indeed, $\ker f$ is a [submodule](../module-theory.md#submodule) of $M$ and $\operatorname{im}f$ is a [submodule](../module-theory.md#submodule) of $N$. If $f\ne0$, simplicity gives $\ker f=0$ and $\operatorname{im}f=N$. In particular, $\operatorname{End}_R(M)$ is a [division ring](../commutative-algebra.md#division-ring).

For a complex [algebra](../algebra.md) and a finite-dimensional simple [module](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics) $M$, the stronger conclusion is

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{End}_R(M)=\mathbb C\,\mathrm{id}_M.}
$$

For $f\in\operatorname{End}_R(M)$, the [fundamental theorem of algebra](../algebra.md#fundamental-theorem-of-algebra) gives an [eigenvalue](../linear-operator-theory.md#eigenvalue) $\lambda\in\mathbb C$. The endomorphism $f-\lambda I$ has nonzero [kernel](../kernel-of-a-linear-map.md), so the general [Schur's lemma](../representation-theory.md#schur-s-lemma) forces it to be zero. Consequently a homomorphism between two such simple [modules](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics) is zero if they are nonisomorphic, and their homomorphism space is one-dimensional if they are isomorphic.

The finite-dimensional qualification is essential for this scalar conclusion. For example, the [field](../algebra.md#field) $\mathbb C(t)$ is a complex [algebra](../algebra.md), simple as a [module](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics) over itself, and its endomorphisms include multiplication by $t$, which is not a complex scalar.

<h4 id="a/2/b">b</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#a/2)

<h5 id="a/2/b/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#a/2/b)

Let $M$ be a nonzero [simple module](../module-theory.md#irreducible-module) for $A=k[X]/(X^3)$ and let $N$ be the action of $X$. Since $X$ is central, $NM$ is a [submodule](../module-theory.md#submodule), and $N^3=0$. If $NM=M$, iterating would give $N^3M=M$, impossible. Thus $NM=0$.

The action therefore factors through $A/(X)\cong k$. A simple [module](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics) over a [field](../algebra.md#field) is one-dimensional: the span of any nonzero vector is already a nonzero [submodule](../module-theory.md#submodule). Conversely, $k$ with $X$ acting as zero is simple. Hence **there is exactly one simple [module](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics) up to [isomorphism](../algebra.md#isomorphism)**, namely

$$
\boxed{A/(X)\cong k,\qquad X\cdot v=0.}
$$

This argument covers all simple [modules](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics), without assuming their finite dimensionality in advance.

<h4 id="a/2/c">c</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#a/2)

<h5 id="a/2/c/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#a/2/c)

On a finite-dimensional $A$-[module](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics) $M$, the actions of $X$ and $Y$ are endomorphisms $P,Q$ satisfying $PQ-QP=I_M$. Taking [traces](../linear-algebra.md#matrix-trace) and using $\operatorname{tr}(PQ)=\operatorname{tr}(QP)$ gives

$$
0=\operatorname{tr}(PQ-QP)=\operatorname{tr}(I_M)=\dim_kM\quad\text{in }k.
$$

Since $k$ has [characteristic zero](../algebra.md#characteristic-zero), this forces $\dim_kM=0$. Thus **the zero [module](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics) is the only finite-dimensional [module](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics)** for this [Weyl algebra](../noncommutative-algebra.md#weyl-algebra). The obstruction is the [Weyl algebra has no nonzero finite-dimensional modules](../noncommutative-algebra.md#weyl-algebra-has-no-nonzero-finite-dimensional-modules).

The [group algebra](../associative-algebra.md#group-algebra) $kG$ is the [vector space](../vector-space.md) with [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) $G$, whose elements are formal sums $\sum_{g\in G}a_gg$ with $a_g\in k$. Extend the [group](../group.md) multiplication bilinearly:

$$
\left(\sum_ga_gg\right)\left(\sum_hb_hh\right)=\sum_{g,h}a_gb_h(gh).
$$

This is an associative unital $k$-[algebra](../algebra.md), with multiplicative identity the [group](../group.md) identity, and $\dim_k kG=|G|$.

<h5 id="a/3/a/ii">ii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a/3/a)

<h6 id="a/3/a/ii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#a/3/a/ii)

Given a [group representation](../representation-theory.md#group-representation) $\rho:G\to\operatorname{GL}_n(k)$, define an action on $k^n$ by

$$
\boxed{\left(\sum_ga_gg\right)\cdot v=\sum_ga_g\rho(g)v.}
$$

It is bilinear. The identity acts as $I_n$, and

$$
\left(\sum_ga_g\rho(g)\right)\left(\sum_hb_h\rho(h)\right)=\sum_{g,h}a_gb_h\rho(gh),
$$

because $\rho$ is a [group homomorphism](../group-theory.md#group-homomorphism). Hence the product action agrees with successive action, proving the [module](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics) axioms. Thus every [group representation](../representation-theory.md#group-representation) extends uniquely by linearity to a $kG$-[module](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics).

<h4 id="a/3/b">b</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#a/3)

<h5 id="a/3/b/i">i</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#a/3/b)

<h6 id="a/3/b/i/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#a/3/b/i)

A [semisimple module](../module-theory.md#semisimple-module) is a direct sum of [simple modules](../module-theory.md#irreducible-module). A finite-dimensional [semisimple algebra](../associative-algebra.md#semisimple-algebra) is one whose left regular [module](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics) is semisimple; equivalently, all its finite-dimensional [modules](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics) are semisimple, or every [submodule](../module-theory.md#submodule) of a finite-dimensional [module](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics) has a complementary [submodule](../module-theory.md#submodule).

The [Maschke theorem](../representation-theory.md#maschke-s-theorem) states that, for a finite [group](../group.md) $G$ and a [field](../algebra.md#field) $k$ with $|G|\ne0$ in $k$, every finite-dimensional $kG$-[module](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics) is semisimple, so $kG$ is a [semisimple algebra](../associative-algebra.md#semisimple-algebra). The assumption is equivalent to saying that the [characteristic of a field](../algebra.md#characteristic-of-a-field) does not divide $|G|$. The averaging construction makes a projection onto a [submodule](../module-theory.md#submodule) equivariant by replacing $P$ with $|G|^{-1}\sum_{g\in G}gPg^{-1}$.

<h5 id="a/3/b/ii">ii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#a/3/b)

<h6 id="a/3/b/ii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#a/3/b/ii)

Let $w=\sum_{g\in G}g$. This is nonzero since its [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) coefficients are all one, and $gw=w$ for every $g\in G$. Hence $kw$ is a one-dimensional left ideal of $kG$.

If the regular [module](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics) is semisimple, $kw$ is a direct summand. Let $\pi:kG\to kw$ be the corresponding $kG$-linear projection, so $\pi(w)=w$. Write $\pi(1)=cw$ for $c\in k$. Then

$$
w=\pi(w)=\sum_{g\in G}\pi(g)=\sum_{g\in G}g\pi(1)=c|G|w.
$$

Because $w\ne0$, $c|G|=1$ in $k$. Thus

$$
\boxed{|G|\ne0\text{ in }k.}
$$

Together with the [Maschke theorem](../representation-theory.md#maschke-s-theorem), this gives the exact [semisimple algebra](../associative-algebra.md#semisimple-algebra) criterion for a [group algebra](../associative-algebra.md#group-algebra).

<h4 id="a/3/c">c</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#a/3)

<h5 id="a/3/c/i">i</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#a/3/c)

<h6 id="a/3/c/i/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#a/3/c/i)

**True.** Every [group](../group.md) of order four is [Abelian](../group.md#abelian-group): if it has an element of order four it is cyclic; otherwise every nonidentity element has order two and $(ab)^{-1}=b^{-1}a^{-1}=ba$, while $(ab)^{-1}=ab$, so $ab=ba$.

Over $\mathbb C$, the [Maschke theorem](../representation-theory.md#maschke-s-theorem) and the [Artin–Wedderburn theorem](../associative-algebra.md#artin-wedderburn-theorem) give

$$
\mathbb CG\cong\prod_{j=1}^rM_{d_j}(\mathbb C).
$$

Since $G$ is [Abelian](../group.md#abelian-group), this [algebra](../algebra.md) is commutative, forcing every $d_j=1$. Its [dimension](../vector-space.md#dimension-vector-space) is four, so $r=4$. Each factor contributes one simple [module](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics); therefore there are exactly **four** up to [isomorphism](../algebra.md#isomorphism).

<h5 id="a/3/c/ii">ii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#a/3/c)

<h6 id="a/3/c/ii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#a/3/c/ii)

**True.** If every [simple module](../module-theory.md#irreducible-module) over $\mathbb CG$ were one-dimensional, the [Maschke theorem](../representation-theory.md#maschke-s-theorem) and the [Artin–Wedderburn theorem](../associative-algebra.md#artin-wedderburn-theorem) would make $\mathbb CG$ a product of copies of $\mathbb C$, hence commutative. But its [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) elements multiply as in $G$, so commutativity of $\mathbb CG$ implies commutativity of $G$. This contradicts the assumption. Therefore a non-[Abelian](../group.md#abelian-group) finite [group](../group.md) has a [simple module](../module-theory.md#irreducible-module) of [dimension](../vector-space.md#dimension-vector-space) greater than one.

<h5 id="a/3/c/iii">iii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#a/3/c)

<h6 id="a/3/c/iii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#a/3/c/iii)

**True.** The polynomial $X^7-1$ has seven distinct roots $\zeta_7^j$ in $\mathbb C$. The [Chinese remainder theorem](../mathematics.md#chinese-remainder-theorem) gives

$$
\mathbb C[X]/(X^7-1)\cong\prod_{j=0}^6\mathbb C.
$$

The simple [modules](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics) are exactly the seven one-dimensional evaluation [modules](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics), on which $X$ acts by $\zeta_7^j$. Thus there are **seven** up to [isomorphism](../algebra.md#isomorphism).

<h5 id="a/3/c/iv">iv</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#a/3/c)

<h6 id="a/3/c/iv/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#a/3/c/iv)

**True; there are exactly five.** On a simple [module](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics), the central element $Y$ acts as zero: its [image](../set-theory.md#image-of-a-function) is a [submodule](../module-theory.md#submodule), and surjectivity would contradict $Y^6=0$. The [nilpotent central elements annihilate simple modules](../module-theory.md#nilpotent-central-elements-annihilate-simple-modules) argument shows that the action factors through

$$
\mathbb C[X,Y]/(X^5-1,Y)\cong\mathbb C[X]/(X^5-1)\cong\mathbb C^5
$$

by the [Chinese remainder theorem](../mathematics.md#chinese-remainder-theorem). Therefore the simple [modules](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics) are one-dimensional with

$$
\boxed{X\mapsto\zeta_5^j,\qquad Y\mapsto0,\qquad j=0,\ldots,4.}
$$

This also rules out hypothetical infinite-dimensional simple [modules](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics), rather than counting only finite-dimensional ones.

<h2 id="b">B</h2>

↑ **Parent:** [2010](2010.md)

<h3 id="b/4">4</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b)

<h4 id="b/4/a">a</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#b/4)

<h5 id="b/4/a/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#b/4/a)

For the [Abelian](../group.md#abelian-group) case of [Cauchy theorem for groups](../finite-group-theory.md#cauchy-theorem-for-groups), use induction on $|G|$. Choose $a\ne e$. If $p$ divides $m=|a|$, then $a^{m/p}$ has order $p$. Otherwise $\langle a\rangle$ is a nontrivial [normal subgroup](../group-theory.md#normal-subgroup) of order prime to $p$, and $G/\langle a\rangle$ has smaller order still divisible by $p$. Induction gives a coset of order $p$; any lift $b$ has order divisible by $p$, because the order of its [image](../set-theory.md#image-of-a-function) divides its own order. A suitable power of $b$ then has order $p$. This uses no classification of finite [Abelian groups](../group.md#abelian-group).

For a finite [p-group](../finite-group-theory.md#p-group), the [class equation](../group-action.md#class-equation) is

$$
|G|=|Z(G)|+\sum_{[g]\ne\{z\}}[G:C_G(g)],
$$

where the sum is over noncentral [conjugacy classes](../group-action.md#conjugacy-class). Each index is a positive power of $p$, hence divisible by $p$. Therefore $p$ divides $|Z(G)|$, and the [center of a group](../group-theory.md#center-of-a-group) is nontrivial.

If $|G|=p^n$ with $n>1$ and $Z(G)\ne G$, this is already a proper nontrivial [normal subgroup](../group-theory.md#normal-subgroup). If $Z(G)=G$, the [group](../group.md) is [Abelian](../group.md#abelian-group); the established case of [Cauchy theorem for groups](../finite-group-theory.md#cauchy-theorem-for-groups) gives a subgroup of order $p$, which is normal and proper. Thus

$$
\boxed{\text{No group of order }p^n\ (n>1)\text{ is simple}.}
$$

<h4 id="b/4/b">b</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#b/4)

<h5 id="b/4/b/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b/4/b)

If $|G|=p^am$ with $p\nmid m$, a [Sylow p-subgroup](../finite-group-theory.md#sylow-subgroup) has order $p^a$. The [Sylow theorems](../finite-group-theory.md#sylow-theorems) say that such subgroups exist; every [p-subgroup](../finite-group-theory.md#p-subgroup) is contained in one; they are all conjugate; and their number $n_p$ divides $m$ and satisfies $n_p\equiv1\pmod p$.

Suppose $|G|=p^2q$ with distinct primes and $G$ were simple. A [Sylow subgroup](../finite-group-theory.md#sylow-subgroup) of order $q$ has order $q$, and $n_q>1$ divides $p^2$, so $n_q=p$ or $p^2$. If $n_q=p$, conjugation gives a nontrivial homomorphism $G\to S_p$. Its [kernel](../kernel-of-a-linear-map.md) is normal and, by simplicity, trivial. Hence $p^2q$ would divide $p!$ by [Lagrange's theorem](../group-theory.md#lagrange-s-theorem), impossible because $p!$ has only one factor of $p$.

If $n_q=p^2$, distinct [Sylow subgroups](../finite-group-theory.md#sylow-subgroup) of order $q$ meet only in the identity and together contain $p^2(q-1)$ nonidentity elements. Only $p^2$ elements of $G$ remain, including the identity. Every [Sylow p-subgroup](../finite-group-theory.md#sylow-subgroup) lies entirely in that remaining set and itself has $p^2$ elements. They must therefore all be the same subgroup, making it normal, again a contradiction. Thus **no group of order $p^2q$ is simple**. If $p=q$, the preceding [p-group](../finite-group-theory.md#p-group) argument applies.

For distinct $p,q$, a finite [Abelian group](../group.md#abelian-group) is the [direct product of groups](../group-theory.md#direct-product-of-groups) of its primary subgroups. A group of order $p^2$ is either cyclic or has every nonidentity element of order $p$, in which case choosing two independent elements gives $C_p\times C_p$. Consequently the possibilities are

$$
\boxed{C_{p^2q},\qquad C_p\times C_{pq}\quad(p\ne q).}
$$

If the primes coincide, the [Fundamental theorem of finitely generated abelian groups](../group.md#fundamental-theorem-of-finitely-generated-abelian-groups) gives instead $C_{p^3}$, $C_{p^2}\times C_p$, and $C_p^3$. This classification is used only for the requested list, not for the proof above.

<h4 id="b/5/a">a</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#b/5)

<h5 id="b/5/a/i">i</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#b/5/a)

<h6 id="b/5/a/i/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#b/5/a/i)

First establish the two general claims about [polycyclic groups](../group-theory.md#polycyclic-group). Intersect a subnormal cyclic-factor series $(G_i)$ with $H\leq G$. Then $H\cap G_i\trianglelefteq H\cap G_{i+1}$, and the inclusion induces an [injective](../algebra.md#injective-function) homomorphism

$$
\frac{H\cap G_{i+1}}{H\cap G_i}\longrightarrow\frac{G_{i+1}}{G_i}.
$$

A subgroup of a [cyclic group](../group.md#cyclic-group) is cyclic, so this is a cyclic-factor [subnormal series](../group-theory.md#subnormal-series) for $H$, after omitting repetitions. This proves [subgroups of polycyclic groups are polycyclic](../group-theory.md#subgroups-of-polycyclic-groups-are-polycyclic).

To prove the generator bound, choose $g_i\in G_i$ whose coset generates $G_i/G_{i-1}$ whenever that factor is nontrivial. Inductively $G_i=\langle G_{i-1},g_i\rangle$, so $G=\langle g_1,\ldots,g_n\rangle$. This is the [generator bound from a cyclic-factor series](../group-theory.md#generator-bound-from-a-cyclic-factor-series): **at most $n$ generators suffice**.

For the first example, take $G=C_2^m$ with its coordinate flag

$$
0<C_2<C_2^2<\cdots<C_2^m.
$$

It has length exactly $m$ and each factor is $C_2$, so all factors are cyclic.

<h5 id="b/5/a/ii">ii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#b/5/a)

<h6 id="b/5/a/ii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#b/5/a/ii)

Take

$$
\boxed{G=S_3\times C_2^{m-2}\quad(m\geq2).}
$$

Start with $1<A_3<S_3$, whose factors are $C_3$ and $C_2$, and then append the $m-2$ coordinate factors of the elementary [Abelian group](../group.md#abelian-group) $C_2^{m-2}$. More explicitly, append $S_3\times C_2^j$ for $1\leq j\leq m-2$. Each subgroup is normal in the next, and the factors consist of one $C_3$ and $m-1$ copies of $C_2$. Thus there are exactly $m$ finite cyclic factors. The [group](../group.md) is non-[Abelian](../group.md#abelian-group) because it contains $S_3$.

<h5 id="b/5/a/iii">iii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#b/5/a)

<h6 id="b/5/a/iii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#b/5/a/iii)

Take the free [Abelian group](../group.md#abelian-group)

$$
\boxed{G=\mathbb Z^m.}
$$

Its coordinate flag $0<\mathbb Z<\mathbb Z^2<\cdots<\mathbb Z^m$ is a [subnormal series](../group-theory.md#subnormal-series) of length $m$, with every factor isomorphic to the infinite [cyclic group](../group.md#cyclic-group) $\mathbb Z$. Hence it has exactly $m$ infinite cyclic factors.

<h4 id="b/5/b">b</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#b/5)

<h5 id="b/5/b/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b/5/b)

In a finite [group](../group.md), choose a maximal chain of [normal subgroups](../group-theory.md#normal-subgroup) from $1$ to $G$. Finiteness ensures such a chain exists, and maximality leaves no [normal subgroup](../group-theory.md#normal-subgroup) of $G$ strictly between successive terms. This is a [chief series](../group-theory.md#chief-series).

The [infinite cyclic group has no chief series](../group-theory.md#infinite-cyclic-group-has-no-chief-series). In any finite chain starting at zero, the first nonzero subgroup is $n\mathbb Z$. But $2n\mathbb Z$ is a nonzero proper subgroup of it and is normal in $\mathbb Z$. Thus zero is not maximal among the normal subgroups in that first term, contradicting the definition.

For any [chief series](../group-theory.md#chief-series), $G_{n-1}$ is a maximal proper [normal subgroup](../group-theory.md#normal-subgroup) of $G$. The [subgroup correspondence theorem](../group-theory.md#subgroup-correspondence-theorem) identifies normal subgroups of $G/G_{n-1}$ with normal subgroups of $G$ containing $G_{n-1}$. Consequently **the top chief factor is simple**.

Other [chief factors](../group-theory.md#chief-factor) need only have no proper nonzero subgroup invariant under the action of the whole [group](../group.md). In $A_4$,

$$
\boxed{1<V_4<A_4}
$$

is a [chief series](../group-theory.md#chief-series), where $V_4=\{1,(12)(34),(13)(24),(14)(23)\}$. Conjugation in $A_4$ permutes the three involutions transitively, so none of its three order-two subgroups is normal in $A_4$. Thus $V_4$ is a minimal nontrivial [normal subgroup](../group-theory.md#normal-subgroup); the top quotient is $C_3$. But the bottom [chief factor](../group-theory.md#chief-factor) $V_4/1\cong C_2\times C_2$ is **not simple**, since it has proper order-two normal subgroups as an abstract [group](../group.md).

<h3 id="b/6">6</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b)

<h4 id="b/6/a">a</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#b/6)

<h5 id="b/6/a/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#b/6/a)

A complex [character of a representation](../representation-theory.md#character-of-a-representation) of a finite [group](../group.md) $G$ is the function

$$
\boxed{\chi(g)=\operatorname{tr}(\rho(g)),}
$$

where $\rho:G\to\operatorname{GL}(V)$ is a finite-dimensional complex [group representation](../representation-theory.md#group-representation). The [character](../representation-theory.md#character-of-a-representation) is a [class function](../representation-theory.md#class-function) because [trace](../linear-algebra.md#matrix-trace) is unchanged by conjugation. Its degree is $\chi(1)=\dim V$.

<h4 id="b/6/b">b</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#b/6)

<h5 id="b/6/b/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b/6/b)

The [row orthogonality relations for a character table](../representation-theory.md#row-orthogonality-relations-for-a-character-table) assert that distinct complex [irreducible characters](../representation-theory.md#irreducible-character) are orthogonal and each has squared [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm) one:

$$
\langle\chi_i,\chi_j\rangle=\delta_{ij}.
$$

Using the linearity and conjugate-linearity of the [character inner product](../representation-theory.md#character-inner-product),

$$
\langle\psi,\psi\rangle=\sum_{i,j}\lambda_i\overline{\lambda_j}\langle\chi_i,\chi_j\rangle=\sum_i|\lambda_i|^2.
$$

Since the multiplicities are nonnegative integers, this is

$$
\boxed{\langle\psi,\psi\rangle=\sum_i\lambda_i^2.}
$$

<h4 id="b/6/c">c</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#b/6)

<h5 id="b/6/c/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#b/6/c)

By the [Maschke theorem](../representation-theory.md#maschke-s-theorem), the restricted [representation](../representation-theory.md#group-representation) is a direct sum of irreducible $H$-[modules](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics). The [row orthogonality relations for a character table](../representation-theory.md#row-orthogonality-relations-for-a-character-table) identify the multiplicity of $\psi_i$ as $d_i=\langle\chi\mathord\downarrow_H,\psi_i\rangle_H$. Therefore

$$
\chi\mathord\downarrow_H=\sum_i d_i\psi_i,\qquad d_i\in\mathbb Z_{\geq0}.
$$

Using the preceding [character inner product](../representation-theory.md#character-inner-product) identity and the irreducibility of $\chi$,

$$
\sum_i d_i^2=\frac1{|H|}\sum_{h\in H}|\chi(h)|^2\leq\frac1{|H|}\sum_{g\in G}|\chi(g)|^2=\frac{|G|}{|H|}.
$$

This is the [character restriction norm bound](../representation-theory.md#character-restriction-norm-bound). When $[G:H]=2$, the nonzero sum of squares of integer multiplicities is either one or two. Hence **the restriction is irreducible, or is the sum of two distinct [irreducible characters](../representation-theory.md#irreducible-character)**, each with multiplicity one. This proves the requested [index-two character restriction dichotomy](../representation-theory.md#index-two-character-restriction-dichotomy) directly; normality of an index-two subgroup is a general [group theory](../group-theory.md) fact.

<h4 id="b/6/d">d</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#b/6)

<h5 id="b/6/d/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#b/6/d)

Use the column order $1,(123),(12)(34),(12345),(13452)$ in $A_5$. The two restrictions of degree four coincide and equal $\theta_4=(4,1,0,-1,-1)$; those of degree five coincide and equal $\theta_5=(5,-1,1,0,0)$. The [centralizer](../group-action.md#centralizer) orders give

$$
\langle\theta_4,\theta_4\rangle=\frac{16}{60}+\frac13+\frac15+\frac15=1,\qquad
\langle\theta_5,\theta_5\rangle=\frac{25}{60}+\frac13+\frac14=1.
$$

Thus both are [irreducible characters](../representation-theory.md#irreducible-character). The remaining given restriction is $\eta=(6,0,-2,1,1)$, and

$$
\langle\eta,\eta\rangle=\frac{36}{60}+\frac44+\frac15+\frac15=2.
$$

It splits as two distinct [irreducible characters](../representation-theory.md#irreducible-character). Neither is $1$, $\theta_4$ or $\theta_5$, since their [character inner products](../representation-theory.md#character-inner-product) with $\eta$ are zero. A finite [group](../group.md) has as many complex [irreducible characters](../representation-theory.md#irreducible-character) as [conjugacy classes](../group-action.md#conjugacy-class), so these five exhaust them. The sum of squares of irreducible degrees is $|A_5|=60$. The first three degrees contribute $1+16+25=42$, leaving $18$ for the two constituents, whose degrees sum to six. Hence both have degree three.

Write one constituent as $\theta=(3,x,y,u,v)$. All five [conjugacy classes](../group-action.md#conjugacy-class) are closed under inversion, so all its values are real: $\chi(g^{-1})=\overline{\chi(g)}$. Orthogonality with $1$, $\theta_4$ and $\theta_5$ gives

$$
\frac3{60}+\frac x3+\frac y4+\frac{u+v}5=0,\qquad
\frac{12}{60}+\frac x3-\frac{u+v}5=0,\qquad
\frac{15}{60}-\frac x3+\frac y4=0.
$$

Solving gives $x=0$, $y=-1$ and $u+v=1$. The squared [norm](../normed-vector-space.md#norm) one gives $9/60+1/4+(u^2+v^2)/5=1$, so $u^2+v^2=3$ and $uv=-1$. Thus $u,v$ are the roots of $t^2-t-1$, namely $\varphi=(1+\sqrt5)/2$ and $\bar\varphi=(1-\sqrt5)/2$. Here the bar labels the other real root, rather than complex conjugation. The other constituent is $\eta-\theta$, so the two last values are interchanged.

The [Character table of the alternating group A5](../representation-theory.md#character-table-of-the-alternating-group-a5) is therefore

$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|rrrrr}
&1&(123)&(12)(34)&(12345)&(13452)\\\hline
1&1&1&1&1&1\\
\theta_3&3&0&-1&\varphi&\bar\varphi\\
\theta'_3&3&0&-1&\bar\varphi&\varphi\\
\theta_4&4&1&0&-1&-1\\
\theta_5&5&-1&1&0&0
\end{array}.}
$$

The [conjugacy class](../group-action.md#conjugacy-class) sizes are $1,20,15,12,12$. A [normal subgroup](../group-theory.md#normal-subgroup) is a union of entire [conjugacy classes](../group-action.md#conjugacy-class) containing the identity, and its order divides $60$ by [Lagrange's theorem](../group-theory.md#lagrange-s-theorem). A nontrivial such union has size at least $13$; the only proper divisors of $60$ at least $13$ are $15,20,30$. Subtracting the identity would require a sum of some of $20,15,12,12$ equal to $14,19,29$, respectively, none of which is possible. Thus **$A_5$ is simple**.
