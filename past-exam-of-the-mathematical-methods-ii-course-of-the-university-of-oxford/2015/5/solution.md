<h1 id="5/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [5](../5.md)

For [phase](../../../phase-waves.md) $kx-\omega t$, a constant-phase point moves at the [phase velocity](../../../phase-velocity.md) $\boxed{v_P=\omega/k}$. A narrow-band wave packet has envelope speed given by the local [group velocity](../../../group-velocity.md) $\boxed{v_G=d\omega/dk}$ on its [dispersion relation](../../../dispersion-relation-split.md).

Since $\omega=kv_P(k)$,

$$
v_G=v_P+k\frac{dv_P}{dk}.
$$

Use the in-medium [wavelength](../../../wavelength.md) $\lambda=2\pi/k$, so $d\lambda/dk=-\lambda/k$. The velocity relation becomes

$$
\boxed{v_G=v_P-\lambda\frac{dv_P}{d\lambda}}.
$$

The stated increase of [phase velocity](../../../phase-velocity.md) with [wavelength](../../../wavelength.md) means $dv_P/d\lambda>0$. Therefore $\boxed{v_G<v_P}$. The inequality alone does not provide their numerical values or guarantee a positive group speed for an arbitrary dispersion law; it fixes their ordering. For ordinary transparent glass in its normal-dispersion band, both are positive and the group speed is the smaller one.
