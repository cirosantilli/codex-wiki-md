# Divisor

↑ **Parent:** [Divisibility](divisibility.md)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divisor)

A divisor of an [integer](integer.md) $n$ is an [integer](integer.md) $d$ satisfying the [divisibility](divisibility.md) relation $d\mid n$. Equivalently, $n=dk$ for some [integer](integer.md) $k$. The word **positive** matters when listing or counting [divisors](divisor.md): unless signs are restricted, both $d$ and $-d$ qualify.

The positive [divisors](divisor.md) of $12$ are

$$
1,2,3,4,6,12.
$$

Their negatives are also [divisors](divisor.md) of $12$, giving twelve [integer divisors](divisor.md) altogether. The same list of positive [divisors](divisor.md) works for $-12$. One has only the positive [divisor](divisor.md) $1$, while zero has every [integer](integer.md) as a [divisor](divisor.md), including zero under our [divisibility](divisibility.md) convention. A nonzero [integer](integer.md) never has zero as a [divisor](divisor.md).

For a [positive integer](positive-integer.md) $n$, the positive [divisors](divisor.md) come in pairs $(d,n/d)$ whose [product](product-of-numbers.md) is $n$. For $12$, the pairs are $(1,12)$, $(2,6)$ and $(3,4)$. Each pair contains a [divisor](divisor.md) at most $\sqrt n$, so searching through candidate [divisors](divisor.md) up to the [square root](square-root.md) suffices to find all pairs. A pair repeats its own member only when $n$ is a [perfect square](square-number.md); for $36$, the middle pair is $(6,6)$. Consequently a [positive integer](positive-integer.md) has an odd number of positive [divisors](divisor.md) exactly when it is a [perfect square](square-number.md).

The [prime factorization](fundamental-theorem-of-arithmetic.md) gives another complete description. Since $12=2^2\cdot3$, its positive [divisors](divisor.md) are precisely $2^i3^j$ with $0\leq i\leq2$ and $0\leq j\leq1$. These independent choices give $(2+1)(1+1)=6$ positive [divisors](divisor.md), the value of the [divisor function](divisor-function.md) $\tau(12)$. A [prime factor](prime-factor.md) is one particular kind of [divisor](divisor.md); $4$, $6$ and $12$ show that [divisors](divisor.md) need not be [prime numbers](prime-number.md).

Thinking in pairs connects exact grouping with [integer factorization](integer-factorization.md): twelve objects can form three groups of four or four groups of three. Thinking in shared [divisors](divisor.md) gives the [greatest common divisor](greatest-common-divisor.md); for $12$ and $18$, the positive common [divisors](divisor.md) are $1,2,3,6$, and the largest is $6$.
