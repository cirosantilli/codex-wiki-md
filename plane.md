# Plane

↑ **Parent:** [Geometry and topology](geometry-and-topology-split.md)

A plane is a two-dimensional [affine subspace](affine-subspace.md): a point $\mathbf a$ together with every [linear combination](linear-combination.md) of two [linearly independent](linear-independence.md) direction [vectors](vector.md) $\mathbf u,\mathbf v$,

$$
P=\{\mathbf a+s\mathbf u+t\mathbf v:s,t\in\mathbb R\}.
$$

In three-dimensional [Euclidean space](euclidean-norm.md), a [Cartesian equation of a plane](cartesian-equation-of-a-plane.md) fixes one [linear equation](linear-equation.md) $\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf x=d$, with a nonzero [normal vector](normal-vector.md) $\mathbf n$. The two free directions are [orthogonal](orthogonal-vectors.md) to $\mathbf n$. A [plane](plane.md) extends indefinitely; a rectangular patch in a drawing marks only part of it, and does not define an edge of the [plane](plane.md).

For example, the [plane](plane.md) $2x-y+2z=3$ contains $\mathbf a=(1,1,1)$. The [vectors](vector.md) $\mathbf u=(1,2,0)$ and $\mathbf v=(0,2,1)$ are [linearly independent](linear-independence.md) and satisfy $(2,-1,2)\cdot\mathbf u=(2,-1,2)\cdot\mathbf v=0$. Hence its points are exactly $(1+s,1+2s+2t,1+t)$: substitution gives $2x-y+2z=3$ for every real $s,t$, and every solution has $s=x-1$, $t=z-1$.

The description separates position from direction. A [translation](translation-geometry.md) of the [plane](plane.md) changes $\mathbf a$ and possibly $d$, while retaining its parallel directions. A [plane](plane.md) through the [origin](origin-mathematics.md) is a [linear subspace](vector-subspace.md); the displayed example is not, since the [zero vector](zero-vector.md) fails its [linear equation](linear-equation.md). This distinction matters when applying [orthogonal projection](orthogonal-projection.md): subtract an anchor point before projecting onto the direction [linear subspace](vector-subspace.md).

Historically, [Euclid](euclid.md)'s [Euclid's Elements by Euclid](euclid-s-elements-by-euclid.md), Book I Definition 7, describes a plane surface through the [straight lines](straight-line.md) lying on it. [David E. Joyce](david-e-joyce.md)'s accompanying guide notes that this older use can include a bounded square or circular figure. That usage differs from the whole unbounded [affine subspace](affine-subspace.md) used here.[Euclid's Elements, Book I Definition 7 and David Joyce's Guide: plane surfaces and bounded figures](euclid-s-elements-by-euclid/book-i-definition-7.md)
