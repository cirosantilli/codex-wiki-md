<h1 id="4/b/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [B](../b.md)

A [geostationary orbit](../../../../geostationary-orbit.md) provides continuous viewing of approximately the same terrestrial region, favouring fast [cloud](../../../../cloud.md) evolution, storm tracking and the diurnal cycle. The large range makes fine spatial resolution demanding, high latitudes have poor viewing geometry, and a single platform has no global coverage. A [polar orbit](../../../../polar-orbit.md) gives much smaller range and broad latitude coverage as [Earth](../../../../earth.md) rotates beneath it, favouring detailed global mapping; any one location is sampled intermittently. A [Sun-synchronous orbit](../../../../sun-synchronous-orbit.md) can hold approximately fixed local solar time for comparing observations, but that sampling can miss much of the diurnal evolution. The science therefore sets a trade between spatial coverage, spatial resolution and temporal sampling.

The [Moon](../../../../moon.md)'s very slow, tidally locked rotation makes a lunar stationary [orbit](../../../../orbit-dynamical-system.md) fundamentally problematic. Let its [orbital angular velocity](../../../../orbital-angular-velocity.md) around [Earth](../../../../earth.md) be $n$, approximately also its spin rate. For lunar [mass](../../../../mass.md) $m$, terrestrial [mass](../../../../mass.md) $M$, and separation $a$, the two-body estimates give

$$
n^2\simeq\frac{GM}{a^3},\qquad r_{\rm sync}=\left(\frac{Gm}{n^2}\right)^{1/3}\simeq a\left(\frac mM\right)^{1/3},\qquad
r_H\simeq a\left(\frac m{3M}\right)^{1/3}.
$$

Thus $r_{\rm sync}\simeq3^{1/3}r_H$ lies outside the lunar [Hill sphere](../../../../hill-sphere.md). Earth's tidal perturbations prevent the desired simple stable, lunar-centred stationary [orbit](../../../../orbit-dynamical-system.md). This is stronger than merely saying that the required [orbit](../../../../orbit-dynamical-system.md) is high; specially maintained [three-body orbits](../../../../three-body-orbit.md) are a different construction.
