# Acceleration

↑ **Parent:** [Velocity](velocity.md)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acceleration)

[Acceleration](acceleration.md) is the [time](time-in-physics.md) [derivative](derivative.md) of [velocity](velocity.md), equivalently the second [derivative](derivative.md) of [position](position.md):

$$
\mathbf a(t)=\frac{d\mathbf v}{dt}=\frac{d^2\mathbf r}{dt^2}.
$$

It is a [vector](vector.md) with [SI](international-system-of-units.md) unit $\mathrm{m/s^2}$. A change in direction produces [acceleration](acceleration.md) even if [speed](speed.md) stays constant. For example, a car rounding a bend at steady [speed](speed.md) needs an inward resultant [force](force-split.md) by [Newton's second law](newton-s-second-law.md). Thus [acceleration](acceleration.md) does not mean only speeding up. When $v>0$, the [dot product](dot-product.md) gives $dv/dt=\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf a/v$: a perpendicular [acceleration](acceleration.md) changes direction without changing [speed](speed.md) at that instant.

Take counterclockwise [circular motion](circular-motion.md) about the origin with [radius](radius.md) $R=2\,\mathrm m$ and constant [angular speed](angular-speed.md) $\omega=0.5\,\mathrm{rad/s}$. The [position](position.md), [velocity](velocity.md) and [acceleration](acceleration.md) are

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf r(t)&=(R\cos(\omega t),R\sin(\omega t)),\\
\mathbf v(t)&=(-R\omega\sin(\omega t),R\omega\cos(\omega t)),\\
\mathbf a(t)&=(-R\omega^2\cos(\omega t),-R\omega^2\sin(\omega t))=-\omega^2\mathbf r(t).
\end{aligned}
$$

The [speed](speed.md) is $R\omega=1\,\mathrm{m/s}$ throughout, while the inward [centripetal acceleration](centripetal-acceleration.md) has magnitude $R\omega^2=v^2/R=0.5\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. At the four cardinal points, reached at $t=0,\pi,2\pi,3\pi\,\mathrm s$, the [velocities](velocity.md) in $\mathrm{m/s}$ are $(0,1),(-1,0),(0,-1),(1,0)$; the [accelerations](acceleration.md) in $\mathrm{m/s^2}$ are $(-0.5,0),(0,-0.5),(0.5,0),(0,0.5)$. The [vectors](vector.md) turn together, remaining perpendicular. [Ling, Sanny and Moebs, section 4.4: uniform circular motion](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/4-4-uniform-and-nonuniform-circular-motion)

<a id="image-constant-speed-with-changing-velocity-and-inward-acceleration-at-four-points-of-a-circle"></a>
![](constant-speed-circular-acceleration.png)

**[Figure 5](#image-constant-speed-with-changing-velocity-and-inward-acceleration-at-four-points-of-a-circle). Constant speed with changing velocity and inward acceleration at four points of a circle**.

The proof outline is just two applications of the [chain rule](chain-rule.md) to $\mathbf r(t)$; $\sin^2(\omega t)+\cos^2(\omega t)=1$ then fixes both magnitudes. The usefulness is geometric: turning requires a [force](force-split.md) even when a [speed](speed.md) reading remains steady, and doubling [speed](speed.md) at fixed [radius](radius.md) quadruples the required [centripetal acceleration](centripetal-acceleration.md). The same mechanism appears in a [circular orbit](circular-orbit.md).

Historically, Newton's _Principia_, Book I, Section II, Proposition IV, relates the inward [forces](force-split.md) of uniform [circular motion](circular-motion.md) to the squared arcs traversed in equal [times](time-in-physics.md), divided by their [radii](radius.md). In modern notation, its dependence on [speed](speed.md) and [radius](radius.md) is $v^2/R$; Newton presents a geometric argument rather than the coordinate [derivatives](derivative.md) above. [Newton, Principia, Book I, Proposition IV; Motte's 1729 translation](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Mathematical_Principles_of_Natural_Philosophy_(1729)/Book_1/Section_2)
