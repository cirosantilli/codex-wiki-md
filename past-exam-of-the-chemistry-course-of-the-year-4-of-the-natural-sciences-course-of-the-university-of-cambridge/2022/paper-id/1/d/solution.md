There is no single first-order [atmospheric lifetime](../../../../../atmospheric-lifetime.md) for an added [carbon dioxide](../../../../../carbon-dioxide.md) pulse. Air–sea gas exchange and adjustment of the surface mixed layer act on years-to-decades scales; redistribution into the ocean interior takes centuries to millennia. These transfers partition carbon between atmosphere and ocean rather than making the entire added carbon inventory disappear. Dissolution also changes the ocean's carbonate speciation: conversion among dissolved [carbon dioxide](../../../../../carbon-dioxide.md), bicarbonate and carbonate buffers the uptake, but provides a finite capacity at a given alkalinity. A substantial perturbation therefore remains airborne after the faster exchanges have adjusted.

Further removal involves dissolution of carbonate sediments and [carbonate compensation](../../../../../carbonate-compensation.md) over several thousand years, followed by the much slower silicate-weathering and burial pathway, typically tens to hundreds of thousands of years. The exact fractions and times depend on pulse size and model conditions. **A thousand years exceeds the fast exchange times but not the complete ocean–solid-Earth adjustment time**, so a residual atmospheric perturbation is expected. An intercomparison found a substantial long tail after ocean equilibration, with further carbonate neutralization on roughly 3–7 kyr scales.[Table 1 (p. 119), physical ocean/solid-Earth adjustment discussion and conclusions (p. 131)](../../../../../atmospheric-lifetime-of-fossil-fuel-carbon-dioxide-by-david-archer-and-others.md)
