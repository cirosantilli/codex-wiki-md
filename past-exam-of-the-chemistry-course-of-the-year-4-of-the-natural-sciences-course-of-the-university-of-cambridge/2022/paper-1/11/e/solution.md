<h1 id="11/e/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [E](../e.md)

A plausible explanation is **partial dissociation leaving a residual surface species**, such as an NH$_x$ fragment, adsorbed nitrogen or hydrogen, after molecular ammonia has finished desorbing. A small residual population can maintain a surface [electric dipole moment](../../../../../electric-dipole-moment.md) and a $0.2$ eV [work function](../../../../../work-function.md) offset. Heating farther can remove, recombine or rearrange those species.

Test this hypothesis by recording [temperature-programmed desorption](../../../../../temperature-programmed-desorption.md) signals for hydrogen and nitrogen as well as ammonia, and by measuring residual nitrogen with [X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy](../../../../../x-ray-photoelectron-spectroscopy.md). A surface-sensitive vibrational spectrum could distinguish NH$_x$ groups from atomic nitrogen. Isotopically labelled ammonia would help assign desorption products. Correlating disappearance of the residual spectral signal with recovery of the clean-surface [work function](../../../../../work-function.md) would be more diagnostic than the absence of an ammonia signal alone. This is a proposed explanation, not a uniquely established identification.
