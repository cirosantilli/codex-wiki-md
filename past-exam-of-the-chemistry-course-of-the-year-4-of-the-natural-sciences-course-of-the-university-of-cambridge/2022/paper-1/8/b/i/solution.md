<h1 id="8/b/i/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [I](../i.md)

Trialkylaluminium compounds commonly form [dimers](../../../../../../dimer.md) with two bridging alkyl groups and four terminal alkyl groups. Each electron-deficient bridge is a [three-centre two-electron bond](../../../../../../three-centre-two-electron-bond.md) spanning Al–C–Al; the carbon–metal bond [electrons](../../../../../../electron.md) are shared with the second aluminium center. A localized ordinary two-center bond to each Al would overcount the [electrons](../../../../../../electron.md).

For $\mathrm{[R_2AlH]_2}$, **hydride bridges are expected to be especially favorable**, giving a four-membered Al–H–Al–H core with two terminal alkyl groups on each Al. The Al–H–Al links are again [three-centre two-electron bonds](../../../../../../three-centre-two-electron-bond.md). Hydride is small and can bridge without the steric cost of a bulky alkyl bridge. Aggregation still depends on substituent size, donor solvent and concentration; this is the natural dimeric model, not a statement that all dialkylaluminium hydrides are dimers under every condition.
