# Cyclic module

↑ **Parent:** [Module theory](module-theory-split.md)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclic_module)

A [module](module-mathematics.md) is cyclic if one element $m$ generates it: every element is $rm$ for some scalar $r\in R$. The generating element need not give unique coefficients. The [module homomorphism](module-homomorphism.md) $R\to M$, $r\mapsto rm$, is [surjective](surjective-function.md). Over a [commutative ring](commutative-ring.md), its [kernel](kernel-of-a-linear-map.md) is the [annihilator](annihilator-ring-theory.md) $\operatorname{Ann}_R(m)$, giving

$$
M\cong R/\operatorname{Ann}_R(m).
$$

Thus [cyclic modules](cyclic-module.md) are exactly the [quotient modules](quotient-module.md) $R/I$ for [ideals](ideal.md) $I$ in $R$. For a left [module](module-mathematics.md) over a [noncommutative ring](noncommutative-ring.md), use a [left ideal](left-ideal.md) and interpret the quotient as a [module](module-mathematics.md), with no claim that it is a [quotient ring](quotient-ring.md). See [John A. Beachy's Abstract Algebra Online, section 10.1](https://faculty.niu.edu/math_beachy/aaol/modules.shtml).

The [module](module-mathematics.md) $\mathbb Z/6\mathbb Z$ is generated by $\overline1$, and also by $\overline5$, whose successive multiples are $\overline0,\overline5,\overline4,\overline3,\overline2,\overline1$. The element $\overline2$ generates only $\{\overline0,\overline2,\overline4\}$, a [submodule](submodule.md) with three elements. A cyclic [module](module-mathematics.md) can be infinite and free, as $\mathbb Z=\mathbb Z\cdot1$ shows, or finite and torsion, as $\mathbb Z/6\mathbb Z$ shows. One generator counts directions of generation, not the number of elements and not freedom from relations.
