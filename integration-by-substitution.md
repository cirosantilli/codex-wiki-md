# Integration by substitution

↑ **Parent:** [Integral](integral-split.md)

If $F'=f$ and $g$ is differentiable, the [chain rule](chain-rule.md) gives $(F\circ g)'(x)=f(g(x))g'(x)$. Taking [antiderivatives](antiderivative.md) gives the substitution rule

$$
\boxed{\int f(g(x))g'(x)\,dx=F(g(x))+C.}
$$

Writing $u=g(x)$ and $du=g'(x)\,dx$ records this [chain rule](chain-rule.md) in reverse. For a [continuous function](continuous-function.md) $f$ and continuously differentiable $g$, the [fundamental theorem of calculus](fundamental-theorem-of-calculus.md) gives the corresponding [definite integral](definite-integral.md) identity

$$
\int_a^b f(g(x))g'(x)\,dx=\int_{g(a)}^{g(b)}f(u)\,du.
$$

The order of the transformed endpoints determines the sign. For example, $\int_0^1 2x\cos(x^2)\,dx=\int_0^1\cos u\,du=\sin1$. If the substitution is used in the opposite direction by solving $x$ as a function of $u$, one must restrict to a suitable inverse branch; the [tangent half-angle substitution](tangent-half-angle-substitution.md) illustrates why separate branches can be necessary even when the original integrand is smooth. The general [change of variables formula](change-of-variables-formula.md) extends substitution to higher dimensions.
