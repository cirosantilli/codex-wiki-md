<h3 id="1/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1/a)

For a [number field](../algebraic-number-theory.md#number-field) $K$ of [degree of a field extension](../algebra.md#degree-of-a-field-extension) $n$, an [integral basis](../algebraic-number-theory.md#integral-basis) is a [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) $\omega_1,\ldots,\omega_n$ over $\mathbb Q$ such that

$$
\mathcal O_K=\mathbb Z\omega_1\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathbb Z\omega_n.
$$

Thus every [algebraic integer](../algebraic-number-theory.md#algebraic-integer) in $K$ has a unique expression with [integer](../integer.md) coefficients in this [basis](../vector-space.md#basis). Merely requiring its entries to be [algebraic integers](../algebraic-number-theory.md#algebraic-integer) would not suffice: for example, $1,2\sqrt2$ is a rational [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) of $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)$ but misses the [algebraic integer](../algebraic-number-theory.md#algebraic-integer) $\sqrt2$ in its integer span.

Here is a proof of the [existence of an integral basis](../algebraic-number-theory.md#existence-of-an-integral-basis). Start with any rational [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) $v_1,\ldots,v_n$. Each $v_j$ has a nonzero [integer](../integer.md) multiple $\alpha_j$ that is an [algebraic integer](../algebraic-number-theory.md#algebraic-integer). Indeed, if

$$
v^m+c_{m-1}v^{m-1}+\cdots+c_0=0,\qquad c_i\in\mathbb Q,
$$

choose a positive [integer](../integer.md) $q$ clearing all the denominators. Then $qv$ satisfies the [monic polynomial](../polynomial.md#monic-polynomial)

$$
X^m+qc_{m-1}X^{m-1}+q^2c_{m-2}X^{m-2}+\cdots+q^mc_0\in\mathbb Z[X].
$$

The $\alpha_j$ are still a rational [basis](../vector-space.md#basis). Put $M=\sum_j\mathbb Z\alpha_j$ and form the [matrix](../vector-space.md#matrix)

$$
T=(\operatorname{Tr}_{K/\mathbb Q}(\alpha_i\alpha_j))_{i,j},\qquad D=\det T.
$$

Its entries are [integers](../integer.md), because products and sums of [algebraic integers](../algebraic-number-theory.md#algebraic-integer) are [algebraic integers](../algebraic-number-theory.md#algebraic-integer) and a rational [algebraic integer](../algebraic-number-theory.md#algebraic-integer) is an [integer](../integer.md). The [nondegeneracy of the trace form of a number field](../algebraic-number-theory.md#nondegeneracy-of-the-trace-form-of-a-number-field) gives $D\ne0$: if $x\ne0$, then $\operatorname{Tr}(xx^{-1})=n\ne0$.

For $x=\sum_j x_j\alpha_j\in\mathcal O_K$, each $\operatorname{Tr}(x\alpha_i)$ is an [integer](../integer.md), so

$$
T(x_1,\ldots,x_n)^T\in\mathbb Z^n.
$$

The [adjugate matrix](../linear-algebra.md#adjugate-matrix) formula for $T^{-1}$ gives $Dx_j\in\mathbb Z$. Consequently

$$
M\subseteq\mathcal O_K\subseteq D^{-1}M.
$$

The [submodule theorem for free modules over a principal ideal domain](../commutative-algebra.md#submodule-theorem-for-free-modules-over-a-principal-ideal-domain) says that every subgroup of the finite-rank [free abelian group](../group-theory.md#free-abelian-group) $D^{-1}M$ is again a [free abelian group](../group-theory.md#free-abelian-group) of rank at most $n$. Since $\mathcal O_K$ contains $M$, its rank is exactly $n$. A [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) of this [free abelian group](../group-theory.md#free-abelian-group) is the required [integral basis](../algebraic-number-theory.md#integral-basis).

<h3 id="1/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

Write $x=r+s\sqrt d$ with $r,s\in\mathbb Q$. The [field trace](../algebraic-number-theory.md#field-trace) and [field norm](../algebraic-number-theory.md#field-norm) are

$$
T=2r,\qquad N=r^2-ds^2.
$$

The element is an [algebraic integer](../algebraic-number-theory.md#algebraic-integer) if and only if $T,N\in\mathbb Z$: necessity follows from its two conjugates; sufficiency follows from the [monic polynomial](../polynomial.md#monic-polynomial) $X^2-TX+N$.

Suppose $x$ is an [algebraic integer](../algebraic-number-theory.md#algebraic-integer). Set $a=2r\in\mathbb Z$. Then

$$
d(2s)^2=T^2-4N\in\mathbb Z.
$$

Write $2s=h/k$ in lowest terms, with $k>0$. Then $k^2\mid d$, and the hypothesis that $d$ is a [square-free integer](../integer.md#square-free-integer) forces $k=1$. Thus $b=2s\in\mathbb Z$, and the remaining condition is

$$
a^2-db^2\equiv0\pmod4.
$$

The only squares modulo four are zero and one. For $d\equiv2\pmod4$, an odd $b$ is impossible, after which $a$ is even. For $d\equiv3\pmod4$, an odd $b$ would require $a^2\equiv3\pmod4$, again impossible. For $d\equiv1\pmod4$, the condition is precisely $a\equiv b\pmod2$.

Conversely, $\sqrt d$ satisfies $X^2-d$, and when $d\equiv1\pmod4$, the element $\omega=(1+\sqrt d)/2$ satisfies

$$
X^2-X+\frac{1-d}{4}\in\mathbb Z[X].
$$

Their integer spans therefore consist of [algebraic integers](../algebraic-number-theory.md#algebraic-integer). In the last case,

$$
\frac{a+b\sqrt d}{2}=\frac{a-b}{2}+b\omega.
$$

This proves the [ring of integers of a quadratic field](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ring-of-integers-of-a-quadratic-field) formula. The required [integral basis](../algebraic-number-theory.md#integral-basis) is

$$
\boxed{\begin{cases}
\{1,\sqrt d\},&d\equiv2,3\pmod4,\\
\{1,(1+\sqrt d)/2\},&d\equiv1\pmod4.
\end{cases}}
$$

Their rational [linear independence](../vector-space.md#linear-independence) follows from $\sqrt d\notin\mathbb Q$.

<h5 id="1/c/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#1/c/i)

Put $\alpha=\sqrt{m_1}$, $\beta=\sqrt{m_2}$ and $F=\mathbb Q(\alpha)$. The hypotheses make $L/\mathbb Q$ a [biquadratic extension](../galois-theory.md#biquadratic-extension). To check the [degree of a field extension](../algebra.md#degree-of-a-field-extension) is four, if $\beta=x+y\alpha$ with rational $x,y$, squaring gives $xy=0$. The case $y=0$ would make $m_2$ a rational square; the case $x=0$ would make $m_2/m_1$ a rational square. Neither can occur for the specified distinct coprime [square-free integers](../integer.md#square-free-integer). Hence $[L:F]=2$.

The nonidentity [field automorphism](../galois-theory.md#field-automorphism) of $L/F$ fixes $\alpha$ and sends $\beta$ to $-\beta$. Thus, for $\gamma=(r+s\alpha)+(t+u\alpha)\beta$, the [field norm](../algebraic-number-theory.md#field-norm) and [field trace](../algebraic-number-theory.md#field-trace) are

$$
\boxed{N_{L/F}(\gamma)=(r+s\alpha)^2-m_2(t+u\alpha)^2,\qquad
\operatorname{Tr}_{L/F}(\gamma)=2(r+s\alpha)}.
$$

If $\gamma$ is an [algebraic integer](../algebraic-number-theory.md#algebraic-integer), so is its conjugate, and their sum and product are [algebraic integers](../algebraic-number-theory.md#algebraic-integer). Conversely, put $T=\operatorname{Tr}_{L/F}(\gamma)$ and $N=N_{L/F}(\gamma)$. The identity

$$
\gamma^2-T\gamma+N=0
$$

shows that $\gamma$ is integral over $\mathbb Z[T,N]$. If $T,N$ are [algebraic integers](../algebraic-number-theory.md#algebraic-integer), the [ring generated by algebraic integers](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ring-generated-by-algebraic-integers) $\mathbb Z[T,N]$ is integral over $\mathbb Z$. Since [integral dependence is transitive](../algebraic-number-theory.md#integral-dependence-is-transitive), $\gamma$ is an [algebraic integer](../algebraic-number-theory.md#algebraic-integer). This proves the [trace and norm integrality criterion for a quadratic extension](../algebraic-number-theory.md#trace-and-norm-integrality-criterion-for-a-quadratic-extension), including elements already in $F$.

<h4 id="1/c/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#1/c)

<h5 id="1/c/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#1/c/ii)

Again put $\alpha=\sqrt{m_1}$ and $\beta=\sqrt{m_2}$. The [ring of integers](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ring-of-integers) of each of the three quadratic subfields has [integral basis](../algebraic-number-theory.md#integral-basis) $1,\alpha$, $1,\beta$, or $1,\alpha\beta$, respectively: their square-free radicands are respectively congruent to $3,2,2$ modulo four. The [field traces](../algebraic-number-theory.md#field-trace) of the [algebraic integer](../algebraic-number-theory.md#algebraic-integer) $\gamma$ to those subfields are

$$
a+b\alpha,\qquad a+c\beta,\qquad a+d\alpha\beta,
\qquad a=2r,\ b=2s,\ c=2t,\ d=2u.
$$

They are [algebraic integers](../algebraic-number-theory.md#algebraic-integer), so comparison with each [integral basis](../algebraic-number-theory.md#integral-basis) gives

$$
\boxed{a,b,c,d\in\mathbb Z}.
$$

This use of all three subfields is needed to control $d$ as well as the other coefficients.

The [field norm](../algebraic-number-theory.md#field-norm) to $\mathbb Q(\alpha)$ is

$$
N_{L/\mathbb Q(\alpha)}(\gamma)
=\frac{a^2+m_1b^2-m_2c^2-m_1m_2d^2}{4}
+\frac{ab-m_2cd}{2}\alpha.
$$

Its two coefficients must be [integers](../integer.md). The second condition says $ab$ is even, since $m_2$ is even. Reducing the numerator of the first coefficient modulo four gives

$$
a^2-b^2-2(c^2+d^2)\equiv0\pmod4.
$$

Reduction modulo two first gives $a\equiv b\pmod2$. Since $ab$ is even, both $a$ and $b$ are even. The same congruence now gives $c^2+d^2\equiv0\pmod2$, or $c\equiv d\pmod2$. Therefore every [algebraic integer](../algebraic-number-theory.md#algebraic-integer) has the form

$$
\gamma=\frac a2+\frac b2\alpha+\frac{c-d}{2}\beta+d w,
\qquad w=\frac{\beta+\alpha\beta}{2},
$$

with four [integer](../integer.md) coefficients.

For the converse, $1,\alpha,\beta$ are [algebraic integers](../algebraic-number-theory.md#algebraic-integer), and

$$
w^2=\frac{m_2(1+m_1)}4+\frac{m_2}2\alpha\in\mathbb Z[\alpha].
$$

Here $4\mid1+m_1$ and $2\mid m_2$, so both coefficients are [integers](../integer.md). The [monic polynomial](../polynomial.md#monic-polynomial) $X^2-w^2$ and [integral dependence is transitive](../algebraic-number-theory.md#integral-dependence-is-transitive) show that $w$ is an [algebraic integer](../algebraic-number-theory.md#algebraic-integer). The four displayed generators are rationally [linearly independent](../vector-space.md#linear-independence), since their coefficient [matrix](../vector-space.md#matrix) in $1,\alpha,\beta,\alpha\beta$ has [determinant](../multilinear-algebra.md#determinant) $1/2$. Hence the [integral basis for coprime biquadratic radicands congruent to three and two](../algebraic-number-theory.md#integral-basis-for-coprime-biquadratic-radicands-congruent-to-three-and-two) is

$$
\boxed{\left\{1,\sqrt{m_1},\sqrt{m_2},\frac{\sqrt{m_2}+\sqrt{m_1m_2}}2\right\}}.
$$

In this formula $\sqrt{m_1m_2}$ denotes the product $\alpha\beta$ of the chosen square roots; this convention also handles negative radicands.

<h4 id="1/c/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#1/c)

<h5 id="1/c/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#1/c/iii)

Put $\alpha=\sqrt{m_1}$, $\beta=\sqrt{m_2}$, and

$$
\omega=\frac{1+\beta}{2}.
$$

The proposed [integral basis for coprime biquadratic radicands congruent to two and one](../algebraic-number-theory.md#integral-basis-for-coprime-biquadratic-radicands-congruent-to-two-and-one) is

$$
\boxed{\{1,\alpha,\omega,\alpha\omega\}}.
$$

Each entry is an [algebraic integer](../algebraic-number-theory.md#algebraic-integer), by the [ring of integers of a quadratic field](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ring-of-integers-of-a-quadratic-field) formula and closure under products. Their coefficient [matrix](../vector-space.md#matrix) in $1,\alpha,\beta,\alpha\beta$ has [determinant](../multilinear-algebra.md#determinant) $1/4$, so they are rationally [linearly independent](../vector-space.md#linear-independence). It remains to show that they span every [algebraic integer](../algebraic-number-theory.md#algebraic-integer).

Write $\gamma=(a+b\alpha+c\beta+d\alpha\beta)/2$ initially with rational $a,b,c,d$. The [field traces](../algebraic-number-theory.md#field-trace) to $\mathbb Q(\alpha)$ and $\mathbb Q(\alpha\beta)$ are $a+b\alpha$ and $a+d\alpha\beta$. Since $m_1\equiv m_1m_2\equiv2\pmod4$, the corresponding [integral basis](../algebraic-number-theory.md#integral-basis) in each subfield gives $a,b,d\in\mathbb Z$. The [field trace](../algebraic-number-theory.md#field-trace) to $\mathbb Q(\beta)$ is $a+c\beta$. Membership in $\mathbb Z[\omega]$ says that $2a,2c$ are [integers](../integer.md) of equal parity. As $a$ is already an [integer](../integer.md), this forces $c\in\mathbb Z$ too.

Now the [field norm](../algebraic-number-theory.md#field-norm) to $\mathbb Q(\beta)$ is

$$
\frac A4+\frac{ac-m_1bd}{2}\beta,
\qquad A=a^2+m_2c^2-m_1b^2-m_1m_2d^2.
$$

By the [ring of integers of a quadratic field](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ring-of-integers-of-a-quadratic-field) formula, $A/2$ is an [integer](../integer.md) and has the same parity as $ac-m_1bd$. Thus $A$ is even, giving $a\equiv c\pmod2$, and

$$
A\equiv2ac-2m_1bd\equiv2ac\pmod4.
$$

Using $m_1\equiv2$, $m_2\equiv1$ and $a\equiv c\pmod2$, this becomes

$$
0\equiv A-2ac\equiv(a-c)^2-2(b^2+d^2)
\equiv-2(b^2+d^2)\pmod4.
$$

Hence $b\equiv d\pmod2$. Finally,

$$
\gamma=\frac{a-c}{2}+\frac{b-d}{2}\alpha+c\omega+d\alpha\omega,
$$

an expression with [integer](../integer.md) coefficients. This proves the asserted [integral basis](../algebraic-number-theory.md#integral-basis) without assuming a general theorem about products of elements of an [integral basis](../algebraic-number-theory.md#integral-basis).

<h3 id="2/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#2/a)

Write $R=\mathcal O_K$. We prove [unique factorization of ideals in a number field](../algebraic-number-theory.md#unique-factorization-of-ideals-in-a-number-field), rather than assuming this conclusion as a property of a [Dedekind domain](../commutative-algebra.md#dedekind-domain). Three preliminary facts about $R$ will be useful. Its [additive group](../group.md#additive-group) is a finite-rank [free abelian group](../group-theory.md#free-abelian-group), so every [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) is finitely generated and the [ascending chain condition](../algebra.md#ascending-chain-condition) holds. It is an [integrally closed domain](../commutative-algebra.md#integrally-closed-domain), by [integral dependence is transitive](../algebraic-number-theory.md#integral-dependence-is-transitive). Finally, every nonzero [prime ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) is a [maximal ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#maximal-ideal). For this last fact, choose $0\ne a\in P$. Multiplication by $a$ is an injective endomorphism of the finite-rank [free abelian group](../group-theory.md#free-abelian-group) $R$ with nonzero [determinant](../multilinear-algebra.md#determinant), so $R/(a)$ is finite. Consequently $R/P$ is a finite [integral domain](../commutative-algebra.md#integral-domain) and hence a [field](../algebra.md#field).

First, every nonzero proper [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) contains a product of nonzero [prime ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal). Otherwise choose a maximal counterexample $J$ under inclusion, using the [ascending chain condition](../algebra.md#ascending-chain-condition). It cannot be a [prime ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal). Choose $x,y\notin J$ with $xy\in J$. The strictly larger [ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) $J+(x)$ and $J+(y)$ each contain a product of nonzero [prime ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal), allowing the empty product $R$ when necessary. Multiplying those products gives a product contained in

$$
(J+(x))(J+(y))\subseteq J,
$$

a contradiction.

Next fix a nonzero [prime ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) $P$, choose $0\ne a\in P$, and choose a product of nonzero [prime ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal)

$$
P_1\cdots P_r\subseteq(a)
$$

with $r$ minimal. Because $(a)\subseteq P$, primality and maximality imply $P_j=P$ for some $j$; reorder so that $P_1=P$. Minimality provides $b\in P_2\cdots P_r$ with $b\notin(a)$, taking the remaining product to be $R$ if $r=1$. Thus

$$
x=b/a\notin R,\qquad xP\subseteq R.
$$

Define the [fractional ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#fractional-ideal)

$$
P^{-1}=\{z\in K:zP\subseteq R\}.
$$

It contains $R$ and $x$. The [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) $PP^{-1}$ lies between $P$ and $R$, so it is $P$ or $R$. If it were $P$, then $xP\subseteq P$. Applying the [determinant trick](../module-theory.md#determinant-trick) to a finite [generating set of an ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#generating-set-of-an-ideal) for $P$ gives a [monic polynomial](../polynomial.md#monic-polynomial) over $R$ satisfied by $x$. Since $R$ is an [integrally closed domain](../commutative-algebra.md#integrally-closed-domain), this would put $x$ in $R$, a contradiction. Therefore

$$
\boxed{PP^{-1}=R}.
$$

This proves that each nonzero [prime ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) is an [invertible fractional ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#invertible-fractional-ideal), independently of the desired factorization theorem.

Now take a nonzero proper [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) $A$ and a [maximal ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#maximal-ideal) $P$ containing it. The [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) $B=P^{-1}A$ satisfies

$$
A\subseteq B\subseteq R,\qquad A=PB.
$$

The first inclusion is strict: if $A=B$, the same $x\in P^{-1}\setminus R$ would stabilize $A$, and the [determinant trick](../module-theory.md#determinant-trick) would again force $x\in R$. Repeating gives a strictly ascending chain until it reaches $R$; the [ascending chain condition](../algebra.md#ascending-chain-condition) ensures this happens after finitely many steps. The identities $A=PB$ then express $A$ as a product of nonzero [prime ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal).

For uniqueness, suppose $P_1\cdots P_r=Q_1\cdots Q_s$. Since the right-hand side is contained in $P_1$, some $Q_j$ is contained in $P_1$. Both are nonzero [maximal ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#maximal-ideal), so $Q_j=P_1$. Multiplication by $P_1^{-1}$ cancels this factor. Repeat. A nonempty product of proper [prime ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) cannot equal $R$, so both products finish together. This proves uniqueness up to reordering, including repeated factors.

<h4 id="2/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

<h5 id="2/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#2/b/i)

By the [correspondence theorem for ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#correspondence-theorem-for-ideals), an [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) of $R/P^n$ has the form $J/P^n$ with $P^n\subseteq J\subseteq R$. If $J\ne R$, apply [unique factorization of ideals in a number field](../algebraic-number-theory.md#unique-factorization-of-ideals-in-a-number-field) to $J$. Every [prime ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) $Q$ dividing $J$ contains $J$ and hence $P^n$. Primality gives $P\subseteq Q$, and maximality gives $Q=P$. Thus $J=P^i$ for some $i\geq1$.

The inclusion $P^n\subseteq P^i$ forces $i\leq n$: if $i>n$, cancellation using the [invertible fractional ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#invertible-fractional-ideal) $P^n$ would give $R\subseteq P^{i-n}$, which is impossible. The zero [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) corresponds to $i=n$, while the unit [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) corresponds to $i=0$. Consequently the nonzero proper [ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) are exactly

$$
\boxed{P^i/P^n,\qquad 1\leq i<n}.
$$

When $n=1$, there are none, in agreement with $R/P$ being a [field](../algebra.md#field).

<h4 id="2/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

<h5 id="2/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#2/b/ii)

For every $i\geq0$, the inclusion $P^{i+1}\subseteq P^i$ is strict: equality, followed by multiplication by the inverse of $P^i$, would give $P=R$. Hence one can choose

$$
\pi\in P^i\setminus P^{i+1}.
$$

Put $J=(\pi)+P^{i+1}$. Then

$$
P^{i+1}\subsetneq J\subseteq P^i.
$$

The classification just proved, applied modulo $P^{i+1}$, leaves no [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) strictly between consecutive powers. It follows that

$$
\boxed{P^i=(\pi)+P^{i+1}}.
$$

For $i=0$, the same argument says $(\pi)+P=R$, using $\pi\notin P$ and maximality.

The extension to every $r\geq1$ follows by [mathematical induction](../foundations-of-mathematics.md#mathematical-induction). Suppose $P^i=(\pi)+P^{i+r}$. Multiplication of the initial equality by $P^r$ gives

$$
P^{i+r}=\pi P^r+P^{i+r+1}.
$$

Substitution yields

$$
P^i=(\pi)+\pi P^r+P^{i+r+1}=(\pi)+P^{i+r+1}.
$$

Thus

$$
\boxed{P^i=(\pi)+P^{i+r}\quad\text{for every }r\geq1}.
$$

Taking $r=n-i$ for $0\leq i<n$ gives

$$
\boxed{P^i/P^n=(\pi+P^n)}.
$$

This is a [principal ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal) of the [quotient ring](../commutative-algebra.md#quotient-ring) $R/P^n$. Its zero [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) is generated by zero, so every [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) of this [quotient ring](../commutative-algebra.md#quotient-ring) is a [principal ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal).

<h4 id="2/b/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

<h5 id="2/b/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#2/b/iii)

**The printed domain assertion is false.** For example, take $K=\mathbb Q$ and $I=(4)$. Then $\mathcal O_K/I=\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z$ has the nonzero [zero divisor](../mathematics.md#zero-divisor) $2+(4)$, so it is not an [integral domain](../commutative-algebra.md#integral-domain) and cannot be a [principal ideal domain](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal-domain). The valid conclusion is that $\mathcal O_K/I$ is a [principal ideal ring](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal-ring). This error is present in the original PDF, not just its transcription.

To prove the corrected conclusion, factor

$$
I=\prod_{j=1}^sP_j^{n_j}
$$

into powers of distinct nonzero [prime ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal). These powers are pairwise [comaximal ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#comaximal-ideals). Indeed, a [maximal ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#maximal-ideal) containing two such powers would contain both underlying [prime ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal), forcing them to be equal. The [Chinese remainder theorem](../mathematics.md#chinese-remainder-theorem) gives

$$
R/I\simeq\prod_{j=1}^s R/P_j^{n_j}.
$$

Every factor is a [principal ideal ring](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal-ring) by the preceding part. Every [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) $H$ in a finite [direct product of rings](../commutative-algebra.md#direct-product-of-rings) is a product $H_1\times\cdots\times H_s$ of [ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) in the factors: multiplication by the coordinate [idempotent elements](../commutative-algebra.md#idempotent) separates the coordinates of every element of $H$. Write $H_j=(h_j)$. Then

$$
H_1\times\cdots\times H_s=((h_1,\ldots,h_s)),
$$

so $R/I$ is a [principal ideal ring](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal-ring). This proves the [principal ideal property of quotients of a Dedekind domain](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal-property-of-quotients-of-a-dedekind-domain) in the present setting. In fact, this finite [quotient ring](../commutative-algebra.md#quotient-ring) is an [integral domain](../commutative-algebra.md#integral-domain) precisely when $I$ is a [prime ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal), in which case it is a [field](../algebra.md#field).

For the final deduction, choose any $0\ne a\in I$ and apply the result to the nonzero proper [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) $(a)$. The [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) $I/(a)$ of $R/(a)$ is generated by one residue class $b+(a)$, which can be represented by $b\in I$. For each $x\in I$, some $r\in R$ satisfies $x-rb\in(a)$. Hence

$$
\boxed{I=(a,b)}.
$$

This proves [two generators for an ideal in a Dedekind domain](../commutative-algebra.md#two-generators-for-an-ideal-in-a-dedekind-domain); moreover, its first generator can be any prescribed nonzero element of $I$.

<h3 id="3/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

Let $d_K$ denote the [field discriminant](../algebraic-number-theory.md#field-discriminant), and put $n=[K:\mathbb Q]\geq2$. Let $r_1$ be the number of real [field embeddings](../galois-theory.md#field-embedding) and $r_2$ the number of conjugate pairs of nonreal [field embeddings](../galois-theory.md#field-embedding), so $n=r_1+2r_2$. The [Minkowski bound for ideal classes](../algebraic-number-theory.md#minkowski-s-bound) provides a nonzero [integral ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#integral-ideal) $A$ satisfying

$$
1\leq N(A)\leq\left(\frac4\pi\right)^{r_2}\frac{n!}{n^n}\sqrt{|d_K|}.
$$

Rearranging gives the [Minkowski lower bound for a number-field discriminant](../algebraic-number-theory.md#minkowski-lower-bound-for-a-number-field-discriminant)

$$
|d_K|\geq\left(\frac\pi4\right)^{2r_2}\left(\frac{n^n}{n!}\right)^2.
$$

Because $2r_2\leq n$ and $\pi/4<1$, this is at least

$$
\left(\frac\pi4\right)^n\left(\frac{n^n}{n!}\right)^2.
$$

Apply the [arithmetic-geometric mean inequality](../mathematical-optimization.md#arithmetic-geometric-mean-inequality) to $1,2,\ldots,n$:

$$
n!\leq\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)^n.
$$

It follows that

$$
|d_K|\geq\left(\frac{\pi n^2}{(n+1)^2}\right)^n
\geq\left(\frac{4\pi}{9}\right)^n>1,
$$

where $n/(n+1)\geq2/3$ for $n\geq2$ was used. Thus

$$
\boxed{|d_K|>1}.
$$

The source writes $|\Delta(K)^2|>1$. If $\Delta(K)$ denotes the embedding [determinant](../multilinear-algebra.md#determinant), its square is $d_K$; if it denotes the [field discriminant](../algebraic-number-theory.md#field-discriminant), the displayed result also implies the stated inequality. The conclusion therefore does not depend on that notational convention.

<h3 id="3/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

To compute the [Ideal class group of Q of square root thirty-five](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ideal-class-group-of-q-of-square-root-thirty-five), put $\theta=\sqrt{35}$. The [ring of integers of a quadratic field](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ring-of-integers-of-a-quadratic-field) formula gives $R=\mathbb Z[\theta]$, and the [field discriminant](../algebraic-number-theory.md#field-discriminant) is $4\cdot35=140$. Both [field embeddings](../galois-theory.md#field-embedding) are real, so the [Minkowski bound for ideal classes](../algebraic-number-theory.md#minkowski-s-bound) is

$$
\frac{2!}{2^2}\sqrt{140}=\sqrt{35}<6.
$$

Every [ideal class](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ideal-class) therefore has an [integral ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#integral-ideal) representative of [ideal norm](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ideal-norm) at most five. By [prime ideal factorization](../algebraic-number-theory.md#prime-ideal-factorization) and multiplicativity of the [ideal norm](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ideal-norm), the [ideal class group](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ideal-class-group) is generated by [prime ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) of [ideal norm](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ideal-norm) at most five.

Apply the [Dedekind factorization theorem](../algebraic-number-theory.md#dedekind-factorization-theorem) to $X^2-35$. At two and five it has a repeated linear factor, giving

$$
(2)=P_2^2,\quad P_2=(2,\theta-1),\quad N(P_2)=2;
\qquad
(5)=P_5^2,\quad P_5=(5,\theta),\quad N(P_5)=5.
$$

At three, $X^2-35\equiv X^2+1$ has no root, so $(3)$ itself is a [prime ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) of [ideal norm](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ideal-norm) nine, outside the bound. These are all the relevant [prime ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal): a [prime ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) of [ideal norm](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ideal-norm) four would have to lie above two with [residue degree](../non-archimedean-analysis.md#residue-degree) two, but the factorization above two has only the single factor of degree one. Consequently $[P_2]$ and $[P_5]$ generate, and each has [order of a group element](../group-theory.md#order-of-a-group-element) dividing two.

The [algebraic integer](../algebraic-number-theory.md#algebraic-integer) $5+\theta$ has [field norm](../algebraic-number-theory.md#field-norm) $25-35=-10$. It belongs to both $P_2$ and $P_5$. Hence its [principal ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal) is contained in $P_2\cap P_5=P_2P_5$. Both have [ideal norm](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ideal-norm) ten, so

$$
(5+\theta)=P_2P_5.
$$

It follows that $[P_5]=[P_2]^{-1}=[P_2]$, and the [ideal class group](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ideal-class-group) is generated by one element of [order of a group element](../group-theory.md#order-of-a-group-element) at most two.

Finally, $P_2$ is not a [principal ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal). If $P_2=(a+b\theta)$ with $a,b\in\mathbb Z$, the equality of [ideal norm](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ideal-norm) and absolute [field norm](../algebraic-number-theory.md#field-norm) would imply

$$
a^2-35b^2=\pm2.
$$

Modulo five this requires $a^2\equiv2$ or $3$, whereas squares modulo five are $0,1,4$. Thus $[P_2]\ne1$, proving

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Cl}(\mathbb Q(\sqrt{35}))\simeq\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z}.
$$

This computes the ordinary [ideal class group](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ideal-class-group), with no positivity restriction on generators.

<h3 id="3/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#3/c)

Let $\alpha=\sqrt[3]{3}$. The [polynomial](../polynomial.md) $X^3-3$ is [Eisenstein](../commutative-algebra.md#eisenstein-criterion) at three, so the [number field](../algebraic-number-theory.md#number-field) has [degree of a field extension](../algebra.md#degree-of-a-field-extension) three. Use the permitted [ring of integers](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ring-of-integers) $R=\mathbb Z[\alpha]$, whose [integral basis](../algebraic-number-theory.md#integral-basis) is $1,\alpha,\alpha^2$. The [field traces](../algebraic-number-theory.md#field-trace) $\operatorname{Tr}(1)=3$, $\operatorname{Tr}(\alpha)=\operatorname{Tr}(\alpha^2)=0$, $\operatorname{Tr}(\alpha^3)=9$, and $\operatorname{Tr}(\alpha^4)=0$ give

$$
d_K=\det\begin{pmatrix}3&0&0\\0&0&9\\0&9&0\end{pmatrix}=-243.
$$

There is one real [field embedding](../galois-theory.md#field-embedding) and one conjugate pair of nonreal [field embeddings](../galois-theory.md#field-embedding). The [Minkowski bound for ideal classes](../algebraic-number-theory.md#minkowski-s-bound) is therefore

$$
\frac4\pi\frac{3!}{3^3}\sqrt{243}=\frac{8\sqrt3}{\pi}<5.
$$

Every [ideal class](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ideal-class) has an [integral ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#integral-ideal) representative of [ideal norm](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ideal-norm) at most four, so it is enough to show that every [prime ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) of [ideal norm](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ideal-norm) at most four is a [principal ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal).

Modulo two,

$$
X^3-3\equiv(X+1)(X^2+X+1),
$$

where the quadratic factor is an [irreducible polynomial](../polynomial.md#irreducible-polynomial) over the [finite field](../algebra.md#finite-field) $\mathbb F_2$. The [Dedekind factorization theorem](../algebraic-number-theory.md#dedekind-factorization-theorem) gives

$$
(2)=P_2Q_2,\quad P_2=(2,\alpha-1),\quad N(P_2)=2,\quad
Q_2=(2,\alpha^2+\alpha+1),\quad N(Q_2)=4.
$$

Now

$$
N_{K/\mathbb Q}(\alpha-1)=2,
\qquad (\alpha-1)(\alpha^2+\alpha+1)=2.
$$

Thus $(\alpha-1)=P_2$, by containment and equality of [ideal norms](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ideal-norm), and cancellation in the second identity gives $(\alpha^2+\alpha+1)=Q_2$. This explicitly handles the [prime ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) of [ideal norm](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ideal-norm) four, as well as the one of [ideal norm](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ideal-norm) two.

Modulo three the [polynomial](../polynomial.md) is $X^3$, so

$$
(3)=P_3^3,\qquad P_3=(3,\alpha)=(\alpha),\qquad N(P_3)=3.
$$

These exhaust the [prime ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) of [ideal norm](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ideal-norm) at most four. Every bounded representative factors into the listed [principal ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal), and hence is a [principal ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal). Therefore the [Class group of Q of cube root of three](../algebraic-number-theory.md#class-group-of-q-of-cube-root-of-three) is

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Cl}(\mathbb Q(\sqrt[3]{3}))=1}.
$$

<h3 id="3/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#3/d)

For $p\equiv1\pmod{20}$, put $\theta=\sqrt{-p}$. Since $-p\equiv3\pmod4$, the [ring of integers of a quadratic field](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ring-of-integers-of-a-quadratic-field) formula gives $R=\mathbb Z[\theta]$. Modulo five,

$$
X^2+p\equiv X^2+1=(X-2)(X+2).
$$

The two roots are distinct, so the [Dedekind factorization theorem](../algebraic-number-theory.md#dedekind-factorization-theorem) gives

$$
(5)=P\overline P,\qquad
P=(5,\theta-2),\quad\overline P=(5,\theta+2),\quad
N(P)=N(\overline P)=5.
$$

In particular $P\ne\overline P$.

We prove that $[P]$ has [order of a group element](../group-theory.md#order-of-a-group-element) greater than $n$. Suppose instead that $P^k$ is a [principal ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal) for some $1\leq k\leq n$. Since $P^k\subseteq R$, its generator is in $R$: write $P^k=(a+b\theta)$ with $a,b\in\mathbb Z$. Taking [ideal norms](../algebraic-number-theory.md#ideal-norm) gives the positive [field norm](../algebraic-number-theory.md#field-norm)

$$
5^k=a^2+pb^2.
$$

But $5^k\leq5^n<p$, so $b=0$. Thus $a^2=5^k$, forcing $k$ to be even and $a=\pm5^{k/2}$. This would give

$$
P^k=(a)=(5)^{k/2}=P^{k/2}\overline P^{k/2},
$$

contrary to [unique factorization of ideals in a number field](../algebraic-number-theory.md#unique-factorization-of-ideals-in-a-number-field) and $P\ne\overline P$. Consequently no such $k$ exists. The [finiteness of the ideal class group](../algebraic-number-theory.md#finiteness-of-the-ideal-class-group) ensures that $[P]$ has finite [order of a group element](../group-theory.md#order-of-a-group-element), so

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{ord}([P])>n}.
$$

For every positive [integer](../integer.md) $n$, the allowed infinitude of [prime numbers](../prime-number.md) in the progression $1\pmod{20}$ supplies one larger than $5^n$. Applying the construction proves [unbounded element orders in imaginary quadratic ideal class groups](../algebraic-number-theory.md#unbounded-element-orders-in-imaginary-quadratic-ideal-class-groups). In particular, this establishes elements of arbitrarily large [order of a group element](../group-theory.md#order-of-a-group-element), rather than merely unbounded [class numbers](../algebraic-number-theory.md#class-number).
