# Catalytic selectivity

↑ **Parent:** [Catalysis](catalysis.md)

[Catalytic selectivity](catalytic-selectivity.md) concerns which [reaction products](product-chemistry.md) form when competing [reaction mechanisms](reaction-mechanism.md) are available. Faster conversion is valuable when a process would otherwise be impractically slow; a suitable [catalyst](catalyst.md) can also favour a useful product within the available time. In an illustrative pair of irreversible [first-order reactions](first-order-reaction.md) A to B and A to D, the initial fraction of converted A going to B is $k_B/(k_B+k_D)$. Multiplying both [rate constants](rate-constant.md) by the same factor changes speed but leaves this fraction unchanged; changing their relative values changes the kinetic product split. This is a finite-time kinetic illustration, not a new [equilibrium constant](equilibrium-constant.md).

A [catalyst](catalyst.md) need not accelerate every competing [chemical reaction](chemical-reaction.md) equally. [Enzymes](enzyme.md), for example, can distinguish [substrates](substrate-biochemistry.md) through their [active sites](active-site.md).[§12.7, Enzyme Structure and Function, Figure 12.25: substrate binding and specificity](chemistry-2e-by-paul-flowers-and-others.md) If every relevant reversible channel eventually equilibrates in a [closed system](closed-system.md), its final [chemical equilibrium](chemical-equilibrium.md) still obeys the endpoint [thermodynamics](thermodynamics-split.md). Selective acceleration, product removal, or external driving can make an observed product distribution depend on time and operating conditions. A claim of improved yield must specify those conditions.
