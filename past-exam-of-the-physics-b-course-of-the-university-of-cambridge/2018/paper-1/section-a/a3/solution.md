<h1 id="section-a/a3/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [A3](../a3.md)

A large gas volume can reduce the effective [mass density](../../../../../density.md) of the water surrounding the hull. In the simple quasihydrostatic model for the [buoyancy of an aerated liquid](../../../../../buoyancy-of-an-aerated-liquid.md), gas [void fraction](../../../../../void-fraction.md) $\alpha$ gives

$$
\rho_{\rm mix}=(1-\alpha)\rho_w+\alpha\rho_g\simeq(1-\alpha)\rho_w.
$$

By [Archimedes' principle](../../../../../archimedes-principle.md), a boat displacing volume $V_{\rm disp}$ then has [buoyant force](../../../../../buoyancy.md) $\rho_{\rm mix}gV_{\rm disp}$. To support the same boat mass $m$, it needs a larger displaced volume $m/\rho_{\rm mix}$, so its draft increases. If that exceeds the available watertight displacement, it can lose flotation or become swamped:

$$
\boxed{\rho_{\rm mix}V_{\max}<m\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\text{insufficient hydrostatic buoyancy}.}
$$

Uneven aeration and strong plume motion can also disturb stability. This explains a possible hazard, not a claim that any bubble plume must sink a boat: upward water flow, turbulence and the distribution of bubbles can exert substantial additional forces.
