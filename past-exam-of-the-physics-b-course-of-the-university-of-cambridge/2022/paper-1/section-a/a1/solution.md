<h1 id="section-a/a1/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [A1](../a1.md)

The transfer is a [Hohmann transfer orbit](../../../../../hohmann-transfer-orbit.md). Write $r_0$ for the original [circular Kepler orbit](../../../../../circular-kepler-orbit.md) radius and $a_t$ for the transfer [ellipse](../../../../../ellipse.md)'s [semi-major axis](../../../../../semi-major-axis.md). The diagram measures both radii from [Earth](../../../../../earth.md)'s centre, giving $r_1=1.2r_0$, and hence

$$
a_t=\frac{r_0+r_1}{2}=1.1r_0.
$$

The [Hohmann transfer time](../../../../../hohmann-transfer-time.md) is half the transfer [orbital period](../../../../../orbital-period.md), taking the satellite to the opposite end of the [elliptic orbit](../../../../../elliptic-orbit.md). By [Kepler's third law](../../../../../kepler-s-third-law.md), relative to the original [geostationary orbit](../../../../../geostationary-orbit.md)'s [orbital period](../../../../../orbital-period.md) $T_0$,

$$
\frac{T_t}{T_0}=\left(\frac{a_t}{r_0}\right)^{3/2},\qquad
\boxed{t=\frac{T_0}{2}(1.1)^{3/2}\simeq13.8\ \mathrm h.}
$$

Using one [sidereal day](../../../../../sidereal-day.md), $T_0=86164\ \mathrm s$, gives $t=4.9703\times10^4\ \mathrm s=13.806\ \mathrm h$; the usual $24\ \mathrm h$ approximation gives $13.844\ \mathrm h$. The arrival impulse changes the [velocity](../../../../../velocity.md) into that of the new [circular Kepler orbit](../../../../../circular-kepler-orbit.md) without adding coast time in the impulsive model.

There is a real discrepancy between the wording and the diagram: an increase in altitude above [Earth](../../../../../earth.md)'s surface is different from the radius increase drawn. If altitude is interpreted literally, let $R_E$ be [Earth](../../../../../earth.md)'s radius. Then

$$
r_1=R_E+1.2(r_0-R_E),\qquad
\boxed{t=\frac{T_0}{2}\left(1.1-0.1\frac{R_E}{r_0}\right)^{3/2}\simeq13.5\ \mathrm h,}
$$

using $R_E\simeq6371\ \mathrm{km}$ and $r_0\simeq42164\ \mathrm{km}$. Thus $13.8\ \mathrm h$ answers the explicitly labelled geometry; $13.5\ \mathrm h$ answers the literal altitude wording.
