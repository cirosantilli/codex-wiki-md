<h1 id="section-a/5/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [5](../5.md)

Use a circular [Kepler orbit](../../../../../kepler-orbit.md) to eliminate the terrestrial gravitational parameter: $T^2/r^3$ is constant for both satellites. The reference orbital [radius](../../../../../radius.md) is $r_1=(6400+150)\,\mathrm{km}=6550\,\mathrm{km}$, measured from Earth's centre. A [geostationary orbit](../../../../../geostationary-orbit.md) is circular, equatorial and prograde, with the sidereal rotation period, approximately $86164\,\mathrm s$. Thus

$$
\boxed{r_{\rm geo}=6550\left(\frac{86164}{87\times60}\right)^{2/3}\,\mathrm{km}
\simeq4.25\times10^4\,\mathrm{km}.}
$$

Its altitude is about $3.61\times10^4\,\mathrm{km}$ above the stated surface. The approximate low-orbit input sets the accuracy; replacing it by a modern measured gravitational parameter would answer a different numerical estimate. A $24$-hour approximation changes the [radius](../../../../../radius.md) by less than $0.2\%$. Matching the period alone would not make an inclined or eccentric [orbit](../../../../../orbit-dynamical-system.md) stationary above one point.
