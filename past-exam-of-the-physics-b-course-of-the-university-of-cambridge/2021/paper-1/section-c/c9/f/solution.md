<h1 id="section-c/c9/f/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [F](../f.md)

Changing shape changes the [demagnetizing field](../../../../../../demagnetizing-field.md), and hence the [magnetization](../../../../../../magnetization.md) induced by the same applied [magnetic field intensity](../../../../../../magnetic-field-intensity.md). For a small linear sample in the ellipsoid approximation, the force magnitude scales with

$$
\chi_{\rm eff}=\frac{\mu-1}{1+N_d(\mu-1)}.
$$

For $\mu>1$, a more elongated body aligned with the field has smaller $N_d$ and a stronger response. A sphere has $N_d=1/3$, giving $\chi_{\rm eff}=3(\mu-1)/(\mu+2)$; a very thin plate with field normal to it has $N_d\to1$, giving $(\mu-1)/\mu$. Thus **an axially slender rod generally experiences a stronger attraction than a sphere or a transverse thin plate of the same material and volume**, within the linear uniform-applied-field approximation.

The shape response is precisely why the $1/\mu$ factor in part (c) cannot apply universally to every small magnetic body. Orientation also matters through the appropriate principal [demagnetizing factor](../../../../../../demagnetizing-factor.md); for general nonellipsoidal bodies the internal field is not uniform and a single scalar correction need not be exact.
