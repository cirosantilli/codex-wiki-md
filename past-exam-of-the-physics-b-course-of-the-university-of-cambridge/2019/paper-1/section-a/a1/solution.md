<h1 id="section-a/a1/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [A1](../a1.md)

For two [circular Kepler orbits](../../../../../circular-kepler-orbit.md) about the same body, [Kepler's third law](../../../../../kepler-s-third-law.md) gives $T^2/r^3=\text{constant}$. The reference radius is measured from [Earth](../../../../../earth.md)'s centre:

$$
r_1=(6400+120)\ \mathrm{km}=6520\ \mathrm{km}.
$$

A [geostationary orbit](../../../../../geostationary-orbit.md) is prograde and equatorial, with one [sidereal day](../../../../../sidereal-day.md) as its [orbital period](../../../../../orbital-period.md). Using $T_g\simeq1436\ \mathrm{min}$ and the supplied $T_1=109\ \mathrm{min}$,

$$
\boxed{r_g=r_1\left(\frac{T_g}{T_1}\right)^{2/3}
\simeq3.64\times10^4\ \mathrm{km}
=3.64\times10^7\ \mathrm m.}
$$

Using $24\ \mathrm h$ instead changes this estimate by less than one per cent. This is the orbital radius, not the altitude; the corresponding altitude is about $3.00\times10^4\ \mathrm{km}$.

There is a numerical inconsistency in the source data. The quoted reference orbit implies $GM_E=4\pi^2r_1^3/T_1^2\simeq2.56\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{m^3\,s^{-2}}$, appreciably below the measured terrestrial value, about $3.99\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{m^3\,s^{-2}}$. Using the latter instead gives the familiar [geostationary orbit](../../../../../geostationary-orbit.md) radius $4.22\times10^4\ \mathrm{km}$. The boxed estimate consistently uses the period actually printed in the question.
