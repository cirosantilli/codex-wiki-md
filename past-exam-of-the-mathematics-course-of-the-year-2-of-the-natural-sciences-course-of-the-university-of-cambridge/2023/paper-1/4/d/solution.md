<h1 id="4/d/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [D](../d.md)

Part (b) shows that the transform of $g_n$ is $q_n(k)e^{-k^2/2}$ with $q_n$ of degree $n$. Part (c) shows that it solves the same [eigenvalue](../../../../../eigenvalue.md) equation as $g_n$, with the same $\mu_n$. The assumed uniqueness of the polynomial solution of each degree therefore forces $q_n=c_np_n$; hence $\widetilde g_n=c_ng_n$.

The constants can also be determined. Substituting $g_n=p_ne^{-x^2/2}$ into the equation gives the [Hermite differential equation](../../../../../hermite-differential-equation.md)

$$
p_n''-2xp_n'-(1+\mu_n)p_n=0.
$$

Comparison of the leading coefficient yields $\mu_n=-(2n+1)$. If the leading coefficient of $p_n$ is $a_n$, part (b) gives leading coefficient $\sqrt{2\pi}(-i)^na_n$ for $q_n$. Thus

$$
\boxed{\widetilde g_n(k)=\sqrt{2\pi}(-i)^ng_n(k),\qquad c_n=\sqrt{2\pi}(-i)^n.}
$$

Thus [Hermite functions are Fourier eigenfunctions](../../../../../hermite-functions-are-fourier-eigenfunctions.md) with the paper's unnormalized [Fourier transform](../../../../../fourier-transform-split.md); dividing that transform by $\sqrt{2\pi}$ gives the unitary eigenvalues $(-i)^n$.
