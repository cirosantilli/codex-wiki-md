<h1 id="9/b/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [B](../b.md)

The [total derivative](../../../../../total-derivative.md) contributes only the [boundary term](../../../../../boundary-term.md)

$$
\int_0^T\frac d{dt}g(x,y)\,dt=g(x(T),y(T))-g(x(0),y(0)).
$$

This is fixed by the endpoint data, so its [first variation](../../../../../first-variation.md) vanishes. It cannot change the [Euler-Lagrange equations](../../../../../euler-lagrange-equation.md) or which path minimizes $F$.

For a direct check, let $h=g_x\dot x+g_y\dot y$. Then

$$
\frac d{dt}h_{\dot x}=g_{xx}\dot x+g_{xy}\dot y=h_x,\qquad\frac d{dt}h_{\dot y}=g_{yx}\dot x+g_{yy}\dot y=h_y.
$$

The equality uses commuting mixed [partial derivatives](../../../../../partial-derivative.md). Thus $\boxed{d g/dt\text{ contributes zero to both Euler-Lagrange equations}}$, as in [total time derivatives preserve fixed-endpoint Euler-Lagrange equations](../../../../../total-time-derivatives-preserve-fixed-endpoint-euler-lagrange-equations.md).
