<h1 id="4/e/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [E](../e.md)

For nonzero $b$, the coefficients satisfy $a_n\sim-b/n^2$, so the analytic [power series](../../../../../power-series.md) has [radius of convergence](../../../../../radius-of-convergence.md) $1$. It converges absolutely also at $x=\pm1$, by comparison with $\sum n^{-2}$.

The real [logarithm](../../../../../logarithm.md) in the prescribed solution requires $x>0$. Consequently

$$
\boxed{0<x<1\text{ is the interval of the represented classical solution}.}
$$

The [series](../../../../../series-mathematics.md) and the function itself have finite limits at $x=1$; in fact the logarithmic companion tends to $1$. Its [derivatives](../../../../../derivative.md) are singular there, so convergence of its values at the endpoint does not make it a classical solution through that [singular point of a differential equation](../../../../../singular-point-of-a-differential-equation.md). At $x=0$ the logarithmic branch diverges. The independent analytic solution $C(1-x)$ is a [polynomial](../../../../../polynomial-split.md) and has no such convergence restriction.
