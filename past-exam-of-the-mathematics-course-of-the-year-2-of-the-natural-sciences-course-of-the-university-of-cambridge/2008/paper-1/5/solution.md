<h1 id="5/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [5](../5.md)

A complex $3\times3$ [matrix](../../../../matrix.md) is [diagonalizable](../../../../diagonalizable-matrix.md) exactly when it has a basis of three linearly independent [eigenvectors](../../../../eigenvector.md). If all its [eigenvalues](../../../../eigenvalue.md) are one, a diagonalization would have $D=I$ and hence $A=PIP^{-1}=I$. Therefore the specified matrix, which is not a scalar [identity matrix](../../../../identity-matrix.md), is **not diagonalizable**.

For the displayed real symmetric matrix, choose the [orthogonal matrix](../../../../orthogonal-matrix.md)

$$
Q=\begin{pmatrix}1/\sqrt2&1/\sqrt2&0\\1/\sqrt2&-1/\sqrt2&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Its columns form an [orthonormal eigenbasis](../../../../orthonormal-eigenbasis.md), and

$$
\boxed{Q^TAQ=\operatorname{diag}(1,3,3).}
$$

In the [orthogonal coordinates](../../../../orthogonal-coordinates.md) $u=(x+y)/\sqrt2$, $v=(x-y)/\sqrt2$, the [quadric surface](../../../../quadric-surface.md) has equation $u^2+3v^2+3z^2=1$. It is a [prolate spheroid](../../../../prolate-spheroid.md), with long semiaxis one in direction $(1,1,0)$ and two equal short semiaxes $1/\sqrt3$. The figure gives its principal-axis sketch.

![](../../../../past-exam-of-the-mathematics-course-of-the-year-2-of-the-natural-sciences-course-of-the-university-of-cambridge/2008/paper-1-principal-axis-spheroid.png)

**[Figure 2](#5/image-the-diagonalized-quadric-is-a-prolate-spheroid-with-semiaxes-one-and-one-over-the-square-root-of-three). The diagonalized quadric is a prolate spheroid with semiaxes one and one over the square root of three**.

A diagonalizable matrix can certainly have exactly two distinct [eigenvalues](../../../../eigenvalue.md): $\operatorname{diag}(1,1,2)$ is already diagonal and has an eigenbasis despite its repeated eigenvalue.
