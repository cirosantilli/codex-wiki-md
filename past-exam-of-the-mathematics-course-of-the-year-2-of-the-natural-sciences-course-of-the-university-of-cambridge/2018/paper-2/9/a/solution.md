<h1 id="9/a/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [A](../a.md)

Assume (I). Existence of a decomposition is part of the hypothesis. If $h_1h_2=k_1k_2$ are two decompositions, multiply on the left by $k_1^{-1}$ and on the right by $h_2^{-1}$ to obtain

$$
k_1^{-1}h_1=k_2h_2^{-1}.
$$

The left side belongs to $H_1$ and the right side to $H_2$, since they are [subgroups](../../../../../subgroup.md). Their common value belongs to $H_1\cap H_2=\{e\}$, so $h_1=k_1$ and $h_2=k_2$. This proves uniqueness, hence (II).

Conversely, assume (II). Existence immediately gives $G=H_1H_2$. If $a\in H_1\cap H_2$, then

$$
a=a\cdot e=e\cdot a
$$

are two allowed factorizations, because the identity is in each [subgroup](../../../../../subgroup.md). Uniqueness makes their first components equal, so $a=e$. Thus $H_1\cap H_2=\{e\}$ and (I) follows.

Therefore **(I) and (II) are equivalent**. This is an [exact factorization of a group](../../../../../exact-factorization-of-a-group.md). For finite [groups](../../../../../group-split.md) it gives $|G|=|H_1||H_2|$, but it does not by itself identify $G$ with a [direct product of groups](../../../../../direct-product-of-groups.md); multiplication between the two factors can be noncommutative.
