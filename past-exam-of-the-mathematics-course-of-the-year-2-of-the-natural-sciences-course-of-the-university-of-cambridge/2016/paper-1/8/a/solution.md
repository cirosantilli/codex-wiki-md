<h1 id="8/a/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [A](../a.md)

For the normalized second-order equation, $x_0$ is an [ordinary point](../../../../../ordinary-point-criterion-for-a-second-order-equation.md) if both coefficient functions $p$ and $q$ are [holomorphic](../../../../../holomorphic-function-split.md) near $x_0$. If it is singular but $(x-x_0)p(x)$ and $(x-x_0)^2q(x)$ are [holomorphic](../../../../../holomorphic-function-split.md) there, it is a [regular singular point](../../../../../regular-singular-point.md). Thus $p$ may have at most a simple pole and $q$ at most a double pole.

The distinction tells us which local series method is justified. At an [ordinary point](../../../../../ordinary-point-criterion-for-a-second-order-equation.md), two independent solutions are represented by ordinary [Taylor series](../../../../../taylor-series.md). At a [regular singular point](../../../../../regular-singular-point.md), the [Frobenius method](../../../../../frobenius-method.md) uses $(x-x_0)^r\sum_{n\geq0}a_n(x-x_0)^n$; the [indicial equation](../../../../../indicial-equation.md) determines the exponents, and logarithmic terms may appear in a second solution. Singularities worse than these are irregular and need not have this Frobenius form.
