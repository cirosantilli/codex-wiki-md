<h1 id="8/iii/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](../iii.md)

The [order of a group element](../../../../../order-of-a-group-element.md) $g$ is the least positive integer $q$ such that $g^q=e$, or infinity if there is no such integer. In a finite group such a $q$ exists, because two powers among $e,g,g^2,\ldots$ must coincide.

The powers $e,g,\ldots,g^{q-1}$ are distinct: a repetition with exponents differing by less than $q$ would contradict its minimality. Division of any exponent by $q$ shows that these are all the elements of the [cyclic subgroup](../../../../../cyclic-subgroup.md) $\langle g\rangle$. Thus $|\langle g\rangle|=q$. Applying [Lagrange's theorem](../../../../../lagrange-s-theorem.md) gives

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{ord}(g)=|\langle g\rangle|\ \mid\ |G|.}
$$

In particular the identity has order one, and every nonidentity element has order greater than one. The divisibility assertion here concerns finite group order.
