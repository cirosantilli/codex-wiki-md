<h1 id="9/iv/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](../iv.md)

Choose the tangent angle $\psi=\arctan y'$ in $(-\pi/2,\pi/2)$, so $1/\sqrt{1+(y')^2}=\cos\psi$. At the maximum, the [first integral](../../../../../first-integral.md) has value $C=y_0(1-\lambda)$. Hence

$$
y\cos\psi-\lambda y=y_0(1-\lambda),\qquad
\boxed{\lambda=\frac{y_0-y\cos\psi}{y_0-y}=1+\frac{y(1-\cos\psi)}{y_0-y}.}
$$

A nonconstant regular branch below its positive maximum has $y>0$ and $\psi\ne0$, giving **$\lambda>1$**. For completeness, strictness follows from the differential equation rather than assuming it at every point: the [Euler-Lagrange equation](../../../../../euler-lagrange-equation.md) simplifies to

$$
yy''=1+(y')^2-\lambda[1+(y')^2]^{3/2}.
$$

At the maximum, $y_0y''=1-\lambda$ implies $\lambda\geq1$. If $\lambda=1$, the positive-height initial data $y=y_0$, $y'=0$ give the constant solution uniquely, contradicting the existence of the lower-height branch. Thus $\lambda=1$ is only the degenerate horizontal solution. The first integral with $\lambda>1$ also excludes $y=0$ on this regular branch.
