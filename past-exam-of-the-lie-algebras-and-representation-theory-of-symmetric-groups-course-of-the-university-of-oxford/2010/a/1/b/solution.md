<h1 id="a/1/b/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [B](../b.md)

Let $\pi:L\to L/I$ be the quotient map. A full flag of ideals in $L/I$ lifts by inverse images to a flag from $I$ to $L$, with dimensions increasing by one. Prepending $0<I$ supplies the required full flag in $L$. Thus **supersolubility lifts across a one-dimensional ideal**.

Over $\mathbb C$, a nonzero soluble $L$ has a one-dimensional ideal: apply part (a) to its adjoint image acting on $L$. A common [eigenvector](../../../../../eigenvector.md) spans an adjoint-invariant line, which is an ideal. The quotient is soluble and has smaller [dimension](../../../../../dimension-vector-space.md), so induction and the lifting result give a full flag of ideals. Therefore a soluble complex [Lie algebra](../../../../../lie-algebra-split.md) is [supersoluble](../../../../../supersolvable-lie-algebra.md).

Conversely this implication needs no [field](../../../../../field.md) restriction. If $L$ has a full flag, its first nonzero ideal $I$ is one-dimensional and abelian, and $L/I$ has a full flag. By induction $L/I$ is soluble, so some term of the derived series of $L$ lies in $I$; its next derived term is zero. Hence

$$
\boxed{L\text{ is supersoluble}\iff L\text{ is soluble}\qquad(L\text{ finite-dimensional over }\mathbb C).}
$$
