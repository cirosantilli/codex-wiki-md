<h1 id="2/e/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [E](../e.md)

In the final round, player 1 strictly accepts every $q_2>0$. In any [subgame perfect equilibrium](../../../../../subgame-perfect-equilibrium.md) it must also accept $q_2=0$ with probability one. Otherwise, if that acceptance probability were $a<1$, offering zero would give player 2 only $a\alpha$. A sufficiently small positive offer would be accepted and yield more. No positive offer maximizes player 2's payoff either, because a smaller positive offer improves it. The same supremum argument rules out a mixed offer distribution attaining the missing maximum. Thus the final-round equilibrium uniquely has **$q_2=0$, accepted**, with payoffs $(0,\alpha)$.

In round one, player 2 therefore rejects $q_1<\alpha$ and accepts $q_1>\alpha$. At $q_1=\alpha$ it is indifferent, but equilibrium again forces acceptance with probability one: if acceptance were less likely, player 1 could improve on any proposed optimum with an offer just above $\alpha$. Since $\alpha<1$, player 1 strictly prefers the resulting positive payoff to rejection.

Consequently **the unique equilibrium outcome is immediate agreement at $q_1=\alpha$, giving payoffs $(1-\alpha,\alpha)$**. The full strategies are: player 1 offers $\alpha$ initially and accepts every nonnegative final offer; player 2 accepts initial offers at least $\alpha$, rejects smaller ones, and offers zero in every reached final-round subgame. These prescriptions cover off-path histories too. Acceptance at indifference follows from the [continuous-offer bargaining acceptance lemma](../../../../../continuous-offer-bargaining-acceptance-lemma.md), rather than an arbitrary tie-breaking convention.
