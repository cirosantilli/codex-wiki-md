<h1 id="1/b/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [B](../b.md)

A [weakly dominant strategy](../../../../../weakly-dominant-strategy.md) must be a [best response](../../../../../best-response.md) to either opponent action. For X this requires

$$
\frac{2t}{3}-P\ge0,\qquad\frac{t-P}{2}\ge0.
$$

The first is stronger because $t>0$. Thus

$$
\boxed{\text{X: }b\text{ is weakly dominant iff }0<P\le\frac{2n}{3(n+3)}.}
$$

For Y the comparisons are $t/3-P\ge0$ and $(t-P)/2\ge0$, giving

$$
\boxed{\text{Y: }b\text{ is weakly dominant iff }0<P\le\frac{n}{3(n+3)}.}
$$

Below each upper endpoint the corresponding [strategy](../../../../../strategy-in-an-infinite-game.md) is strictly better against both opponent actions; at the endpoint one comparison ties and the other is strict. Thus the answers also satisfy the convention that weak dominance requires a strict advantage somewhere. Since [expected payoffs](../../../../../expected-payoff.md) are linear in the opponent's mixing [probability](../../../../../probability.md), checking both opponent [pure strategies](../../../../../pure-strategy.md) suffices for every opponent [mixed strategy](../../../../../mixed-strategy.md).
