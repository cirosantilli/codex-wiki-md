The alphabet of a [first-order language](../../../../../../first-order-language.md) separates logical symbols from nonlogical symbols. In the standard presentation here the logical symbols are the variables $x_0,x_1,\ldots$, equality $\dot=$, [negation](../../../../../../negation.md) $\neg$, [logical implication](../../../../../../logical-implication.md) $\to$, the [universal quantification](../../../../../../universal-quantification.md) $\forall$, and the punctuation symbols parentheses and comma. The nonlogical symbols are [constant symbols](../../../../../../constant-symbol.md), [function symbols](../../../../../../function-symbol.md) of each specified finite arity, and [predicate symbols](../../../../../../predicate-symbol.md) of each specified finite arity, such as $f_n^{(k)}$ and $P_n^{(k)}$.

Constants can equivalently be treated as zero-ary [function symbols](../../../../../../function-symbol.md). Their arities belong to the language specification. Other [logical connectives](../../../../../../logical-connective.md), and $\exists x\,\phi$ as an abbreviation for $\neg\forall x\,\neg\phi$, need not be primitive alphabet symbols. This distinction matters in counting literal symbol occurrences.
