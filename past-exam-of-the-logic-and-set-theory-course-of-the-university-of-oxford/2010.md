↑ **Parent:** [A](#a)

<h4 id="a/1/a">a</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#a/1)

<h5 id="a/1/a/i">i</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a/1/a)

<h6 id="a/1/a/i/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#a/1/a/i)

The alphabet of a [first-order language](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-language) separates logical symbols from nonlogical symbols. In the standard presentation here the logical symbols are the variables $x_0,x_1,\ldots$, equality $\dot=$, [negation](../theoretical-computer-science.md#negation) $\neg$, [logical implication](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-implication) $\to$, the [universal quantification](../mathematical-logic.md#universal-quantification) $\forall$, and the punctuation symbols parentheses and comma. The nonlogical symbols are [constant symbols](../mathematical-logic.md#constant-symbol), [function symbols](../mathematical-logic.md#function-symbol) of each specified finite arity, and [predicate symbols](../mathematical-logic.md#predicate-symbol) of each specified finite arity, such as $f_n^{(k)}$ and $P_n^{(k)}$.

Constants can equivalently be treated as zero-ary [function symbols](../mathematical-logic.md#function-symbol). Their arities belong to the language specification. Other [logical connectives](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-connective), and $\exists x\,\phi$ as an abbreviation for $\neg\forall x\,\neg\phi$, need not be primitive alphabet symbols. This distinction matters in counting literal symbol occurrences.

<h5 id="a/1/a/ii">ii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a/1/a)

<h6 id="a/1/a/ii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#a/1/a/ii)

The [first-order terms](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-term) are the least collection containing every variable and constant and closed under formation of $f(t_1,\ldots,t_k)$ whenever $f$ has arity $k$ and the $t_j$ are terms. A [closed term](../mathematical-logic.md#closed-term) has no variables.

The [first-order formulas](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-formula) are the least collection containing the [atomic formulas](../mathematical-logic.md#atomic-formula) $t_1\dot=t_2$ and $P(t_1,\ldots,t_k)$ of the appropriate arities, and closed under [negation](../theoretical-computer-science.md#negation), [logical implication](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-implication), and [universal quantification](../mathematical-logic.md#universal-quantification): from formulas $\phi,\psi$ one may form $\neg\phi$, $(\phi\to\psi)$, and $\forall x_i\phi$, with the chosen fully parenthesized grammar. Only finite expressions obtained by these rules are formulas. No additional strings belong to either collection.

Terms denote elements under an [assignment in a first-order structure](../mathematical-logic.md#assignment-in-a-first-order-structure); formulas receive [truth values](../mathematical-logic.md#truth-value) in a [first-order structure](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-structure). The two syntactic categories have different formation rules even when they use some of the same punctuation.

<h4 id="a/1/b">b</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#a/1)

<h5 id="a/1/b/i">i</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#a/1/b)

<h6 id="a/1/b/i/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#a/1/b/i)

**False.** A ternary atomic predicate gives a counterexample:

$$
\boxed{\phi=P_0^{(3)}(x_0,x_0,x_0).}
$$

This string contains one opening parenthesis and two commas. Thus its number of opening parentheses is smaller than its number of commas. A high-arity argument list contributes several commas while requiring only one pair of parentheses.

<h5 id="a/1/b/ii">ii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#a/1/b)

<h6 id="a/1/b/ii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#a/1/b/ii)

**False.** Choose a variable $x_j$ different from $x_i$ and take

$$
\boxed{\phi=\forall x_j\,P_0^{(1)}(x_j).}
$$

The variable $x_i$ does not occur at all, so in particular it is not free. Yet the formula has one universal-quantifier symbol and zero occurrences of $x_i$. The hypothesis on free occurrences of one variable does not control quantifiers binding other variables.

<h5 id="a/1/b/iii">iii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#a/1/b)

<h6 id="a/1/b/iii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#a/1/b/iii)

**False.** Let $\phi$ be $x_i\dot=x_i$ and let $t=x_j$ with $j\ne i$. There is no quantifier that could capture a variable, so $t$ is free for $x_i$ in $\phi$. [Capture-avoiding substitution](../computability-theory.md#capture-avoiding-substitution) gives

$$
\boxed{\phi[t/x_i]=x_j\dot=x_j.}
$$

The number of occurrences of $x_i$ falls from two to zero, contradicting the proposed strict increase. Even [capture-avoiding substitution](../computability-theory.md#capture-avoiding-substitution) of $x_i$ itself would leave the formula unchanged and refute the strict inequality.

<h5 id="a/1/b/iv">iv</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#a/1/b)

<h6 id="a/1/b/iv/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#a/1/b/iv)

**True.** In the formation rules, commas occur only between arguments in a function application or an atomic predicate application. Each such argument list includes an opening parenthesis. Subsequent construction of larger terms or formulas retains those symbols.

Equivalently prove by simultaneous [structural induction](../foundations-of-mathematics.md#structural-induction) on terms and formulas that an expression containing no opening parenthesis contains no comma. Variables and constants have neither; a comma-bearing function or predicate application already has an opening parenthesis; equality, connectives, and quantifiers cannot introduce a comma outside their constituent expressions. Taking the contrapositive proves the claim.

<h4 id="a/1/c">c</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#a/1)

<h5 id="a/1/c/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#a/1/c)

A collection of [logical connectives](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-connective) is adequate, or has [functional completeness](../analysis-of-boolean-functions.md#functional-completeness), if every [Boolean function](../analysis-of-boolean-functions.md#boolean-function) of finitely many [propositional variables](../mathematical-logic.md#propositional-variable) can be expressed by a formula using those connectives and variables, with the usual convention for nullary truth constants stated if those are included.

An adequate example is $\boxed{\{\neg,\to\}}$. An inadequate example is $\boxed{\{\to\}}$: formulas using [logical implication](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-implication) alone preserve truth when every input is true, so cannot express [negation](../theoretical-computer-science.md#negation). These examples concern truth functions, rather than mere syntactic formation of formulas.

<h4 id="a/1/d">d</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#a/1)

<h5 id="a/1/d/i">i</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#a/1/d)

<h6 id="a/1/d/i/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#a/1/d/i)

Use [structural induction](../foundations-of-mathematics.md#structural-induction) on formulas. Every term denotes the unique element. Every atomic predicate is true by hypothesis, and every equality is true because its two terms denote that same element.

If both immediate subformulas of an [logical implication](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-implication) are true, their [logical implication](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-implication) is true. A universal or [existential quantification](../mathematical-logic.md#existential-quantification) ranges over the singleton domain; its matrix is true for the only possible value by induction, so either quantified formula is true. These are all the formation cases in the reduced language. Hence

$$
\boxed{\text{Every formula of the reduced language is true under every assignment in this structure.}}
$$

<h5 id="a/1/d/ii">ii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#a/1/d)

<h6 id="a/1/d/ii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#a/1/d/ii)

**No.** The full language can express $\neg(x_0\dot=x_0)$, which is false in the singleton structure from part (i). Every formula of the reduced language is true in that structure under every [assignment in a first-order structure](../mathematical-logic.md#assignment-in-a-first-order-structure). Therefore no formula of the reduced language can be logically equivalent to this negated equality.

This is a [singleton truth-preservation obstruction to expressive completeness](../analysis-of-boolean-functions.md#singleton-truth-preservation-obstruction-to-expressive-completeness). Adding both quantifiers to [logical implication](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-implication) does not repair the failure: on this particular nonempty singleton model, quantification cannot create a false formula from true [atomic formulas](../mathematical-logic.md#atomic-formula).

<h3 id="a/2">2</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a)

<h4 id="a/2/a">a</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#a/2)

<h5 id="a/2/a/i">i</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a/2/a)

<h6 id="a/2/a/i/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#a/2/a/i)

Let $f=\alpha\to(\beta\to\gamma)$ and $h=\alpha\to\beta$. Temporarily assume $f$, $h$, and $\alpha$. [Modus ponens rule](../mathematical-logic.md#implication-elimination-rule) first gives $\beta\to\gamma$ from $f$ and $\alpha$, then $\beta$ from $h$ and $\alpha$, and finally $\gamma$.

Apply the [deduction theorem for propositional logic](../mathematical-logic.md#deduction-theorem-for-propositional-logic) successively to discharge $\alpha$, then $h$, then $f$. The result is

$$
\boxed{(\alpha\to(\beta\to\gamma))\to((\alpha\to\beta)\to(\alpha\to\gamma)),}
$$

the required axiom scheme. This derivation uses only assumptions, [modus ponens rule](../mathematical-logic.md#implication-elimination-rule), and the added deduction rule; no [negation](../theoretical-computer-science.md#negation) axiom is involved.

<h5 id="a/2/a/ii">ii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a/2/a)

<h6 id="a/2/a/ii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#a/2/a/ii)

**The requested derivation does not follow from the printed axiom set.** Use a two-valued matrix interpretation with ordinary material [logical implication](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-implication) and designated value $1$, but interpret the unary symbol $\neg$ by the constant function $N(0)=N(1)=0$.

Every instance of the first axiom $\alpha\to(\beta\to\alpha)$ is valid. In the replacement axiom, both $N(\beta)\to\alpha$ and $N(\alpha)\to\beta$ have value $1$, so its whole [logical implication](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-implication) is valid as well. [Modus ponens rule](../mathematical-logic.md#implication-elimination-rule) preserves the designated value. The deduction rule is sound for semantic consequence under this interpretation because [logical implication](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-implication) is the ordinary truth-functional [logical implication](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-implication). Induction on derivations, allowing arbitrary sets of assumptions, therefore makes every theorem valid in this matrix.

But the old contraposition axiom has value

$$
(N(\beta)\to N(\alpha))\to(\alpha\to\beta)
=1\to(\alpha\to\beta),
$$

which is false when $\alpha=1$ and $\beta=0$. Consequently

$$
\boxed{\text{The printed replacement system does not derive the old third axiom.}}
$$

This is a [truth-matrix countermodel for a Hilbert axiom set](../mathematical-logic.md#truth-matrix-countermodel-for-a-hilbert-axiom-set). A nonstandard interpretation of a primitive symbol is legitimate for proving syntactic nonderivability: only preservation by the displayed axioms and rules is required. The source would need a stronger [negation](../theoretical-computer-science.md#negation) axiom or an extra [negation](../theoretical-computer-science.md#negation) principle to support its requested conclusion.

<h5 id="a/2/a/iii">iii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a/2/a)

<h6 id="a/2/a/iii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#a/2/a/iii)

The old system does derive the replacement axiom. Here is a syntactic proof using only its axioms, [modus ponens rule](../mathematical-logic.md#implication-elimination-rule), and the permitted [deduction theorem for propositional logic](../mathematical-logic.md#deduction-theorem-for-propositional-logic).

First derive explosion. From $U$ and $\neg U$, the first axiom gives $\neg V\to\neg U$. The old third axiom then gives $U\to V$, and [modus ponens rule](../mathematical-logic.md#implication-elimination-rule) with $U$ gives any desired $V$.

Next derive the classical reductio rule. Suppose $\Gamma,\neg B$ derives both $C$ and $\neg C$. Let $T$ be the theorem $B\to B$, obtainable by applying the [deduction theorem for propositional logic](../mathematical-logic.md#deduction-theorem-for-propositional-logic) to the identity derivation from assumption $B$. Explosion under $\Gamma,\neg B$ gives $\neg T$, so the [deduction theorem for propositional logic](../mathematical-logic.md#deduction-theorem-for-propositional-logic) gives $\Gamma\vdash\neg B\to\neg T$. The old third axiom with $\alpha=T$, $\beta=B$ yields $\Gamma\vdash T\to B$. Since $T$ is a theorem, $\Gamma\vdash B$. Thus a contradiction under $\neg B$ permits deriving $B$ with that temporary assumption discharged.

Now assume $h=\neg\beta\to\alpha$ and $\neg\alpha$, and temporarily also assume $\neg\beta$. [Modus ponens rule](../mathematical-logic.md#implication-elimination-rule) gives $\alpha$, contradicting $\neg\alpha$. The just-proved reductio rule discharges $\neg\beta$ and gives $\beta$. Finally discharge $\neg\alpha$ and $h$ by the [deduction theorem for propositional logic](../mathematical-logic.md#deduction-theorem-for-propositional-logic):

$$
\boxed{(\neg\beta\to\alpha)\to(\neg\alpha\to\beta).}
$$

No propositional completeness theorem is needed in this derivation.

<h5 id="a/2/a/iv">iv</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a/2/a)

<h6 id="a/2/a/iv/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#a/2/a/iv)

Only one [logical implication](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-implication) of the printed equivalence is justified:

$$
\boxed{\Gamma\vdash_{L'_0}\phi\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\Gamma\vdash_{L_0}\phi.}
$$

The new system's first axiom is an old axiom, its replacement axiom is derivable in the old system by part (iii), [modus ponens rule](../mathematical-logic.md#implication-elimination-rule) is shared, and its deduction rule is admissible in the old system by the theorem allowed in the source. Induction on a new-system derivation therefore translates it into an old-system derivation with the same assumptions.

The reverse [logical implication](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-implication) is false for the displayed systems. Take $\Gamma=\varnothing$ and let $\phi$ be an instance of the old contraposition axiom, for example with distinct [propositional variables](../mathematical-logic.md#propositional-variable) in place of $\alpha,\beta$. It is an old-system theorem, but the matrix countermodel in part (ii) refutes its derivability in the new system. Thus the requested two-way equivalence cannot be concluded from these printed axioms.

<h4 id="a/2/b">b</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#a/2)

<h5 id="a/2/b/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#a/2/b)

Write $U=\forall x_i\neg\phi$ and $H=\neg U\to\psi$. All derivations below use the printed replacement axiom, so they do not rely on the false equivalence from part (a).

The identity [logical implication](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-implication) $\neg A\to\neg A$ follows by the deduction rule. Substituting $\alpha=\neg A$, $\beta=A$ into the replacement axiom and applying [modus ponens rule](../mathematical-logic.md#implication-elimination-rule) therefore gives double-negation elimination:

$$
\vdash\neg\neg A\to A.
$$

Universal instantiation gives $\vdash U\to\neg\phi$. Combine it with $\vdash\neg\neg U\to U$ using [modus ponens rule](../mathematical-logic.md#implication-elimination-rule) under a temporary assumption and then the deduction rule, obtaining $\vdash\neg\neg U\to\neg\phi$.

In the replacement axiom now substitute $\alpha=\neg\phi$ and $\beta=\neg U$. Its antecedent is precisely the last theorem, and its consequent is $\neg\neg\phi\to\neg U$. Thus

$$
\vdash\neg\neg\phi\to\neg\forall x_i\neg\phi.
$$

Under the fixed assumption $H$ and temporary assumption $\neg\neg\phi$, [modus ponens rule](../mathematical-logic.md#implication-elimination-rule) gives $\neg U$ and then $\psi$. Discharge the temporary assumption to obtain $H\vdash\neg\neg\phi\to\psi$.

Finally apply universal generalization. The variable $x_i$ is not free in $H$, because it is bound in $U$ and is not free in $\psi$ by hypothesis. Hence the side condition is satisfied and

$$
\boxed{\{\neg\forall x_i\neg\phi\to\psi\}\vdash\forall x_i(\neg\neg\phi\to\psi).}
$$

Generalization is applied after the temporary assumption has been discharged; applying it while that assumption remained open would not be justified.

<h3 id="a/3">3</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a)

<h4 id="a/3/a">a</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#a/3)

<h5 id="a/3/a/i">i</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a/3/a)

<h6 id="a/3/a/i/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#a/3/a/i)

A [consistent set of formulae](../mathematical-logic.md#consistent-set-of-formulae), restricted here to sentences, does not derive a contradiction in the chosen classical deductive system. Equivalently there is no sentence $\theta$ for which both $\Sigma\vdash\theta$ and $\Sigma\vdash\neg\theta$ hold. By explosion, an inconsistent set derives every sentence; a consistent set does not.

This is a syntactic condition about finite derivations, rather than a definition in terms of models.

<h5 id="a/3/a/ii">ii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a/3/a)

<h6 id="a/3/a/ii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#a/3/a/ii)

A set of sentences is [maximal consistent](../mathematical-logic.md#maximal-consistent-set-of-first-order-sentences) if it is consistent and no proper extension by further sentences remains consistent. Equivalently it is consistent, deductively closed, and decides every sentence: for each $\theta$, exactly one of $\theta$ and $\neg\theta$ belongs to it.

For example, if $\Sigma\vdash\theta$ but $\theta\notin\Sigma$, adding $\theta$ does not create a new contradiction, contrary to maximality. Thus maximality includes deductive closure, not only having as many independent axioms as possible.

<h5 id="a/3/a/iii">iii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a/3/a)

<h6 id="a/3/a/iii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#a/3/a/iii)

A set of sentences is [satisfiable](../mathematical-logic.md#satisfiable-first-order-theory) if some nonempty [first-order structure](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-structure) in its language makes every sentence in the set true. Because the formulas are sentences, their truth is independent of the [assignment in a first-order structure](../mathematical-logic.md#assignment-in-a-first-order-structure).

This is a semantic existence condition. Its equivalence with syntactic consistency is the content of the classical [Gödel's completeness theorem](../mathematical-logic.md#godel-s-completeness-theorem), together with soundness.

<h4 id="a/3/b">b</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#a/3)

<h5 id="a/3/b/i">i</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#a/3/b)

<h6 id="a/3/b/i/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#a/3/b/i)

Order the consistent sets of sentences extending $\Sigma$ by inclusion. This collection is nonempty. The union of a chain is consistent: any derivation of a contradiction uses finitely many assumptions, which all lie in one member of the chain. [Zorn's lemma](../set-theory.md#zorn-s-lemma) therefore supplies a maximal member, which is a maximal consistent extension of $\Sigma$.

For a [countable](../cardinal-number.md#countable-set) language one can instead enumerate all sentences and successively add either a sentence or its [negation](../theoretical-computer-science.md#negation) while preserving consistency. At least one choice is consistent, since inconsistency of both extensions, together with the [deduction theorem for propositional logic](../mathematical-logic.md#deduction-theorem-for-propositional-logic) and classical reasoning, would make the previous stage inconsistent. The union of the stages is consistent by finite use of assumptions and decides every sentence, so it is maximal consistent.

The chain proof covers arbitrary set-sized languages; no restriction on the language [cardinality](../cardinal-number.md#cardinality) was printed here. The later prohibition on choice applies to the cardinal-arithmetic question, not this logic construction.

<h5 id="a/3/b/ii">ii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#a/3/b)

<h6 id="a/3/b/ii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#a/3/b/ii)

Suppose a structure satisfies every sentence of $\Sigma$. By the [soundness theorem for first-order logic](../mathematical-logic.md#soundness-theorem-for-first-order-logic), every sentence derivable from $\Sigma$ is true in this structure. In particular it cannot derive both a sentence and its [negation](../theoretical-computer-science.md#negation), because they cannot both be true under the usual semantics.

Therefore $\boxed{\Sigma\text{ satisfiable}\ \Longrightarrow\ \Sigma\text{ consistent}}$. This direction uses soundness alone, without constructing a model from a proof-theoretic assumption.

<h4 id="a/3/c">c</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#a/3)

<h5 id="a/3/c/i">i</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#a/3/c)

<h6 id="a/3/c/i/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#a/3/c/i)

In the [Henkin construction](../mathematical-logic.md#henkin-construction), first extend the consistent sentence set to a complete consistent theory with witnesses in an expanded language containing fresh constants. Its [term model](../mathematical-logic.md#term-model) has [closed terms](../mathematical-logic.md#closed-term) modulo provable equality as elements:

$$
[t]=[u]\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad T\vdash t\dot=u.
$$

Functions are interpreted by applying their symbols to representative terms, and an atomic predicate holds of term classes exactly when the corresponding atomic sentence belongs to $T$. Equality axioms make these definitions independent of representatives. The [Henkin witness property](../mathematical-logic.md#henkin-witness-property) handles the existential step in the truth lemma, so the resulting structure satisfies $T$ and hence the original $\Sigma$.

An arbitrary sentence set need not already have enough [closed terms](../mathematical-logic.md#closed-term) or witnesses. Calling this a term model for $\Sigma$ means a term-model construction of a model satisfying $\Sigma$, usually through that witnessed completion; it does not assert that every arbitrary $\Sigma$ itself already supplies the construction.

<h5 id="a/3/c/ii">ii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#a/3/c)

<h6 id="a/3/c/ii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#a/3/c/ii)

The downward [Löwenheim-Skolem theorem](../mathematical-logic.md#lowenheim-skolem-theorem) says that a structure in a [countable](../cardinal-number.md#countable-set) language has a [countable](../cardinal-number.md#countable-set) [elementary substructure](../mathematical-logic.md#elementary-substructure) containing any specified finite or [countable](../cardinal-number.md#countable-set) set of its elements. More generally the size bound is $\max(|\mathcal L|,|C|,\aleph_0)$ for a required parameter set $C$. In particular any satisfiable theory in a [countable](../cardinal-number.md#countable-set) language has a model of size at most [countable](../cardinal-number.md#countable-set).

To see the last assertion directly from the completeness construction, add only countably many witness constants in the [Henkin construction](../mathematical-logic.md#henkin-construction). There are then countably many finite [closed terms](../mathematical-logic.md#closed-term), and taking their quotient cannot increase [cardinality](../cardinal-number.md#cardinality). The resulting term model is therefore [countable](../cardinal-number.md#countable-set). For the elementary-substructure version, perform the witness construction inside the given structure, choosing actual witnesses and closing under the [function symbols](../mathematical-logic.md#function-symbol). The induced substructure is elementary by the [Tarski-Vaught test](../mathematical-logic.md#tarski-vaught-test). This is the same witness mechanism as the completeness construction, with the ambient structure supplying the chosen witnesses.

The upward companion states that a theory with an infinite model has models in every sufficiently large infinite cardinal, at least $|\mathcal L|+\aleph_0$. Add that many pairwise unequal constants, apply compactness, and then the size-bounded construction. The [countable](../cardinal-number.md#countable-set) consequence is the part needed below.

$$
\forall x\,\neg(x<x),\qquad
\forall x\forall y\forall z\,((x<y\wedge y<z)\to x<z),
$$



$$

The first three give strict linear order; the next gives density; the final two exclude endpoints. Domains are nonempty under the adopted first-order semantics, and these axioms force them to be infinite.

<h5 id="a/3/d/iii">iii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#a/3/d)

<h6 id="a/3/d/iii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#a/3/d/iii)

Enumerate both [countable](../cardinal-number.md#countable-set) models. Build an increasing sequence of finite order-preserving partial [bijections](../function.md#bijection). At an even stage, take the first unmatched element of the first model. Its position relative to the finitely many already matched points determines an open interval or exterior ray in the second model. Density and absence of endpoints supply an unused point there, so the map extends.

At an odd stage perform the symmetric operation starting from the second model, extending the inverse map. Every required interval has infinitely many points, so avoiding the finitely many used values is possible. The union is a total [bijection](../function.md#bijection), by the alternating enumeration requirements, and it preserves and reflects order because every finite stage does. Hence it is an [isomorphism of structures](../mathematical-logic.md#structure-isomorphism).

This is the [back-and-forth method](../model-theory.md#back-and-forth-method), proving [countable](../cardinal-number.md#countable-set) categoricity of the dense-order theory.

<h5 id="a/3/d/iv">iv</h5>

The recursive definitions of [ordinal addition](../ordinal.md#ordinal-addition) and [ordinal multiplication](../ordinal.md#ordinal-multiplication) are

$$

<h5 id="b/6/a/solution">Solution</h5>

[Hartogs theorem](../set-theory.md#hartogs-theorem) says that for every set $A$ there is a least [ordinal](../ordinal.md) $h(A)$ that does not inject into $A$. This theorem requires no [axiom of choice](../set-theory.md#axiom-of-choice).

To prove it, collect all [well-orderings](../set.md#well-order) of subsets of $A$. They are set-sized data, coded by pairs from $\mathcal P(A)$ and $\mathcal P(A\times A)$. Each has a unique [ordinal](../ordinal.md) order type, so replacement gives a set $H$ of their order types. If $\eta\in H$ and $\xi<\eta$, restricting the corresponding [well-order](../set.md#well-order) to an initial segment shows $\xi\in H$. Hence $H$ is an [ordinal](../ordinal.md). If this [ordinal](../ordinal.md) itself injected into $A$, its transported [well-order](../set.md#well-order) would put $H$ among its own elements, impossible for an [ordinal](../ordinal.md). Every smaller [ordinal](../ordinal.md) does inject by definition. Thus $h(A)=H$ has the asserted property.

**The recursion printed in the source needs two boundary restrictions.** Its new-row successor clause allows $\beta=\alpha$, which would assign a value at $(\alpha,\alpha+1)$ outside the declared domain. Also, at $\alpha=0$, its row-start clause gives $g_1(0,0)=0$ while its column-start clause gives $g_1(0,0)=g_1(0,0)+1$. Taken literally, these clauses do not define a function.

The intended extension fills the new row first and the remaining new column second. Restrict the row successor clause to $\beta<\alpha$, and apply the column-start clause only when $\alpha>0$. With these two restrictions the domains are disjoint:

$$
\alpha\times\alpha,\qquad\{(\alpha,\beta):\beta\leq\alpha\},\qquad
\{(\beta,\alpha):\beta<\alpha\}.
$$

All successors here are [ordinal](../ordinal.md) successors. Simultaneous [transfinite induction](../ordinal.md#transfinite-induction) proves a stronger result: $g_\alpha$ is a [bijection](../function.md#bijection) of $\alpha\times\alpha$ onto an [ordinal](../ordinal.md) $\gamma_\alpha$, and the functions extend one another.

At zero this is the [empty function](../function.md#empty-function). Assuming it at $\alpha$, the least missing [ordinal](../ordinal.md) is $\gamma_\alpha$. Recursion down the new row gives values $\gamma_\alpha+\beta$ for $\beta\leq\alpha$; recursion down the remaining column gives $\gamma_\alpha+(\alpha+1)+\beta$ for $\beta<\alpha$. These enumerate two disjoint consecutive [ordinal](../ordinal.md) intervals above the old range, so the extension is well defined and bijective, with

$$
\boxed{\gamma_{\alpha+1}=\gamma_\alpha+(\alpha+1)+\alpha.}
$$

At a limit $\lambda$, take the union of the earlier functions. Compatibility makes it a function, and every pair of coordinates below $\lambda$ is already present at some earlier stage. Its range is the [ordinal](../ordinal.md) $\bigcup_{\alpha<\lambda}\gamma_\alpha$, and injectivity is preserved by the compatible union. This completes the construction of the [row-and-column enumeration of an ordinal square](../cardinal-number.md#row-and-column-enumeration-of-an-ordinal-square).

For finite stages $\gamma_n=n^2$. For example the table of $g_3$, with the first coordinate indexing rows, is

$$
\begin{pmatrix}0&3&7\\1&2&8\\4&5&6\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The remaining parts refer to this domain-consistent construction. The source's literal boundary claims are preserved in the archive, rather than silently asserted as valid.

<h4 id="b/6/b">b</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#b/6)

<h5 id="b/6/b/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b/6/b)

Let $\kappa=|\alpha|$ be the infinite [initial ordinal](../cardinal-number.md#initial-ordinal) of the given [ordinal](../ordinal.md). A successor adds only one point, so $|\alpha+1|=\kappa$: one can absorb the additional point by shifting the initial copy of $\omega$ and fixing the rest.

The successor construction from part (a) partitions the new range into the old range, a new row of size $|\alpha+1|$, and a new column of size $|\alpha|$. By hypothesis each part has [cardinality](../cardinal-number.md#cardinality) at most $\kappa$, and the old range already has [cardinality](../cardinal-number.md#cardinality) $\kappa$.

A finite [disjoint union](../set-theory.md#disjoint-union) of copies of a well-orderable infinite cardinal has that same [cardinality](../cardinal-number.md#cardinality) without invoking the square theorem being proved. Explicitly, write each $\xi<\kappa$ uniquely as $\omega\eta+n$ with $n<\omega$. Interleave three copies by

$$
(\xi,j)\longmapsto\omega\eta+3n+j,\qquad j<3.
$$

This stays below $\kappa$ because an infinite [initial ordinal](../cardinal-number.md#initial-ordinal) is a limit [ordinal](../ordinal.md) and is closed under finite successor steps below it. It is [injective](../algebra.md#injective-function) by uniqueness of the quotient and finite remainder. The reverse [injection](../algebra.md#injective-function) is immediate, so the [Cantor-Schröder-Bernstein theorem](../cardinal-number.md#cantor-schroder-bernstein-theorem) proves the finite-union claim.

Applying that bound to the three range pieces yields

$$
\boxed{|\gamma_{\alpha+1}|=\kappa=|\alpha+1|.}
$$

Only the well-orderability of [ordinals](../ordinal.md), not a choice principle for arbitrary families of sets, was used.

<h4 id="b/6/c">c</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#b/6)

<h5 id="b/6/c/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#b/6/c)

If zero is admitted as a limit case, the assertion there is immediate since $\gamma_0=0$. For a nonzero limit, let $\kappa=|\lambda|$, an infinite [initial ordinal](../cardinal-number.md#initial-ordinal). There are two cases; separating them avoids choosing an entire family of [bijections](../function.md#bijection) at earlier stages.

If $\kappa<\lambda$, the hypothesis applies at $\kappa$. Part (a) gives a [bijection](../function.md#bijection) $g_\kappa:\kappa^2\to\gamma_\kappa$, whose range has [cardinality](../cardinal-number.md#cardinality) $\kappa$ by hypothesis. Fix one [bijection](../function.md#bijection) $\lambda\to\kappa$; taking its product gives $\lambda^2\cong\kappa^2$. Since $g_\lambda$ is also bijective onto its range, these maps give $\boxed{|\gamma_\lambda|=\kappa=|\lambda|}$.

If $\lambda=\kappa$ is itself an [initial ordinal](../cardinal-number.md#initial-ordinal), suppose $\gamma_\lambda>\kappa$. Continuity gives $\gamma_\lambda=\bigcup_{\alpha<\kappa}\gamma_\alpha$, so some earlier $\gamma_\alpha$ contains the [ordinal](../ordinal.md) $\kappa$. If $\alpha$ is finite then $\gamma_\alpha=\alpha^2$ is finite, impossible. If $\alpha$ is infinite, the hypothesis gives $|\gamma_\alpha|=|\alpha|<\kappa$, again impossible because its [ordinal](../ordinal.md) range contains an initial segment of size $\kappa$.

Thus $\gamma_\lambda\leq\kappa$. Conversely, [transfinite induction](../ordinal.md#transfinite-induction) gives $\gamma_\alpha\geq\alpha$: each successor appends a nonempty row and limits take unions. In particular $\gamma_\lambda\geq\lambda=\kappa$. Hence $\gamma_\lambda=\kappa$ in this case, proving the required cardinal equality as well. The argument includes $\lambda=\omega$, whose earlier stages are finite.

<h4 id="b/6/d">d</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#b/6)

<h5 id="b/6/d/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#b/6/d)

The finite-stage calculation gives $\gamma_\omega=\sup_{n<\omega}n^2=\omega$, so the desired equality holds at the first infinite [ordinal](../ordinal.md). Apply [transfinite induction](../ordinal.md#transfinite-induction) to all subsequent infinite [ordinals](../ordinal.md). The successor step is part (b), and the limit step is part (c). Thus

$$
|\gamma_\alpha|=|\alpha|\quad\text{for every infinite ordinal }\alpha.
$$

By the [well-ordering theorem](../set-theory.md#well-ordering-theorem), any infinite cardinal in the question may be represented by an infinite [initial ordinal](../cardinal-number.md#initial-ordinal) $\kappa$. The corrected recursion supplies a [bijection](../function.md#bijection) $g_\kappa:\kappa\times\kappa\to\gamma_\kappa$, and the just-proved equality gives $|\gamma_\kappa|=\kappa$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\kappa\cdot\kappa=\kappa.}
$$

The proof used only finite-copy interleaving before reaching this conclusion; using the square equality itself to estimate the successor or limit stages would have been circular.
