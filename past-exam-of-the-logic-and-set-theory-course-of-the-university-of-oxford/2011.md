A [logical derivation](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-derivation) of $\alpha$ from $\Gamma$ is a finite sequence $\delta_1,\ldots,\delta_n=\alpha$ in which each line belongs to $\Gamma$, is an instance of one of the three specified axiom schemes, or follows from two earlier lines $\delta_j$ and $\delta_j\to\delta_i$ by the [modus ponens rule](../mathematical-logic.md#implication-elimination-rule). **$\Gamma\vdash\alpha$ means that such a finite derivation exists.** In particular, a derivation uses only finitely many assumptions even if $\Gamma$ is infinite.

<h5 id="a/1/a/ii">ii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a/1/a)

<h6 id="a/1/a/ii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#a/1/a/ii)

**A theorem is derivable with no assumptions:** $\vdash\alpha$ abbreviates $\varnothing\vdash\alpha$. Its [logical derivation](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-derivation) consists entirely of axiom instances and applications of the [modus ponens rule](../mathematical-logic.md#implication-elimination-rule) to earlier lines.

<h5 id="a/1/b/i">i</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#a/1/b)

<h6 id="a/1/b/i/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#a/1/b/i)

The following is a [logical derivation](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-derivation) using only A1, A2 and the [modus ponens rule](../mathematical-logic.md#implication-elimination-rule). The first two lines are assumptions:

$$
\begin{array}{rll}
1.&\alpha\to\beta&\text{assumption},\\
2.&\beta\to\gamma&\text{assumption},\\
3.&(\beta\to\gamma)\to(\alpha\to(\beta\to\gamma))&\mathrm{A1},\\
4.&\alpha\to(\beta\to\gamma)&\mathrm{MP}\ (2,3),\\
5.&(\alpha\to(\beta\to\gamma))\to((\alpha\to\beta)\to(\alpha\to\gamma))&\mathrm{A2},\\
6.&(\alpha\to\beta)\to(\alpha\to\gamma)&\mathrm{MP}\ (4,5),\\
7.&\alpha\to\gamma&\mathrm{MP}\ (1,6).
\end{array}
$$

Thus $\boxed{\{\alpha\to\beta,\beta\to\gamma\}\vdash\alpha\to\gamma}$.

<h5 id="a/1/b/ii">ii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#a/1/b)

<h6 id="a/1/b/ii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#a/1/b/ii)

An instance of A3 is

$$
(\neg\alpha\to\neg\beta)\to(\beta\to\alpha).
$$

The second assumed theorem and the [modus ponens rule](../mathematical-logic.md#implication-elimination-rule) therefore give $\Gamma\vdash\beta\to\alpha$. Apply the preceding implication-composition derivation to this theorem and $\Gamma\vdash\neg\alpha\to\beta$. We obtain $\Gamma\vdash\neg\alpha\to\alpha$. The permitted theorem $(\neg\alpha\to\alpha)\to\alpha$ and one more [modus ponens rule](../mathematical-logic.md#implication-elimination-rule) application yield $\boxed{\Gamma\vdash\alpha}$.

<h4 id="a/1/c">c</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#a/1)

<h5 id="a/1/c/i">i</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#a/1/c)

<h6 id="a/1/c/i/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#a/1/c/i)

[Logical equivalence](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-equivalence) means that the two [propositional formulas](../mathematical-logic.md#propositional-formula) receive the same [truth value](../mathematical-logic.md#truth-value) under every [Boolean valuation](../mathematical-logic.md#boolean-valuation) of the [propositional variables](../mathematical-logic.md#propositional-variable). Equivalently, $\alpha\leftrightarrow\beta$ is a [tautology](../mathematical-logic.md#tautology):

$$
\boxed{\forall v\quad v(\alpha)=v(\beta).}
$$

<h5 id="a/1/c/ii">ii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#a/1/c)

<h6 id="a/1/c/ii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#a/1/c/ii)

Assign to each [propositional formula](../mathematical-logic.md#propositional-formula) its finite symbol length. The set of lengths of formulas [logically equivalent](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-equivalence) to $\alpha$ is a nonempty subset of the [natural numbers](../arithmetic.md#natural-number), because it contains the length of $\alpha$. The [well-ordering principle](../arithmetic.md#well-ordering-principle-for-the-natural-numbers) for the [natural numbers](../arithmetic.md#natural-number) gives a least such length, and hence a shortest equivalent formula.

**A shortest equivalent formula need not be unique.** For example $(p_0\to p_0)$ and $(p_0\leftrightarrow p_0)$ are distinct [tautologies](../mathematical-logic.md#tautology) of the same minimal length. A shorter formula would contain no binary connective and hence be a variable with some number of negations, which is not a [tautology](../mathematical-logic.md#tautology). This counts each [propositional variable](../mathematical-logic.md#propositional-variable) as one symbol, as in the stated language, and proves that a [shortest equivalent formula need not be unique](../mathematical-logic.md#shortest-equivalent-formula-need-not-be-unique).

<h5 id="a/1/c/iii">iii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#a/1/c)

<h6 id="a/1/c/iii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#a/1/c/iii)

A [conjunctive normal form](../theoretical-computer-science.md#conjunctive-normal-form) is a finite conjunction of clauses, each clause being a finite disjunction of literals. A literal is a [propositional variable](../mathematical-logic.md#propositional-variable) or the negation of one. Thus its shape is

$$
\boxed{\bigwedge_{i=1}^m\left(\bigvee_{j=1}^{n_i}\ell_{ij}\right),\qquad\ell_{ij}\in\{p_k,\neg p_k:k\in\omega\}.}
$$

A single clause or single literal is allowed; the necessary parentheses can be inserted according to the grammar.

<h5 id="a/1/c/iv">iv</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#a/1/c)

<h6 id="a/1/c/iv/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#a/1/c/iv)

First eliminate implication and biconditional using the [logical equivalences](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-equivalence)

$$
A\to B\equiv\neg A\lor B,\qquad A\leftrightarrow B\equiv(\neg A\lor B)\land(\neg B\lor A).
$$

Push negations inwards using [De Morgan's laws](../theoretical-computer-science.md#de-morgan-s-laws) and eliminate double negations. [Structural induction](../foundations-of-mathematics.md#structural-induction) leaves only literals joined by conjunction and disjunction.

Now induct on that remaining expression. A literal is already in [conjunctive normal form](../theoretical-computer-science.md#conjunctive-normal-form). A conjunction of two [conjunctive normal forms](../theoretical-computer-science.md#conjunctive-normal-form) is again one after associating its clauses. For a disjunction, write the two inductively obtained forms as $\bigwedge_i C_i$ and $\bigwedge_j D_j$ and use distributivity:

$$
\boxed{\left(\bigwedge_i C_i\right)\lor\left(\bigwedge_j D_j\right)\equiv\bigwedge_{i,j}(C_i\lor D_j).}
$$

Each $C_i\lor D_j$ is a clause. All operations preserve [logical equivalence](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-equivalence), proving the required form exists.

A [first-order structure](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-structure) for this language is $\mathcal A=(A,f^{\mathcal A})$, where $A$ is a **nonempty set** and $f^{\mathcal A}:A\to A$ is a total [function](../function.md). The logical equality symbol is interpreted as actual [equality](../set-theory.md#equality-mathematics) on $A$; there are no other nonlogical symbols to interpret.

<h4 id="a/2/b">b</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#a/2)

<h5 id="a/2/b/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#a/2/b)

Use an [involution with exactly one fixed point](../group-theory.md#involution-with-exactly-one-fixed-point):

$$
\boxed{\phi=\forall x\,f(f(x))=x\ \land\ \exists z\bigl(f(z)=z\land\forall x\,(f(x)=x\to x=z)\bigr).}
$$

The first conjunct makes every orbit of the [function](../function.md) $f$ have size one or two. The second says precisely one orbit has size one. A finite model therefore has $1+2m$ elements, an odd number.

Conversely, on any set of size $2m+1$, fix one element and partition the remaining $2m$ elements into pairs; let $f$ swap the two members of each pair. This defines a model of $\phi$. The case $m=0$ gives a one-element model. Thus the finite model sizes are **exactly the positive odd integers**.

<h4 id="a/2/c">c</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#a/2)

<h5 id="a/2/c/i">i</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#a/2/c)

<h6 id="a/2/c/i/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#a/2/c/i)

Extend the [first-order variable assignment](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-variable-assignment) recursively to [first-order terms](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-term) by

$$
\widetilde v(x_i)=v(x_i),\qquad\widetilde v(f(t))=f^{\mathcal A}(\widetilde v(t)).
$$

For atomic formulas, $\mathcal A\models t=s[v]$ exactly when $\widetilde v(t)=\widetilde v(s)$. Extend to the Boolean connectives by their [truth tables](../mathematical-logic.md#truth-table), for example implication is false exactly when its antecedent is true and its consequent false. Quantifiers use modified [first-order variable assignments](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-variable-assignment):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal A\models\forall x_i\,\theta[v]&\iff\text{for every }a\in A,\quad\mathcal A\models\theta[v(x_i\mapsto a)],\\
\mathcal A\models\exists x_i\,\theta[v]&\iff\text{for some }a\in A,\quad\mathcal A\models\theta[v(x_i\mapsto a)].
\end{aligned}
$$

This recursion defines [satisfaction for a set structure](../mathematical-logic.md#satisfaction-for-a-set-structure) for every [first-order formula](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-formula).

<h5 id="a/2/c/ii">ii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#a/2/c)

<h6 id="a/2/c/ii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#a/2/c/ii)

[Semantic consequence under an assignment](../mathematical-logic.md#semantic-consequence-under-an-assignment) means that for every [first-order structure](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-structure) $\mathcal A$ with the relevant [first-order variable assignment](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-variable-assignment) $v$ into its domain,

$$
\boxed{\bigl(\mathcal A\models\gamma[v]\text{ for every }\gamma\in\Gamma\bigr)\ \Longrightarrow\ \mathcal A\models\phi[v].}
$$

More explicitly, in a fixed structure this is the satisfaction implication at $v$; logical consequence requires that implication in all structures and assignments. An assignment takes values in a particular domain, so a single set map is not silently shared between unrelated domains.

By induction on [first-order formulas](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-formula), their [truth values](../mathematical-logic.md#truth-value) depend only on the values of their [free variables](../mathematical-logic.md#free-variable). A [first-order sentence](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-sentence) has none. If all formulas here are sentences, every displayed satisfaction statement, and therefore the consequence condition, is independent of $v$.

<h4 id="a/2/d">d</h4>

The antecedent says that the nonempty domain has **at most two elements**: its two witnesses need not be distinct. The consequent says it has exactly one element. Thus

$$
\boxed{\mathcal A\models\psi\iff |A|\ne2.}
$$

One-element structures satisfy both sides; domains of size at least three, including infinite domains, make the antecedent false. The [function](../function.md) $f$ has no bearing on this condition.

<h4 id="a/2/e">e</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#a/2)

<h5 id="a/2/e/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [E](#a/2/e)

The [compactness theorem](../mathematical-logic.md#compactness-theorem) states that a set of [first-order sentences](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-sentence) has a model if every finite subset has a model.

For each $n\geq1$, let $\chi_n$ assert that at least $n$ distinct elements exist. Consider $T=\{\phi\}\cup\{\chi_n:n\geq1\}$. A finite subset requires only some finite lower bound $N$ on the size. Choose an odd integer $m\geq N$; the given property of $\phi$ supplies a model of that size, satisfying the finite subset. By the [compactness theorem](../mathematical-logic.md#compactness-theorem), $T$ has a model. Its domain has at least $n$ elements for every $n$, so **$\phi$ has an infinite model**. This argument applies to every sentence with the finite-model-size property in (b), irrespective of its behavior on infinite structures.

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a)

<h4 id="a/3/a">a</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#a/3)

<h5 id="a/3/a/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#a/3/a)

A [logical derivation](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-derivation) is a finite proof in the specified calculus, with conclusion $\Gamma\vdash\phi$. Assumptions from $\Gamma$ and instances of A1–A7 are available; the rules are the [modus ponens rule](../mathematical-logic.md#implication-elimination-rule), [universal generalization rule](../mathematical-logic.md#universal-generalization-rule), and [weakening](../mathematical-logic.md#weakening).

In judgement form the [universal generalization rule](../mathematical-logic.md#universal-generalization-rule) takes $\Delta\vdash\theta$ to $\Delta\vdash\forall x_i\theta$ only if $x_i$ is not free in any undischarged assumption in $\Delta$. [Weakening](../mathematical-logic.md#weakening) permits $\Delta\vdash\theta$ to become $\Delta\cup\Sigma\vdash\theta$. All instances of A4 and A5 must meet their stated substitution and [free variable](../mathematical-logic.md#free-variable) conditions. **Derivability means existence of such a finite proof with assumptions contained in $\Gamma$.** Equivalently one tracks the finite assumption set used by each proof line.

<h4 id="a/3/b">b</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#a/3)

<h5 id="a/3/b/i">i</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#a/3/b)

<h6 id="a/3/b/i/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#a/3/b/i)

The [first-order term](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-term) $t$ is [free for substitution](../mathematical-logic.md#free-for-substitution) for $x_i$ in $\phi$ if, at every free occurrence of $x_i$ to be replaced, no enclosing quantifier binds a variable occurring in $t$. Equivalently, the substitution introduces no [variable capture](../computability-theory.md#variable-capture). Only free occurrences of $x_i$ are replaced.

<h5 id="a/3/b/ii">ii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#a/3/b)

<h6 id="a/3/b/ii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#a/3/b/ii)

Take $\phi=\forall x_1\,(x_0=x_1)$. The term $x_2$ is [free for substitution](../mathematical-logic.md#free-for-substitution) for $x_0$: its variable is not bound by the enclosing quantifier. The term $x_1$ is not [free for substitution](../mathematical-logic.md#free-for-substitution) for $x_0$, because insertion under $\forall x_1$ captures that occurrence.

Thus the first substitution gives $\forall x_1(x_2=x_1)$; the second, illegitimate substitution would give $\forall x_1(x_1=x_1)$.

<h5 id="a/3/b/iii">iii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#a/3/b)

<h6 id="a/3/b/iii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#a/3/b/iii)

In a two-element [first-order structure](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-structure), $\phi=\forall x_1(x_0=x_1)$ is false for every [first-order variable assignment](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-variable-assignment). Blind substitution of $x_1$ for $x_0$ yields $\forall x_1(x_1=x_1)$, which is true. This does not express the original property evaluated at the value of the inserted term: it has changed a free parameter into a [bound variable](../mathematical-logic.md#bound-variable).

For a direct failure of the substitution axiom, use $\theta(x_0)=\exists x_1(x_0\ne x_1)$. On a two-element domain, $\forall x_0\theta(x_0)$ is true, whereas the captured instance $\exists x_1(x_1\ne x_1)$ is false. Consequently an unrestricted A4 would prove a false implication. **The [free for substitution](../mathematical-logic.md#free-for-substitution) condition is necessary for soundness.**

<h4 id="a/3/c">c</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#a/3)

<h5 id="a/3/c/i">i</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#a/3/c)

<h6 id="a/3/c/i/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#a/3/c/i)

Write $E(x,y)$ for the displayed equality predicate. A6 and A4 give $\vdash E(x,x)$, establishing a [reflexive relation](../set-theory.md#reflexive-relation).

An instance of A7, replacing just the first occurrence of $x$ in $E(x,x)$, is

$$
E(x,y)\to(E(x,x)\to E(y,x)).
$$

Assume $E(x,y)$, apply the [modus ponens rule](../mathematical-logic.md#implication-elimination-rule) twice using reflexivity, and obtain $E(y,x)$. The [deduction theorem for propositional logic](../mathematical-logic.md#deduction-theorem-for-propositional-logic) discharges the assumption. This proves symmetry.

For transitivity, A7 supplies

$$
E(y,z)\to(E(x,y)\to E(x,z)),
$$

by replacing $y$ in the atomic formula $E(x,y)$. From assumptions $E(x,y)$ and $E(y,z)$, two applications of the [modus ponens rule](../mathematical-logic.md#implication-elimination-rule) give $E(x,z)$. Discharging assumptions and universally generalizing gives

$$
\boxed{\vdash\forall x\,E(x,x),\quad\vdash\forall x\forall y(E(x,y)\to E(y,x)),\quad\vdash\forall x\forall y\forall z((E(x,y)\land E(y,z))\to E(x,z)).}
$$

Thus the predicate defines an [equivalence relation](../set-theory.md#equivalence-relation) within the calculus.

<h5 id="a/3/c/ii">ii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#a/3/c)

<h6 id="a/3/c/ii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#a/3/c/ii)

**No such first-order axiom collection can force actual identity when the displayed symbol is treated as an ordinary relation.** The [duplication obstruction to axiomatizing identity](../mathematical-logic.md#duplication-obstruction-to-axiomatizing-identity) proves this even for an infinite collection of extra axioms.

Start with any nonempty [first-order structure](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-structure) $\mathcal A$ in which the symbol is identity. Form $B=A\times\{0,1\}$ and the surjection $\pi:B\to A$. Interpret each relation by pulling it back along $\pi$; in particular,

$$
E^{\mathcal B}((a,i),(b,j))\iff a=b.
$$

Interpret every function on first coordinates as in $\mathcal A$ and choose second coordinate zero; likewise lift constants with second coordinate zero. Induction on [first-order terms](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-term) gives $\pi(t^{\mathcal B}(v))=t^{\mathcal A}(\pi v)$. Induction on [first-order formulas](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-formula) then gives

$$
\mathcal B\models\theta[v]\iff\mathcal A\models\theta[\pi v].
$$

The quantifier steps use surjectivity of $\pi$, so every witness in $A$ lifts to $B$.

Every proposed axiom valid with actual equality is therefore true in $\mathcal B$ under every assignment, along with A1–A7. But $E^{\mathcal B}((a,0),(a,1))$ holds for two different domain elements. Thus those axioms cannot enforce identity. The usual interpretation of [logical equality](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-equality) as identity is a semantic requirement.

<h3 id="b/4">4</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b)

<h4 id="b/4/a">a</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#b/4)

<h5 id="b/4/a/i">i</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#b/4/a)

<h6 id="b/4/a/i/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#b/4/a/i)

An approximation is a function $s:n+1\to X$ with $s(0)=x_0$ and $s(k+1)=g(s(k))$ for $k<n$. By induction on $n$, there is such a function: start with $\{(0,x_0)\}$ and append the uniquely specified value $g(s(n))$.

Any two approximations agree on their common domain, by induction from their common initial value and the same transition [function](../function.md) $g$. The collection $\mathcal S$ of all approximations is a set, being a definable subset of $\mathcal P(\omega\times X)$. Therefore $f=\bigcup\mathcal S$ is a [function](../function.md). Existence of approximations of every finite length gives $\operatorname{dom}(f)=\omega$. Each defining equation appears in a sufficiently long approximation, so

$$
\boxed{f:\omega\to X,\qquad f(0)=x_0,\qquad f(n+1)=g(f(n)).}
$$

This proves the [natural-number recursion theorem](../ordinal.md#natural-number-recursion-theorem) without the [axiom of choice](../set-theory.md#axiom-of-choice); no arbitrary sequence of choices was made.

<h5 id="b/4/a/ii">ii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#b/4/a)

<h6 id="b/4/a/ii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#b/4/a/ii)

Suppose $h:\omega\hookrightarrow X$ is an [injection](../algebra.md#injective-function). Shift its image by defining

$$
g(h(n))=h(n+1),\qquad g(x)=x\quad(x\notin h[\omega]).
$$

The two pieces have disjoint images, so $g$ is injective, but $h(0)$ is omitted. Thus $g$ is not surjective.

Conversely, let $g:X\to X$ be an [injection](../algebra.md#injective-function) which omits some $a\in X$. The [natural-number recursion theorem](../ordinal.md#natural-number-recursion-theorem) defines $h(0)=a$, $h(n+1)=g(h(n))$. If $h(m)=h(n)$ with $m<n$, repeated injectivity cancels $m$ iterations and gives $a=g^{n-m}(a)$, putting $a$ in the image of $g$, a contradiction. Hence $h$ is injective. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\omega\preceq X\iff X\text{ admits an injective nonsurjective self-map}.}
$$

Choosing one witness $a$ from a single known nonempty set uses no [axiom of choice](../set-theory.md#axiom-of-choice). This is the [Dedekind-infinite characterization](../set.md#dedekind-infinite-characterization).

<h4 id="b/4/b">b</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#b/4)

<h5 id="b/4/b/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b/4/b)

The [Cantor-Schröder-Bernstein theorem](../cardinal-number.md#cantor-schroder-bernstein-theorem) states that [injections](../algebra.md#injective-function) $A\hookrightarrow B$ and $B\hookrightarrow A$ imply a [bijection](../function.md#bijection) $A\cong B$.

Let $\mathfrak c=|\mathbb R|$ and $\mathcal D=\{S\subseteq\mathbb R:S\text{ is dense in }\mathbb R\}$. Clearly $|\mathcal D|\leq2^{\mathfrak c}$. For the reverse bound, let $C$ be the usual [Cantor set](../geometry-and-topology.md#cantor-set), of [cardinality](../cardinal-number.md#cardinality) $\mathfrak c$. It is closed with empty interior, so $D=\mathbb R\setminus C$ is dense. The map

$$
\mathcal P(C)\longrightarrow\mathcal D,\qquad A\longmapsto D\cup A
$$

is injective, because $A=(D\cup A)\cap C$. Applying the [Cantor-Schröder-Bernstein theorem](../cardinal-number.md#cantor-schroder-bernstein-theorem) gives

$$
\boxed{|\mathcal D|=2^{\mathfrak c}=2^{2^{\aleph_0}}.}
$$

One can see $|C|=\mathfrak c$ by coding [binary sequences](../sequence-and-series.md#bitstream) as ternary expansions using only digits zero and two, together with the usual $|\{0,1\}^{\omega}|=|\mathbb R|$. The same fixed $D$ works for all subsets. This is the [cardinality of the family of dense subsets of the reals](../topology.md#cardinality-of-the-family-of-dense-subsets-of-the-reals).

<h3 id="b/5">5</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b)

<h4 id="b/5/a">a</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#b/5)

<h5 id="b/5/a/i">i</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#b/5/a)

<h6 id="b/5/a/i/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#b/5/a/i)

A [transitive set](../set-theory.md#transitive-set) $X$ is one for which every member is a subset: $\forall y\in X\,(y\subseteq X)$, equivalently $\bigcup X\subseteq X$. If $x\in y\in X$, the subset condition puts $x\in X$. Conversely, if this membership implication always holds, every element of each $y\in X$ belongs to $X$, so $y\subseteq X$. Thus

$$
\boxed{X\text{ is transitive}\iff\forall x\forall y\,(x\in y\in X\to x\in X).}
$$

<h5 id="b/5/a/ii">ii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#b/5/a)

<h6 id="b/5/a/ii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#b/5/a/ii)

Let $a\in b\in\mathcal P(X)$. Since $b\subseteq X$, we have $a\in X$. Transitivity of $X$ gives $a\subseteq X$, so $a\in\mathcal P(X)$. This is exactly the membership criterion for a [transitive set](../set-theory.md#transitive-set), proving **the [power set](../set.md#power-set) of a [transitive set](../set-theory.md#transitive-set) is transitive**.

<h4 id="b/5/b">b</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#b/5)

<h5 id="b/5/b/i">i</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b/5/b)

<h6 id="b/5/b/i/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#b/5/b/i)

A [well-order](../set.md#well-order) is a strict total order in which every nonempty subset has a least element. A [Von Neumann ordinal](../ordinal.md) is a [transitive set](../set-theory.md#transitive-set) well-ordered by membership. Thus its predecessors are precisely its members, and its ordering is the restriction of $\in$.

<h5 id="b/5/b/ii">ii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b/5/b)

<h6 id="b/5/b/ii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#b/5/b/ii)

Put $\alpha=\bigcup X$. Every element of $\alpha$ belongs to some [ordinal](../ordinal.md) in $X$ and is itself an [ordinal](../ordinal.md). If $u\in v\in\alpha$, choose $\beta\in X$ with $v\in\beta$; transitivity of $\beta$ gives $u\in\beta\subseteq\alpha$. Thus $\alpha$ is a [transitive set](../set-theory.md#transitive-set) of [ordinals](../ordinal.md).

Any two [ordinals](../ordinal.md) are comparable by membership or equality, so membership strictly totally orders $\alpha$. Every nonempty subset $A\subseteq\alpha$ has an $\in$-minimal element by the [Axiom of foundation](../set-theory.md#axiom-of-regularity), and totality makes it the least element. Hence **$\bigcup X$ is an [ordinal](../ordinal.md)**, including $\bigcup\varnothing=0$.

<h4 id="b/5/c">c</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#b/5)

<h5 id="b/5/c/i">i</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#b/5/c)

<h6 id="b/5/c/i/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#b/5/c/i)

Use [transfinite induction](../ordinal.md#transfinite-induction). For any $\beta<\alpha$, the induction hypothesis and strict increase give $\beta\leq F(\beta)<F(\alpha)$. Thus every $\beta<\alpha$ belongs to $F(\alpha)$, so $\alpha\subseteq F(\alpha)$, i.e.

$$
\boxed{\alpha\leq F(\alpha).}
$$

This includes $\alpha=0$ and does not yet use continuity of the [normal function on an ordinal](../ordinal.md#normal-function-on-an-ordinal).

<h5 id="b/5/c/ii">ii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#b/5/c)

<h6 id="b/5/c/ii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#b/5/c/ii)

By the [natural-number recursion theorem](../ordinal.md#natural-number-recursion-theorem), let $\alpha_0=0$ and $\alpha_{n+1}=F(\alpha_n)$. These [ordinals](../ordinal.md) form a nondecreasing sequence by the preceding bound. If $\alpha_{n+1}=\alpha_n$ for some $n$, it is already a fixed point.

Otherwise the sequence is strictly increasing. Its supremum $\lambda=\bigcup_{n<\omega}\alpha_n$ is a [limit ordinal](../ordinal.md#limit-ordinal), and the sequence is cofinal in it. Continuity and monotonicity give

$$
F(\lambda)=\sup_{\beta<\lambda}F(\beta)=\sup_{n<\omega}F(\alpha_n)=\sup_{n<\omega}\alpha_{n+1}=\lambda.
$$

For the middle equality, every $\beta<\lambda$ is below some $\alpha_n$, while every $\alpha_n<\lambda$. Therefore **$F$ has a fixed point**. The sequence is a set by [Axiom schema of replacement](../set-theory.md#axiom-schema-of-replacement), since $F$ is a definable [set-theoretic class function](../set-theory.md#set-theoretic-class-function).

<h5 id="b/5/c/iii">iii</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#b/5/c)

<h6 id="b/5/c/iii/solution">Solution</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#b/5/c/iii)

Repeat the preceding construction starting at $\alpha_0=\gamma+1$, for any given [ordinal](../ordinal.md) $\gamma$. A stabilized value or the strictly increasing sequence's supremum is a fixed point at least $\gamma+1$. Thus fixed points occur above every [ordinal](../ordinal.md).

If the fixed points formed a set $S$, their union would be an [ordinal](../ordinal.md) bounding them all. Applying the construction above that bound contradicts it. Hence

$$
\boxed{\{\alpha\in\mathrm{ON}:F(\alpha)=\alpha\}\text{ is a proper class}.}
$$

This is the unboundedness part of the [fixed point of a normal ordinal function](../ordinal.md#fixed-point-of-a-normal-ordinal-function) theorem.

<h3 id="b/6">6</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b)

<h4 id="b/6/a">a</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#b/6)

<h5 id="b/6/a/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#b/6/a)

The four principles are:

- [Axiom of choice](../set-theory.md#axiom-of-choice): for every family $(A_i)_{i\in I}$ of nonempty sets, there exists a [function](../function.md) $c$ on $I$ with $c(i)\in A_i$ for all $i$.
- [Cardinal comparability principle](../set-theory.md#cardinal-comparability-principle): for any sets $A,B$, there is an [injection](../algebra.md#injective-function) $A\hookrightarrow B$ or an [injection](../algebra.md#injective-function) $B\hookrightarrow A$.
- [Well-ordering theorem](../set-theory.md#well-ordering-theorem): every set admits a [well-order](../set.md#well-order).
- [Zorn's lemma](../set-theory.md#zorn-s-lemma): every nonempty [partially ordered set](../set.md#partially-ordered-set) in which every chain has an upper bound has a maximal element.

In the last assertion the upper bound belongs to the partially ordered set; it need not belong to the chain.

<h4 id="b/6/b">b</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#b/6)

<h5 id="b/6/b/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b/6/b)

Assume the [well-ordering theorem](../set-theory.md#well-ordering-theorem). Every set with a chosen [well-order](../set.md#well-order) has an [order isomorphism](../set.md#order-isomorphism) to a unique [ordinal](../ordinal.md). Any two [ordinals](../ordinal.md) are comparable, and the smaller embeds into the larger as an initial segment. Transporting that embedding back to the original sets proves the [cardinal comparability principle](../set-theory.md#cardinal-comparability-principle).

Conversely, assume the [cardinal comparability principle](../set-theory.md#cardinal-comparability-principle) and let $X$ be any set. The [Hartogs theorem](../set-theory.md#hartogs-theorem), provable in [ZF](../set-theory.md#zermelo-fraenkel-set-theory), supplies an [ordinal](../ordinal.md) $h(X)$ for which no [injection](../algebra.md#injective-function) $h(X)\hookrightarrow X$ exists. Comparison forces an [injection](../algebra.md#injective-function) $X\hookrightarrow h(X)$. Pulling back the [well-order](../set.md#well-order) on its image gives a [well-order](../set.md#well-order) on $X$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\mathrm{ZF}\vdash\mathrm{CC}\leftrightarrow\mathrm{WO}.}
$$

The assumed ordinal facts are that every well-ordered set has an ordinal order type and that any two ordinals are comparable; the other assumed theorem is precisely the stated [Hartogs theorem](../set-theory.md#hartogs-theorem).

<h4 id="b/6/c">c</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#b/6)

<h5 id="b/6/c/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#b/6/c)

For the standard definition of [algebraic independence](../algebra.md#algebraic-independence), finite tuples are required to have distinct entries. Let $\mathcal P$ be the set of all subsets of $L$ with [algebraic independence](../algebra.md#algebraic-independence) over $K$, ordered by inclusion. It is nonempty because $\varnothing\in\mathcal P$.

If $\mathcal C$ is a chain in $\mathcal P$, put $U=\bigcup\mathcal C$. A finite tuple of distinct elements of $U$ lies in one member of $\mathcal C$: choose a member containing each element and take the largest of those finitely many members. A polynomial relation on that tuple would then be a polynomial relation in that member, contradicting its [algebraic independence](../algebra.md#algebraic-independence). Thus $U\in\mathcal P$ and is an upper bound. For the empty chain, $\varnothing$ is an upper bound. The [Zorn's lemma](../set-theory.md#zorn-s-lemma) now gives a maximal member $B$, which is a [transcendence basis](../algebra.md#transcendence-basis) by the specified maximality criterion. To also recover the usual field-theoretic definition, if $a\in L$ were transcendental over $K(B)$, clearing denominators in a polynomial relation would show that $B\cup\{a\}$ still has [algebraic independence](../algebra.md#algebraic-independence) over $K$, contradicting maximality. Thus $L$ is algebraic over $K(B)$. Hence **every field extension has a [transcendence basis](../algebra.md#transcendence-basis)**.

There is a literal issue in the printed definition: it does not require the tuple entries to be distinct. With that wording, any nonempty $X$ fails the test: for $a\in X$, the nonzero polynomial $X_1-X_2$ vanishes on $(a,a)$, in every characteristic. Thus under the literal printed test the family is exactly $\{\varnothing\}$, and $\varnothing$ is its maximal member. The same chain-and-[Zorn's lemma](../set-theory.md#zorn-s-lemma) argument still proves existence for that literal notion, but it is not the usual [transcendence basis](../algebra.md#transcendence-basis) notion. **The conventional result above uses distinct tuple entries.**

<h4 id="b/6/d">d</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#b/6)

<h5 id="b/6/d/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#b/6/d)

Let $\mathcal O$ be the set of strict total orders on $\kappa$. Each is a subset of $\kappa\times\kappa$, so the [square of an infinite cardinal](../cardinal-number.md#square-of-an-infinite-cardinal) gives

$$
|\mathcal O|\leq2^{|\kappa\times\kappa|}=2^\kappa.
$$

For the lower bound, use the set $\kappa\times\{0,1\}$ and order the two-element blocks by their first coordinates, using the [well-order](../set.md#well-order) of $\kappa$. For every $A\subseteq\kappa$, put $(\alpha,0)$ before $(\alpha,1)$ exactly when $\alpha\in A$, reversing them otherwise. This defines a strict total order for each $A$, and different subsets give different orders. Transport these orders to $\kappa$ along a fixed [bijection](../function.md#bijection) $\kappa\times\{0,1\}\cong\kappa$. Thus $2^\kappa\leq|\mathcal O|$, and the [Cantor-Schröder-Bernstein theorem](../cardinal-number.md#cantor-schroder-bernstein-theorem) gives

$$
\boxed{|\mathcal O|=2^\kappa.}
$$

If using the customary [axiom of choice](../set-theory.md#axiom-of-choice) development of [infinite cardinal arithmetic](../cardinal-number.md#infinite-cardinal-arithmetic), its use here is precisely to supply $\kappa\times\kappa\cong\kappa$ and $\kappa\times2\cong\kappa$. The ordering construction makes no further choices. It proves the [cardinality of the family of strict total orders](../cardinal-number.md#cardinality-of-the-family-of-strict-total-orders). For $\kappa$ itself an [initial ordinal](../cardinal-number.md#initial-ordinal), these particular product identities can also be proved in [ZF](../set-theory.md#zermelo-fraenkel-set-theory) by well-ordering pairs according to their maximum coordinate, so full [axiom of choice](../set-theory.md#axiom-of-choice) is stronger than this instance intrinsically requires.
