<h4 id="a/1/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#a/1)

<h5 id="a/1/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#a/1/i)

A [Boolean valuation](../mathematical-logic.md#boolean-valuation) is an arbitrary [function](../function.md) $v$ assigning one of the two [truth values](../mathematical-logic.md#truth-value) to each [propositional variable](../mathematical-logic.md#propositional-variable). It extends uniquely to all [propositional formulas](../mathematical-logic.md#propositional-formula) by following the construction of the [propositional formula](../mathematical-logic.md#propositional-formula):

$$
\widetilde v(p)=v(p),\quad
\widetilde v(\neg A)=\neg\widetilde v(A),\quad
\widetilde v(A\wedge B)=\widetilde v(A)\wedge\widetilde v(B),\quad
\widetilde v(A\vee B)=\widetilde v(A)\vee\widetilde v(B).
$$

For [logical implication](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-implication), $\widetilde v(A\to B)$ is false exactly when $\widetilde v(A)$ is true and $\widetilde v(B)$ is false; otherwise it is true. Here the operations on the right are [Boolean operations](../theoretical-computer-science.md#boolean-operation) on [truth values](../mathematical-logic.md#truth-value). [Structural recursion](../programming-language.md#structural-recursion) gives existence, and [structural induction](../foundations-of-mathematics.md#structural-induction) gives uniqueness: once the immediate subformulas have their values, the outer connective fixes the resulting value.

<h4 id="a/1/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#a/1)

<h5 id="a/1/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#a/1/ii)

Two [propositional formulas](../mathematical-logic.md#propositional-formula) are [logically equivalent](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-equivalence) if every [Boolean valuation](../mathematical-logic.md#boolean-valuation) gives them the same [truth value](../mathematical-logic.md#truth-value). A [disjunctive normal form](../theoretical-computer-science.md#disjunctive-normal-form) is a [logical disjunction](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-disjunction) of [logical conjunctions](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-conjunction) of [literals](../theoretical-computer-science.md#boolean-literal), with a [literal](../theoretical-computer-science.md#boolean-literal) being a [propositional variable](../mathematical-logic.md#propositional-variable) or its [negation](../theoretical-computer-science.md#negation).

Let $p_1,\ldots,p_n$ include all [propositional variables](../mathematical-logic.md#propositional-variable) appearing in $\phi$. For each [Boolean valuation](../mathematical-logic.md#boolean-valuation) $s\in\{T,F\}^n$, put

$$
M_s=\bigwedge_{j=1}^n L_{s,j},\qquad
L_{s,j}=\begin{cases}p_j&s_j=T,\\\neg p_j&s_j=F.\end{cases}
$$

The [minterm](../mathematical-logic.md#minterm) $M_s$ is true exactly for [Boolean valuations](../mathematical-logic.md#boolean-valuation) whose restrictions to these [propositional variables](../mathematical-logic.md#propositional-variable) are $s$. Consequently

$$
\boxed{\phi\equiv\bigvee_{s:\,\widetilde s(\phi)=T}M_s.}
$$

Indeed, an [Boolean valuation](../mathematical-logic.md#boolean-valuation) making $\phi$ true makes its own [minterm](../mathematical-logic.md#minterm) true, while a true [minterm](../mathematical-logic.md#minterm) in the displayed [logical disjunction](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-disjunction) certifies the truth of $\phi$. There are only finitely many [Boolean valuations](../mathematical-logic.md#boolean-valuation), so this is a finite [disjunctive normal form](../theoretical-computer-science.md#disjunctive-normal-form). If there are no satisfying [Boolean valuations](../mathematical-logic.md#boolean-valuation), use $p\wedge\neg p$ for any [propositional variable](../mathematical-logic.md#propositional-variable) $p$; this is a [disjunctive normal form](../theoretical-computer-science.md#disjunctive-normal-form) for falsity without needing an empty [logical disjunction](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-disjunction) convention.

<h4 id="a/1/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#a/1)

<h5 id="a/1/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#a/1/iii)

Assume first that $\phi$ is false on the all-true and all-false [Boolean valuations](../mathematical-logic.md#boolean-valuation). Use the truth-table normal form from part (ii). Every satisfying [Boolean valuation](../mathematical-logic.md#boolean-valuation) on the finitely many [propositional variables](../mathematical-logic.md#propositional-variable) must have at least one true [propositional variable](../mathematical-logic.md#propositional-variable) and at least one false [propositional variable](../mathematical-logic.md#propositional-variable). Its [minterm](../mathematical-logic.md#minterm) therefore has the form

$$
C_s\wedge\neg D_s,
$$

where $C_s$ conjoins its true [propositional variables](../mathematical-logic.md#propositional-variable) and $D_s$ disjoins its false [propositional variables](../mathematical-logic.md#propositional-variable); both lists are nonempty. This is equivalent to $\neg(C_s\to D_s)$. If the satisfying [Boolean valuations](../mathematical-logic.md#boolean-valuation) are indexed by $1,\ldots,N$, [De Morgan's laws](../theoretical-computer-science.md#de-morgan-s-laws) gives

$$
\boxed{\phi\equiv\bigvee_{i=1}^N\neg(C_i\to D_i)
\equiv\neg\bigwedge_{i=1}^N(C_i\to D_i).}
$$

If there are no satisfying [Boolean valuations](../mathematical-logic.md#boolean-valuation), take $N=1$ and $C_1=D_1=p$: the result is $\neg(p\to p)$, again always false.

Conversely, on the all-true [Boolean valuation](../mathematical-logic.md#boolean-valuation) every nonempty $D_i$ is true, so every $C_i\to D_i$ is true. On the all-false [Boolean valuation](../mathematical-logic.md#boolean-valuation) every nonempty $C_i$ is false, so every [logical implication](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-implication) is again true. Negating their [logical conjunction](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-conjunction) gives false on both [Boolean valuations](../mathematical-logic.md#boolean-valuation). [Logical equivalence](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-equivalence) transfers these values to $\phi$. The usual nonempty meaning of [logical conjunction](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-conjunction) and [logical disjunction](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-disjunction) of [propositional variables](../mathematical-logic.md#propositional-variable) is used here; allowing empty $C_i$ or $D_i$ without qualification would destroy this converse.

<h4 id="a/2/i">i</h4>

<h5 id="a/2/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#a/2/i)

A [logical derivation](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-derivation) from $\Gamma$ is a finite [sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#sequence) of [propositional formulas](../mathematical-logic.md#propositional-formula), each either a member of $\Gamma$, an instance of one of the three [axioms](../mathematical-logic.md#axiom), or obtained from earlier lines by one of the two displayed [inference rules](../mathematical-logic.md#inference-rule). The notation $\Gamma\vdash\alpha$ means that some such [logical derivation](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-derivation) ends in $\alpha$. Writing $\vdash\alpha$ means derivability with no assumptions.

[Propositional consistency](../mathematical-logic.md#propositional-consistency) means that there is no [propositional formula](../mathematical-logic.md#propositional-formula) $\theta$ for which both $\Gamma\vdash\theta$ and $\Gamma\vdash\neg\theta$. Equivalently not every [propositional formula](../mathematical-logic.md#propositional-formula) is derivable. To check the equivalence directly for this calculus, use the identity theorem proved in (ii): from $\theta\to\theta$ and L3 obtain $\theta\to(\neg\theta\to\eta)$; two uses of [modus ponens rule](../mathematical-logic.md#implication-elimination-rule) give arbitrary $\eta$. Thus a contradictory theory proves everything. Conversely a theory proving everything proves a [propositional formula](../mathematical-logic.md#propositional-formula) and its [negation](../theoretical-computer-science.md#negation).

<h4 id="a/2/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#a/2)

<h5 id="a/2/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#a/2/ii)

Choose an auxiliary [propositional formula](../mathematical-logic.md#propositional-formula) $\beta$. The two instances of L1 are

$$
\alpha\to(\beta\to\alpha),\qquad
\alpha\to(\neg\beta\to\alpha).
$$

The instance of L2 with $\gamma=\alpha$ is

$$
(\alpha\to(\beta\to\alpha))\to
((\alpha\to(\neg\beta\to\alpha))\to(\alpha\to\alpha)).
$$

Apply [modus ponens rule](../mathematical-logic.md#implication-elimination-rule) first with the first L1 instance and then with the second. This is an assumption-free [logical derivation](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-derivation), so

$$
\boxed{\vdash\alpha\to\alpha.}
$$

In particular no semantic completeness theorem or [deduction theorem for propositional logic](../mathematical-logic.md#deduction-theorem-for-propositional-logic) has been assumed to obtain this identity theorem.

<h4 id="a/2/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#a/2)

<h5 id="a/2/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#a/2/iii)

An instance of L3 is

$$
(\alpha\to\beta)\to(\alpha\to(\neg\beta\to\gamma)).
$$

Using the first assumed [logical derivation](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-derivation) and [modus ponens rule](../mathematical-logic.md#implication-elimination-rule) gives $\Gamma\vdash\alpha\to(\neg\beta\to\gamma)$. The L2 instance for these same three [propositional formulas](../mathematical-logic.md#propositional-formula) is

$$
(\alpha\to(\beta\to\gamma))\to
((\alpha\to(\neg\beta\to\gamma))\to(\alpha\to\gamma)).
$$

Use the other assumed [logical derivation](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-derivation) and then the newly obtained [logical derivation](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-derivation) in two applications of [modus ponens rule](../mathematical-logic.md#implication-elimination-rule). Therefore

$$
\boxed{\Gamma\vdash\alpha\to\gamma.}
$$

This [conditional modus ponens](../mathematical-logic.md#conditional-modus-ponens) construction is the step needed to discharge assumptions from a [modus ponens rule](../mathematical-logic.md#implication-elimination-rule) line in part (iv).

<h4 id="a/2/iv">iv</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#a/2)

<h5 id="a/2/iv/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#a/2/iv)

For this calculus the [deduction theorem for propositional logic](../mathematical-logic.md#deduction-theorem-for-propositional-logic) is

$$
\boxed{\Gamma\cup\{\alpha\}\vdash\beta
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\Gamma\vdash\alpha\to\beta.}
$$

For the reverse [logical implication](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-implication), retain the [logical derivation](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-derivation) from $\Gamma$, add the assumption $\alpha$, and use [modus ponens rule](../mathematical-logic.md#implication-elimination-rule).

For the forward [logical implication](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-implication), induct on a [logical derivation](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-derivation) from $\Gamma\cup\{\alpha\}$. For each line $\delta$ we construct a [logical derivation](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-derivation) of $\alpha\to\delta$ from $\Gamma$. If $\delta=\alpha$, use (ii). If $\delta$ is an axiom instance or belongs to $\Gamma$, it is already derivable from $\Gamma$; L1 gives $\delta\to(\alpha\to\delta)$, and [modus ponens rule](../mathematical-logic.md#implication-elimination-rule) finishes that case.

If $\delta$ comes from $\eta$ and $\eta\to\delta$ by [modus ponens rule](../mathematical-logic.md#implication-elimination-rule), the [induction hypothesis](../foundations-of-mathematics.md#induction-hypothesis) supplies

$$
\Gamma\vdash\alpha\to\eta,\qquad
\Gamma\vdash\alpha\to(\eta\to\delta).
$$

Part (iii) supplies $\Gamma\vdash\alpha\to\delta$.

If $\delta$ comes by the elimination rule E from $\eta\to\delta$ and $\neg\eta\to\delta$, the [induction hypothesis](../foundations-of-mathematics.md#induction-hypothesis) supplies

$$
\Gamma\vdash\alpha\to(\eta\to\delta),\qquad
\Gamma\vdash\alpha\to(\neg\eta\to\delta).
$$

The L2 instance with $(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=(\alpha,\eta,\delta)$ combines these by two applications of [modus ponens rule](../mathematical-logic.md#implication-elimination-rule). Thus the rule E also survives the discharge construction. These are all possible lines, proving the theorem for the final line $\beta$.

<h4 id="a/2/v">v</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#a/2)

<h5 id="a/2/v/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [V](#a/2/v)

Suppose both extensions were inconsistent. By the [explosion](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-falsity-elimination-rule) argument in (i), the extension by $\phi$ proves $\neg\phi$, and the extension by $\neg\phi$ proves $\phi$. The [deduction theorem for propositional logic](../mathematical-logic.md#deduction-theorem-for-propositional-logic) therefore yields

$$
\Gamma\vdash\phi\to\neg\phi,\qquad
\Gamma\vdash\neg\phi\to\phi.
$$

The identity theorem gives $\vdash\neg\phi\to\neg\phi$ and $\vdash\phi\to\phi$. Apply E to the first displayed [logical implication](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-implication) and the identity [logical implication](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-implication) with antecedent $\neg\phi$ to obtain $\Gamma\vdash\neg\phi$. Apply E to the identity [logical implication](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-implication) with antecedent $\phi$ and the second displayed [logical implication](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-implication) to obtain $\Gamma\vdash\phi$. This contradicts [propositional consistency](../mathematical-logic.md#propositional-consistency) of $\Gamma$.

Consequently **at least one of the two one-formula extensions is [propositionally consistent](../mathematical-logic.md#propositional-consistency)**. The [formal proof](../mathematical-logic.md#formal-proof) uses the source's own calculus, including its rule E, rather than assuming an unproved completeness theorem for this presentation.

<h5 id="a/3/i/solution">Solution</h5>

The [first-order terms](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-term) are the smallest collection containing every variable and $c$ and containing $F(s,t)$ whenever $s,t$ are [first-order terms](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-term). The [atomic formulas](../mathematical-logic.md#atomic-formula) are exactly $s\approx t$ for [first-order terms](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-term) $s,t$. [Logical formulas](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-formula) are then obtained from [atomic formulas](../mathematical-logic.md#atomic-formula) by the [logical connectives](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-connective) and [quantifiers](../mathematical-logic.md#quantifier): if $\theta,\psi$ are [logical formulas](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-formula) and $x$ a variable, then $\neg\theta$, $(\theta\wedge\psi)$, $(\theta\vee\psi)$, $(\theta\to\psi)$, $\forall x\theta$ and $\exists x\theta$ are [logical formulas](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-formula). These closure rules, with no additional strings admitted, define the syntax; a presentation using fewer primitive connectives treats the others as abbreviations.

[Semantic entailment](../mathematical-logic.md#semantic-entailment) is the condition

$$
\boxed{\Gamma\models\phi\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\text{for every }\mathfrak A\text{ and every assignment }v,
\bigl(\mathfrak A\models\gamma[v]\text{ for all }\gamma\in\Gamma\bigr)
\Longrightarrow\mathfrak A\models\phi[v].}
$$

The same [first-order variable assignment](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-variable-assignment) is used for all premises and the conclusion. This matters when [free variables](../mathematical-logic.md#free-variable) occur; for [first-order sentences](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-sentence) the [truth value](../mathematical-logic.md#truth-value) is independent of the [first-order variable assignment](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-variable-assignment).

<h4 id="a/3/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#a/3)

<h5 id="a/3/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#a/3/ii)

For independence, construct four [first-order structures](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-structure), each satisfying exactly the three required remaining [axioms](../mathematical-logic.md#axiom) and failing the chosen one.

To fail [associativity](../algebraic-operation.md#associative-property), use the carrier $\{0,1,2\}$, interpret $c$ as $0$, and define the commutative operation by its table:

$$
\begin{array}{c|ccc}
F&0&1&2\\\hline
0&0&1&2\\
1&1&0&1\\
2&2&1&0
\end{array}
$$

The first row and column give a two-sided identity, and every element is its own inverse because the diagonal entries are $0$. [Symmetry](../physics.md#symmetry-physics) gives [commutativity](../algebraic-operation.md#commutativity). But

$$
F(F(1,1),2)=2\ne0=F(1,F(1,2)),
$$

so [associativity](../algebraic-operation.md#associative-property) fails.

To fail the identity axiom, take $\{0,1\}$, $c=0$, and the constant operation $F(x,y)=0$. It is associative and commutative, and every element has a two-sided partner producing $c$; but $F(1,c)=0\ne1$.

To fail the inverse axiom, use [natural numbers](../arithmetic.md#natural-number) under [addition](../arithmetic.md#addition) with $c=0$. [Addition](../arithmetic.md#addition) is associative and commutative and has the required identity, but $1$ has no additive inverse in this carrier.

To fail [commutativity](../algebraic-operation.md#commutativity), use the [symmetric group](../finite-group-theory.md#symmetric-group) $S_3$ under [permutation](../combinatorics.md#permutation) composition with $c$ its identity. [Associativity](../algebraic-operation.md#associative-property), identity and inverses are [group](../group.md) properties, while $(12)(23)\ne(23)(12)$. Each countermodel witnesses the failure of [semantic entailment](../mathematical-logic.md#semantic-entailment) from the other three to the failed [first-order sentence](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-sentence). Hence **the four source [axioms](../mathematical-logic.md#axiom) are independent**.

<h4 id="a/4/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#a/4)

<h5 id="a/4/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#a/4/i)

[Soundness](../mathematical-logic.md#soundness) states that [formal derivability](../mathematical-logic.md#formal-derivability) implies [semantic entailment](../mathematical-logic.md#semantic-entailment):

$$
\boxed{\Gamma\vdash_K\phi\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\Gamma\models\phi.}
$$

For [first-order sentences](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-sentence) this means that every [first-order structure](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-structure) satisfying all assumptions also satisfies every [first-order sentence](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-sentence) formally derived from them. In the [logical formula](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-formula) version one uses the usual restrictions on generalization from assumptions with [free variables](../mathematical-logic.md#free-variable).

Strong completeness states the converse:

$$
\boxed{\Gamma\models\phi\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\Gamma\vdash_K\phi.}
$$

Equivalently, every syntactically consistent [set](../set.md) of [first-order sentences](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-sentence) has a [first-order model](../mathematical-logic.md#model-of-a-first-order-theory). Equality is interpreted as actual identity, and its usual logical [axioms](../mathematical-logic.md#axiom) and substitution rules are part of the standard calculus. Both assertions hold for arbitrary [sets](../set.md) of assumptions, rather than only [finite sets](../set.md#finite-set).

<h4 id="a/4/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#a/4)

<h5 id="a/4/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#a/4/ii)

The [compactness theorem](../mathematical-logic.md#compactness-theorem) says that a [set](../set.md) $T$ of [first-order sentences](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-sentence) has a [first-order model](../mathematical-logic.md#model-of-a-first-order-theory) if and only if every finite [subset](../set.md#subset) of $T$ has a [first-order model](../mathematical-logic.md#model-of-a-first-order-theory). One direction follows by using the same [first-order model](../mathematical-logic.md#model-of-a-first-order-theory) for each [subset](../set.md#subset).

For the nontrivial direction suppose every finite [subset](../set.md#subset) has a [first-order model](../mathematical-logic.md#model-of-a-first-order-theory), but $T$ has none. Semantically $T\models\bot$, so completeness gives a formal [logical derivation](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-derivation) $T\vdash\bot$. A formal [logical derivation](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-derivation) is finite and uses only finitely many assumptions, say $T_0\subseteq T$. Thus $T_0\vdash\bot$. [Soundness](../mathematical-logic.md#soundness) would make $T_0$ unsatisfiable, contradicting the hypothesis. Here $\bot$ can be any fixed contradictory [first-order sentence](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-sentence), such as $\exists x\,(x\ne x)$, if it is not a primitive logical symbol. Therefore **finite satisfiability implies satisfiability**.

<h4 id="a/4/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#a/4)

<h5 id="a/4/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#a/4/iii)

Take $S_1$ to consist of the three usual [first-order sentences](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-sentence)

$$
\forall x\ E(x,x),\qquad
\forall x\forall y\,(E(x,y)\to E(y,x)),\qquad
\forall x\forall y\forall z\,((E(x,y)\wedge E(y,z))\to E(x,z)).
$$

These assert [reflexivity](../set-theory.md#reflexive-relation), [symmetry](../physics.md#symmetry-physics) and [transitivity](../set-theory.md#transitive-relation) and therefore define exactly the [equivalence relations](../set-theory.md#equivalence-relation).

For $S_2$, adjoin to $S_1$ the single [first-order sentence](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-sentence)

$$
\forall x\exists y\,[y\ne x\wedge E(x,y)\wedge
\forall z\,(E(x,z)\to(z=x\vee z=y))].
$$

Its witness $y$ gives a second member of each class, while its final universal clause excludes all others. Hence every class has exactly two members, and conversely such a relation satisfies it. This remains a [finite set](../set.md#finite-set) of [first-order sentences](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-sentence).

For each positive [integer](../integer.md) $n$, write

$$
\theta_n(x)=\exists y_1\cdots\exists y_n
\left(\bigwedge_{i=1}^n E(x,y_i)\wedge
\bigwedge_{1\le i<j\le n}y_i\ne y_j\right).
$$

Then

$$
\boxed{S_3=S_1\cup\{\forall x\,\theta_n(x):n\ge1\}.}
$$

Every class in a [first-order model](../mathematical-logic.md#model-of-a-first-order-theory) has at least $n$ elements for every $n$, so cannot be finite. Conversely every infinite [equivalence class](../set-theory.md#equivalence-class) supplies each finite collection of distinct witnesses. The displayed infinite scheme therefore has exactly the requested [first-order models](../mathematical-logic.md#model-of-a-first-order-theory).

<h4 id="a/4/iv">iv</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#a/4)

<h5 id="a/4/iv/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#a/4/iv)

Suppose such a [first-order theory](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-theory) $S$ existed. Add two new constants $c,d$ and consider

$$
T=S\cup\{\neg E(c,d)\}\cup
\{\theta_n(c),\theta_n(d):n\ge1\},
$$

where $\theta_n$ is the finite lower-bound [logical formula](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-formula) from (iii).

Every finite [subset](../set.md#subset) of $T$ has a [first-order model](../mathematical-logic.md#model-of-a-first-order-theory). Choose a [first-order structure](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-structure) with exactly two equivalence classes: one countably infinite, and one finite of size at least the largest bound appearing in that [subset](../set.md#subset). Interpret $c$ in the infinite class and $d$ in the finite class. The [first-order structure](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-structure) satisfies all of $S$, by its assumed characterization, and meets the finite demands on both named classes.

[Compactness theorem](../mathematical-logic.md#compactness-theorem) in the language with the two new constants gives a [first-order model](../mathematical-logic.md#model-of-a-first-order-theory) of all of $T$. Its classes of $c$ and $d$ are distinct and both infinite. Its reduct still satisfies $S$, which is impossible if $S$ characterizes exactly one infinite class. Hence

$$
\boxed{\text{Exactly one infinite equivalence class is not first-order axiomatizable}.}
$$

The finite approximations work because any finite list of [lower bounds](../set.md#lower-bound-in-a-partially-ordered-set) permits the second class to remain finite; the entire list prevents that escape.

<h5 id="b/5/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#b/5/i)

The [axiom of infinity](../set-theory.md#axiom-of-infinity) asserts that an [inductive set](../set-theory.md#inductive-set) exists: some [set](../set.md) $I$ contains $0=\varnothing$ and contains $x^+=x\cup\{x\}$ whenever it contains $x$. Inside one such $I$, [axiom of separation](../set-theory.md#axiom-schema-of-specification) defines

$$
\omega=\{x\in I:x\text{ belongs to every inductive subset of }I\}.
$$

This [intersection](../set.md#set-intersection) is itself inductive and is contained in every [inductive set](../set-theory.md#inductive-set), by intersecting that [set](../set.md) with $I$. It is therefore the [set](../set.md) of [natural numbers](../arithmetic.md#natural-number). If a property holds at $0$ and passes from $n$ to $n^+$, [axiom of separation](../set-theory.md#axiom-schema-of-specification) forms the [subset](../set.md#subset) of $\omega$ on which it holds. That [subset](../set.md#subset) is inductive, so must equal $\omega$. This proves [mathematical induction](../foundations-of-mathematics.md#mathematical-induction), including properties with [set](../set.md) parameters.

[Recursion](../algorithm.md#recursion-computer-science) on $\omega$ defines the arithmetic operations by

$$
m+0=m,\qquad m+n^+=(m+n)^+,\qquad
m\cdot0=0,\qquad m\cdot n^+=m\cdot n+m.
$$

Existence and uniqueness follow from the [recursion theorem on the natural numbers](../foundations-of-mathematics.md#recursion-theorem-on-the-natural-numbers).

For [associativity](../algebraic-operation.md#associative-property) of [addition](../arithmetic.md#addition) fix $m,n$ and induct on $k$. At $k=0$ both sides equal $m+n$. If the equality holds at $k$, then

$$
m+(n+k^+)=m+(n+k)^+=(m+(n+k))^+
=((m+n)+k)^+=(m+n)+k^+.
$$

Thus

$$
\boxed{m+(n+k)=(m+n)+k.}
$$

Every step after the [induction hypothesis](../foundations-of-mathematics.md#induction-hypothesis) is a defining successor equation, so [commutativity](../algebraic-operation.md#commutativity) has not been used prematurely.

<h4 id="b/5/ii">ii</h4>

<h5 id="b/5/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

Induct on $n$. The initial equation is $0+0=0$ by the [zero](../polynomial.md#zero-of-a-function) clause. If $0+n=n$, the successor clause gives

$$
0+n^+=(0+n)^+=n^+.
$$

Consequently

$$
\boxed{0+n=n\quad(n\in\omega).}
$$

This proves the left-identity property independently of the right-identity equation built into the recursive definition.

<h4 id="b/5/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#b/5)

<h5 id="b/5/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

Fix $m$ and induct on $n$. At $n=0$,

$$
m^++0=m^+=(m+0)^+=m+0^+.
$$

If $m^++n=m+n^+$, then

$$
m^++n^+=(m^++n)^+=(m+n^+)^+=m+(n^+)^+.
$$

The last expression is the right-hand side required at the successor $n^+$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{m^++n=m+n^+\quad(m,n\in\omega).}
$$

In particular both sides also equal $(m+n)^+$ by the [recursion](../algorithm.md#recursion-computer-science) equation for the second argument.

Fix $m,n$ and induct on $k$. For $k=0$,

$$
m\cdot(n+0)=m\cdot n=m\cdot n+0=m\cdot n+m\cdot0.
$$

For the induction step, use the [multiplication](../arithmetic.md#multiplication) [recursion](../algorithm.md#recursion-computer-science), the [induction hypothesis](../foundations-of-mathematics.md#induction-hypothesis), and [addition](../arithmetic.md#addition) [associativity](../algebraic-operation.md#associative-property) from (i):

\begin{aligned}
m\cdot(n+k^+)&=m\cdot(n+k)^+\\

&=m\cdot n+(m\cdot k+m)\\
&=m\cdot n+m\cdot k^+.
\end{aligned}

Thus

$$
\boxed{m\cdot(n+k)=m\cdot n+m\cdot k.}
$$

This establishes the indicated distributive law without assuming [multiplication](../arithmetic.md#multiplication) is commutative.

<h4 id="b/5/v">v</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#b/5)

<h5 id="b/5/v/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [V](#b/5/v)

Fix $m$ and induct on $n$. The initial equality $m+0=0+m$ follows from the defining [zero](../polynomial.md#zero-of-a-function) clause and (ii). Assuming $m+n=n+m$, the successor clause and (iii) give

$$
m+n^+=(m+n)^+=(n+m)^+=n+m^+=n^++m.
$$

This proves

$$
\boxed{m+n=n+m\quad(m,n\in\omega).}
$$

The extension to arbitrary [ordinals](../ordinal.md) is false. By the [limit](../calculus.md#limit-of-a-function) clause for [ordinal addition](../ordinal.md#ordinal-addition),

$$
1+\omega=\sup_{n<\omega}(1+n)=\omega,
$$

whereas $\omega+1$ is the [successor ordinal](../ordinal.md#successor-ordinal) of $\omega$ and is strictly greater than it. Therefore

$$
\boxed{1+\omega\ne\omega+1.}
$$

Finite [addition](../arithmetic.md#addition) is commutative; [ordinal addition](../ordinal.md#ordinal-addition) records the order of concatenated well-orders and need not be commutative.

<h3 id="b/6">6</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b)

<h4 id="b/6/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#b/6)

Use the [Kuratowski ordered pair](../set.md#kuratowski-ordered-pair)

$$
\boxed{\langle x,y\rangle=\{\{x\},\{x,y\}\}.}
$$

The [axiom of pairing](../set-theory.md#axiom-of-pairing) first constructs $\{x\}$ and $\{x,y\}$, then constructs the displayed [pair](../set.md#pair) of those [sets](../set.md), proving that it is a [set](../set.md).

For any $a,b$, the [intersection](../set.md#set-intersection) of the members of this [pair](../set.md#pair) is $\{a\}$, and their [union](../set.md#set-union) is $\{a,b\}$. Thus equality of two [ordered pairs](../set.md#ordered-pair) first gives $\{a\}=\{c\}$, hence $a=c$, and then $\{a,b\}=\{a,d\}$. If $b=a$, this latter [set](../set.md) is a [singleton](../set.md#singleton-mathematics) and forces $d=a$. If $b\ne a$, its unique member distinct from $a$ is $b$, forcing $d=b$. Hence equality of [ordered pairs](../set.md#ordered-pair) gives equality of both coordinates.

For a [set](../set.md) $R$ of [ordered pairs](../set.md#ordered-pair), both coordinates of every [pair](../set.md#pair) belong to

$$
U=\bigcup\bigcup R,
$$

which is a [set](../set.md) by two applications of the [Axiom of union](../set-theory.md#axiom-of-union). [Axiom of separation](../set-theory.md#axiom-schema-of-specification) inside $U$ gives

$$
\operatorname{dom}R=\{x\in U:\exists y\,\langle x,y\rangle\in R\},\qquad
\operatorname{ran}R=\{y\in U:\exists x\,\langle x,y\rangle\in R\}.
$$

These are [sets](../set.md), including when $R$ is empty.

The [Axiom of foundation](../set-theory.md#axiom-of-regularity) asserts that every nonempty [set](../set.md) $A$ has an element $a$ with $a\cap A=\varnothing$. If a [descending membership sequence](../set-theory.md#descending-membership-sequence) $f:\omega\to V$ existed, [replacement](../set-theory.md#axiom-schema-of-replacement) would give the nonempty range [set](../set.md) $S=\{f(n):n\in\omega\}$. [Foundation](../set-theory.md#axiom-of-regularity) would give some $f(n)$ disjoint from $S$. But $f(n^+)\in f(n)$ and $f(n^+)\in S$, contradicting that disjointness. Thus [foundation](../set-theory.md#axiom-of-regularity) rules out every such [sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#sequence).

Conversely suppose there is no [descending membership sequence](../set-theory.md#descending-membership-sequence), but [foundation](../set-theory.md#axiom-of-regularity) fails. There is then a nonempty [set](../set.md) $A$ such that $a\cap A\ne\varnothing$ for every $a\in A$. The [axiom of choice](../set-theory.md#axiom-of-choice) selects a [function](../function.md) $h$ on $A$ with $h(a)\in a\cap A$. Choose $a_0\in A$ and use [recursion](../algorithm.md#recursion-computer-science) to define

$$
f(0)=a_0,\qquad f(n^+)=h(f(n)).
$$

Every value remains in $A$, and $f(n^+)\in f(n)$ for every $n$. This is the forbidden [sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#sequence). Therefore, with the stated [choice](../set-theory.md#axiom-of-choice) assumption, **[foundation](../set-theory.md#axiom-of-regularity) is equivalent to the absence of [descending membership sequences](../set-theory.md#descending-membership-sequence)**. The selection step explains exactly where [choice](../set-theory.md#axiom-of-choice) is used; [dependent choice](../set-theory.md#axiom-of-dependent-choice) would suffice.

<h4 id="b/7/i">i</h4>

<h5 id="b/7/i/solution">Solution</h5>

The [Cantor-Schröder-Bernstein theorem](../cardinal-number.md#cantor-schroder-bernstein-theorem) says that [injections](../algebra.md#injective-function) $X\to Y$ and $Y\to X$ imply a [bijection](../function.md#bijection) $X\to Y$. Thus mutual cardinal inequalities imply equal cardinalities, without a [choice](../set-theory.md#axiom-of-choice) assumption.

Write $\mathfrak c=2^{\aleph_0}=|\mathbb R|$. Standard [cardinal arithmetic](../cardinal-number.md#cardinal-arithmetic) gives $\mathfrak c\cdot\mathfrak c=\mathfrak c$ and $\mathfrak c^{\aleph_0}=\mathfrak c$. The correspondence $x+iy\leftrightarrow(x,y)$ is a [bijection](../function.md#bijection) $\mathbb C\leftrightarrow\mathbb R^2$. Hence

$$
\boxed{|\mathbb C|=\mathfrak c.}
$$

Equivalently, the real axis supplies the lower inequality and a coding of two real coordinates supplies the upper one; Cantor-Schröder-Bernstein then gives the equality.

<h4 id="b/7/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [7](#b/7)

<h5 id="b/7/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#b/7/ii)

The [set](../set.md) of all [functions](../function.md) from a [set](../set.md) of size $\mathfrak c$ to another [set](../set.md) of size $\mathfrak c$ has size $\mathfrak c^{\mathfrak c}$. [Cardinal exponentiation](../cardinal-number.md#cardinal-exponentiation) and the law $(\kappa^\lambda)^\mu=\kappa^{\lambda\mu}$ give

$$
\mathfrak c^{\mathfrak c}
=(2^{\aleph_0})^{\mathfrak c}
=2^{\aleph_0\cdot\mathfrak c}
=2^{\mathfrak c}.
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{|\mathbb R^{\mathbb C}|=2^{\mathfrak c}.}
$$

The [lower bound](../set.md#lower-bound-in-a-partially-ordered-set) can also be seen by taking characteristic [functions](../function.md) of all [subsets](../set.md#subset) of $\mathbb C$. [Cantor's theorem](../set.md#cantor-s-theorem) makes this strictly larger than $\mathfrak c$, unlike the continuous-function space in the next part.

<h4 id="b/7/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [7](#b/7)

<h5 id="b/7/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#b/7/iii)

The [set](../set.md) $D=\mathbb Q+i\mathbb Q$ is [countable](../cardinal-number.md#countable-set) and dense in $\mathbb C$. Restriction to $D$ is [injective](../algebra.md#injective-function) on [continuous functions](../calculus.md#continuous-function) $\mathbb C\to\mathbb R$: if two such [functions](../function.md) agree there, their equality [set](../set.md) is closed and contains a [dense set](../topology.md#dense-set), so is all of $\mathbb C$. Equivalently a [sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#sequence) of points of $D$ converging to any chosen point and [continuity](../calculus.md#continuous-function) determine its value uniquely.

Thus the family has [cardinality](../cardinal-number.md#cardinality) at most

$$
|\mathbb R^D|=\mathfrak c^{\aleph_0}
=(2^{\aleph_0})^{\aleph_0}=2^{\aleph_0}=\mathfrak c.
$$

For the [lower bound](../set.md#lower-bound-in-a-partially-ordered-set), every [real number](../arithmetic.md#real-number) determines a different constant [continuous function](../calculus.md#continuous-function). Cantor-Schröder-Bernstein yields

$$
\boxed{|C(\mathbb C,\mathbb R)|=\mathfrak c.}
$$

The reduction to countably many values is the feature that separates this answer from unrestricted [functions](../function.md).

<h4 id="b/7/iv">iv</h4>

<h5 id="b/7/iv/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#b/7/iv)

Every strictly increasing [sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#sequence) is in $\mathbb N^{\mathbb N}$, whose [cardinality](../cardinal-number.md#cardinality) is $\aleph_0^{\aleph_0}=\mathfrak c$. This gives the [upper bound](../set.md#upper-bound-in-a-partially-ordered-set).

For each binary [sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#sequence) $\epsilon=(\epsilon_n)_{n\in\mathbb N}$, define

$$
a_n=2n+\epsilon_n.
$$

Then $a_{n+1}-a_n=2+\epsilon_{n+1}-\epsilon_n\ge1$, so the [sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#sequence) is strictly increasing. Distinct binary [sequences](../sequence-and-series.md#sequence) give distinct increasing [sequences](../sequence-and-series.md#sequence). This injects a [set](../set.md) of size $2^{\aleph_0}$ into the desired family, giving the matching [lower bound](../set.md#lower-bound-in-a-partially-ordered-set). Hence

$$
\boxed{\text{cardinality}=\mathfrak c.}
$$

One may also identify an increasing [sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#sequence) with its infinite range [subset](../set.md#subset) of $\mathbb N$; the explicit binary coding establishes the [lower bound](../set.md#lower-bound-in-a-partially-ordered-set) without discarding finite [subsets](../set.md#subset) by cardinal subtraction.

<h5 id="b/7/v/solution">Solution</h5>

The family is a [subset](../set.md#subset) of all complex [sequences](../sequence-and-series.md#sequence), so its [cardinality](../cardinal-number.md#cardinality) is at most

$$
|\mathbb C^{\mathbb N}|=\mathfrak c^{\aleph_0}=\mathfrak c.

For each $z\in\mathbb C$, the [sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#sequence) $(z,0,0,\ldots)$ belongs to the family because its displayed [series](../sequence-and-series.md#series-mathematics) has just one nonzero [first-order term](../mathematical-logic.md#first-order-term). These [sequences](../sequence-and-series.md#sequence) are distinct as $z$ varies, giving a [lower bound](../set.md#lower-bound-in-a-partially-ordered-set) $\mathfrak c$. Thus

$$
\boxed{\text{cardinality}=\mathfrak c.}
$$

The source condition is [convergence](../calculus.md#convergence) of $\sum_j a_j^2$, without absolute-value bars. The argument uses exactly that printed condition and makes no assertion that it is equivalent to square summability of complex [sequences](../sequence-and-series.md#sequence). The same [upper and lower bounds](../set.md#upper-and-lower-bounds) would also work for the stronger absolute-square condition, but no alteration of the source is needed.

<h5 id="b/7/vi/solution">Solution</h5>

Inclusion gives $|\mathbb R\setminus\mathbb Q|\le\mathfrak c$. If $\kappa=|\mathbb R\setminus\mathbb Q|$, the disjoint decomposition of $\mathbb R$ gives

$$
\mathfrak c=\aleph_0+\kappa.
$$

The [irrational numbers](../algebra.md#irrational-number) are infinite, for instance $n+\sqrt2$ are pairwise distinct irrationals. Standard infinite cardinal [addition](../arithmetic.md#addition) gives $\aleph_0+\kappa=\max(\aleph_0,\kappa)$. Since [Cantor's theorem](../set.md#cantor-s-theorem) gives $\mathfrak c>\aleph_0$, the equality forces $\kappa=\mathfrak c$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{|\mathbb R\setminus\mathbb Q|=\mathfrak c.}
$$

This is the permitted cardinal-arithmetic [formal proof](../mathematical-logic.md#formal-proof); it does not subtract infinite cardinals as though they were finite numbers.

<h3 id="b/8">8</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b)

<h4 id="b/8/a">a</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [8](#b/8)

<h5 id="b/8/a/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#b/8/a)

[Zorn's lemma](../set-theory.md#zorn-s-lemma) states that a nonempty [partially ordered set](../set.md#partially-ordered-set) in which every [chain](../set.md#chain-in-a-partial-order) has an [upper bound](../set.md#upper-bound-in-a-partially-ordered-set) contains a [maximal element](../set.md#maximal-element-of-a-partially-ordered-set). Here a [chain](../set.md#chain-in-a-partial-order) means a [subset](../set.md#subset) totally ordered by the inherited order; it need not have a greatest member.

View every [partial function](../function.md#partial-function) as its graph, a [subset](../set.md#subset) of $\mathbb R\times\mathbb R$. The family $\mathcal F$ is a [set](../set.md) by [axiom of separation](../set-theory.md#axiom-schema-of-specification) from the [power set](../set.md#power-set) of this product. Let $\mathcal C\subseteq\mathcal F$ be a [chain](../set.md#chain-in-a-partial-order) and put $h=\bigcup\mathcal C$. If two members of the [union](../set.md#set-union) assign values to the same input, the [chain](../set.md#chain-in-a-partial-order) property makes one graph contain the other, so those values agree. Thus $h$ is a [partial function](../function.md#partial-function).

If $x\in\operatorname{dom}h$ and $q\in\mathbb Q$, choose a [chain](../set.md#chain-in-a-partial-order) member containing $x$ in its domain. Its rational-scalar closure gives $qx\in\operatorname{dom}h$. If $x,y\in\operatorname{dom}h$, choose two [chain](../set.md#chain-in-a-partial-order) members containing them; the larger one contains both, and therefore contains $x+y$ with value $h(x)+h(y)$. Hence $h$ satisfies the required additive identity and domain closure. Consequently

$$
\boxed{\bigcup\mathcal C\in\mathcal F,}
$$

and it is an [upper bound](../set.md#upper-bound-in-a-partially-ordered-set) for the [chain](../set.md#chain-in-a-partial-order). For the empty [chain](../set.md#chain-in-a-partial-order) its [union](../set.md#set-union) is the [empty function](../function.md#empty-function), which satisfies the defining [logical implications](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-implication) vacuously, so this case is covered too.

<h4 id="b/8/b">b</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [8](#b/8)

<h5 id="b/8/b/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#b/8/b)

A nonempty [weakly linear function](../function.md#weakly-linear-partial-function) has a domain $D$ closed under rational scalars and [addition](../arithmetic.md#addition), hence a [vector subspace](../vector-space.md#vector-subspace) of $\mathbb R$ over $\mathbb Q$. Additivity first gives $f(0)=0$ and $f(-x)=-f(x)$, then $f(nx)=nf(x)$ for [integers](../integer.md) $n$. If $q=m/n$ with $n>0$, rational closure puts $qx$ in $D$ and

$$
n f(qx)=f(nqx)=f(mx)=m f(x).
$$

Thus $f(qx)=qf(x)$: it is a $\mathbb Q$-linear map. The [empty function](../function.md#empty-function) is not maximal because it extends to the [function](../function.md) on $\{0\}$ with value [zero](../polynomial.md#zero-of-a-function).

Suppose a maximal $f$ had proper domain $D\ne\mathbb R$. Choose $t\notin D$ and any prescribed real value $a$, for instance $a=0$. Every element of $D+\mathbb Qt$ has a unique expression $d+qt$: if two expressions coincide and the coefficients differ, then $t$ would belong to $D$. Define

$$
h(d+qt)=f(d)+qa.
$$

Uniqueness makes this well-defined. The displayed [logical formula](../mathematical-logic.md#logical-formula) and the $\mathbb Q$-linearity of $f$ show directly that $h$ is additive and has a domain closed under rational scalars. Hence it is a [weakly linear function](../function.md#weakly-linear-partial-function) and extends $f$. It is a proper extension because $t$ is now in its domain, contradicting maximality. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{dom}f=\mathbb R\quad\text{for every maximal }f\in\mathcal F.}
$$

Part (a) and [Zorn's lemma](../set-theory.md#zorn-s-lemma) guarantee the existence of maximal extensions as well as this description of their domains.

<h4 id="b/8/c">c</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [8](#b/8)

<h5 id="b/8/c/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#b/8/c)

Irrationality of $\sqrt2$ makes the expression $q+r\sqrt2$ unique for rational $q,r$: equality of two such expressions with different $r$ would express $\sqrt2$ as a [rational number](../number-theory.md#rational-number). Therefore the rule selecting the coefficient $r$ defines a [function](../function.md) on $A$. Its domain is a [vector subspace](../vector-space.md#vector-subspace) over $\mathbb Q$, and direct coefficient [addition](../arithmetic.md#addition) and rational scaling show it is $\mathbb Q$-linear, hence weakly linear. In particular

$$
f(1)=0,\qquad f(\sqrt2)=1.
$$

Use [Zorn's lemma](../set-theory.md#zorn-s-lemma) on the nonempty family of weakly [linear extensions](../set.md#linear-extension) of this particular [function](../function.md). [Chain](../set.md#chain-in-a-partial-order) [unions](../set.md#set-union) remain extensions by (a); hence there is a maximal extension $h$. Any further extension in $\mathcal F$ would also contain the original [function](../function.md), so this $h$ is maximal in $\mathcal F$. Part (b) therefore gives $\operatorname{dom}h=\mathbb R$.

A [real-linear map](../vector-space.md#real-linear-map) $\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ is [multiplication](../arithmetic.md#multiplication) by its value at $1$. Were $h$ real-linear, it would satisfy

$$
h(\sqrt2)=\sqrt2\,h(1)=0,
$$

contradicting the inherited value $h(\sqrt2)=1$. Thus **a [weakly linear function](../function.md#weakly-linear-partial-function) on all of $\mathbb R$ need not be real-linear**. Its [rational homogeneity of an additive function](../analysis.md#rational-homogeneity-of-an-additive-function) is insufficient for real homogeneity. This particular extension is also discontinuous: [rational numbers](../number-theory.md#rational-number) approaching $\sqrt2$ have value [zero](../polynomial.md#zero-of-a-function), while the value at their [limit](../calculus.md#limit-of-a-function) is one.
