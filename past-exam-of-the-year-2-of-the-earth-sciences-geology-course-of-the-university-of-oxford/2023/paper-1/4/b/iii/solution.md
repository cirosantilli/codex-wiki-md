<h1 id="4/b/iii/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](../iii.md)

**The rate of surface modification** controls how quickly a [fault scarp](../../../../../../fault-scarp.md) is erased. Strong rainfall, runoff, slope failure and easily eroded [lithologies](../../../../../../lithology.md) can round or dissect a scarp; dry conditions and resistant rock often preserve it longer. [Alluvial fan](../../../../../../alluvial-fan.md) deposition and [aggradation](../../../../../../aggradation.md) can bury a scarp even if erosion is slow. A young marker surface can preserve an offset that an older, strongly modified surface no longer records.

**The balance between [fault slip rate](../../../../../../fault-slip-rate.md), recurrence interval and marker age** controls whether renewed displacement outruns this modification. Rapid or repeated [normal faulting](../../../../../../normal-faulting.md) can maintain a clear front, whereas a long interval after a small displacement permits smoothing. A very young fan deposited after the event may have no offset, while an older surface may retain cumulative rather than single-event [fault throw](../../../../../../fault-throw.md). These two controls explain why absence of an obvious scarp is not proof of an inactive [fault](../../../../../../geological-fault.md).
