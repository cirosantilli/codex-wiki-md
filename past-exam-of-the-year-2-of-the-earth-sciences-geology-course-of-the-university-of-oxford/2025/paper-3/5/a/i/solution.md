<h1 id="5/a/i/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [I](../i.md)

A [Sun-synchronous orbit](../../../../../../sun-synchronous-orbit.md) maintains approximately constant orientation relative to the mean Sun, so corresponding passes over a location occur at nearly the same local solar time. It is usually a nearly polar, slightly retrograde [low Earth orbit](../../../../../../low-earth-orbit.md). Earth rotates beneath the successive tracks, allowing broad coverage; the satellite is not stationary over a location and does not necessarily revisit that location every day.

It is attained by selecting altitude, [orbital eccentricity](../../../../../../orbital-eccentricity.md) and [orbital inclination](../../../../../../orbital-inclination.md) so that [nodal precession](../../../../../../nodal-precession.md) caused by Earth's equatorial bulge matches the mean apparent annual motion of the Sun, about $360^\circ$ per year eastward. To leading order,

$$
\dot\Omega=-\frac32J_2 n\left(\frac{R_E}{a(1-e^2)}\right)^2\cos i.
$$

Here $a$ is the [semi-major axis](../../../../../../semi-major-axis.md), $e$ the [orbital eccentricity](../../../../../../orbital-eccentricity.md), $i$ the [orbital inclination](../../../../../../orbital-inclination.md), $n=\sqrt{GM_E/a^3}$ the [mean motion](../../../../../../mean-motion.md), and $J_2$ Earth's dimensionless quadrupole coefficient. A retrograde inclination has $\cos i<0$, giving the required positive precession; typical imaging altitudes yield inclinations around $97^\circ$–$99^\circ$. The launch establishes the required orbital plane, and subsequent orbit maintenance limits drift.

This geometry suits repeated optical and [multispectral imaging](../../../../../../multispectral-imaging.md) of land, vegetation and oceans, because comparable local time reduces illumination and shadow differences. [Landsat](../../../../../../landsat.md) and [Sentinel-2](../../../../../../sentinel-2.md) are examples. Radar missions such as [Sentinel-1](../../../../../../sentinel-1.md) also use this orbit for regular global sampling even though their illumination is supplied by the radar. Constant local time helps comparison but does not remove seasonal changes in solar elevation.[“Polar Orbits” and “Sun-Synchronous Orbits”: near-polar global sampling and controlled precession maintaining local solar time.](../../../../../../catalog-of-earth-satellite-orbits-by-nasa-earth-observatory.md)
