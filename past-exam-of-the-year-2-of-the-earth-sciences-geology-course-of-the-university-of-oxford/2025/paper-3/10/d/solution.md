<h1 id="10/d/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [D](../d.md)

The [stability boundary in a mineral activity diagram](../../../../../stability-boundary-in-a-mineral-activity-diagram.md) requires the two coexisting minerals to be stable against the third. For the forward dissolution directions of (I) and (II),

$$
\frac{\Delta_rG_I}{2RT\ln10}=y-(3.44-x),\qquad
\frac{\Delta_rG_{II}}{2RT\ln10}=y-(-7.59-3x).
$$

A negative value favours [corundum](../../../../../corundum.md) over the corresponding [silicate mineral](../../../../../silicate-mineral.md). For (III),

$$
\frac{\Delta_rG_{III}}{RT\ln10}=2x+11.03,
$$

so low silica activity favours [nepheline](../../../../../nepheline.md) over [albite](../../../../../albite.md), and high silica activity favours [albite](../../../../../albite.md). Combining these comparisons gives the stable portions:

- **(I): $x<-5.515$**, the nepheline–corundum boundary to the left of the intersection.
- **(II): $x>-5.515$**, the albite–corundum boundary to its right.
- **(III): $x=-5.515$, $y>8.955$**, the nepheline–albite boundary above the intersection.

The opposite extension of each line is [metastable](../../../../../metastability.md) because the third mineral has lower thermodynamic potential there. All three minerals can coexist at the intersection in this fixed-temperature, fixed-pressure activity model.
