<h1 id="2/e/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [E](../e.md)

The coarse angular fragments in the light-coloured band identify a **[fault breccia](../../../../../fault-breccia.md)**, probably cemented and accompanied by finer comminuted material. Repeated brittle fracture, clast rotation and crushing during [fault slip](../../../../../fault-slip.md) produce the fragments; fluid flow can later fill pores and fractures with mineral cement. If the finer material is cohesive it can be termed [cataclasite](../../../../../cataclasite.md); a loose, very fine seam is [fault gouge](../../../../../fault-gouge.md). The photograph alone does not establish all those [cohesion](../../../../../cohesion.md) and grain-size distinctions.

Slip is commonly concentrated on a narrow principal slip surface or fine-grained seam at a margin or inside a broader damaged band. Thus the coarsest, widest breccia is not automatically the material that accommodated the largest shear displacement. In this image the sharp, finer-looking boundaries of the coarse band are the best candidates for localized slip. Identifying the uniquely most displaced seam would require displaced markers, thin sections or additional exposure; the coarse band itself records substantial distributed damage.
