<h1 id="11/b/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [B](../b.md)

Four documented examples, each tied to its actual context, are:

- **Geographic range:** widespread marine [bivalve](../../../../../bivalve.md) genera survived the end-[Cretaceous](../../../../../cretaceous.md) event better than geographically restricted genera. Spatial spread can reduce exposure to some geographically heterogeneous stresses, although this does not guarantee survival of a global perturbation.[Abstract: lower extinction intensity in geographically widespread genera](../../../../../selectivity-of-end-cretaceous-marine-bivalve-extinctions-by-david-jablonski-and-others.md)
- **[Body size](../../../../../body-size.md):** large-bodied [mammals](../../../../../mammal.md) were disproportionately lost during the late [Quaternary](../../../../../quaternary.md). The global analysis documents size selectivity across continents and trophic groups; this is not evidence that large [body size](../../../../../body-size.md) always raises [extinction](../../../../../extinction.md) risk in every geological interval.[Abstract: size-selective losses over the studied late-Quaternary intervals](../../../../../body-size-downgrading-of-mammals-over-the-late-quaternary-by-felisa-smith-and-others.md)
- **Physiological buffering and respiratory capacity:** poor capacity to tolerate elevated [carbon dioxide](../../../../../carbon-dioxide.md) and associated [oxygen](../../../../../oxygen.md) stress has been linked to higher vulnerability among end-[Permian](../../../../../permian.md) marine groups. This is a paleophysiological interpretation informed by living relatives and [fossil](../../../../../fossil.md) traits, rather than a direct measurement of a [fossil](../../../../../fossil.md) animal’s tolerance.[Abstract and section Extinction in the oceans: hypercapnia vulnerability and physiological selectivity](../../../../../paleophysiology-and-end-permian-mass-extinction-by-andrew-h-knoll-and-others.md)
- **[Sexual dimorphism](../../../../../sexual-dimorphism.md) as a proxy for male reproductive investment:** greater male–female carapace differences in [fossil](../../../../../fossil.md) cytheroid [ostracods](../../../../../ostracod.md) were associated with higher estimated [extinction](../../../../../extinction.md) rates. The measured [fossil](../../../../../fossil.md) trait is dimorphism; interpreting it as investment requires the biological correspondence observed in living relatives.[Abstract: dimorphic fossil carapaces and association with estimated extinction rates](../../../../../high-male-sexual-investment-as-a-driver-of-extinction-in-fossil-ostracods-by-maria-joao-fernandes-martins-and-others.md)

These examples demonstrate event- and group-dependent selectivity. They do not define one universal vulnerability ranking: the [bivalve](../../../../../bivalve.md) study, for instance, did not find body-size selectivity at the same end-Cretaceous genus-level event.
