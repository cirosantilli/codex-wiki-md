<h1 id="1/b/v/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [V](../v.md)

**No field has six elements.** A [finite field](../../../../../finite-field.md) has positive [characteristic](../../../../../characteristic-of-a-field.md): the sequence $0,1,2\cdot1,\ldots$ must repeat. By part (a), this characteristic is a [prime number](../../../../../prime-number-split.md) $p$. Translation by $1$ partitions the field into cycles of length $p$, since returning to a starting point after $k$ steps is equivalent to $k1=0$. Thus $p$ divides six and $p=2$ or $p=3$.

The multiples of $1$ form a copy of the [prime field](../../../../../prime-field.md) $\mathbb F_p$. For completeness, the inverse of a nonzero residue $a$ lies among these multiples: the integer [Bezout identity](../../../../../bezout-s-identity.md) $ab+cp=1$ gives $(a1)(b1)=1$. The entire field is a [vector space](../../../../../vector-space-split.md) over this subfield. A maximal [linearly independent set](../../../../../linear-independence.md) is finite and spans, so it is a [basis](../../../../../basis.md) of some positive size $d$. Unique basis coordinates give exactly $p^d$ elements. This proves [finite field cardinality is a prime power](../../../../../finite-field-cardinality-is-a-prime-power.md) here. But six is neither a power of two nor a power of three, a contradiction.
