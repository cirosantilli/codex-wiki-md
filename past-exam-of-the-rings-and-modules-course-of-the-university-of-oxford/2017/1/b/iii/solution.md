↑ **Parent:** [Iii](../iii.md)

**No such ring exists:** every [finite integral domain is a field](../../../../../finite-integral-domain-is-a-field.md). Here is the proof. For $a\ne0$, the map $L_a:R\to R$ defined by $L_a(x)=ax$ is [injective](../../../../../injective-function.md), since $ax=ay$ gives $a(x-y)=0$ and therefore $x=y$. An [injective](../../../../../injective-function.md) self-map of a [finite set](../../../../../finite-set.md) is [surjective](../../../../../surjective-function.md), so some $x$ satisfies $ax=1$. Since an [integral domain](../../../../../integral-domain.md) is commutative, $xa=1$ as well. Thus every nonzero element has a [multiplicative inverse](../../../../../multiplicative-inverse.md), making $R$ a [field](../../../../../field.md).

The conclusion still holds if an identity was not initially required. Surjectivity first gives an element $e$ with $ae=a$. Then $a(ex-x)=0$ implies $ex=x$ for every $x$, and commutativity makes $e$ a two-sided identity. Applying the preceding argument with $e$ supplies all inverses.
