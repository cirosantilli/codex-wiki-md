## 1

↑ **Parent:** [2023](2023.md)

<h3 id="1/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1/a)

<h5 id="1/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#1/a/i)

Multiplication in a [direct product of rings](../commutative-algebra.md#direct-product-of-rings) is coordinatewise. Thus $(r_1,\ldots,r_n)$ has a two-sided inverse exactly when each $r_j$ has a two-sided inverse in $R_j$, and then

$$
(r_1,\ldots,r_n)^{-1}=(r_1^{-1},\ldots,r_n^{-1}).
$$

Consequently its [unit group](../algebra.md#unit-group) is the [direct product of groups](../group-theory.md#direct-product-of-groups)

$$
\boxed{U(R_1\times\cdots\times R_n)=U(R_1)\times\cdots\times U(R_n)}.
$$

The identification preserves multiplication because both sides use coordinatewise multiplication. This is the [unit group of a direct product of rings](../commutative-algebra.md#unit-group-of-a-direct-product-of-rings) formula.

<h4 id="1/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1/a)

<h5 id="1/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#1/a/ii)

Write $T=\prod_{j=1}^nR_j$, and let $\rho_j:T\to R_j$ be the coordinate [ring homomorphism](../commutative-algebra.md#ring-homomorphism). Set $I_j=\rho_j(I)$. Each $I_j$ is an [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal): it is an additive subgroup, and multiplying a lift in $I$ on either side by a tuple with prescribed $j$th coordinate proves closure under left and right multiplication by $R_j$.

Certainly $I\subseteq\prod_jI_j$. Conversely, let $a=(a_1,\ldots,a_n)\in\prod_jI_j$. For each $j$, choose $b^{(j)}\in I$ with $j$th coordinate $a_j$, and let $e_j$ have $1_{R_j}$ in coordinate $j$ and zero elsewhere. Since $I$ is an [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal), $e_jb^{(j)}\in I$, and

$$
a=\sum_{j=1}^ne_jb^{(j)}\in I.
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{I=I_1\times\cdots\times I_n}.
$$

The finite sum is essential to this proof of [ideals of a finite product of rings](../commutative-algebra.md#ideals-of-a-finite-product-of-rings).

<h4 id="1/a/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1/a)

<h5 id="1/a/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#1/a/iii)

The element $-1_R$ lies in the [unit group](../algebra.md#unit-group) and satisfies $(-1_R)^2=1_R$. Its [order of a group element](../group-theory.md#order-of-a-group-element) divides two and, by [Lagrange's theorem](../group-theory.md#lagrange-s-theorem), divides the odd number $|U(R)|$. Its order is therefore one, so $-1_R=1_R$ and $2\cdot1_R=0$.

It follows that

$$
\boxed{\mathbb F_2\longrightarrow R,\qquad\overline a\longmapsto a\cdot1_R}
$$

is a well-defined unital [ring homomorphism](../commutative-algebra.md#ring-homomorphism). For a nonzero [ring](../commutative-algebra.md#ring), this says its [characteristic of a ring](../commutative-algebra.md#characteristic-of-a-ring) is two. If the [zero ring](../commutative-algebra.md#zero-ring) is permitted, the same map still exists. This is the [odd unit group forces characteristic two](../commutative-algebra.md#odd-unit-group-forces-characteristic-two) argument, with that harmless exception to the characteristic wording.

<h3 id="1/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

<h5 id="1/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#1/b/i)

Since the [irreducible element](../commutative-algebra.md#irreducible-element) $x$ is not a [unit](../algebra.md#unit-in-a-ring), the [principal ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal) $(x)$ is proper. Suppose $(x)\subseteq J\subseteq R$. As $R$ is a [principal ideal domain](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal-domain), write $J=(d)$. Then $x=dr$ for some $r\in R$. The definition of an [irreducible element](../commutative-algebra.md#irreducible-element) says that $d$ or $r$ is a [unit](../algebra.md#unit-in-a-ring).

If $d$ is a [unit](../algebra.md#unit-in-a-ring), then $J=R$. If $r$ is a [unit](../algebra.md#unit-in-a-ring), then $d$ and $x$ are [associate elements](../commutative-algebra.md#associate-element), so $J=(x)$. Thus $(x)$ is a [maximal ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#maximal-ideal). By the permitted criterion that a [quotient ring](../commutative-algebra.md#quotient-ring) by a [maximal ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#maximal-ideal) is a [field](../algebra.md#field),

$$
\boxed{R/(x)\text{ is a field}}.
$$

<h4 id="1/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

<h5 id="1/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#1/b/ii)

Every multiple of $x_1\cdots x_r$ is a multiple of each $x_j$, giving one inclusion. For the reverse inclusion, a [principal ideal domain](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal-domain) is a [unique factorization domain](../algebra.md#unique-factorization-domain), so each [irreducible element](../commutative-algebra.md#irreducible-element) $x_j$ is a [prime element](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-element).

Let $a\in\bigcap_{j=1}^r(x_j)$. The case $a=0$ is immediate. Otherwise, write $a=x_1b$. Since $x_2\mid a$ and the [prime element](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-element) $x_2$ cannot divide the non-[associate](../commutative-algebra.md#associate-element) [irreducible element](../commutative-algebra.md#irreducible-element) $x_1$, it divides $b$. Write $a=x_1x_2b_2$, and repeat. At stage $j$, the [prime element](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-element) $x_j$ divides none of $x_1,\ldots,x_{j-1}$, so it divides the remaining factor. Hence $x_1\cdots x_r\mid a$, proving

$$
\boxed{(x_1\cdots x_r)=(x_1)\cap\cdots\cap(x_r)}.
$$

<h4 id="1/b/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

<h5 id="1/b/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#1/b/iii)

The [polynomial ring](../commutative-algebra.md#polynomial-ring) $\mathbb F_2[X]$ is a [euclidean domain](../commutative-algebra.md#euclidean-domain) under [polynomial degree](../polynomial.md#degree-of-a-polynomial), hence a [principal ideal domain](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal-domain). By part (b)(i), each

$$
K_j=\mathbb F_2[X]/(f_j)
$$

is a [field](../algebra.md#field). By [polynomial division](../polynomial.md#polynomial-division), every residue class has exactly one representative of [polynomial degree](../polynomial.md#degree-of-a-polynomial) less than $n_j$. Thus $1,\overline X,\ldots,\overline X^{n_j-1}$ form a [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) over $\mathbb F_2$, and $|K_j|=2^{n_j}$.

For $j\ne k$, the [ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) $(f_j)$ and $(f_k)$ are distinct [maximal ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#maximal-ideal), so they are [comaximal ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#comaximal-ideals): their sum is an [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) properly containing $(f_j)$ and must be the whole [ring](../commutative-algebra.md#ring). The [Chinese remainder theorem for ideals](../mathematics.md#chinese-remainder-theorem-for-ideals) and part (b)(ii) therefore give the [ring isomorphism](../algebra.md#ring-isomorphism)

$$
\boxed{\mathbb F_2[X]/(f_1\cdots f_r)\ \cong\ \prod_{j=1}^rK_j,\qquad\overline g\longmapsto(g+(f_j))_{j=1}^r}.
$$

In particular, this is a product of [finite fields](../algebra.md#finite-field) of the required sizes. The map preserves multiplication as well as addition and is surjective by the [Chinese remainder theorem for ideals](../mathematics.md#chinese-remainder-theorem-for-ideals).

<h3 id="1/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/c/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#1/c)

<h5 id="1/c/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#1/c/i)

Choose a generator $u$ of the [cyclic group](../group.md#cyclic-group) $U(R)$, so $u^p=1$. Part (a)(iii) gives a unital [ring homomorphism](../commutative-algebra.md#ring-homomorphism) $\mathbb F_2\to R$. Its image lies in the centre of $R$, so even if $R$ is not a [commutative ring](../commutative-algebra.md#commutative-ring), evaluating a [polynomial](../polynomial.md) at $u$ defines a [ring homomorphism](../commutative-algebra.md#ring-homomorphism)

$$
\phi:\mathbb F_2[X]\longrightarrow R,\qquad\sum_ja_jX^j\longmapsto\sum_ja_ju^j.
$$

Because $\phi(X^p-1)=0$, it factors through the [quotient ring](../commutative-algebra.md#quotient-ring) to give

$$
\boxed{\overline\phi:\mathbb F_2[X]/(X^p-1)\longrightarrow R,\qquad\overline X\longmapsto u}.
$$

Its image is the [subring](../commutative-algebra.md#subring) $S=\mathbb F_2[u]$. Every [unit](../algebra.md#unit-in-a-ring) of $S$ is a [unit](../algebra.md#unit-in-a-ring) of $R$, because an inverse in $S$ also lies in $R$. Conversely, every member of $U(R)$ is $u^j$ for some $j$, and both $u^j$ and its inverse $u^{p-j}$ lie in $S$. Hence

$$
\boxed{U(S)=U(R)}.
$$

The inverse argument matters: merely belonging to a [subring](../commutative-algebra.md#subring) would not by itself make an ambient [unit](../algebra.md#unit-in-a-ring) invertible there.

<h4 id="1/c/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#1/c)

<h5 id="1/c/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#1/c/ii)

Using the assumed [square-free polynomial](../polynomial.md#square-free-polynomial) property, factor

$$
X^p-1=f_1\cdots f_r
$$

into distinct [monic polynomials](../polynomial.md#monic-polynomial) that are [irreducible polynomials](../polynomial.md#irreducible-polynomial), with $n_j=\deg f_j$. Part (b)(iii) identifies $T=\mathbb F_2[X]/(X^p-1)$ with $\prod_jK_j$, where $|K_j|=2^{n_j}$.

By the [first isomorphism theorem for rings](../commutative-algebra.md#first-isomorphism-theorem-for-rings), the image $S$ from part (c)(i) is $T/J$, where $J$ is the [kernel of a ring homomorphism](../commutative-algebra.md#kernel-of-a-ring-homomorphism). By part (a)(ii), $J=\prod_jJ_j$ for [ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) $J_j\subseteq K_j$. A [field](../algebra.md#field) has only the zero [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) and the whole [field](../algebra.md#field) as [ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal), so each $K_j/J_j$ is either $K_j$ or the [zero ring](../commutative-algebra.md#zero-ring). Consequently, for a subset $D\subseteq\{1,\ldots,r\}$,

$$
S\cong\prod_{j\in D}K_j.
$$

This is the [quotient of a finite product of fields](../commutative-algebra.md#quotient-of-a-finite-product-of-fields) principle. The subset is nonempty because $|U(S)|=p>2$, whereas the [zero ring](../commutative-algebra.md#zero-ring) has only one [unit](../algebra.md#unit-in-a-ring). By part (a)(i),

$$
\boxed{p=|U(S)|=\prod_{j\in D}(2^{n_j}-1)}.
$$

Each factor is a positive [integer](../integer.md). Since $p$ is a [prime number](../prime-number.md), exactly one factor is $p$ and all remaining factors are one. Thus

$$
\boxed{p=2^n-1\text{ for some }n\geq2},
$$

so $p$ is a [Mersenne prime](../integer.md#mersenne-prime). If $n=ab$ with $a,b>1$, then $2^a-1$ would be a proper divisor of $2^{ab}-1$; thus $n$ is itself a [prime number](../prime-number.md), although only the displayed form is needed.

The assumed [square-free polynomial](../polynomial.md#square-free-polynomial) property can also be checked directly: in [characteristic of a ring](../commutative-algebra.md#characteristic-of-a-ring) two, the [formal derivative](../galois-theory.md#formal-derivative) of $X^p-1$ is $X^{p-1}$ because $p$ is odd. These two [polynomials](../polynomial.md) have [greatest common divisor](../divisibility.md#greatest-common-divisor) one, since $X$ does not divide $X^p-1$, so no repeated [irreducible polynomial](../polynomial.md#irreducible-polynomial) factor occurs.

↑ **Parent:** [2023](2023.md)

<h3 id="2/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#2/a)

<h5 id="2/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#2/a/i)

A subset $E\subseteq M$ is a [basis of a module](../module-theory.md#basis-of-a-module) if every $m\in M$ has a unique expression

$$
\boxed{m=\sum_{e\in E}r_ee,\qquad r_e\in R,\qquad r_e=0\text{ for all but finitely many }e}.
$$

Uniqueness concerns the entire family of coefficients, including the zero coefficients. Equivalently, $E$ generates $M$ and is [linearly independent in a module](../module-theory.md#linear-independence-in-a-module): every finite relation $\sum_{e\in H}r_ee=0$, with $H\subseteq E$, has all $r_e=0$.

A [module](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics) is a [free module](../module-theory.md#free-module) if it has a [basis of a module](../module-theory.md#basis-of-a-module). An infinite [basis of a module](../module-theory.md#basis-of-a-module) still uses only finite sums in each expression; arbitrary infinite sums are not part of the [module](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics) operations.

<h4 id="2/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#2/a)

<h5 id="2/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#2/a/ii)

**Yes.** An $\mathbb F$-[module](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics) over a [field](../algebra.md#field) is a [vector space](../vector-space.md). Start with a finite generating list. If it is [linearly dependent](../vector-space.md#linear-dependence), one vector has a nonzero coefficient in a [linear relation](../vector-space.md#linear-relation); dividing by that coefficient expresses the vector using the others, so it can be removed without changing the [span](../vector-space.md#linear-span). Repeating terminates with a [linearly independent](../vector-space.md#linear-independence) generating list, which is a [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) and therefore a [basis of a module](../module-theory.md#basis-of-a-module).

Thus every [finitely generated module](../module-theory.md#finitely-generated-module) over a [field](../algebra.md#field) is a [free module](../module-theory.md#free-module). For the zero [module](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics), the empty list is the required [basis of a module](../module-theory.md#basis-of-a-module).

<h4 id="2/a/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#2/a)

<h5 id="2/a/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#2/a/iii)

In the [direct sum of modules](../module-theory.md#direct-sum-of-modules) $M=N\oplus P$, every $m\in M$ has a unique expression $m=n+p$, with $n\in N$ and $p\in P$. Define

$$
\boxed{\pi:M\to M,\qquad\pi(n+p)=p}.
$$

The uniqueness makes this well-defined. For $m=n+p$, $m'=n'+p'$ and $r\in R$,

$$
\pi(m+m')=p+p'=\pi(m)+\pi(m'),\qquad\pi(rm)=rp=r\pi(m),
$$

so $\pi$ is a [module homomorphism](../module-theory.md#module-homomorphism). Finally, $\pi(n+p)=0$ exactly when $p=0$, equivalently $m\in N$. Therefore $\boxed{\ker\pi=N}$. Its image is $P$, and $\pi^2=\pi$, so it is the projection onto that [direct summand](../vector-space.md#direct-summand).

<h4 id="2/a/iv">iv</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#2/a)

<h5 id="2/a/iv/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#2/a/iv)

Put $N=\langle E'\rangle$ and $P=\langle E''\rangle$, the [submodules](../module-theory.md#submodule) generated by the two parts of the [basis of a module](../module-theory.md#basis-of-a-module). Every finite expression in the [basis of a module](../module-theory.md#basis-of-a-module) splits into its $E'$ terms and its $E''$ terms. Thus $M=N+P$.

If $m\in N\cap P$, there are finite expressions

$$
m=\sum_{e\in H'}a_ee=\sum_{e\in H''}b_ee,
\qquad H'\subseteq E',\quad H''\subseteq E''.
$$

Subtracting gives a finite relation among distinct members of $E$, because $E'\cap E''=\varnothing$. Their [linear independence in a module](../module-theory.md#linear-independence-in-a-module) forces every coefficient to vanish, so $m=0$. Hence

$$
\boxed{M=\langle E'\rangle\oplus\langle E''\rangle}.
$$

This proves the [basis partition gives a module direct sum](../module-theory.md#basis-partition-gives-a-module-direct-sum) lemma, also when one part is empty.

<h4 id="2/a/v">v</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#2/a)

<h5 id="2/a/v/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [V](#2/a/v)

Choose a countable generating family $(n_k)$ for $N$. For each $k$, the expression of $n_k$ in the [basis of a module](../module-theory.md#basis-of-a-module) $E$ has a finite set $H_k$ of nonzero coefficients, its [coordinate support in a free module](../module-theory.md#coordinate-support-in-a-free-module). Let

$$
E'=\bigcup_kH_k.
$$

By the [countable union of countable sets](../cardinal-number.md#countable-union-of-countable-sets) property, $E'$ is a [countable set](../cardinal-number.md#countable-set). Every $n_k$ belongs to $\langle E'\rangle$, so all finite $R$-linear combinations of the $n_k$ do too. Therefore

$$
\boxed{N\subseteq\langle E'\rangle\quad\text{for a countable }E'\subseteq E}.
$$

Here and below, [countable](../cardinal-number.md#countable-set) includes finite. The same construction applies to a finite or empty generating family. This is the [countable basis support lemma](../module-theory.md#countable-basis-support-lemma).

<h3 id="2/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

<h5 id="2/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#2/b/i)

For $j\in\mathbb N_0$, let $e_j\in B$ have $e_j(j)=1$ and $e_j(k)=0$ for $k\ne j$. If $f,g\in F$ and $z\in\mathbb Z$, then $f+g$ can be nonzero only where $f$ or $g$ is nonzero, and $zf$ can be nonzero only where $f$ is nonzero. These are finite sets. Since $0\in F$, this proves that $F$ is a [submodule](../module-theory.md#submodule).

Every $f\in F$ has the finite expression

$$
f=\sum_{j:\,f(j)\ne0}f(j)e_j.
$$

Evaluating at coordinate $k$ recovers the coefficient of $e_k$, so the expression is unique. Thus $\{e_j:j\in\mathbb N_0\}$ is a [basis of a module](../module-theory.md#basis-of-a-module) for $F$, and

$$
\boxed{F\cong\bigoplus_{j\in\mathbb N_0}\mathbb Z}
$$

is a [free module](../module-theory.md#free-module). By contrast, the ambient [module of integer sequences](../module-theory.md#module-of-integer-sequences) $B=\prod_{j\in\mathbb N_0}\mathbb Z$ allows arbitrary integer sequences.

<h4 id="2/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

<h5 id="2/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#2/b/ii)

Under the temporary assumption that $E$ is a [basis of a module](../module-theory.md#basis-of-a-module) for $B$, part (a)(v) applied to the countably generated [submodule](../module-theory.md#submodule) $F$ gives a [countable set](../cardinal-number.md#countable-set) $E'\subseteq E$ such that $F\subseteq C=\langle E'\rangle$. By part (a)(iv),

$$
B=C\oplus\langle E\setminus E'\rangle.
$$

Use part (a)(iii) to project onto the second [direct summand](../vector-space.md#direct-summand). This gives a [module homomorphism](../module-theory.md#module-homomorphism) $\pi:B\to B$ with

$$
\boxed{F\subseteq\ker\pi=C}.
$$

It remains to check the required size of this [kernel](../kernel-of-a-linear-map.md). Enumerate $E'$ as $b_1,b_2,\ldots$, with the finite case treated the same way. Every element of $C$ is a finite sum of these elements with [integer](../integer.md) coefficients. For each $m$, the set $\langle b_1,\ldots,b_m\rangle$ is the image of the [countable set](../cardinal-number.md#countable-set) $\mathbb Z^m$, so it is [countable](../cardinal-number.md#countable-set). Taking their [countable union](../set.md#countable-union) proves

$$
\boxed{\ker\pi\text{ is countable}}.
$$

This is the [countability of countably generated abelian groups](../group.md#countability-of-countably-generated-abelian-groups) argument; it uses the countability of the scalar [ring](../commutative-algebra.md#ring) $\mathbb Z$.

<h4 id="2/b/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

<h5 id="2/b/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#2/b/iii)

Consider

$$
W=\{w\in B:w(j)\in\{-2^j,2^j\}\text{ for every }j\in\mathbb N_0\}.
$$

The map sending a sequence of signs $(\varepsilon_j)$ to $(\varepsilon_j2^j)$ is a bijection from $\{-1,1\}^{\mathbb N_0}$ onto $W$. This is an [uncountable set](../cardinal-number.md#uncountable-set) by the [Cantor diagonal argument](../cardinal-number.md#cantor-s-diagonal-argument): given a proposed list $w_0,w_1,\ldots$ of its elements, define $w(j)=-w_j(j)$. Then $w\in W$ differs from $w_j$ at coordinate $j$ for every $j$.

Since $\ker\pi$ is [countable](../cardinal-number.md#countable-set), $W$ cannot be contained in it. Consequently there exists

$$
\boxed{w\in W\setminus\ker\pi,\qquad\pi(w)\ne0}.
$$

<h4 id="2/b/iv">iv</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

<h5 id="2/b/iv/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#2/b/iv)

For every $i\in\mathbb N_0$, truncate $w$ before coordinate $i$:

$$
f_i(j)=\begin{cases}w(j),&j<i,\\0,&j\geq i.\end{cases}
$$

This $f_i$ lies in $F$. For $j<i$, $(w-f_i)(j)=0$. For $j\geq i$, $(w-f_i)(j)=w(j)=\pm2^j$, which is divisible by $2^i$. In particular the integer sequence

$$
g_i(j)=\begin{cases}0,&j<i,\\w(j)/2^i,&j\geq i\end{cases}
$$

belongs to $B$ and satisfies $w=f_i+2^ig_i$.

Apply the [module homomorphism](../module-theory.md#module-homomorphism) $\pi$. Since $f_i\in F\subseteq\ker\pi$,

$$
\pi(w)=2^i\pi(g_i)\qquad\text{for every }i\in\mathbb N_0.
$$

Fixing any coordinate $j$, the [integer](../integer.md) $\pi(w)(j)$ is therefore divisible by $2^i$ for every $i$. The only such [integer](../integer.md) is zero: if $a\ne0$, choose $i$ with $2^i>|a|$, which rules out $a=2^ib$ for any nonzero [integer](../integer.md) $b$. Thus every coordinate of $\pi(w)$ vanishes, contradicting part (b)(iii).

It follows that

$$
\boxed{B=\prod_{j\in\mathbb N_0}\mathbb Z\text{ is not a free }\mathbb Z\text{-module}}.
$$

The decisive property is [vanishing of infinitely divisible integer sequences](../module-theory.md#vanishing-of-infinitely-divisible-integer-sequences), namely $\bigcap_{i\geq0}2^iB=\{0\}$.

## 3

↑ **Parent:** [2023](2023.md)

<h3 id="3/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

<h5 id="3/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#3/a/i)

Since $A^2+I=0$, its [minimal polynomial](../linear-operator-theory.md#minimal-polynomial) $m_A$ divides $X^2+1$. The [polynomial](../polynomial.md) $X^2+1$ is an [irreducible polynomial](../polynomial.md#irreducible-polynomial) over the [rational numbers](../number-theory.md#rational-number): a reducible quadratic would have a rational root, whereas $r^2+1>0$ for every $r\in\mathbb Q$.

Because $n>0$, the constant [polynomial](../polynomial.md) one evaluates to the nonzero [identity matrix](../vector-space.md#identity-matrix) $I$, so $m_A\ne1$. Both $m_A$ and $X^2+1$ are [monic polynomials](../polynomial.md#monic-polynomial); hence the only remaining divisor is

$$
\boxed{m_A(X)=X^2+1}.
$$

<h4 id="3/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

<h5 id="3/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#3/a/ii)

The nonconstant [invariant factors of a linear operator](../linear-operator-theory.md#invariant-factors-of-a-linear-operator) satisfy $d_1\mid\cdots\mid d_k=m_A$. By part (a)(i), $m_A=X^2+1$ is an [irreducible polynomial](../polynomial.md#irreducible-polynomial), so every $d_j$ must equal $X^2+1$. Their [polynomial degrees](../polynomial.md#degree-of-a-polynomial) sum to $n$, giving $n=2k$.

The [companion matrix](../linear-operator-theory.md#companion-matrix) of $X^2+1$, in the [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) $(1,X)$ of $\mathbb Q[X]/(X^2+1)$, is

$$
C=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Therefore, for each fixed even $n$, every such $A$ has [rational canonical form](../linear-operator-theory.md#rational-canonical-form)

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{diag}(\underbrace{C,\ldots,C}_{n/2\text{ blocks}})}.
$$

There are no such [matrices](../vector-space.md#matrix) when $n$ is odd. This is an instance of the [rational canonical form with irreducible minimal polynomial](../linear-operator-theory.md#rational-canonical-form-with-irreducible-minimal-polynomial) principle; equality of [minimal polynomials](../linear-operator-theory.md#minimal-polynomial) alone does not determine [rational canonical form](../linear-operator-theory.md#rational-canonical-form) in general.

<h4 id="3/a/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

<h5 id="3/a/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#3/a/iii)

Let $v\in\mathbb Q^2$ be nonzero. If the columns $v,Av$ were [linearly dependent](../vector-space.md#linear-dependence) over the [rational numbers](../number-theory.md#rational-number), then $Av=\lambda v$ for some $\lambda\in\mathbb Q$. Applying $A$ once more would give

$$
-v=A^2v=\lambda Av=\lambda^2v,
$$

so $(\lambda^2+1)v=0$. Since $v\ne0$, this forces $\lambda^2=-1$, impossible in $\mathbb Q$. Thus the two columns are [linearly independent](../vector-space.md#linear-independence), and the square [matrix](../vector-space.md#matrix)

$$
\boxed{E=\begin{pmatrix}|&|\\v&Av\\|&|\end{pmatrix}}
$$

is an [invertible matrix](../linear-algebra.md#invertible-matrix).

<h4 id="3/a/iv">iv</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

<h5 id="3/a/iv/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#3/a/iv)

In the [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) $(v,Av)$, the [linear map](../vector-space.md#linear-map) given by $A$ sends $v$ to the second [basis](../vector-space.md#basis) vector and sends $Av$ to $A^2v=-v$. Equivalently,

$$
AE=(Av,-v)=E\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Multiplying on the left by the [matrix inverse](../matrix-inverse.md) $E^{-1}$ gives

$$
\boxed{E^{-1}AE=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}}.
$$

This [change of basis](../linear-algebra.md#change-of-basis) produces the [companion matrix](../linear-operator-theory.md#companion-matrix) found in part (a)(ii).

<h3 id="3/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

<h5 id="3/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#3/b/i)

The [Gaussian integers](../commutative-algebra.md#gaussian-integer) form an [integral domain](../commutative-algebra.md#integral-domain), since they are a [subring](../commutative-algebra.md#subring) of the [complex numbers](../complex-analysis.md#complex-number). Use the [Gaussian integer norm](../commutative-algebra.md#gaussian-integer-norm)

$$
N(a+bi)=a^2+b^2=|a+bi|^2,
$$

which is a positive [integer](../integer.md) for nonzero $a+bi$ and satisfies $N(\alpha\beta)=N(\alpha)N(\beta)$.

For $\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb Z[i]$ with $\beta\ne0$, write $\alpha/\beta=x+iy$. Choose [integers](../integer.md) $m,n$ with $|x-m|\leq1/2$ and $|y-n|\leq1/2$, put $q=m+in$, and set $r=\alpha-\beta q$. Then

$$
\alpha=\beta q+r,\qquad
N(r)=N(\beta)\big((x-m)^2+(y-n)^2\big)\leq\tfrac12N(\beta)<N(\beta).
$$

Thus division with remainder has either $r=0$ or a strictly smaller [Gaussian integer norm](../commutative-algebra.md#gaussian-integer-norm). This proves that $N$ is a [euclidean function](../commutative-algebra.md#euclidean-function) and

$$
\boxed{\mathbb Z[i]\text{ is a Euclidean domain}}.
$$

<h4 id="3/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

<h5 id="3/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#3/b/ii)

Over a [principal ideal domain](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal-domain) $D$, an $m$-by-$n$ [matrix](../vector-space.md#matrix) is in [Smith normal form](../algebra.md#smith-normal-form) if all entries off the main diagonal vanish and its nonzero diagonal entries $d_1,\ldots,d_r$ occur first and satisfy

$$
\boxed{d_1\mid d_2\mid\cdots\mid d_r}.
$$

All remaining diagonal entries are zero. The zero [matrix](../vector-space.md#matrix) is allowed, with $r=0$. For an arbitrary [matrix](../vector-space.md#matrix) $M$, the [Smith normal form](../algebra.md#smith-normal-form) theorem gives [invertible matrices](../linear-algebra.md#invertible-matrix) $P\in\operatorname{GL}_m(D)$ and $Q\in\operatorname{GL}_n(D)$ with

$$
PMQ=\operatorname{diag}(d_1,\ldots,d_r,0,\ldots,0),
$$

where the right side is interpreted as a rectangular diagonal [matrix](../vector-space.md#matrix) when necessary. The $d_j$ are determined up to multiplication by [units](../algebra.md#unit-in-a-ring). Positivity can be required over $\mathbb Z$, but is not a condition over the [Gaussian integers](../commutative-algebra.md#gaussian-integer).

<h4 id="3/b/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

<h5 id="3/b/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#3/b/iii)

Regard $A$ as a [matrix](../vector-space.md#matrix) over the [rational numbers](../number-theory.md#rational-number). Part (a)(ii), with $n=2$, gives its [characteristic polynomial](../linear-operator-theory.md#characteristic-polynomial) $X^2+1$. Therefore, writing $i$ for the [imaginary unit](../complex-analysis.md#imaginary-unit),

$$
\det(iI-A)=i^2+1=0.
$$

The [matrix](../vector-space.md#matrix) $M=iI-A$ is not zero: otherwise $A=iI$ would have noninteger diagonal entries. It consequently has [rank of a matrix](../vector-space.md#matrix-rank) one over the [fraction field](../commutative-algebra.md#field-of-fractions) $\mathbb Q(i)$ of the [Gaussian integers](../commutative-algebra.md#gaussian-integer).

By part (b)(i), $\mathbb Z[i]$ is a [euclidean domain](../commutative-algebra.md#euclidean-domain), hence a [principal ideal domain](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal-domain), so apply [Smith normal form](../algebra.md#smith-normal-form). There exist $P,Q\in\operatorname{GL}_2(\mathbb Z[i])$ such that

$$
P(iI-A)Q=\begin{pmatrix}d&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad d\ne0.
$$

Indeed, invertible multiplication preserves [rank of a matrix](../vector-space.md#matrix-rank), so exactly one diagonal entry is nonzero. For $e_2=(0,1)^{\mathsf T}$, multiplication on the right gives $P(iI-A)Qe_2=0$. Since $P$ is an [invertible matrix](../linear-algebra.md#invertible-matrix),

$$
\boxed{(iI-A)Qe_2=0}.
$$

The [Smith normal form supplies a kernel basis](../algebra.md#smith-normal-form-supplies-a-kernel-basis) lemma ensures that the null vector $Qe_2$ is a column of an [invertible matrix](../linear-algebra.md#invertible-matrix) over $\mathbb Z[i]$; an arbitrary nonzero solution over $\mathbb Q(i)$ would not itself establish that requirement.

<h4 id="3/b/iv">iv</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

<h5 id="3/b/iv/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#3/b/iv)

Each entry of the [matrix](../vector-space.md#matrix) $Q$ from part (b)(iii) has unique real and imaginary [integer](../integer.md) coefficients. Write

$$
Q=U+iV,\qquad U,V\in M_2(\mathbb Z).
$$

It already belongs to the [general linear group](../group-theory.md#general-linear-group) $\operatorname{GL}_2(\mathbb Z[i])$. Expanding the relation from part (b)(iii) gives

$$
0=(iI-A)(U+iV)e_2=(-AU-V)e_2+i(U-AV)e_2.
$$

The real and imaginary parts must both vanish, so

$$
\boxed{AUe_2=-Ve_2,\qquad AVe_2=Ue_2,\qquad U+iV\in\operatorname{GL}_2(\mathbb Z[i])}.
$$

These are the required identities, with $e_2=(0,1)^{\mathsf T}$.
