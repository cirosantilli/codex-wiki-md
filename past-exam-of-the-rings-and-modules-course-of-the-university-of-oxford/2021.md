<h4 id="1/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1/a)

<h5 id="1/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#1/a/i)

We prove the stated [Eisenstein criterion](../commutative-algebra.md#eisenstein-criterion) directly. Assume $n\geq1$. Suppose $f=gh$ in the [polynomial ring](../commutative-algebra.md#polynomial-ring) $R[X]$. Since the [principal ideal domain](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal-domain) $R$ is an [integral domain](../commutative-algebra.md#integral-domain), [polynomial degree](../polynomial.md#degree-of-a-polynomial) is additive under [polynomial multiplication](../polynomial.md#polynomial-multiplication), and the product of the [leading coefficients](../polynomial.md#leading-coefficient-of-a-polynomial) of $g,h$ is the [leading coefficient](../polynomial.md#leading-coefficient-of-a-polynomial) $1$ of $f$. These two [coefficients](../vector-space.md#coefficient) are therefore [units](../algebra.md#unit-in-a-ring). Multiplying $g$ and $h$ by inverse [units](../algebra.md#unit-in-a-ring), without changing their product, makes both factors [monic polynomials](../polynomial.md#monic-polynomial).

If either factor is a [constant polynomial](../polynomial.md#constant-polynomial), that factor is now $1$, so the original factor was a [unit](../algebra.md#unit-in-a-ring). It remains to rule out $r=\deg g>0$ and $s=\deg h>0$.

The [prime element](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-element) $p$ generates a [maximal ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#maximal-ideal) in the [principal ideal domain](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal-domain) $R$. Indeed, if $(p)\subseteq(d)$, then $p=de$, and the [irreducible element](../commutative-algebra.md#irreducible-element) property of $p$ forces either $(d)=R$ or $(d)=(p)$. Consequently $k=R/(p)$ is a [field](../algebra.md#field). Apply [reduction modulo an ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#reduction-modulo-an-ideal) to every [coefficient](../vector-space.md#coefficient). The [monic polynomial](../polynomial.md#monic-polynomial) factors retain their [polynomial degrees](../polynomial.md#degree-of-a-polynomial), and the hypotheses give

$$
\overline g\,\overline h=\overline f=X^n\quad\text{in }k[X].
$$

The [polynomial ring](../commutative-algebra.md#polynomial-ring) over a [field](../algebra.md#field) is a [unique factorization domain](../algebra.md#unique-factorization-domain), and $X$ is an [irreducible polynomial](../polynomial.md#irreducible-polynomial). Thus its only [monic polynomial](../polynomial.md#monic-polynomial) divisors in $X^n$ are powers of $X$. Hence $\overline g=X^r$ and $\overline h=X^s$. Both have zero [constant term](../polynomial.md#constant-term), so $p\mid g(0)$ and $p\mid h(0)$. This gives $p^2\mid g(0)h(0)=a_0$, a contradiction.

The [monic polynomial](../polynomial.md#monic-polynomial) $f$ has positive [polynomial degree](../polynomial.md#degree-of-a-polynomial), so it is a nonzero [nonunit](../algebra.md#nonunit). Every factorization therefore has a [unit](../algebra.md#unit-in-a-ring) factor, proving

$$
\boxed{f\text{ is irreducible in }R[X]}.
$$

<h4 id="1/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1/a)

<h5 id="1/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#1/a/ii)

Regard $\mathbb R[X,Y]$ as the [polynomial ring](../commutative-algebra.md#polynomial-ring) $(\mathbb R[X])[Y]$. The [coefficient](../vector-space.md#coefficient) [ring](../commutative-algebra.md#ring) $\mathbb R[X]$ is a [Euclidean domain](../commutative-algebra.md#euclidean-domain), hence a [principal ideal domain](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal-domain). The element $X$ is a [prime element](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-element): the [quotient ring](../commutative-algebra.md#quotient-ring) $\mathbb R[X]/(X)\cong\mathbb R$ is an [integral domain](../commutative-algebra.md#integral-domain).

The [monic polynomial](../polynomial.md#monic-polynomial) $Y^2+X$ has its other [coefficients](../vector-space.md#coefficient) divisible by $X$, while $X^2\nmid X$. Part (a)(i), with $X$ playing the role of $p$ and $Y$ the [indeterminate](../polynomial.md#indeterminate), proves that $Y^2+X$ is an [irreducible polynomial](../polynomial.md#irreducible-polynomial).

Now use the [automorphism of a polynomial ring](../commutative-algebra.md#automorphism-of-a-polynomial-ring)

$$
\tau:\mathbb R[X,Y]\longrightarrow\mathbb R[X,Y],\qquad
\tau(X)=X,\quad\tau(Y)=Y+1.
$$

Its inverse fixes $X$ and sends $Y$ to $Y-1$. A [ring isomorphism](../algebra.md#ring-isomorphism) preserves [units](../algebra.md#unit-in-a-ring) and factorizations, hence preserves [irreducible elements](../commutative-algebra.md#irreducible-element). Since

$$
\tau(Y^2+X)=(Y+1)^2+X=Y^2+2Y+1+X,
$$

we obtain

$$
\boxed{Y^2+X\text{ and }Y^2+2Y+1+X\text{ are irreducible in }\mathbb R[X,Y]}.
$$

Applying the [Eisenstein criterion](../commutative-algebra.md#eisenstein-criterion) directly to the second expression with the [prime element](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-element) $X$ would fail, since $X\nmid2$; the change of [indeterminate](../polynomial.md#indeterminate) supplies the required argument.

<h3 id="1/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

<h5 id="1/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#1/b/i)

All [principal ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal) in this part are taken inside the [subring](../commutative-algebra.md#subring) $T$, rather than inside $\mathbb R[X]$. First, $T$ is a [subring](../commutative-algebra.md#subring): sums and [additive inverses](../arithmetic.md#additive-inverse) retain zero [coefficient](../vector-space.md#coefficient) of $X$, and the [coefficient](../vector-space.md#coefficient) of $X$ in a product $uv$ is $u(0)[X]v+v(0)[X]u=0$. It contains $1$.

The [principal ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal) $I=(X^3)_T=X^3T$ is precisely

$$
I=\left\{a_3X^3+\sum_{j\geq5}a_jX^j:\text{only finitely many }a_j\ne0\right\}.
$$

Indeed, multiplying an element of $T$ by $X^3$ gives a possible term of [polynomial degree](../polynomial.md#degree-of-a-polynomial) $3$ and terms of [polynomial degrees](../polynomial.md#degree-of-a-polynomial) at least $5$, with no $X^4$ term. Conversely, dividing any displayed [polynomial](../polynomial.md) by $X^3$ gives a [polynomial](../polynomial.md) with no $X$ term, hence an element of $T$.

Given $p\in T$, let $a,b,c$ be its [coefficients](../vector-space.md#coefficient) of $1,X^2,X^4$, respectively. Subtracting these terms leaves an element of $I$, so

$$
\boxed{p+I=a+bX^2+cX^4+I}.
$$

For uniqueness, if two such representatives differ by an element of $I$, their difference has possible [monomials](../polynomial.md#monomial) only $1,X^2,X^4$, whereas every element of $I$ has zero [coefficients](../vector-space.md#coefficient) at all three. Comparing [coefficients](../vector-space.md#coefficient) gives $a=a'$, $b=b'$, $c=c'$.

In particular, $X^4\notin I$. Treating $I$ as the [principal ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal) of $\mathbb R[X]$ instead would incorrectly discard this surviving term.

<h4 id="1/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

<h5 id="1/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#1/b/ii)

Set $I=(X^3)_T$ and $J=(Y^3)_{\mathbb R[Y]}$. Part (b)(i) gives a unique representative $a+bX^2+cX^4$ for each element of the [quotient ring](../commutative-algebra.md#quotient-ring) $T/I$. By [polynomial division](../polynomial.md#polynomial-division), each element of the [quotient ring](../commutative-algebra.md#quotient-ring) $\mathbb R[Y]/J$ likewise has a unique representative $a+bY+cY^2$. Thus the proposed map $\Phi$ is well-defined and a [bijection](../function.md#bijection).

It preserves [addition](../arithmetic.md#addition) and $1$. To verify [multiplication](../arithmetic.md#multiplication), all terms of [polynomial degree](../polynomial.md#degree-of-a-polynomial) at least $5$ lie in $I$. Hence

$$
\begin{aligned}
&(a+bX^2+cX^4)(a'+b'X^2+c'X^4)+I\\
&\qquad=aa'+(ab'+ba')X^2+(ac'+bb'+ca')X^4+I.
\end{aligned}
$$

In the other [quotient ring](../commutative-algebra.md#quotient-ring), $Y^3=0$, giving exactly the same [coefficient](../vector-space.md#coefficient) formula:

$$
(a+bY+cY^2)(a'+b'Y+c'Y^2)+J
=aa'+(ab'+ba')Y+(ac'+bb'+ca')Y^2+J.
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{\Phi:T/(X^3)_T\xrightarrow{\ \cong\ }\mathbb R[Y]/(Y^3),\qquad
a+bX^2+cX^4+I\longmapsto a+bY+cY^2+J}
$$

is a [ring isomorphism](../algebra.md#ring-isomorphism). Both sides are examples of a [truncated polynomial ring](../commutative-algebra.md#truncated-polynomial-ring); the generator on the left is the class of $X^2$, whose square is nonzero and whose cube vanishes.

<h4 id="1/b/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

<h5 id="1/b/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#1/b/iii)

**No such property exists.** The [ring isomorphism](../algebra.md#ring-isomorphism) in part (b)(ii) identifies [quotient rings](../commutative-algebra.md#quotient-ring) by elements having different [irreducible element](../commutative-algebra.md#irreducible-element) behaviour.

The element $X^3$ is an [irreducible element](../commutative-algebra.md#irreducible-element) of $T$. It is nonzero and a [nonunit](../algebra.md#nonunit). Every nonzero [nonunit](../algebra.md#nonunit) of $T$ has [polynomial degree](../polynomial.md#degree-of-a-polynomial) at least $2$: the only possible [polynomials](../polynomial.md) of smaller [polynomial degree](../polynomial.md#degree-of-a-polynomial) are [constant polynomials](../polynomial.md#constant-polynomial), and every nonzero [constant polynomial](../polynomial.md#constant-polynomial) is a [unit](../algebra.md#unit-in-a-ring). If $X^3=uv$ with $u,v\in T$ both [nonunits](../algebra.md#nonunit), additivity of [polynomial degree](../polynomial.md#degree-of-a-polynomial) in the [integral domain](../commutative-algebra.md#integral-domain) $\mathbb R[X]$ would give

$$
3=\deg u+\deg v\geq2+2=4,
$$

which is impossible. Thus one factor must be a [unit](../algebra.md#unit-in-a-ring).

In $\mathbb R[Y]$, however, $Y^3=Y\cdot Y^2$ is a product of two [nonunits](../algebra.md#nonunit), so $Y^3$ is not an [irreducible element](../commutative-algebra.md#irreducible-element). If a [ring isomorphism invariant](../commutative-algebra.md#ring-isomorphism-invariant) $P$ characterized [irreducible elements](../commutative-algebra.md#irreducible-element) by the indicated [quotient ring](../commutative-algebra.md#quotient-ring), the [irreducible element](../commutative-algebra.md#irreducible-element) $X^3\in T$ would imply that $T/(X^3)_T$ has $P$. Its isomorphic [quotient ring](../commutative-algebra.md#quotient-ring) $\mathbb R[Y]/(Y^3)$ would then have $P$, incorrectly making $Y^3\in\mathbb R[Y]$ an [irreducible element](../commutative-algebra.md#irreducible-element).

Consequently,

$$
\boxed{\text{irreducibility cannot be determined from the isomorphism type of }R/(x)}.
$$

<h4 id="1/b/iv">iv</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

<h5 id="1/b/iv/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iv](#1/b/iv)

The required property is

$$
\boxed{P:\quad\text{being an integral domain}}.
$$

For a nonzero element $x$ of an [integral domain](../commutative-algebra.md#integral-domain) $R$, this follows from

$$
\begin{aligned}
x\text{ is a prime element}
&\iff (x)\text{ is a prime ideal}\\
&\iff R/(x)\text{ is an integral domain}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Indeed, $\overline a\,\overline b=0$ in the [quotient ring](../commutative-algebra.md#quotient-ring) exactly when $x\mid ab$. The [prime element](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-element) condition says this forces $x\mid a$ or $x\mid b$, exactly the absence of [zero divisors](../mathematics.md#zero-divisor) in the [quotient ring](../commutative-algebra.md#quotient-ring). The [nonunit](../algebra.md#nonunit) condition makes the [quotient ring](../commutative-algebra.md#quotient-ring) nonzero. Being an [integral domain](../commutative-algebra.md#integral-domain) is preserved by every [ring isomorphism](../algebra.md#ring-isomorphism).

With the usual definition of a [prime element](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-element), $x\ne0$ is understood. The [prime ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal) formulation works for every [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) of any [commutative ring](../commutative-algebra.md#commutative-ring), including the zero [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal); it avoids this convention about elements.

<h5 id="2/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#2/a/i)

Choose a finite [generating set of a module](../module-theory.md#generating-set-of-a-module) $n_1,\ldots,n_t$ for the [finitely generated module](../module-theory.md#finitely-generated-module) $N$. For any $m\in M$, the [surjectivity](../algebra.md#surjective-function) of the [module homomorphism](../module-theory.md#module-homomorphism) $\phi$ gives $n\in N$ with $\phi(n)=m$. Write $n=\sum_{j=1}^t r_jn_j$. Since $\phi$ is a [module homomorphism](../module-theory.md#module-homomorphism),

$$
m=\phi\left(\sum_{j=1}^t r_jn_j\right)=\sum_{j=1}^t r_j\phi(n_j).
$$

Thus

$$
\boxed{M=R\phi(n_1)+\cdots+R\phi(n_t)}
$$

is a [finitely generated module](../module-theory.md#finitely-generated-module). The argument includes $N=0$, in which case [surjectivity](../algebra.md#surjective-function) forces $M=0$ and the empty [generating set of a module](../module-theory.md#generating-set-of-a-module) suffices.

<h4 id="2/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#2/a)

<h5 id="2/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#2/a/ii)

Let $n_1,\ldots,n_t$ generate the [submodule](../module-theory.md#submodule) $N$, and let $m_1+N,\ldots,m_s+N$ generate the [quotient module](../module-theory.md#quotient-module) $M/N$. Choose each $m_i\in M$ as a representative of its class. For any $m\in M$, there are $r_1,\ldots,r_s\in R$ such that

$$
m+N=\sum_{i=1}^s r_i(m_i+N).
$$

Therefore $m-\sum_i r_im_i\in N$. Expressing that difference using the [generating set of a module](../module-theory.md#generating-set-of-a-module) $n_1,\ldots,n_t$ gives

$$
m=\sum_{i=1}^s r_im_i+\sum_{j=1}^t u_jn_j.
$$

Hence

$$
\boxed{M=Rm_1+\cdots+Rm_s+Rn_1+\cdots+Rn_t}
$$

is a [finitely generated module](../module-theory.md#finitely-generated-module). This proves that [finite generation is preserved by module extensions](../module-theory.md#finite-generation-is-preserved-by-module-extensions).

<h3 id="2/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

Let $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ denote the standard [basis of a module](../module-theory.md#basis-of-a-module) of the [free module](../module-theory.md#free-module) $R^n$. Since the [module homomorphism](../module-theory.md#module-homomorphism) $\phi:R^m\to M$ is [surjective](../algebra.md#surjective-function), choose $v_j\in R^m$ such that $\phi(v_j)=\psi(e_j)$ for each $j$. Define

$$
\boxed{q:R^n\longrightarrow R^m,\qquad q\left(\sum_{j=1}^n r_je_j\right)=\sum_{j=1}^n r_jv_j}.
$$

Uniqueness of coordinates in a [basis of a module](../module-theory.md#basis-of-a-module) makes this well-defined, and the formula preserves [addition](../arithmetic.md#addition) and [scalar multiplication](../vector-space.md#scalar-multiplication), so $q$ is a [module homomorphism](../module-theory.md#module-homomorphism). For every $x=\sum_jr_je_j$,

$$
\phi(q(x))=\sum_jr_j\phi(v_j)=\sum_jr_j\psi(e_j)=\psi(x).
$$

Thus $\boxed{\phi\circ q=\psi}$. This is the [lifting property of a free module](../module-theory.md#lifting-property-of-a-free-module); only the [surjectivity](../algebra.md#surjective-function) of $\phi$ is needed for this part.

<h3 id="2/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#2/c)

Put $K=\ker\phi$, $H=\ker\psi$, and $Q=\operatorname{Im}q$. The [kernel of a module homomorphism](../module-theory.md#kernel-of-a-module-homomorphism) $H$ is a [submodule](../module-theory.md#submodule), so its image $L=q(H)$ is a [submodule](../module-theory.md#submodule). For $x\in H$, $\phi(q(x))=\psi(x)=0$, showing $L\subseteq K$. Thus the [quotient module](../module-theory.md#quotient-module) $K/L$ exists. Also $L\subseteq Q$.

Consider the restriction to $K$ of the natural [module homomorphism](../module-theory.md#module-homomorphism) $R^m\to R^m/Q$:

$$
\alpha:K\longrightarrow R^m/Q,\qquad y\longmapsto y+Q.
$$

Its [kernel of a module homomorphism](../module-theory.md#kernel-of-a-module-homomorphism) is $K\cap Q$. We have $L\subseteq K\cap Q$. Conversely, if $y\in K\cap Q$, choose $x\in R^n$ with $y=q(x)$. Then

$$
\psi(x)=\phi(q(x))=\phi(y)=0,
$$

so $x\in H$ and $y\in L$. Hence $\boxed{K\cap Q=L}$.

The map $\alpha$ is [surjective](../algebra.md#surjective-function). Given $v+Q\in R^m/Q$, use the [surjectivity](../algebra.md#surjective-function) of $\psi$ to choose $x\in R^n$ with $\psi(x)=\phi(v)$. Set $y=v-q(x)$. Then

$$
\phi(y)=\phi(v)-\psi(x)=0,\qquad y+Q=v+Q.
$$

Thus $y\in K$ is a preimage of the arbitrary class $v+Q$.

By the [first isomorphism theorem for modules](../module-theory.md#first-isomorphism-theorem-for-modules), $\alpha$ induces the [module isomorphism](../module-theory.md#module-isomorphism)

$$
\boxed{\overline\alpha:\ker\phi/L\xrightarrow{\ \cong\ }R^m/\operatorname{Im}q,\qquad
y+L\longmapsto y+\operatorname{Im}q}.
$$

Explicitly, replacing $y$ by $y+\ell$ with $\ell\in L$ leaves its class modulo $Q$ unchanged, proving well-definedness. Its formula preserves [addition](../arithmetic.md#addition) and [scalar multiplication](../vector-space.md#scalar-multiplication). If its value is zero, $y\in K\cap Q=L$, so $y+L=0$, proving [injectivity](../algebra.md#injective-function); [surjectivity](../algebra.md#surjective-function) was proved above. These checks give all the requested properties of the induced map.

<h3 id="2/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#2/d)

Assume $H=\ker\psi$ is a [finitely generated module](../module-theory.md#finitely-generated-module). The restriction $q:H\to L$ is a [surjective](../algebra.md#surjective-function) [module homomorphism](../module-theory.md#module-homomorphism), so part (a)(i) makes $L$ a [finitely generated module](../module-theory.md#finitely-generated-module).

The [free module](../module-theory.md#free-module) $R^m$ has its finite standard [basis of a module](../module-theory.md#basis-of-a-module). Its [quotient module](../module-theory.md#quotient-module) $R^m/\operatorname{Im}q$ is therefore a [finitely generated module](../module-theory.md#finitely-generated-module) by part (a)(i). Part (c) transfers this property to the isomorphic [quotient module](../module-theory.md#quotient-module) $\ker\phi/L$. Finally apply part (a)(ii) to the [submodule](../module-theory.md#submodule) $L\subseteq\ker\phi$. It follows that

$$
\boxed{\ker\psi\text{ finitely generated}\ \Longrightarrow\ \ker\phi\text{ finitely generated}}.
$$

More concretely, take the images under $q$ of finitely many generators of $H$, and adjoin lifts in $\ker\phi$ of finitely many generators of $\ker\phi/L$. This finite list generates $\ker\phi$. The argument shows why [finite presentation of a module](../module-theory.md#finite-presentation-of-a-module) is independent of the chosen finite [generating set of a module](../module-theory.md#generating-set-of-a-module).

## 3

↑ **Parent:** [2021](2021.md)

<h3 id="3/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

**The exact orders printed in the question are inconsistent.** The source PDF says that the [orders of group elements](../group-theory.md#order-of-a-group-element) $a,b,c$ are $4,12,24$, respectively, and also imposes $3a+2b=0$. Multiplying this relation by $6$ and using $12b=0$ gives $18a=0$. Since $4a=0$ also holds, subtracting $4(4a)=0$ gives $2a=0$, contradicting the stated [order of a group element](../group-theory.md#order-of-a-group-element) $4$. Thus

$$
\boxed{\text{no group satisfies all the hypotheses literally as printed}}.
$$

The natural [abelian group presentation](../group.md#abelian-group-presentation) interpretation is to impose the relations $4a=12b=24c=0$ and $3a+2b=0$, with no further relations, without requiring the orders of the images of the generators to stay $4,12,24$. Under this interpretation the calculation is as follows. Since $3a=-a$ follows from $4a=0$, the mixed relation gives $a=2b$. Consequently $8b=0$, and together with $12b=0$ this gives $4b=0$. Conversely, $a=2b$, $4b=0$, and $24c=0$ imply every original relation. Eliminating $a$ leaves two independent [cyclic groups](../group.md#cyclic-group):

$$
\boxed{G_{\mathrm{pres}}\cong\mathbb Z_4\oplus\mathbb Z_{24},\qquad n=2,\quad d_1=4,\quad d_2=24}.
$$

To verify that these relations force no further identifications, map $a,b,c$ to $(2,0),(1,0),(0,1)$ in $\mathbb Z_4\oplus\mathbb Z_{24}$. All defining relations hold, and the inverse [group homomorphism](../group-theory.md#group-homomorphism) sends the two standard generators to $b,c$. These [group homomorphisms](../group-theory.md#group-homomorphism) are mutually inverse. The [divisibility](../divisibility.md) condition $4\mid24$ gives the requested [invariant factors of a finitely generated module](../module-theory.md#invariant-factor-of-a-finitely-generated-module) over $\mathbb Z$. In this presented [group](../group.md), $a,b,c$ actually have orders $2,4,24$.

Equivalently, the relation [matrix](../vector-space.md#matrix)

$$
\begin{pmatrix}4&0&0\\0&12&0\\0&0&24\\3&2&0\end{pmatrix}
$$

has nonzero [Smith normal form](../algebra.md#smith-normal-form) entries $1,4,24$, with the trivial $\mathbb Z_1$ factor omitted. The displayed [direct sum of modules](../module-theory.md#direct-sum-of-modules) is the answer to the presentation interpretation; the inconsistency of the literal exact-order formulation remains.

<h3 id="3/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

<h5 id="3/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#3/b/i)

Every multiple of $dh$ is a multiple of both $d$ and $h$, so $dh\mathbb Z\subseteq d\mathbb Z\cap h\mathbb Z$.

For the reverse inclusion, let $z=du=hv$ lie in this [intersection of ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#intersection-of-ideals). Since $d,h$ are [coprime integers](../divisibility.md#coprime-integers), [Bézout's identity](../algebra.md#bezout-s-identity) supplies $r,s\in\mathbb Z$ with $rd+sh=1$. Multiplying by $u$ gives

$$
u=rdu+shu=rhv+shu=h(rv+su).
$$

Thus $h\mid u$, and $dh\mid du=z$. Consequently

$$
\boxed{d\mathbb Z\cap h\mathbb Z=dh\mathbb Z}.
$$

Here [Bézout's identity](../algebra.md#bezout-s-identity) follows from the allowed [principal ideal domain](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal-domain) property of $\mathbb Z$: the [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) $d\mathbb Z+h\mathbb Z$ is generated by their [greatest common divisor](../divisibility.md#greatest-common-divisor), which is $1$.

<h4 id="3/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

<h5 id="3/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#3/b/ii)

Define the [group homomorphism](../group-theory.md#group-homomorphism)

$$
\theta:\mathbb Z\longrightarrow\mathbb Z_d\oplus\mathbb Z_h,\qquad
t\longmapsto(t+d\mathbb Z,t+h\mathbb Z).
$$

By part (b)(i), its [kernel of a group homomorphism](../group-theory.md#kernel-of-a-group-homomorphism) is

$$
\ker\theta=d\mathbb Z\cap h\mathbb Z=dh\mathbb Z.
$$

It is [surjective](../algebra.md#surjective-function). Indeed, choose $r,s\in\mathbb Z$ with $rd+sh=1$ by [Bézout's identity](../algebra.md#bezout-s-identity). For any prescribed classes $u+d\mathbb Z$ and $v+h\mathbb Z$, the [integer](../integer.md)

$$
t=ush+vrd
$$

satisfies $t\equiv u\pmod d$ and $t\equiv v\pmod h$. The [first isomorphism theorem for groups](../group-theory.md#first-isomorphism-theorem) therefore gives

$$
\boxed{\mathbb Z_{dh}\xrightarrow{\ \cong\ }\mathbb Z_d\oplus\mathbb Z_h,\qquad
t+dh\mathbb Z\longmapsto(t+d\mathbb Z,t+h\mathbb Z)}.
$$

This proves the required isomorphism and is the [Chinese remainder theorem](../mathematics.md#chinese-remainder-theorem) for [coprime integers](../divisibility.md#coprime-integers). It also shows that $(1+d\mathbb Z,1+h\mathbb Z)$ generates the [direct sum of modules](../module-theory.md#direct-sum-of-modules) when these additive [groups](../group.md) are viewed as $\mathbb Z$-[modules](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics).

<h3 id="3/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/c/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#3/c)

<h5 id="3/c/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#3/c/i)

Let $N=|G|$ be a [square-free integer](../integer.md#square-free-integer). If $N=1$, then $G$ is the trivial [cyclic group](../group.md#cyclic-group). Otherwise, finiteness means that a [direct sum of modules](../module-theory.md#direct-sum-of-modules) decomposition into [cyclic groups](../group.md#cyclic-group) has only finitely many nontrivial factors, each of finite [group order](../group.md#order-of-a-finite-group). Omit trivial factors and write

$$
G\cong\mathbb Z_{n_1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathbb Z_{n_r},\qquad
n_j>1,\qquad N=\prod_{j=1}^r n_j.
$$

The [integers](../integer.md) $n_i,n_j$ are [coprime integers](../divisibility.md#coprime-integers) whenever $i\ne j$. If a [prime number](../prime-number.md) $p$ divided both, then $p^2\mid N$, contradicting the [square-free integer](../integer.md#square-free-integer) hypothesis.

Moreover, $n_1\cdots n_{j-1}$ and $n_j$ are [coprime integers](../divisibility.md#coprime-integers): any common [prime divisor](../prime-number.md#prime-factor) would divide one of the earlier factors as well as $n_j$. Thus part (b)(ii) can be applied repeatedly to combine factors, giving

$$
\boxed{G\cong\mathbb Z_{n_1\cdots n_r}=\mathbb Z_N}.
$$

In particular $G$ is a [cyclic group](../group.md#cyclic-group). Under the displayed identifications, the element whose coordinate in every factor is the class of $1$ is a [generator of a group](../group.md#generator-of-a-group).

<h4 id="3/c/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#3/c)

<h5 id="3/c/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#3/c/ii)

Write $q=|\mathbb F|$. The [unit group](../algebra.md#unit-group) $U(\mathbb F)=\mathbb F\setminus\{0\}$ is a [finite abelian group](../group.md#finite-abelian-group) of [group order](../group.md#order-of-a-finite-group) $q-1$, since [multiplication](../arithmetic.md#multiplication) in a [field](../algebra.md#field) satisfies [commutativity](../algebraic-operation.md#commutativity). The [structure theorem for finitely generated abelian groups](../group.md#fundamental-theorem-of-finitely-generated-abelian-groups), applied to this finite [group](../group.md), gives [invariant factors of a finitely generated module](../module-theory.md#invariant-factor-of-a-finitely-generated-module)

$$
U(\mathbb F)\cong\mathbb Z_{d_1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathbb Z_{d_r},\qquad
1<d_1\mid d_2\mid\cdots\mid d_r,
$$

after omitting trivial factors. If there are no factors, the [unit group](../algebra.md#unit-group) is trivial and hence a [cyclic group](../group.md#cyclic-group).

Otherwise put $D=d_r$. Every element $u\in U(\mathbb F)$ satisfies $u^D=1$, because the [order of a group element](../group-theory.md#order-of-a-group-element) in each coordinate divides $D$. Hence all $q-1$ distinct nonzero elements of $\mathbb F$ are [roots of a polynomial](../polynomial.md#root-of-a-polynomial) $X^D-1$. The [root bound for a polynomial](../polynomial.md#lagrange-root-bound-over-a-field) over a [field](../algebra.md#field) gives

$$
q-1\leq D.
$$

For completeness, that bound follows by induction from the [factor theorem](../polynomial.md#factor-theorem): if a nonzero [polynomial](../polynomial.md) $f$ vanishes at $a$, write $f=(X-a)g$; every other [root of a polynomial](../polynomial.md#root-of-a-polynomial) is a root of $g$, because a [field](../algebra.md#field) has no [zero divisors](../mathematics.md#zero-divisor). Each new distinct root therefore uses one unit of [polynomial degree](../polynomial.md#degree-of-a-polynomial).

On the other hand, the [group order](../group.md#order-of-a-finite-group) in the decomposition is

$$
q-1=d_1\cdots d_r\geq d_r=D.
$$

Equality is forced, so $d_1\cdots d_{r-1}=1$. As every retained factor is greater than $1$, there can be only one. Consequently

$$
\boxed{U(\mathbb F)\cong\mathbb Z_{q-1}\text{ is cyclic}}.
$$

The same proof establishes the more general result [finite multiplicative subgroup of a field is cyclic](../algebra.md#finite-multiplicative-subgroup-of-a-field-is-cyclic). The [square-free integer](../integer.md#square-free-integer) result in part (c)(i) alone would not suffice, since $q-1$ need not be a [square-free integer](../integer.md#square-free-integer); the [root bound for a polynomial](../polynomial.md#lagrange-root-bound-over-a-field) rules out multiple [invariant factors of a finitely generated module](../module-theory.md#invariant-factor-of-a-finitely-generated-module) even then.
