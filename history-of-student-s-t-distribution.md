<h1 id="history-of-student-s-t-distribution">History of Student's t-distribution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [Student's t-distribution](student-s-t-distribution.md)

[William Sealy Gosset](william-sealy-gosset.md) worked at Guinness as a chemist and brewer and developed methods of [statistical inference](statistical-inference-split.md) for small samples. He published under the pseudonym "Student". His employment as a chemist is independently recorded by MacTutor; the Dictionary of Irish Biography identifies him as an applied statistician and brewer and explains the pseudonym policy. [Biography, opening paragraphs and discussion of the name Student](william-sealy-gosset-by-john-joseph-o-connor-and-edmund-frederick-robertson.md) [Philip J. Boland, Gosset, William Sealy ('Student'), opening paragraphs and pseudonym discussion; published and last revised October 2009](gosset-william-sealy-%E2%80%98student%E2%80%99-by-philip-j-boland.md)

The 1908 paper [The Probable Error of a Mean](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Probable_Error_of_a_Mean) appeared in Biometrika 6(1), pages 1–25, with the byline "Student". Its introduction identifies the uncertainty introduced by estimating a [standard deviation](standard-deviation.md) from few observations and explicitly assumes a [normal distribution](normal-distribution.md). Sections II and III develop the relation between sample spread and the standardized [sample mean](sample-mean.md). Its notation uses a sample spread with divisor $n$ and calls the ratio $z$; with that convention, the modern $T$ equals $\sqrt{n-1}\,z$. The modern exact [independence](independent-random-variables.md) argument is given under [studentized normal sample mean](studentized-normal-sample-mean.md). [1908 original text: byline, Introduction, Section I definition of sample spread, and Sections II–III](the-probable-error-of-a-mean-by-william-sealy-gosset.md)

Section VI describes a physical sampling check: 3,000 cards carrying existing paired measurements were shuffled, then divided into 750 groups of four. He compared observed frequencies of sample spread and standardized [sample mean](sample-mean.md) with theoretical frequencies. This was a check using finite, grouped, approximately [normal distribution](normal-distribution.md) data; it did not prove exact calibration for arbitrary populations. [Section VI, Practical Test of the foregoing Equations: cards, groups of four and limitations from nonnormality and coarse grouping](the-probable-error-of-a-mean-by-william-sealy-gosset.md)
