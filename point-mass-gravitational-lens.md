# Point-mass gravitational lens

↑ **Parent:** [Thin gravitational lens equation](thin-gravitational-lens-equation.md)

A [point-mass gravitational lens](point-mass-gravitational-lens.md) is the ideal [gravitational lens](gravitational-lens.md) obtained by concentrating a positive [mass](mass.md) $M$ at one location. Use [geometrical optics](geometrical-optics.md), the [weak-field approximation](weak-field-approximation.md), small angles in [radians](radian.md), and a stationary lens whose deflecting region is short compared with the observer-lens and lens-source distances. At [impact parameter](impact-parameter.md) $b$, require $b\gg r_s=2GM/c^2$, the [Schwarzschild radius](schwarzschild-radius.md), and a ray passing outside any opaque body. The physical bend has magnitude $\widehat\alpha=4GM/(c^2b)$ from [Schwarzschild light deflection](schwarzschild-light-deflection.md); $G$ is the [gravitational constant](gravitational-constant.md) and $c$ the [speed of light](speed-of-light.md). Multiple images can occur within this [weak-field approximation](weak-field-approximation.md): [strong gravitational lensing](strong-gravitational-lensing.md) does not require a ray to approach a [black hole](black-hole.md) closely.[Section 1.2, printed p. 2: geometrical optics, weak fields and slowly moving lenses; compact-object near-field propagation excluded](gravitational-lensing-by-matthias-bartelmann.md)

Let $D_l$, $D_s$ and $D_{ls}$ be [angular diameter distances](angular-diameter-distance.md) from observer to lens, observer to source, and lens to source, respectively. They refer to the background geometry used in the [thin gravitational lens equation](thin-gravitational-lens-equation.md). In nearby [Euclidean geometry](euclidean-geometry.md), $D_{ls}=D_s-D_l$; in [cosmology](cosmology-split.md), do not make that subtraction. The angular source coordinate $\boldsymbol\beta$ is the direction the source would have without this lens; the observed image coordinate is $\boldsymbol\theta$. Both are measured relative to the observer-lens [optical axis](optical-axis.md).[Section 2.1.3, pp. 8-9, equations (13)-(15), including the warning that lens-source angular diameter distance is not the difference of the observer distances](lectures-on-gravitational-lensing-by-ramesh-narayan-and-matthias-bartelmann.md)

For the flat sketch below, extend the observer-side [light ray](light-ray.md) backwards to the source plane. Its transverse position would be $D_s\boldsymbol\theta$, whereas the actual source is at $D_s\boldsymbol\beta$. Their separation is $D_{ls}\widehat{\boldsymbol\alpha}$. Here $\widehat{\boldsymbol\alpha}$ is defined outward along the lens-plane impact vector, as in the [thin gravitational lens equation](thin-gravitational-lens-equation.md); the physical ray bends toward the [mass](mass.md). Substituting $\boldsymbol b=D_l\boldsymbol\theta$ gives

$$
\boldsymbol\beta=\boldsymbol\theta-\frac{D_{ls}}{D_s}\widehat{\boldsymbol\alpha},\qquad
\widehat{\boldsymbol\alpha}=\frac{4GM}{c^2D_l}\frac{\boldsymbol\theta}{|\boldsymbol\theta|^2},\qquad
\theta_E^2=\frac{4GM}{c^2}\frac{D_{ls}}{D_lD_s}.
$$

The same [thin gravitational lens equation](thin-gravitational-lens-equation.md) uses [angular diameter distances](angular-diameter-distance.md) in the cosmological setting. The factor $D_{ls}/D_s$ converts the physical bend into the reduced angular deflection; these are different angles.
