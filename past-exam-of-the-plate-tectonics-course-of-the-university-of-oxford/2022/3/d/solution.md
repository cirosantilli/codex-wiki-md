<h1 id="3/d/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [D](../d.md)

Using the preceding conductive estimate, warmer incoming crust is less dense. Interpret the given $\alpha=10^{-5}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}$ as the volumetric coefficient entering $\delta\rho/\rho=-\alpha\Delta T$. The representative contrast gives

$$
\frac{|\delta\rho|}{\rho_s}=\alpha\Delta\overline T_c\simeq0.0035.
$$

For $\rho_s\simeq3000\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, that is only about $10.5\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$. Let $L$ be the vertical thickness of the anomalous slab material in a compensated column. Its mass deficit per unit area is approximately $\rho_s\alpha\Delta\overline T_c L$. If uplift adds compensating column mass $\rho_{\rm ref}\Delta H$, isostatic balance gives the [thermal buoyancy support of topography](../../../../thermal-buoyancy-support-of-topography.md):

$$
\Delta H\simeq\frac{\rho_s}{\rho_{\rm ref}}\alpha\Delta\overline T_c L.
$$

The reference density depends on which interfaces move in the assumed column model. For approximately equal densities and a near-horizontal $7$ km crustal layer, this gives **$\Delta H\simeq25$ m**; using $\rho_s=3000$ and $\rho_{\rm ref}=3300\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ gives about **$22$ m**. A modest dip changes the vertical intersection to approximately $h_c/\cos\theta$ and does not change this order of magnitude. Different graphical flux choices and uncertain geometry change the number; they do not justify replacing a $7$ km crustal anomaly by a whole $100$ km lithospheric anomaly without additional information.

Thus **the crust-only thermal contrast can supply tens of metres of isostatic relief, not kilometres**. Its sign would favor greater thermal support from the warmer Juan de Fuca crust, so it cannot by itself explain why much of the central [Andes](../../../../andes.md) stands higher than [Cascadia](../../../../cascadia-subduction-zone.md). Crustal shortening and roots, whole-slab thermal structure, phase transformations, hydration, magmatic addition and dynamic stresses can dominate. If the stated expansion coefficient instead meant a linear solid expansion coefficient, the first-order density coefficient would be about three times larger; the estimate would still be well below kilometre-scale mountain relief.

To test [isostasy](../../../../isostasy.md), jointly measure [surface topography](../../../../surface-topography.md), [free-air gravity anomalies](../../../../free-air-gravity-anomaly.md), [Bouguer gravity anomalies](../../../../bouguer-gravity-anomaly.md) and crustal thickness from [receiver functions](../../../../receiver-function.md) and [seismic refraction surveys](../../../../seismic-refraction-survey.md). An [Airy isostasy](../../../../airy-isostasy.md) interpretation predicts crustal roots beneath high terrain and a corresponding regional negative Bouguer signal; thermal or compositional compensation predicts different density structure. Compare gravity and [geoid](../../../../geoid.md) observations with compensated-column predictions over a range of wavelengths, and use [gravity-topography admittance](../../../../gravity-topography-admittance.md) to test local versus [flexural isostasy](../../../../flexural-isostasy.md). A small long-wavelength free-air anomaly can support compensation, but is not a sufficient test by itself. [GNSS](../../../../gnss.md) uplift, leveling and dated marine or river terraces constrain transient or sustained vertical motion; [seismic tomography](../../../../seismic-tomography.md) and rock-density estimates constrain deeper buoyancy. Residual relief or gravity after these corrections may require [dynamic topography](../../../../dynamic-topography.md) or tectonic support. **An actively deforming subduction margin need not satisfy instantaneous local isostatic equilibrium**, even if the estimate above is a useful scale comparison.
