<h1 id="2/d/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [D](../d.md)

**Left: ordinary frictional strengthening with depth followed by thermally activated [material creep](../../../../creep-deformation.md). Right: a very weak shallow faulted layer above a narrow strong region near 10 km, followed by essentially the same weakening [material creep](../../../../creep-deformation.md) branch.** These are the shapes actually plotted in Figure 2. The right curve is not a strong upper crust over a uniformly weak lower crust; neither diagram displays a second mantle-strength peak.

For the left curve, increasing overburden increases the effective normal load on [faults](../../../../geological-fault.md). A schematic [Coulomb friction](../../../../coulomb-friction.md) law is

$$
\tau_f=C+\mu(\sigma_n-P_f),
$$

where $C$ is cohesion, $\mu$ the [coefficient of friction](../../../../coefficient-of-friction.md), $\sigma_n$ the compressive normal [stress](../../../../stress.md) and $P_f$ the [pore pressure](../../../../pore-pressure.md). With ordinary rock friction and approximately [hydrostatic pressure](../../../../hydrostatic-pressure.md), $\sigma_n-P_f$ increases roughly linearly with depth. The stress needed for [frictional sliding](../../../../frictional-sliding.md) therefore increases, as in the straight shallow branch. [Byerlee's law](../../../../byerlee-s-law.md) summarizes the relatively high friction found in many laboratory [rocks](../../../../rock-geology.md).[Friction of rocks by James Byerlee](../../../../friction-of-rocks-by-james-byerlee.md)

At higher [temperature](../../../../temperature-split.md), [dislocation creep](../../../../dislocation-creep.md) or another ductile mechanism accommodates the imposed deformation at lower [stress](../../../../stress.md). For example,

$$
\dot\epsilon=A\sigma^n\exp\!\left(-\frac{Q}{RT}\right),\qquad
\sigma_d=\left(\frac{\dot\epsilon}{A}\right)^{1/n}\exp\!\left(\frac{Q}{nRT}\right).
$$

Here $\dot\epsilon$ is [strain rate](../../../../strain-rate.md), $Q$ an [activation energy](../../../../activation-energy.md), $R$ the [molar gas constant](../../../../molar-gas-constant.md) and $T$ absolute [temperature](../../../../temperature-split.md). At fixed [strain rate](../../../../strain-rate.md), increasing $T$ lowers the required [stress](../../../../stress.md). The operative envelope is approximately $\min(\sigma_f,\sigma_d)$, expressed in a consistent stress convention; the peak marks the [brittle-ductile transition](../../../../brittle-ductile-transition.md). This accounts for the left curve's peak near 100 MPa and its decline toward small strength by about 20 km.

For the right curve, normal high-friction sliding with depth-independent fluid-pressure ratio cannot explain the nearly vertical weak shallow branch. Plausible weakening includes high [pore pressure](../../../../pore-pressure.md) tracking the overburden, low-friction [fault gouge](../../../../fault-gouge.md), or a connected network of pre-existing weak [faults](../../../../geological-fault.md). Roughly constant small $\sigma_n-P_f$ yields roughly constant small [frictional strength](../../../../frictional-strength.md). The abrupt increase near the base of this layer requires a change of conditions: for example, the weak fluid-rich fault network terminates or becomes discontinuous, leaving stronger intact or less altered [rock](../../../../rock-geology.md) below. A change in [mineralogy](../../../../mineralogy.md) or deformation localization can also give a contrast. **Simply saying “there are fluids” does not explain the return to high strength.** Below the strong region, thermal [material creep](../../../../creep-deformation.md) again lowers the strength.

The figure alone does not identify the weakening mechanism or a particular lithological boundary. In particular, a 10 km strength maximum is not evidence for a 10 km deep [Mohorovičić discontinuity](../../../../mohorovicic-discontinuity.md). The strong-fault/weak-fault evidence and the distinction between fault strength and surrounding crustal strength are discussed in [Stress orientations at intermediate angles to the San Andreas Fault, California by Jeanne L. Hardebeck and Andrew J. Michael](../../../../stress-orientations-at-intermediate-angles-to-the-san-andreas-fault-california-by-jeanne-l-hardebeck-and-andrew-j-michael.md).
