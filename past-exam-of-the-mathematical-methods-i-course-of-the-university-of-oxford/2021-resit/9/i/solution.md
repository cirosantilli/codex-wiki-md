<h1 id="9/i/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [I](../i.md)

Use $\mathbf w=-\mathbf u-\mathbf v$ and bilinearity and antisymmetry of the [cross product](../../../../cross-product.md):

$$
\mathbf v\times\mathbf w=-\mathbf v\times\mathbf u-\mathbf v\times\mathbf v=\mathbf u\times\mathbf v,
\qquad
\mathbf w\times\mathbf u=-\mathbf u\times\mathbf u-\mathbf v\times\mathbf u=\mathbf u\times\mathbf v.
$$

Consequently,

$$
\boxed{\mathbf u\times\mathbf v=\mathbf v\times\mathbf w=\mathbf w\times\mathbf u,\qquad
\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf u,\mathbf v,\mathbf w\}=\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf u,\mathbf v\}.}
$$

If $\mathbf u,\mathbf v$ are [linearly independent](../../../../linear-independence.md), this [linear span](../../../../linear-span.md) is the [plane](../../../../plane.md) through the origin with [normal vector](../../../../normal-vector.md) $\mathbf u\times\mathbf v$. If they are dependent, all three nonzero [vectors](../../../../vector.md) lie on one [line](../../../../straight-line.md) through the origin, and their [linear span](../../../../linear-span.md) has dimension one. For example, $\mathbf u=\mathbf v=(1,0,0)$ and $\mathbf w=(-2,0,0)$ obey the hypothesis but span only a [line](../../../../straight-line.md). Nonzero [vectors](../../../../vector.md) alone do not force a two-dimensional [linear span](../../../../linear-span.md).
