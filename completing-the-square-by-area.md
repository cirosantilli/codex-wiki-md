# Completing the square by area

↑ **Parent:** [Completing the square](completing-the-square.md)

Start with a [square](square.md) of side length $x>0$ and add two [rectangles](rectangle.md), each with side lengths $x$ and $3$. The total [area](surface-area.md) is $x^2+6x$. Place the [rectangles](rectangle.md) along two adjacent sides of the [square](square.md): the missing corner is a $3$ by $3$ [square](square.md), of [area](surface-area.md) $9$. Filling it produces a [square](square.md) of side length $x+3$.

<a id="image-two-rectangles-and-a-missing-corner-explain-x-plus-3-2-x-2-plus-6x-plus-9"></a>
![](completing-square-area-assembly.png)

**[Figure 5](#image-two-rectangles-and-a-missing-corner-explain-x-plus-3-2-x-2-plus-6x-plus-9). Two rectangles and a missing corner explain $(x+3)^2=x^2+6x+9$**. An original [area](surface-area.md) assembly, drawn at the stipulated positive side length $x=2$. The corner has [area](surface-area.md) $9$; the remaining constant $13-9=4$ gives $x^2+6x+13=(x+3)^2+4$. The diagram uses positive side lengths, while the [polynomial identity](polynomial-identity.md) holds for every $x$ in the [real numbers](real-number.md).

The [area](surface-area.md) picture explains why the half-coefficient is squared. It also separates two operations that are easy to confuse: adding $9$ changes $x^2+6x$, whereas adding and subtracting $9$ preserves the expression. When solving an [equation](equation.md) instead, adding $9$ to one side requires adding $9$ to the other side too.
