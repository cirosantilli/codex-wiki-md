<h3 id="1/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1/a)

<h5 id="1/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [I](#1/a/i)

If $a=ub$ with $u$ a [unit in a ring](../algebra.md#unit-in-a-ring), then $a$ lies in the [principal ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal) generated by $b$. This gives $(a)\subseteq(b)$. Since $u$ has a [multiplicative inverse](../arithmetic.md#multiplicative-inverse), $b=u^{-1}a$, giving the reverse inclusion. Therefore

$$
\boxed{a=ub,\ u\in R^\times\quad\Longrightarrow\quad(a)=(b).}
$$

Thus [associate elements](../commutative-algebra.md#associate-element) always generate the same [principal ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal); the [associator ring](../commutative-algebra.md#associator-ring) condition asks whether equality of these [ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) also forces the generators to differ by a [unit](../algebra.md#unit-in-a-ring).

<h4 id="1/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1/a)

<h5 id="1/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Ii](#1/a/ii)

Suppose $R$ is an [integral domain](../commutative-algebra.md#integral-domain) and $(a)=(b)$. If $a=0$, then $(b)=0$, so $b=0$ and $a=1\cdot b$.

Otherwise membership in the equal [principal ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal) supplies $r,s\in R$ with $a=rb$ and $b=sa$. Substituting gives $a=rsa$, or $(1-rs)a=0$. There are no nonzero [zero divisors](../mathematics.md#zero-divisor) in an [integral domain](../commutative-algebra.md#integral-domain), and $a\ne0$, so $rs=1$. Hence $r$ is a [unit](../algebra.md#unit-in-a-ring) with inverse $s$, and $a=rb$ makes $a$ and $b$ [associate elements](../commutative-algebra.md#associate-element). We have proved

$$
\boxed{\text{Every integral domain is an associator ring.}}
$$

<h4 id="1/a/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#1/a)

<h5 id="1/a/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Iii](#1/a/iii)

The [ideal correspondence theorem](../commutative-algebra.md#correspondence-theorem-for-ideals) identifies the [ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) of $\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z$ with the [ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) of $\mathbb Z$ containing $4\mathbb Z$. The possible positive generators divide $4$, giving exactly

$$
\boxed{\{\overline0\},\qquad
(\overline2)=\{\overline0,\overline2\},\qquad
\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z.}
$$

The [units](../algebra.md#unit-in-a-ring) are $\overline1$ and $\overline3$. The generators of the three displayed [ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) are, respectively,

$$
\{\overline0\},\qquad\{\overline2\},\qquad
\{\overline1,\overline3\}.
$$

Within the last pair, multiplication by the [unit](../algebra.md#unit-in-a-ring) $\overline3$ exchanges the generators. The other two sets are singletons, whose members are [associate elements](../commutative-algebra.md#associate-element) to themselves by multiplication by $\overline1$. Thus equal [principal ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal) always have associate generators: this is an [associator ring](../commutative-algebra.md#associator-ring).

However, $\overline2\ne\overline0$ and $\overline2^2=\overline0$, so $\overline2$ is a nonzero [zero divisor](../mathematics.md#zero-divisor). Therefore

$$
\boxed{\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z\text{ is an associator ring but not an integral domain}.}
$$

<h3 id="1/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#1/b)

For two factors, the source's $R_1\oplus R_2$ is the [direct product of rings](../commutative-algebra.md#direct-product-of-rings) with componentwise operations. Its [principal ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal) satisfy

$$
\bigl((a_1,a_2)\bigr)=(a_1)\times(a_2),
$$

One inclusion follows by projection to the factors. For the reverse inclusion, multiplication by $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ puts $(a_1,0)$ and $(0,a_2)$ in the generated [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal); taking [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) combinations separately produces every pair in $(a_1)\times(a_2)$.

Suppose $(a_1,a_2)$ and $(b_1,b_2)$ generate the same [principal ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal). Projecting to the factors gives $(a_i)=(b_i)$ for $i=1,2$. Since each factor is an [associator ring](../commutative-algebra.md#associator-ring), there are [units](../algebra.md#unit-in-a-ring) $u_i\in R_i$ with $a_i=u_ib_i$. The pair $(u_1,u_2)$ is a [unit](../algebra.md#unit-in-a-ring) of the [direct product of rings](../commutative-algebra.md#direct-product-of-rings), with inverse $(u_1^{-1},u_2^{-1})$, and

$$
(a_1,a_2)=(u_1,u_2)(b_1,b_2).
$$

Hence

$$
\boxed{R_1\oplus R_2\text{ is an associator ring}.}
$$

Repeating this argument proves the same assertion for every finite [direct product of rings](../commutative-algebra.md#direct-product-of-rings).

<h3 id="1/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#1/c)

Put $A=R/(p^k)$. A [principal ideal domain](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal-domain) is a [unique factorization domain](../algebra.md#unique-factorization-domain), so every nonzero element of this [quotient ring](../commutative-algebra.md#quotient-ring) can be written

$$
\overline a=\overline{p}^{\,e}\overline s,
\qquad 0\leq e<k,\qquad p\nmid s.
$$

Indeed, remove the largest power of the [prime element](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-element) $p$ from a representative that is not divisible by $p^k$. Since $s$ and $p^k$ have [greatest common divisor](../divisibility.md#greatest-common-divisor) $1$ up to a [unit](../algebra.md#unit-in-a-ring), [Bézout's identity](../algebra.md#bezout-s-identity) in the [principal ideal domain](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal-domain) gives $xs+yp^k=1$. Thus $\overline s$ is a [unit](../algebra.md#unit-in-a-ring) in $A$.

Consequently every nonzero [principal ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal) in $A$ is generated by one of $1,\overline p,\ldots,\overline p^{\,k-1}$. Include the zero [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) by assigning it exponent $e=k$. These [ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) are distinct: their inverse images in $R$ are $(1),(p),\ldots,(p^k)$, and equality of two different powers would, by cancellation in the [integral domain](../commutative-algebra.md#integral-domain), make the nonunit $p$ a [unit](../algebra.md#unit-in-a-ring).

Now suppose $(\overline a)=(\overline b)$. If this is the zero [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal), both generators are zero and are [associate elements](../commutative-algebra.md#associate-element). Otherwise their exponents agree, so write

$$
\overline a=\overline p^{\,e}\overline s,\qquad
\overline b=\overline p^{\,e}\overline t
$$

with $\overline s,\overline t$ both [units](../algebra.md#unit-in-a-ring). Then

$$
\overline a=(\overline s\,\overline t^{-1})\overline b,
$$

and the multiplier is a [unit](../algebra.md#unit-in-a-ring). Thus

$$
\boxed{R/(p^k)\text{ is an associator ring for every }k\geq1.}
$$

The exponent detects the [principal ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal), while its remaining factor is invertible; this is why [zero divisors](../mathematics.md#zero-divisor) cause no obstruction here.

<h3 id="1/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#1/d)

By the [first isomorphism theorem for rings](../commutative-algebra.md#first-isomorphism-theorem-for-rings), the given surjection identifies $S$ with $R/\ker\phi$. Since $R$ is a [principal ideal domain](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal-domain), write $\ker\phi=(a)$.

If $a=0$, the [quotient ring](../commutative-algebra.md#quotient-ring) is $R$, an [integral domain](../commutative-algebra.md#integral-domain), so part (a)(ii) applies. If $a$ is a [unit](../algebra.md#unit-in-a-ring), the [quotient ring](../commutative-algebra.md#quotient-ring) is the [zero ring](../commutative-algebra.md#zero-ring); its unique element is associate to itself by multiplication by its identity, so it too is an [associator ring](../commutative-algebra.md#associator-ring).

In the remaining case, [unique factorization](../algebra.md#unique-factorization-in-an-integral-domain) gives

$$
a=\varepsilon p_1^{k_1}\cdots p_r^{k_r},
$$

where $\varepsilon$ is a [unit](../algebra.md#unit-in-a-ring), the $p_i$ are pairwise nonassociate [prime elements](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-element), and $k_i\geq1$. The [principal ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal) $(p_i^{k_i})$ are pairwise comaximal: different prime factors have [greatest common divisor](../divisibility.md#greatest-common-divisor) $1$, and [Bézout's identity](../algebra.md#bezout-s-identity) gives a linear combination equal to $1$. The [Chinese remainder theorem](../mathematics.md#chinese-remainder-theorem) therefore gives a [ring isomorphism](../algebra.md#ring-isomorphism)

$$
R/(a)\ \cong\ \prod_{i=1}^r R/(p_i^{k_i}).
$$

Every factor is an [associator ring](../commutative-algebra.md#associator-ring) by part (c), and their finite [direct product of rings](../commutative-algebra.md#direct-product-of-rings) is an [associator ring](../commutative-algebra.md#associator-ring) by part (b). A [ring isomorphism](../algebra.md#ring-isomorphism) preserves [units](../algebra.md#unit-in-a-ring) and [principal ideals](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal), so the property transfers to $S$. Hence

$$
\boxed{S\text{ is an associator ring}.}
$$

↑ **Parent:** [2015](2015.md)

<h3 id="2/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#2/a)

A [Euclidean domain](../commutative-algebra.md#euclidean-domain) is a [principal ideal domain](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal-domain). The [structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain](../module-theory.md#structure-theorem-for-finitely-generated-modules-over-a-principal-ideal-domain) therefore gives, for every [finitely generated module](../module-theory.md#finitely-generated-module) $M$ over $R$,

$$
M\cong R^r\oplus R/(d_1)\oplus\cdots\oplus R/(d_s),
$$

where the $d_i$ are nonzero nonunits and $d_1\mid d_2\mid\cdots\mid d_s$. The factors $d_i$ are unique up to multiplication by [units](../algebra.md#unit-in-a-ring); $r$ is uniquely determined.

The [rank of a module](../module-theory.md#rank-of-a-module-over-an-integral-domain) is this integer $r$. Equivalently, with $K$ the [field of fractions](../commutative-algebra.md#field-of-fractions) of $R$,

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{rank}_R M=\dim_K(K\otimes_R M)=r.}
$$

The [tensor product of modules](../module-theory.md#tensor-product-of-modules) kills each displayed torsion summand after extending scalars to $K$, while sending $R^r$ to $K^r$, explaining the equality.

If $M$ is a [torsion-free module](../module-theory.md#torsion-free-module), no summand $R/(d_i)$ can occur: its nonzero element $1+(d_i)$ is killed by the nonzero scalar $d_i$. Hence $s=0$ and

$$
\boxed{M\cong R^r,\quad\text{so $M$ is a free module}.}
$$

This also covers $M=0$, with $r=0$.

<h3 id="2/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#2/b)

Let $x,y\in\mathbb Q$. Choose a positive [integer](../integer.md) $D$ clearing their denominators and write $x=m/D$, $y=n/D$ with $m,n\in\mathbb Z$. If $x$ and $y$ are not both zero, then

$$
nx-my=0
$$

is a relation with [integer](../integer.md) coefficients not both zero. If both vanish, use $1\cdot x+0\cdot y=0$. Thus every pair is [linearly dependent](../vector-space.md#linear-dependence) over $\mathbb Z$.

A [submodule](../module-theory.md#submodule) $M\subseteq\mathbb Q$ is a [torsion-free module](../module-theory.md#torsion-free-module): if $kz=0$ for a nonzero [integer](../integer.md) $k$ and a [rational number](../number-theory.md#rational-number) $z$, then $z=0$. If $M$ is also a [finitely generated module](../module-theory.md#finitely-generated-module), part (a), applied to the [Euclidean domain](../commutative-algebra.md#euclidean-domain) $\mathbb Z$, makes it a [free module](../module-theory.md#free-module). Its [rank](../module-theory.md#rank-of-a-module-over-an-integral-domain) cannot exceed one, since two different members of a [basis of a module](../module-theory.md#basis-of-a-module) would be linearly independent, contradicting the relation just proved. Since $M\ne0$, its rank cannot be zero. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb Z}M=1,\qquad M\cong\mathbb Z.}
$$

The denominator-and-[greatest common divisor](../divisibility.md#greatest-common-divisor) construction in part (c) gives an explicit version of this result for a [finitely generated subgroup of the rational additive group](../group.md#finitely-generated-subgroup-of-the-rational-additive-group).

<h3 id="2/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#2/c)

Using the common denominator $70$, the three given generators become

$$
\frac25=\frac{28}{70},\qquad
\frac37=\frac{30}{70},\qquad
\frac12=\frac{35}{70}.
$$

Thus their [submodule](../module-theory.md#submodule) is contained in $(1/70)\mathbb Z$. For the reverse inclusion, the following explicit [integer](../integer.md) linear combination supplies the missing generator:

$$
2\cdot28-3\cdot30+35=1,
$$

so

$$
2\cdot\frac25-3\cdot\frac37+\frac12=\frac1{70}.
$$

Therefore $1/70$ belongs to the generated [submodule](../module-theory.md#submodule), and all its [integer](../integer.md) multiples belong as well. We have established equality in both directions:

$$
M=\frac1{70}\mathbb Z.
$$

Finally, $k/70=0$ for an [integer](../integer.md) $k$ forces $k=0$, so the single generator is linearly independent. Hence the required [basis of a module](../module-theory.md#basis-of-a-module) is

$$
\boxed{\left\{\frac1{70}\right\}.}
$$

<h3 id="2/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [2](#2)

<h4 id="2/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#2/d)

Suppose a finite set of [rational numbers](../number-theory.md#rational-number) $q_1,\ldots,q_m$ generated $\mathbb Q$ as a [module](../module-theory.md#module-mathematics) over $\mathbb Z$. A positive common denominator $D$ makes every $Dq_i$ an [integer](../integer.md). For every [integer](../integer.md) linear combination, therefore,

$$
D\sum_i n_iq_i\in\mathbb Z.
$$

The entire generated [submodule](../module-theory.md#submodule) is contained in $(1/D)\mathbb Z$. But $1/(2D)$ is a [rational number](../number-theory.md#rational-number) outside that set: $1/(2D)=k/D$ would require the noninteger $k=1/2$. This contradicts generation of all of $\mathbb Q$. Consequently

$$
\boxed{\mathbb Q\text{ is not finitely generated as a }\mathbb Z\text{-module}.}
$$

Each finite set of generators imposes a common denominator; the full [rational number](../number-theory.md#rational-number) system has no such bound.

## 3

↑ **Parent:** [2015](2015.md)

<h3 id="3/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [A](#3/a)

For a nonzero [polynomial](../polynomial.md) $f=\sum_i a_it^i\in\mathbb Z[t]$, its [polynomial content](../commutative-algebra.md#polynomial-content) is the positive [greatest common divisor](../divisibility.md#greatest-common-divisor) of its coefficients:

$$
c(f)=\gcd(a_0,a_1,\ldots)>0.
$$

For nonzero $g\in\mathbb Q[t]$, choose a positive [integer](../integer.md) $n$ with $ng\in\mathbb Z[t]$ and define its [rational polynomial content](../commutative-algebra.md#rational-polynomial-content) by

$$
\boxed{c(g)=\frac{c(ng)}n.}
$$

To check independence of the denominator, write $g=f/n=h/m$ with $f,h\in\mathbb Z[t]$ and $n,m>0$. Then $mf=nh$, while multiplication of all coefficients by a positive [integer](../integer.md) gives

$$
mc(f)=c(mf)=c(nh)=nc(h).
$$

Dividing by $mn$ proves $c(f)/n=c(h)/m$, as required.

For multiplication, write $g_i=f_i/n_i$. The integer-coefficient content identity permitted in the source gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
c(g_1g_2)
&=\frac{c(f_1f_2)}{n_1n_2}
=\frac{c(f_1)c(f_2)}{n_1n_2}\\
&=c(g_1)c(g_2).
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus

$$
\boxed{c(g_1g_2)=c(g_1)c(g_2).}
$$

We will also use the normalization $g/c(g)\in\mathbb Z[t]$, with content one: if $g=f/n$, then $g/c(g)=f/c(f)$. It is a [primitive polynomial](../commutative-algebra.md#primitive-polynomial). For a nonzero rational constant $r$, the same definition gives $c(r)=|r|$.

<h3 id="3/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [B](#3/b)

The set $\widetilde P$ contains zero. It is closed under negatives because $-f\in P$ whenever $f\in P$. If $f,g\in P$ and $n,m>0$, then

$$
\frac fn+\frac gm=\frac{mf+ng}{nm}\in\widetilde P,
$$

since $P$ is an [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) of $\mathbb Z[t]$. Also, any $q\in\mathbb Q[t]$ can be written $q=h/d$ with $h\in\mathbb Z[t]$ and $d>0$, giving

$$
q\frac fn=\frac{hf}{dn}\in\widetilde P.
$$

This proves that $\widetilde P$ is an [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) of $\mathbb Q[t]$.

Clearly $P\subseteq\widetilde P\cap\mathbb Z[t]$, by taking denominator one. Conversely, if $h\in\widetilde P\cap\mathbb Z[t]$, then $nh\in P$ for some positive [integer](../integer.md) $n$. The condition $P\cap\mathbb Z=\{0\}$ implies $n\notin P$. Since $P$ is a [prime ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal), $nh\in P$ forces $h\in P$. Hence

$$
\boxed{\widetilde P\cap\mathbb Z[t]=P.}
$$

In particular, $\widetilde P$ is nonzero because it contains the nonzero [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) $P$, and it is proper: otherwise its intersection with $\mathbb Z[t]$ would contain $1$. This is the concrete [extension of an ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#extension-of-an-ideal) and [contraction of an ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#contraction-of-an-ideal) calculation for the [localization](../commutative-algebra.md#localization-of-a-ring) that inverts nonzero [integers](../integer.md).

<h3 id="3/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [C](#3/c)

The nonzero [ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#ideal) $\widetilde P$ is principal because $\mathbb Q[t]$ is a [principal ideal domain](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal-domain). Choose a nonzero generator $h\in\mathbb Q[t]$. Part (a) gives

$$
f=\frac{h}{c(h)}\in\mathbb Z[t],\qquad c(f)=1.
$$

The nonzero rational scalar $c(h)$ is a [unit](../algebra.md#unit-in-a-ring) in $\mathbb Q[t]$, so this rescaling does not change the generated [principal ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal):

$$
\boxed{\widetilde P=(f)_{\mathbb Q[t]},\qquad c(f)=1.}
$$

Part (b) now places $f$ in $P$, since $f\in\widetilde P\cap\mathbb Z[t]$. Thus $(f)_{\mathbb Z[t]}\subseteq P$.

For the reverse inclusion, let $g\in P$ be nonzero. The displayed equality supplies $q\in\mathbb Q[t]$ with $g=fq$. Multiplicativity of [rational polynomial content](../commutative-algebra.md#rational-polynomial-content) gives

$$
c(g)=c(f)c(q)=c(q).
$$

Since $g$ has [integer](../integer.md) coefficients, $c(g)$ is a positive [integer](../integer.md). The normalization from part (a) gives $q/c(q)\in\mathbb Z[t]$, so multiplying it by the integer $c(q)=c(g)$ proves $q\in\mathbb Z[t]$. Consequently $g\in(f)_{\mathbb Z[t]}$. The zero [polynomial](../polynomial.md) belongs as well, proving

$$
\boxed{P=(f)_{\mathbb Z[t]}.}
$$

This is the divisibility descent in [Gauss lemma for polynomials](../commutative-algebra.md#gauss-lemma-for-polynomials): dividing an integer-coefficient [polynomial](../polynomial.md) by a [primitive polynomial](../commutative-algebra.md#primitive-polynomial) cannot introduce a denominator into an exact quotient. Here it yields the description of [prime ideals of the integer polynomial ring above zero](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideals-of-the-integer-polynomial-ring-above-zero). The generator has positive [polynomial degree](../polynomial.md#degree-of-a-polynomial), since a primitive constant would be a [unit](../algebra.md#unit-in-a-ring) and would put $1$ in $P$.

<h3 id="3/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [D](#3/d)

Take $I=(2,t)\subseteq\mathbb Z[t]$. The [ring homomorphism](../commutative-algebra.md#ring-homomorphism)

$$
\theta:\mathbb Z[t]\longrightarrow\mathbb F_2,
\qquad \theta(g)=g(0)\pmod2
$$

is surjective. Its [kernel of a ring homomorphism](../commutative-algebra.md#kernel-of-a-ring-homomorphism) is exactly $I$: a [polynomial](../polynomial.md) maps to zero precisely when its constant coefficient is even, in which case it has the form $2a+th(t)$ with $a\in\mathbb Z$ and $h\in\mathbb Z[t]$. By the [first isomorphism theorem for rings](../commutative-algebra.md#first-isomorphism-theorem-for-rings),

$$
\mathbb Z[t]/I\cong\mathbb F_2.
$$

This [quotient ring](../commutative-algebra.md#quotient-ring) is a [field](../algebra.md#field), so $I$ is a [maximal ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#maximal-ideal) and in particular a [prime ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#prime-ideal).

Suppose $I=(h)$ were a [principal ideal](../commutative-algebra.md#principal-ideal). Because $2\in I$, the nonzero [polynomial](../polynomial.md) $h$ divides the constant $2$, forcing its [polynomial degree](../polynomial.md#degree-of-a-polynomial) to be zero: $h=d$ for a nonzero [integer](../integer.md) $d$. But $t\in I$ also makes $d$ divide the coefficient $1$ of $t$, so $d=\pm1$. This would give $I=\mathbb Z[t]$, contradicting the properness of the [kernel of a ring homomorphism](../commutative-algebra.md#kernel-of-a-ring-homomorphism) $\ker\theta$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{(2,t)\text{ is a prime ideal of }\mathbb Z[t]
\text{ that is not principal}.}
$$

There is no conflict with part (c): $I\cap\mathbb Z=2\mathbb Z\ne\{0\}$. The zero-intersection hypothesis is exactly what excludes this example.
