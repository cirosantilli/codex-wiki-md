<h1 id="1/b/solution">Solution</h1>

↑ **Parent:** [B](../b.md)

Suppose, for a contradiction, that the monic [irreducible polynomials](../../../../irreducible-polynomial.md) in the [polynomial ring](../../../../polynomial-ring.md) $k[t]$ form a finite list $p_1,\ldots,p_r$. The list is nonempty because $t$ itself is monic and irreducible. Put

$$
F(t)=p_1(t)\cdots p_r(t)+1.
$$

This has positive [degree](../../../../degree-of-a-polynomial.md), so it is a nonzero [nonunit](../../../../nonunit.md). Every such [polynomial](../../../../polynomial-split.md) over a [field](../../../../field.md) has an [irreducible polynomial](../../../../irreducible-polynomial.md) as a factor: repeatedly factor a reducible polynomial, with the positive degrees strictly decreasing until an irreducible factor is reached. Dividing that factor by its [leading coefficient](../../../../leading-coefficient-of-a-polynomial.md) makes it monic; call it $q$.

By the assumed completeness of the list, $q=p_j$ for some $j$. But then $p_j$ divides both $F$ and $p_1\cdots p_r$, hence their difference $1$. A positive-degree [polynomial](../../../../polynomial-split.md) cannot divide $1$. This contradiction proves **there are infinitely many monic irreducible polynomials over every field**. This is the polynomial version of [Euclid's theorem](../../../../euclid-s-theorem.md); it does not require the field itself to be infinite.
