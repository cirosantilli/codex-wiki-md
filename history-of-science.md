## Material culture of science

↑ **Parent:** [History of science](history-of-science.md)

The [scientific instruments](physics.md#scientific-instrument), specimens, buildings, records and embodied skills through which scientific work is performed and communicated. Explaining a result may require reconstructing apparatus dimensions, maintenance, preparation, authorship and access, as well as the published argument.

↑ **Parent:** [History of science](history-of-science.md)

The French royal contracted tax-collection institution. It provides relevant context for [Antoine Lavoisier](chemistry.md#antoine-lavoisier)'s financial and official position and the prosecution of its former members.

### Farmers-general

↑ **Parent:** [Ferme générale](#ferme-generale)

Officeholders in the [Ferme générale](#ferme-generale); the label identifies a fiscal and administrative role rather than a scientific profession.

## Historiography of science

↑ **Parent:** [History of science](history-of-science.md)

Study of how accounts of the scientific past are selected, organized and justified. Explaining why an investigator accepted a claim is distinct from endorsing that claim.

### Whig history of science

↑ **Parent:** [Historiography of science](#historiography-of-science)

A retrospective account organized around the apparent inevitability of later scientific success. The problem is explanatory selection, not the mere recognition that some theories explain evidence better than others.

### Historical presentism

↑ **Parent:** [Historiography of science](#historiography-of-science)

Interpreting past inquiry primarily through later categories, problems or standards. Distinct from [presentism](physics.md#presentism-philosophy-of-time).

Primary sentence excerpt reproduced by Museo Galileo. Specifies the finding of vehement suspicion of heresy, prohibition of the Dialogue, imprisonment and penance. Its date must not be replaced by 1616 or 1630.

### Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina by Galileo Galilei

↑ **Parent:** [History of science bibliography](#history-of-science-bibliography)

[https://web.stanford.edu/~jsabol/certainty/readings/Galileo-LetterDuchessChristina.pdf](https://web.stanford.edu/~jsabol/certainty/readings/Galileo-LetterDuchessChristina.pdf)

Primary letter in English translation. The passages on sense experience, necessary demonstrations and nonliteral interpretation explain a religious argument for intellectual jurisdiction, not a rejection of all scriptural authority.

↑ **Parent:** [History of science bibliography](#history-of-science-bibliography)

Primary communication: introduction dated 30 June 1858; read 1 July; this record identifies printing on 20 August. Includes Darwin extracts and Wallace's independent essay. ### Vestiges of the Natural History of Creation by Robert Chambers

↑ **Parent:** [History of science bibliography](#history-of-science-bibliography)

Primary first-edition text, originally anonymous. Chapters General Considerations respecting the Origin of the Animated Tribes and Hypothesis of the Development of the Vegetable and Animal Kingdoms are cited. This is not a late edition silently treated as the 1844 argument.

### Philosophie zoologique, living and inorganic bodies by Jean-Baptiste Lamarck

↑ **Parent:** [History of science bibliography](#history-of-science-bibliography)

[https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Philosophie_zoologique_(1809)/Seconde_Partie/Premier_Chapitre](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Philosophie_zoologique_(1809)/Seconde_Partie/Premier_Chapitre)

Primary Part II, chapter I. Contrasts the organization of living bodies with inorganic aggregation, describing contained [fluids](fluid-mechanics.md#fluid) and containing [tissues](anatomy.md#tissue-biology). The claim of natural physical causes does not remove these organizational distinctions.

### Philosophie zoologique, organic and inorganic productions by Jean-Baptiste Lamarck

↑ **Parent:** [History of science bibliography](#history-of-science-bibliography)

Primary [volume](geometry-and-topology.md#volume) I, printed p. 92, with the preceding discussion at p. 91: [organisms](organism.md) produce compounds; their remains become materials subsequently simplified; the explicit cross-reference is to Hydrogéologie, p. 112.

### Mixed mathematics

↑ **Parent:** [Natural philosophy](#natural-philosophy)

Early modern mathematical disciplines applied to physical or practical objects, including [astronomy](astronomy.md), [optics](optics.md) and [mechanics](physics.md#mechanics). The social standing and explanatory claims of practitioners varied; this is not simply a synonym for the present administrative category of applied [mathematics](mathematics.md).

### Natural philosopher

↑ **Parent:** [Natural philosophy](#natural-philosophy)

An investigator of nature within historical traditions of [natural philosophy](#natural-philosophy), rather than necessarily a member of the later profession of [physics](physics.md).

### Demonstration in early modern natural philosophy

↑ **Parent:** [Natural philosophy](#natural-philosophy)

A proposed [proof](foundations-of-mathematics.md#mathematical-proof) of a physical claim, as distinguished in a particular controversy from a calculation that succeeds under a supposition. Historical standards of [demonstration](#demonstration-in-early-modern-natural-philosophy) and the authority to judge one must be reconstructed from the participants' arguments.

### Scholastic natural philosophy

↑ **Parent:** [Natural philosophy](#natural-philosophy)

University traditions that investigated nature through authoritative texts, commentary and disputed questions. Acceptance of a textual framework could coexist with disagreement about particular explanations.

### Aristotelian natural philosophy

↑ **Parent:** [Natural philosophy](#natural-philosophy)

A tradition of explanations of natural change drawing on [Aristotle](README.md#aristotle)'s distinctions among matter, form, capacities and causes. Its medieval and early modern versions were interpreted traditions, not identical endorsements of every ancient conclusion.[book II, chapters 1–3](#physics-by-aristotle)

#### Four causes

↑ **Parent:** [Aristotelian natural philosophy](#aristotelian-natural-philosophy)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_causes)

[Aristotle](README.md#aristotle)'s four explanatory questions concern what something is made of, its form, the source of change and its end. The [final cause](#final-cause) is the last of these; this is not a list of four modern interacting forces.[book II, chapter 3](#physics-by-aristotle)

##### Final cause

↑ **Parent:** [Four causes](#four-causes)

The end for the sake of which a process or organized activity is explained in [Aristotelian natural philosophy](#aristotelian-natural-philosophy). Such an explanation should be distinguished from a modern dynamical [force law](force.md#force-law).
