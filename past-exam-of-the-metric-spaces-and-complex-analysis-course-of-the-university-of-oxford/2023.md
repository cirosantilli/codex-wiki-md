<h4 id="1/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [a](#1/a)

<h5 id="1/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [i](#1/a/i)

A [metric space](../topological-analysis.md#metric-space) $(X,d)$ is a [bounded set](../topological-analysis.md#bounded-set) if there is a finite constant $M\geq0$ such that

$$
\boxed{d(x,y)\leq M\quad\text{for every }x,y\in X}.
$$

For nonempty $X$, this is equivalent to containment in an [open ball](../topology.md#open-ball) of finite radius: the [triangle inequality](../topological-analysis.md#triangle-inequality) converts a radius bound into a diameter bound, and conversely a diameter bound gives a ball of any slightly larger radius about a chosen point.

It is a [complete metric space](../topological-analysis.md#complete-metric-space) if every [Cauchy sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#cauchy-sequence) in $X$ has a [limit of a sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#limit-of-a-sequence) belonging to $X$. Explicitly, the hypothesis on $(x_n)$ is

$$
\forall\epsilon>0\ \exists N\ \forall m,n\geq N:\ d(x_m,x_n)<\epsilon,
$$

and [completeness](../topological-analysis.md#completeness) requires an $x\in X$ with $d(x_n,x)\to0$. Thus

$$
\boxed{\text{every Cauchy sequence converges in }X}.
$$

Boundedness controls distances; [completeness](../topological-analysis.md#completeness) ensures that these limiting points remain in the space.

↑ **Parent:** [a](#1/a)

<h5 id="1/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [ii](#1/a/ii)

A [subset](../set.md#subset) $F\subseteq X$ is a [closed set](../topology.md#closed-set) in $X$ when its [complement](../set.md#complement-of-a-set) is an [open set](../topology.md#open-set) in the [metric topology](../topological-analysis.md#metric-topology). Equivalently,

$$
\boxed{x\in X\setminus F\ \Longrightarrow\ \exists r>0:\ B(x,r)\cap F=\varnothing}.
$$

The [open ball](../topology.md#open-ball) is taken inside the ambient [metric space](../topological-analysis.md#metric-space) $X$; closedness always depends on that ambient space.

<h4 id="1/a/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [a](#1/a)

<h5 id="1/a/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [iii](#1/a/iii)

First suppose $F$ is a [closed set](../topology.md#closed-set). If $x\notin F$, the definition in part (ii) gives an [open ball](../topology.md#open-ball) $B(x,r)$ disjoint from $F$. Thus every $y\in F$ has $d(x,y)\geq r$, and the [distance to a set](../topological-analysis.md#distance-to-a-set) satisfies $\operatorname{dist}(x,F)\geq r>0$. Its contrapositive is the required implication.

Conversely, suppose that zero [distance to a set](../topological-analysis.md#distance-to-a-set) forces membership. For $x\notin F$ we have $\operatorname{dist}(x,F)>0$. Choose $0<r<\operatorname{dist}(x,F)$. Then $B(x,r)\cap F=\varnothing$, proving $X\setminus F$ is an [open set](../topology.md#open-set). Hence

$$
\boxed{F\text{ is closed}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \forall x\in X,\quad\operatorname{dist}(x,F)=0\Rightarrow x\in F}.
$$

For $F=\varnothing$, use the extended-real convention $\inf\varnothing=+\infty$: both assertions hold, with the implication vacuous. Part (b) only uses nonempty sets, so all its [distances to a set](../topological-analysis.md#distance-to-a-set) are ordinary finite real numbers.

<h3 id="1/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [1](#1)

<h4 id="1/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [b](#1/b)

<h5 id="1/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [i](#1/b/i)

Write $h(A,B)=\sup_{a\in A}\operatorname{dist}(a,B)$, so that $\rho(A,B)=\max\{h(A,B),h(B,A)\}$ is the [Hausdorff distance](../topological-analysis.md#hausdorff-distance). Since $X$ is a [bounded set](../topological-analysis.md#bounded-set), there is a finite $M$ with $d(x,y)\leq M$. Nonemptiness of $A,B$ therefore gives $0\leq h(A,B)\leq M$, so $\rho$ is finite. Its symmetry and nonnegativity are immediate, as is $\rho(A,A)=0$.

If $\rho(A,B)=0$, then every $a\in A$ has zero [distance to a set](../topological-analysis.md#distance-to-a-set) $B$. Since $B$ is a [closed set](../topology.md#closed-set), part (a)(iii) gives $A\subseteq B$. Interchanging the sets gives $B\subseteq A$. Thus $\rho(A,B)=0$ implies $A=B$. Closedness is essential here: without it a set and its [closure](../topology.md#closure-topology) have [Hausdorff distance](../topological-analysis.md#hausdorff-distance) zero.

For the [triangle inequality](../topological-analysis.md#triangle-inequality), fix $a\in A$ and $\epsilon>0$. The [approximation property of an infimum](../real-analysis.md#approximation-property-of-an-infimum) supplies $b\in B$ with $d(a,b)<\operatorname{dist}(a,B)+\epsilon$, and then $c\in C$ with $d(b,c)<\operatorname{dist}(b,C)+\epsilon$. Consequently,

$$
\operatorname{dist}(a,C)\leq d(a,c)<\operatorname{dist}(a,B)+h(B,C)+2\epsilon.
$$

Let $\epsilon\downarrow0$ and take the [supremum](../real-analysis.md#supremum) over $a$ to obtain $h(A,C)\leq h(A,B)+h(B,C)$. Applying this inequality in the reverse direction as well yields

$$
\boxed{\rho(A,C)\leq\rho(A,B)+\rho(B,C)}.
$$

All [metric](../topological-analysis.md#metric) axioms follow. Boundedness was used to ensure finiteness, and closedness to distinguish different sets; neither was needed for the [triangle inequality](../topological-analysis.md#triangle-inequality).

<h4 id="1/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [b](#1/b)

<h5 id="1/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [ii](#1/b/ii)

The [intersection](../set.md#set-intersection) $G$ is a [closed set](../topology.md#closed-set) because arbitrary [intersections](../set.md#set-intersection) of [closed sets](../topology.md#closed-set) are closed. Its nonemptiness needs a construction: bounded closed sets need not be [compact sets](../topology.md#compact-space) in this setting.

Fix $n$, $x_n\in F_n$, and $\eta>0$. Recursively choose $x_{k+1}\in F_{k+1}$, for $k\geq n$, using the [approximation property of an infimum](../real-analysis.md#approximation-property-of-an-infimum) so that

$$
d(x_k,x_{k+1})<2^{-k}+\eta\,2^{n-k-1}.
$$

This is possible because $x_k\in F_k$ and $\operatorname{dist}(x_k,F_{k+1})\leq\rho(F_k,F_{k+1})\leq2^{-k}$. The two bounds form convergent [geometric series](../sequence-and-series.md#geometric-series), so the [triangle inequality](../topological-analysis.md#triangle-inequality) shows $(x_k)_{k\geq n}$ is a [Cauchy sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#cauchy-sequence). By [completeness](../topological-analysis.md#completeness) it converges to some $x\in X$.

For each fixed $j$, all sufficiently late $x_k$ lie in $F_j$, by nesting. Closedness of $F_j$ gives $x\in F_j$, and hence $x\in G$. In particular, starting with any $x_1\in F_1$ proves $G\ne\varnothing$. Moreover,

$$
d(x_n,x)\leq\sum_{k=n}^{\infty}\bigl(2^{-k}+\eta\,2^{n-k-1}\bigr)=2^{1-n}+\eta.
$$

For each starting point $x_n$, let $\eta\downarrow0$ in this bound on its [distance to a set](../topological-analysis.md#distance-to-a-set) $G$. This does not assert that a nearest point exists. It proves $\operatorname{dist}(x_n,G)\leq2^{1-n}$. Since $G\subseteq F_n$, the other directed [Hausdorff distance](../topological-analysis.md#hausdorff-distance) is zero, and

$$
\boxed{G\in\mathcal F(X),\qquad\rho(F_n,G)\leq2^{1-n}\longrightarrow0}.
$$

This is the [Summably nested Hausdorff intersection theorem](../topological-analysis.md#summably-nested-hausdorff-intersection-theorem); the summable movement replaces any need for [compactness](../topology.md#compact-space).

<h4 id="1/b/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [b](#1/b)

<h5 id="1/b/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [iii](#1/b/iii)

Let $(F_n)$ be a [Cauchy sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#cauchy-sequence) for the [Hausdorff distance](../topological-analysis.md#hausdorff-distance), and put

$$
\delta_n=\sup_{p,q\geq n}\rho(F_p,F_q),\qquad A_n=\overline{\bigcup_{m\geq n}F_m}.
$$

The [bounded set](../topological-analysis.md#bounded-set) hypothesis makes $\delta_n$ finite, and the [Cauchy sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#cauchy-sequence) property gives $\delta_n\to0$. Each $A_n$ is nonempty and a [closed set](../topology.md#closed-set), with $A_{n+1}\subseteq A_n$.

We need estimates that survive taking the [closure](../topology.md#closure-topology). For every nonempty $F$, the [distance to a set](../topological-analysis.md#distance-to-a-set) is [Lipschitz continuous](../real-analysis.md#lipschitz-continuity) with constant one: the [triangle inequality](../topological-analysis.md#triangle-inequality) gives $\operatorname{dist}(x,F)\leq d(x,y)+\operatorname{dist}(y,F)$, and interchanging $x,y$ proves

$$
|\operatorname{dist}(x,F)-\operatorname{dist}(y,F)|\leq d(x,y).
$$

For every $m\geq n$, points of $F_m$ have distance at most $\delta_n$ from $F_n$. The same holds throughout their union and its [closure](../topology.md#closure-topology), by this [continuity](../calculus.md#continuous-function). Since $F_n\subseteq A_n$,

$$
\rho(F_n,A_n)\leq\delta_n.
$$

More generally, if $m\geq n$, every point in $\bigcup_{k\geq n}F_k$ is within $\delta_n$ of $F_m\subseteq A_m$. Passing to its [closure](../topology.md#closure-topology) again, and using $A_m\subseteq A_n$, gives

$$
\rho(A_n,A_m)\leq\delta_n.
$$

Choose strictly increasing indices $n_j$ with $\delta_{n_j}\leq2^{-j}$. Then $(A_{n_j})$ satisfies part (ii), so it converges in [Hausdorff distance](../topological-analysis.md#hausdorff-distance) to the nonempty [closed set](../topology.md#closed-set) $G=\bigcap_j A_{n_j}=\bigcap_n A_n$, with $\rho(A_{n_j},G)\leq2^{1-j}$.

For $n\geq n_j$, the [triangle inequality](../topological-analysis.md#triangle-inequality) gives the explicit full-sequence bound

$$
\rho(F_n,G)\leq\rho(F_n,F_{n_j})+\rho(F_{n_j},A_{n_j})+\rho(A_{n_j},G)
\leq2\delta_{n_j}+2^{1-j}\leq2^{2-j}.
$$

It tends to zero as $j\to\infty$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{X\text{ complete}\ \Longrightarrow\ (\mathcal F(X),\rho)\text{ complete}}.
$$

This proves [Completeness of the Hausdorff hyperspace](../topological-analysis.md#completeness-of-the-hausdorff-hyperspace) using the proposed tail closures, rather than assuming their diameters shrink.

A [metric space](../topological-analysis.md#metric-space) $X$ is a [compact space](../topology.md#compact-space) if every [open cover](../topology.md#open-cover) $X=\bigcup_{\alpha\in I}U_\alpha$, with all $U_\alpha$ open in $X$, has a [finite subcover](../topology.md#finite-subcover): finitely many indices $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_r$ cover $X$.

It is a [sequentially compact space](../geometry-and-topology.md#sequentially-compact-space) if every [sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#sequence) $(x_n)$ in $X$ has a [convergent subsequence](../real-analysis.md#convergent-subsequence) $(x_{n_k})$ whose [limit of a sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#limit-of-a-sequence) belongs to $X$. In formulas,

$$
\boxed{\forall(x_n)\subseteq X\ \exists x\in X,\ n_1<n_2<\cdots:\ d(x_{n_k},x)\to0}.
$$

A [finite subcover](../topology.md#finite-subcover) is the defining test for [compactness](../topology.md#compact-space); a [convergent subsequence](../real-analysis.md#convergent-subsequence) is the defining test for [sequential compactness](../geometry-and-topology.md#sequentially-compact-space).

<h4 id="2/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [a](#2/a)

<h5 id="2/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [ii](#2/a/ii)

Let $(x_n)$ be any [sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#sequence) in the [compact metric space](../topological-analysis.md#compact-metric-space) $X$. There must be an $x\in X$ such that every [open ball](../topology.md#open-ball) about $x$ contains $x_n$ for infinitely many indices $n$. Otherwise, for each $x\in X$ choose a ball $B(x,r_x)$ containing only finitely many terms, counted by indices. These balls are an [open cover](../topology.md#open-cover). A [finite subcover](../topology.md#finite-subcover) would imply that only finitely many indices occur anywhere in $X$, a contradiction.

Now choose successively $n_k>n_{k-1}$ with $x_{n_k}\in B(x,1/k)$; infinitely many available indices make this possible. Then $d(x_{n_k},x)<1/k\to0$, so $(x_{n_k})$ is a [convergent subsequence](../real-analysis.md#convergent-subsequence). Thus

$$
\boxed{\text{compact metric space}\ \Longrightarrow\ \text{sequentially compact space}}.
$$

The proof counts indices rather than distinct points, so it also handles a value repeated infinitely often.

<h3 id="2/b">b</h3>

<h4 id="2/b/i">i</h4>

<h5 id="2/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [i](#2/b/i)

The source prints $m\ne0$ in the zero case. Since the coordinates are indexed by positive integers, that overlaps its $m=n$ case and does not define a sequence. We use the intended coordinate vectors, with $e_m^{(n)}=1$ for $m=n$ and $e_m^{(n)}=0$ for $m\ne n$.

Every $e^{(n)}$ belongs to $X$, because its entries are bounded by one and its coordinate sequence eventually vanishes. If $n\ne k$, their [supremum norm](../normed-vector-space.md#supremum-norm) distance is

$$
\boxed{d(e^{(n)},e^{(k)})=1}.
$$

Indeed, the difference is $1$ at coordinate $n$, $-1$ at coordinate $k$, and zero elsewhere. No subsequence is a [Cauchy sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#cauchy-sequence), since arbitrarily late distinct terms remain distance one apart. Every [convergent sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#convergent-sequence) is a [Cauchy sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#cauchy-sequence), so no [convergent subsequence](../real-analysis.md#convergent-subsequence) exists. Therefore $\boxed{X\text{ is not sequentially compact}}$.

<h4 id="2/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [b](#2/b)

<h5 id="2/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [ii](#2/b/ii)

Yes. Let $(x^{(k)})_{k\geq1}$ be any [sequence](../sequence-and-series.md#sequence) in $A$, where $x^{(k)}=(x_m^{(k)})_{m\geq1}$. At coordinate $m$, the values lie in the [compact interval](../real-analysis.md#compact-interval) $[-1/m,1/m]$. By part (a)(ii) we can successively extract a [convergent subsequence](../real-analysis.md#convergent-subsequence) for coordinate one, a further one for coordinate two, and so on. Taking the diagonal indices gives a single subsequence $(x^{(k_j)})$ for which

$$
x_m^{(k_j)}\longrightarrow x_m\quad\text{for every fixed }m.
$$

The [closed interval](../real-analysis.md#closed-real-interval) bound passes to the limit, so $|x_m|\leq1/m$. Hence $x_m\to0$ and $x=(x_m)\in A$.

Coordinate convergence alone would not imply [supremum norm](../normed-vector-space.md#supremum-norm) convergence. Here the common tail bound supplies the missing estimate. Given $\epsilon>0$, choose $M$ with $2/(M+1)<\epsilon$. For $m>M$,

$$
|x_m^{(k_j)}-x_m|\leq2/m\leq2/(M+1)<\epsilon.
$$

For the finitely many $m\leq M$, coordinate convergence makes all differences less than $\epsilon$ once $j$ is large. Therefore $d(x^{(k_j)},x)<\epsilon$, and

$$
\boxed{A\text{ is sequentially compact in the supremum norm metric}}.
$$

This is an instance of [compactness from uniformly vanishing sequence tails](../geometry-and-topology.md#compactness-from-uniformly-vanishing-sequence-tails). The common $1/m$ bound is exactly what the coordinate vectors in part (i) lack.

<h3 id="2/c">c</h3>

<h4 id="2/c/i">i</h4>

<h5 id="2/c/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [i](#2/c/i)

By the permitted [sequential compactness](../geometry-and-topology.md#sequentially-compact-space) of the [product metric](../topological-analysis.md#product-metric) space $X\times X$, the sequence of pairs $(\phi^{(k)}x,\phi^{(k)}y)$ has a [convergent subsequence](../real-analysis.md#convergent-subsequence), and hence a [Cauchy subsequence](../sequence-and-series.md#cauchy-subsequence). Choose two of its indices $r<s$ sufficiently late that

$$
d(\phi^{(r)}x,\phi^{(s)}x)+d(\phi^{(r)}y,\phi^{(s)}y)<\epsilon.
$$

The distance-increasing assumption iterates: $d(\phi^{(r)}u,\phi^{(r)}v)\geq d(u,v)$. With $n=s-r\geq1$, use $u=x,v=\phi^{(n)}x$, and then $u=y,v=\phi^{(n)}y$, to obtain

$$
d(x,\phi^{(n)}x)+d(y,\phi^{(n)}y)\leq d(\phi^{(r)}x,\phi^{(s)}x)+d(\phi^{(r)}y,\phi^{(s)}y)<\epsilon.
$$

Both summands are nonnegative, so

$$
\boxed{d(x,\phi^{(n)}x)<\epsilon,\qquad d(y,\phi^{(n)}y)<\epsilon}.
$$

The use of paired iterates ensures that the same positive return time works for both points.

<h4 id="2/c/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [c](#2/c)

<h5 id="2/c/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [ii](#2/c/ii)

Choose $n\geq1$ as in part (i). Since distances between iterates are nondecreasing, the [triangle inequality](../topological-analysis.md#triangle-inequality) gives

$$
d(\phi x,\phi y)\leq d(\phi^{(n)}x,\phi^{(n)}y)
\leq d(x,y)+d(x,\phi^{(n)}x)+d(y,\phi^{(n)}y)<d(x,y)+2\epsilon.
$$

Letting $\epsilon\downarrow0$ and combining with the given reverse bound proves that $\phi$ is an [isometry](../riemannian-geometry.md#isometry):

$$
\boxed{d(\phi x,\phi y)=d(x,y)\quad(x,y\in X)}.
$$

For surjectivity, fix $x\in X$ and use part (i) with $y=x$ and $\epsilon=1/k$ to choose $n_k\geq1$ satisfying $d(x,\phi^{(n_k)}x)<1/k$. Put $z_k=\phi^{(n_k-1)}x$, interpreting $\phi^{(0)}$ as the identity. By [sequential compactness](../geometry-and-topology.md#sequentially-compact-space), a [convergent subsequence](../real-analysis.md#convergent-subsequence) satisfies $z_{k_j}\to z\in X$. The assumed [continuity](../calculus.md#continuous-function) yields

$$
\phi z=\lim_j\phi z_{k_j}=\lim_j\phi^{(n_{k_j})}x=x.
$$

Thus every $x$ has a preimage, and $\boxed{\phi\text{ is surjective}}$. If $X$ is empty, both conclusions hold vacuously. This proves the [distance-increasing self-map theorem for sequentially compact metric spaces](../geometry-and-topology.md#distance-increasing-self-map-theorem-for-sequentially-compact-metric-spaces).

<h3 id="3/a">a</h3>

<h4 id="3/a/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [a](#3/a)

At every $z=x+iy\in U$, the [Cauchy-Riemann equations](../analysis.md#cauchy-riemann-equations) are

$$
\boxed{u_x=v_y,\qquad u_y=-v_x}.
$$

To derive them from [complex differentiability](../holomorphic-function.md#complex-differentiability), approach $z$ along real increments $h$. The [complex derivative](../holomorphic-function.md#complex-derivative) gives

$$
f'(z)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(z+h)-f(z)}h=u_x+iv_x.
$$

Approaching along imaginary increments $ih$ instead gives

$$
f'(z)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(z+ih)-f(z)}{ih}=v_y-iu_y.
$$

These limits exist because $f$ is [holomorphic](../holomorphic-function.md). Equating their real and imaginary parts proves the equations, together with $f'=u_x+iv_x=v_y-iu_y$.

<h3 id="3/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [b](#3/b)

A sufficient condition is that $U$ is a [connected space](../geometry-and-topology.md#connected-space). Suppose, towards a contradiction, that $|f|$ is the same constant $R$ throughout $U$. If $R=0$, then $f=0$, contradicting $f(a)\ne f(b)$.

If $R>0$, differentiate $u^2+v^2=R^2$ in $x$ and $y$, and use the [Cauchy-Riemann equations](../analysis.md#cauchy-riemann-equations) from part (a). They give

$$
uu_x+vv_x=0,\qquad vu_x-uv_x=0.
$$

The coefficient [matrix](../vector-space.md#matrix) for $(u_x,v_x)$ has [determinant](../multilinear-algebra.md#determinant) $-(u^2+v^2)=-R^2\ne0$. Hence $u_x=v_x=0$, so $f'=0$ everywhere. A [holomorphic function](../holomorphic-function.md) with zero [complex derivative](../holomorphic-function.md#complex-derivative) is locally constant: within a small disc, differentiate along a straight segment. Local constancy and [connectedness](../geometry-and-topology.md#connected-space) imply constancy on $U$, again a contradiction.

Consequently,

$$
\boxed{U\text{ connected and }f(a)\ne f(b)\ \Longrightarrow\ \exists c,d\in U:\ |f(c)|\ne|f(d)|}.
$$

This is the [constant-modulus holomorphic function](../holomorphic-function.md#constant-modulus-holomorphic-function) principle. The extra hypothesis matters: on two disjoint [open discs](../topology.md#open-disc), define $f=1$ on one and $f=-1$ on the other. It is [holomorphic](../holomorphic-function.md) and nonconstant, yet $|f|=1$ everywhere.

<h3 id="3/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [c](#3/c)

The function $h(z)=1/(z+1)$ is [holomorphic](../holomorphic-function.md) on $U$ and has

$$
\frac1{z+1}=\frac{x+1-iy}{(x+1)^2+y^2}.
$$

Its imaginary part is exactly the prescribed $v$. Thus $f-h$ is a real-valued [holomorphic function](../holomorphic-function.md). The [Cauchy-Riemann equations](../analysis.md#cauchy-riemann-equations) force both partial derivatives of its real part to vanish. Since $\mathbb C\setminus\{-1\}$ is a [connected space](../geometry-and-topology.md#connected-space), $f-h$ is a single real constant. All possibilities are therefore

$$
\boxed{f(z)=\frac1{z+1}+C,\qquad C\in\mathbb R}.
$$

Conversely every such function has the stated imaginary part. The data determine $f$ only up to this real additive constant.

<h3 id="3/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [d](#3/d)

The [identity theorem](../complex-analysis.md#identity-theorem) states: if $V\subseteq\mathbb C$ is a nonempty [connected](../geometry-and-topology.md#connected-space) [open set](../topology.md#open-set) and $p,q$ are [holomorphic functions](../holomorphic-function.md) on $V$, and their equality set has a [limit point](../topology.md#limit-point) in $V$, then

$$
\boxed{p=q\quad\text{throughout }V}.
$$

The interior location of the [limit point](../topology.md#limit-point) and [connectedness](../geometry-and-topology.md#connected-space) are essential hypotheses.

For the proof put $h=p-q$. At any $z_0\in V$, its convergent [Taylor series](../calculus.md#taylor-series) either has every coefficient zero, making $h$ zero on a neighbourhood, or has a first nonzero coefficient, of degree $m\geq0$. In the latter case,

$$
h(z)=(z-z_0)^m k(z),\qquad k(z_0)\ne0,
$$

where $k$ is [holomorphic](../holomorphic-function.md) near $z_0$. By [continuity](../calculus.md#continuous-function), $k$ is nonzero on a smaller disc. Thus any zero at $z_0$ is isolated unless $h$ vanishes on a neighbourhood. An accumulation of distinct zeros at the assumed interior [limit point](../topology.md#limit-point) therefore forces the neighbourhood alternative.

Let $E$ be the set of points of $V$ with a neighbourhood on which $h=0$. We have just proved $E\ne\varnothing$, and $E$ is an [open set](../topology.md#open-set). It is also a [closed set](../topology.md#closed-set) relative to $V$: a point of its [closure](../topology.md#closure-topology) outside $E$ would be approached by distinct points of $E$, hence by zeros, and the same [Taylor series](../calculus.md#taylor-series) argument would place it in $E$. Since $V$ is [connected](../geometry-and-topology.md#connected-space), $E=V$. Thus $h=0$ on $V$, proving the theorem.

<h3 id="3/e">e</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [3](#3)

<h4 id="3/e/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [e](#3/e)

Define $F(z)=\overline{f(\overline z)}$. To apply the [identity theorem](../complex-analysis.md#identity-theorem), we first verify that $F$ is [holomorphic](../holomorphic-function.md); mere [complex conjugation](../complex-analysis.md#complex-conjugation) of the values of $f$ would not suffice. For $h\ne0$,

$$
\frac{F(z+h)-F(z)}h
=\overline{\frac{f(\overline z+\overline h)-f(\overline z)}{\overline h}}
\longrightarrow\overline{f'(\overline z)}.
$$

Thus $F$ is an [entire function](../holomorphic-function.md#entire-function), just as $f$ is. At each positive integer $n$, the point $1/n$ is real and $f(1/n)$ is real, so $F(1/n)=f(1/n)$. These distinct points accumulate at $0\in\mathbb C$. The [identity theorem](../complex-analysis.md#identity-theorem) on the [connected](../geometry-and-topology.md#connected-space) [complex plane](../complex-analysis.md#complex-plane) gives

$$
\boxed{f(z)=\overline{f(\overline z)}\quad(z\in\mathbb C)}.
$$

This is [conjugate reflection of a holomorphic function](../holomorphic-function.md#conjugate-reflection-of-a-holomorphic-function), with the reality data forcing equality to its reflection.

<h3 id="4/a">a</h3>

<h4 id="4/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [a](#4/a)

<h5 id="4/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [i](#4/a/i)

A function has an [isolated singularity](../isolated-singularity.md) at $a$ if, for some $r>0$, it is defined and [holomorphic](../holomorphic-function.md) on the punctured disc $0<|z-a|<r$, while a [holomorphic](../holomorphic-function.md) extension through $a$ has not been assumed. In particular, its domain omits $a$, or its assigned value there fails to give such an extension. The key local condition is

$$
\boxed{f\text{ holomorphic on }B(a,r)\setminus\{a\}}.
$$

The convention includes [removable singularities](../isolated-singularity.md#removable-singularity), even when a suitable extension would eliminate the missing point.

<h4 id="4/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [a](#4/a)

<h5 id="4/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [ii](#4/a/ii)

An [isolated singularity](../isolated-singularity.md) at $a$ is a [removable singularity](../isolated-singularity.md#removable-singularity) when there is a [holomorphic function](../holomorphic-function.md) $F$ on a full disc $B(a,r)$ agreeing with $f$ away from $a$. Equivalently,

$$
\boxed{\lim_{z\to a}f(z)=L\in\mathbb C,\qquad F(a)=L}.
$$

The [Riemann removable singularity theorem](../isolated-singularity.md#riemann-removable-singularity-theorem) also characterizes this by boundedness of $f$ on a sufficiently small punctured disc. A preassigned incorrect value at $a$ is replaced by $L$.

<h4 id="4/a/iii">iii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [a](#4/a)

<h5 id="4/a/iii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [iii](#4/a/iii)

A [holomorphic function](../holomorphic-function.md) has a zero of order $n$ at $a$ when it is [holomorphic](../holomorphic-function.md) on a neighbourhood of $a$ and factors as

$$
\boxed{f(z)=(z-a)^n h(z),\qquad h\text{ holomorphic},\quad h(a)\ne0}.
$$

Equivalently, $f(a)=f'(a)=\cdots=f^{(n-1)}(a)=0$ and $f^{(n)}(a)\ne0$. The [order of a zero of a holomorphic function](../holomorphic-function.md#order-of-a-zero-of-a-holomorphic-function) is the degree of the first nonzero term of its [Taylor series](../calculus.md#taylor-series).

<h4 id="4/a/iv">iv</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [a](#4/a)

<h5 id="4/a/iv/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [iv](#4/a/iv)

A function has a [pole](../isolated-singularity.md#pole) of order $n$ at $a$ when, on a punctured neighbourhood,

$$
\boxed{f(z)=\frac{h(z)}{(z-a)^n},\qquad h\text{ holomorphic near }a,\quad h(a)\ne0}.
$$

Equivalently, $(z-a)^n f(z)$ has a [removable singularity](../isolated-singularity.md#removable-singularity) whose extension is nonzero at $a$. After shrinking the neighbourhood so $h\ne0$, its reciprocal $1/f$ extends with a zero of order $n$. In particular, $|f(z)|\to\infty$ at a [pole](../isolated-singularity.md#pole).

<h3 id="4/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#4)

<h4 id="4/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [b](#4/b)

We prove the [Casorati-Weierstrass theorem](../isolated-singularity.md#casorati-weierstrass-theorem) by contradiction. Fix an allowed $\rho$. If the stated image were not a [dense set](../topology.md#dense-set) in $\mathbb C$, there would be a disc $B(w,\epsilon)$ disjoint from it. Hence

$$
|f(z)-w|\geq\epsilon\qquad(0<|z-a|<\rho).
$$

The [holomorphic function](../holomorphic-function.md) $h(z)=1/(f(z)-w)$ is bounded by $1/\epsilon$ on that punctured disc. By the permitted [Riemann removable singularity theorem](../isolated-singularity.md#riemann-removable-singularity-theorem), it extends to a [holomorphic function](../holomorphic-function.md) $H$ on the full disc.

If $H(a)\ne0$, then $w+1/H$ is [holomorphic](../holomorphic-function.md) near $a$ and agrees with $f$ away from $a$. This makes $a$ a [removable singularity](../isolated-singularity.md#removable-singularity) of $f$.

If $H(a)=0$, then $H$ is not identically zero, since $h$ is nowhere zero away from $a$. Its [Taylor series](../calculus.md#taylor-series) therefore has a finite first nonzero coefficient: otherwise the [identity theorem](../complex-analysis.md#identity-theorem) would make it identically zero. Write $H(z)=(z-a)^m K(z)$ with $m\geq1$ and $K(a)\ne0$. Then

$$
f(z)=w+\frac{(z-a)^{-m}}{K(z)}
$$

has a [pole](../isolated-singularity.md#pole) of order $m$ at $a$, since multiplying by $(z-a)^m$ gives the nonzero limit $1/K(a)$.

Both cases contradict an [essential singularity](../isolated-singularity.md#essential-singularity). Thus

$$
\boxed{\overline{f(B(a,\rho)\setminus\{a\})}=\mathbb C\quad\text{for every allowed }\rho>0}.
$$

<h3 id="4/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#4)

<h4 id="4/c/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [c](#4/c)

Use the local factorization for a [pole](../isolated-singularity.md#pole) of order $n$:

$$
f(z)=(z-a)^{-n}h(z),\qquad h(a)\ne0.
$$

Shrink the disc so that the [holomorphic function](../holomorphic-function.md) $h$ has no zeros there. Differentiating and dividing gives the [logarithmic derivative](../analytic-number-theory.md#logarithmic-derivative)

$$
\boxed{\frac{f'(z)}{f(z)}=-\frac n{z-a}+\frac{h'(z)}{h(z)}}.
$$

The second term is [holomorphic](../holomorphic-function.md) across $a$. Since $n\geq1$, the first term cannot cancel. Hence $f'/f$ has a [simple pole](../isolated-singularity.md#simple-pole), and the coefficient of $(z-a)^{-1}$ in its [Laurent series](../analysis.md#laurent-series) is

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Res}_a(f'/f)=-n}.
$$

This is the pole case of the [integer residue of a logarithmic derivative](../complex-analysis.md#integer-residue-of-a-logarithmic-derivative).

<h3 id="4/d">d</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [4](#4)

<h4 id="4/d/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [d](#4/d)

Let $F=e^g$. It is [holomorphic](../holomorphic-function.md) and nowhere zero on a punctured neighbourhood of $a$, and the [chain rule](../calculus.md#chain-rule) gives

$$
\frac{F'}F=g'.
$$

If $F$ had a [pole](../isolated-singularity.md#pole) of order $n\geq1$, part (c) would imply $\operatorname{Res}_a(g')=-n$. But the derivative of a [holomorphic function](../holomorphic-function.md) on a punctured disc has [residue](../analysis.md#residue) zero. Indeed, write its [Laurent series](../analysis.md#laurent-series)

$$
g(z)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb Z}c_k(z-a)^k,\qquad g'(z)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb Z}k c_k(z-a)^{k-1}.
$$

Termwise differentiation is valid on every smaller closed annulus. The only possible $(z-a)^{-1}$ term comes from $k=0$ and has coefficient zero. Thus

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Res}_a(g')=0\ne-n},
$$

a contradiction. Therefore $\boxed{e^g\text{ cannot have a pole at }a}$. This proves [an exponential has no isolated pole](../isolated-singularity.md#an-exponential-has-no-isolated-pole), including when $g$ has an [essential singularity](../isolated-singularity.md#essential-singularity); no choice of a [holomorphic logarithm](../complex-analysis.md#holomorphic-logarithm) on the entire punctured disc was assumed.

The [simple zero](../holomorphic-function.md#simple-zero) assumption gives the local factorization

$$

where $k$ is [holomorphic](../holomorphic-function.md). In a small disc $k$ stays nonzero, so $q=g/k$ is [holomorphic](../holomorphic-function.md) there. Expanding $q$ to first order,

$$
\frac{g(z)}{h(z)}=\frac{q(z)}{z-a}=\frac{q(a)}{z-a}+\text{a function holomorphic near }a.

The [residue](../analysis.md#residue) is therefore

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Res}_a\left(\frac gh\right)=q(a)=\frac{g(a)}{h'(a)}}.
$$

If $g(a)=0$, the quotient has a [removable singularity](../isolated-singularity.md#removable-singularity) rather than a [simple pole](../isolated-singularity.md#simple-pole), and the same formula correctly gives zero. This is the [residue formula for a quotient at a simple denominator zero](../analysis.md#residue-formula-for-a-quotient-at-a-simple-denominator-zero).

<h3 id="5/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [5](#5)

<h4 id="5/b/solution">Solution</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [b](#5/b)

The [improper integral](../real-analysis.md#improper-integral) converges: its integrand is bounded near zero and at most $x^{-n}$ for $x\geq1$, which is integrable because $n\geq2$.

Set $\theta=\pi/n$, $\omega=e^{2i\theta}$ and $\zeta=e^{i\theta}$. Apply the [residue theorem](../analysis.md#residue-theorem) to $F(z)=1/(1+z^n)$ on the positively oriented sector contour consisting of the segment $0\to R$, the arc $Re^{it}$ for $0\leq t\leq2\theta$, and the segment $R\omega\to0$, with $R>1$. No [pole](../isolated-singularity.md#pole) lies on the boundary. Exactly one root of $z^n=-1$ is inside this sector, namely $\zeta$; it is a [simple pole](../isolated-singularity.md#simple-pole). Part (a) gives

$$
\operatorname{Res}_{\zeta}F=\frac1{n\zeta^{n-1}}=-\frac\zeta n.
$$

On the returning ray, put $z=\omega x$ with $x$ decreasing from $R$ to zero. Since $\omega^n=1$, the two ray contributions are

$$
\int_0^R\frac{dx}{1+x^n}+\int_R^0\frac{\omega\,dx}{1+x^n}=(1-\omega)I_R,
\qquad I_R=\int_0^R\frac{dx}{1+x^n}.
$$

On the arc, $|1+z^n|\geq R^n-1$, so the [estimation lemma](../complex-analysis.md#estimation-lemma) bounds its [contour integral](../complex-analysis.md#contour-integral) by

$$
\frac{2\pi R}{n(R^n-1)}\longrightarrow0.
$$

The [residue theorem](../analysis.md#residue-theorem) and the convergence already established now give

$$
(1-e^{2i\theta})I=-\frac{2\pi i}{n}e^{i\theta}.
$$

Using $1-e^{2i\theta}=-2ie^{i\theta}\sin\theta$, we conclude

$$
\boxed{\int_0^\infty\frac{dx}{1+x^n}=\frac\pi{n\sin(\pi/n)}\qquad(n\geq2)}.
$$

The sector also works for $n=2$, when it is the upper semicircle. Its angle is chosen so that $z^n$ has the same value on the two rays.

## 6

↑ **Parent:** [2023](2023.md)

<h3 id="6/a">a</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#6)

<h4 id="6/a/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [a](#6/a)

<h5 id="6/a/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [i](#6/a/i)

A [conformal map](../geometry-and-topology.md#conformal-map) at a point $z_0$ is a map that preserves oriented angles between regular differentiable curves through $z_0$. In the differential formulation, it is real differentiable at $z_0$ and its derivative is an orientation-preserving similarity: a rotation followed by multiplication of lengths by a positive constant. The map is conformal on $U$ when it has this property at every point:

$$
\boxed{D f(z_0)=\lambda R_\alpha,\qquad\lambda>0}.
$$

Here $R_\alpha$ is a planar rotation. Conformality is a local property and does not itself require global injectivity. We use the oriented convention standard in [complex analysis](../complex-analysis.md); a reflection reverses oriented angles.

<h4 id="6/a/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [a](#6/a)

<h5 id="6/a/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [ii](#6/a/ii)

At $z_0$, [complex differentiability](../holomorphic-function.md#complex-differentiability) gives

$$
f(z_0+h)=f(z_0)+f'(z_0)h+o(|h|).
$$

Writing $f'(z_0)=\lambda e^{i\alpha}$, the assumption $f'(z_0)\ne0$ gives $\lambda>0$. Its real derivative is therefore the similarity $h\mapsto\lambda e^{i\alpha}h$, a rotation through $\alpha$ followed by scaling by $\lambda$.

More explicitly, for two differentiable curves $\gamma_1,\gamma_2$ with $\gamma_j(0)=z_0$ and nonzero tangent vectors, the [chain rule](../calculus.md#chain-rule) gives

$$
(f\circ\gamma_j)'(0)=f'(z_0)\gamma_j'(0),\qquad
\frac{(f\circ\gamma_2)'(0)}{(f\circ\gamma_1)'(0)}=\frac{\gamma_2'(0)}{\gamma_1'(0)}.
$$

Taking the [complex argument](../complex-analysis.md#argument-complex-analysis) of this ratio shows that the oriented angle is preserved, while nonvanishing ensures both image tangents remain regular. Thus

$$
\boxed{f\text{ holomorphic and }f'\ne0\text{ on }U\ \Longrightarrow\ f\text{ conformal on }U}.
$$

<h3 id="6/b">b</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#6)

<h4 id="6/b/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [b](#6/b)

<h5 id="6/b/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [i](#6/b/i)

For $z=x+iy\in\mathbb H$, direct calculation gives

$$
|f(z)|^2=\frac{x^2+(y-1)^2}{x^2+(y+1)^2}<1.
$$

Conversely, solving $w=(z-i)/(z+i)$ gives

$$
z=i\frac{1+w}{1-w},\qquad\operatorname{Im}z=\frac{1-|w|^2}{|1-w|^2}>0\quad(|w|<1).
$$

The denominator cannot vanish inside the [unit disc](../topology.md#unit-disc), so these inverse formulas prove $\boxed{f(\mathbb H)=\mathbb D}$. This is the [Cayley transform between the half-plane and disk](../complex-analysis.md#cayley-transform-between-the-half-plane-and-disk).

For $z\in U$, we also require $|z|>1$. In inverse coordinates this becomes

$$
\frac{|1+w|}{|1-w|}>1\ \Longleftrightarrow\ |1+w|^2-|1-w|^2=4\operatorname{Re}w>0.
$$

The equivalence holds in both directions, so

$$
\boxed{f(U)=\{w\in\mathbb C:|w|<1,\ \operatorname{Re}w>0\}}.
$$

Thus the exterior of the unit semicircle maps to the right half of the [unit disc](../topology.md#unit-disc). The circular arc maps to its imaginary diameter, which is excluded because the source inequality is strict.

<h4 id="6/b/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [b](#6/b)

<h5 id="6/b/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [ii](#6/b/ii)

Let $V=f(U)$, the right half-disc from part (i). Write $w=re^{it}$ with $0<r<1$ and $-\pi/2<t<\pi/2$. Squaring doubles the angle and squares the radius, giving a [holomorphic](../holomorphic-function.md) bijection

$$
w\longmapsto w^2:\ V\longrightarrow W=\mathbb D\setminus(-1,0].
$$

Its inverse is the unique square root with positive real part, and its derivative $2w$ is nonzero on $V$. The cut includes zero, because $0\notin V$.

Next, the [Möbius transformation](../group-theory.md#mobius-transformation) $T(s)=i(1-s)/(1+s)$ is a [conformal](../geometry-and-topology.md#conformal-map) bijection from $\mathbb D$ onto $\mathbb H$: it is the inverse [Cayley transform between the half-plane and disk](../complex-analysis.md#cayley-transform-between-the-half-plane-and-disk) composed with $s\mapsto-s$. For $s=-t$, $0\leq t<1$, it gives

$$
T(-t)=i\frac{1+t}{1-t}.
$$

Consequently $T((-1,0])=\{iy:y\geq1\}$. Since $T$ is a bijection on the whole disc, deleting this interval deletes exactly that ray in the image.

The required [function composition](../function.md#function-composition) is therefore

$$
\boxed{g(z)=i\frac{1-f(z)^2}{1+f(z)^2}=\frac{2z}{1-z^2}}.
$$

Each component is a [conformal](../geometry-and-topology.md#conformal-map) bijection between the indicated domains. The only possible denominator zeros of the simplified expression are $z=\pm1$, outside $U$, and

$$
g'(z)=\frac{2(1+z^2)}{(1-z^2)^2}\ne0\quad(z\in U),
$$

since $i$ is on the excluded semicircle and $-i$ is outside the [upper half-plane](../complex-analysis.md#upper-half-plane-complex-analysis). This also checks the local [conformality](../geometry-and-topology.md#conformality) of the [conformal map from the exterior half-disc to a slit half-plane](../geometry-and-topology.md#conformal-map-from-the-exterior-half-disc-to-a-slit-half-plane).

<h3 id="6/c">c</h3>

↑ **Parent:** [6](#6)

<h4 id="6/c/i">i</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [c](#6/c)

<h5 id="6/c/i/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [i](#6/c/i)

Suppose $F:\mathbb D\to\mathbb C$ were a [holomorphic](../holomorphic-function.md) bijection. By the allowed nonvanishing derivative result, $F'(z)\ne0$ throughout $\mathbb D$. The [holomorphic inverse function theorem](../geometry-and-topology.md#holomorphic-inverse-function-theorem) gives a [holomorphic](../holomorphic-function.md) inverse locally at every image point. Since $F$ is globally bijective, these local inverses agree with its global inverse $H:\mathbb C\to\mathbb D$.

Thus $H$ is an [entire function](../holomorphic-function.md#entire-function) with $|H|<1$. The [Liouville theorem](../complex-analysis.md#liouville-theorem) forces $H$ to be constant, contradicting its being an inverse. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\text{there is no holomorphic bijection }\mathbb D\to\mathbb C}.
$$

This is the analytic obstruction behind the fact that the [complex plane and unit disc are homeomorphic but not conformally equivalent](../geometry-and-topology.md#complex-plane-and-unit-disc-are-homeomorphic-but-not-conformally-equivalent).

<h4 id="6/c/ii">ii</h4>

↑ **Parent:** [c](#6/c)

<h5 id="6/c/ii/solution">Solution</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [ii](#6/c/ii)

Yes. An explicit [holomorphic](../holomorphic-function.md) surjection is the [rational function](../isolated-singularity.md#rational-function)

$$
\boxed{F(z)=\left(\frac z{1-z}\right)^2,\qquad |z|<1}.
$$

Its denominator never vanishes inside the [unit disc](../topology.md#unit-disc). To prove surjectivity, take any $w\in\mathbb C$ and choose a square root $r$ of $w$ with $\operatorname{Re}r\geq0$; one of the two opposite roots always has this property. Define $z=r/(1+r)$. Then $1+r\ne0$ and

$$
|1+r|^2-|r|^2=1+2\operatorname{Re}r>0,
$$

so $|z|<1$. Also $z/(1-z)=r$, and consequently $F(z)=r^2=w$.

This is a [holomorphic surjection from the unit disc onto the complex plane](../holomorphic-function.md#holomorphic-surjection-from-the-unit-disc-onto-the-complex-plane). It does not contradict part (i): it is not injective. For example, $F(i/(1+i))=F(-i/(1-i))=-1$, with two distinct arguments of modulus $1/\sqrt2$.
